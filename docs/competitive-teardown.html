Competitive teardown · Strength training software

The Iron Ledger Brief

Seven apps, what each one solved, and what all of them left on the table — read as a build spec for a tracker that has to beat a spreadsheet before it beats anything else.

The teardowns

Every app here is good at exactly one thing and mediocre at the rest. That's the opportunity: nobody has assembled the good parts, because each company's business model quietly prevents it.

Strong

iOS · freemium
Does best

The logging loop. Set entry is genuinely frictionless — previous set pre-filled, rest timer auto-starts on completion, no confirmation taps. This is the interaction gold standard.

Falls short

Stops at data capture. Charts a lift and nothing more. Free tier caps custom routines. Development has been slow for years.

StealAuto-start the rest timer the instant a set is marked done. Never make the user press "start timer."

Hevy

iOS + web · freemium
Does best

Feel and polish. Clean typography, unlimited free logging, exercise animations, real cross-device web app. Most people's default recommendation for good reason.

Falls short

Reported to need a connection to log — brutal in a basement gym. Three-month graph history on free. A social feed users repeatedly say they didn't want.

StealThe visual density — large tap targets, generous spacing, high contrast. Skip the social layer entirely.

Boostcamp

iOS + web · free tier is real
Does best

Solves "what should I run?" for free. 11,000+ programs including the community classics — nSuns, GZCLP, PPL, 5/3/1 — with a desktop program builder that syncs to mobile.

Falls short

Program-centric to a fault. Weaker at freeform logging and at analysis once you're off-plan or improvising.

StealBuild programs on a big screen, run them on a small one. The two contexts need different interfaces.

Liftosaur

iOS + web · open source
Does best

Progression as code. Liftoscript expresses any progression rule you can describe — AMRAP-driven jumps, double progression, autoregulated backoffs. The only app with no ceiling on program logic.

Falls short

Demands a programmer's mindset. A beginner cannot use the thing that makes it special, so its best feature is invisible to most of its audience.

StealThe expressive progression engine — but drive it from a visual builder, with the script as an escape hatch underneath.

Alpha Progression

iOS + Android · ~$79.99/yr
Does best

The right primary metric. Tracks weekly sets per muscle group against evidence-based hypertrophy targets, rather than charting individual lifts in isolation. Uses logged RPE/RIR to adjust the next session.

Falls short

Everything meaningful sits behind the subscription, and the analysis is presented as verdicts rather than as something you can interrogate.

StealMuscle-group volume as the headline number. This is the single most important idea on this page.

RP Hypertrophy

~$34.99/mo · the science flagship
Does best

True autoregulation. Asks after each session about pump, soreness, and joint feel, then moves your volume inside MEV–MAV–MRV landmarks across a 4–6 week wave into a deload.

Falls short

A jargon wall on day one, an aggressive price, and reports of being web-only mid-session. It teaches the vocabulary after demanding you use it.

StealPost-session subjective feedback as an input to next week's volume. Leave the jargon behind.

Fitbod

iOS + Android · ~$95.99/yr
Does best

Zero-decision onboarding. Generates a session on the spot around your available equipment and recovery state. The lowest-friction start in the category.

Falls short

A fresh workout every time actively undermines progressive overload — you can't beat last week's numbers on a lift you didn't do last week. Convenience sold at the cost of the mechanism.

StealThe equipment-aware substitution prompt: "no cable machine today — here are three swaps that train the same thing."

Why the spreadsheet still wins

You set the bar at "better than Google Sheets," which is the correct bar and a harder one than it sounds. Serious lifters keep going back to spreadsheets despite a decade of apps, and it isn't nostalgia — it's four specific capabilities that no app on the list fully replicates.

What a spreadsheet gives you Why apps lose it
Whole-block visibility You see twelve weeks at once. Apps show you today, and maybe a chart. You cannot see the shape of your training.
Structural freedom Myo-reps, cluster sets, tempo work, drop sets, AMRAP finishers — apps model sets×reps×weight and choke on anything else.
Formulas you control Your own e1RM math, your own progression rule, visible and editable. Apps hide the logic and ask for trust.
Permanence The file is yours forever. No history paywall, no shutdown, no export request.

What spreadsheets are genuinely terrible at is the one moment that matters most: pinch-zooming a grid with chalky hands, ninety seconds into rest, trying to tap the right cell. No rest timer, no auto-fill of last week's numbers, no charts without building them yourself.

So the target isn't "a nicer spreadsheet" or "another Hevy." It's spreadsheet transparency with app ergonomics — keep the whole-block view and the visible math, add the ten-second logging loop.

Five failures shared by all of them

Data goes in, insight doesn't come out

You log two hundred sessions and get a line chart. Nothing says "your bench e1RM has been flat for five weeks while your volume climbed — that's a recovery problem, not an effort problem." The analysis layer is where every one of these apps stops.

Offline is an afterthought

Gyms are basements. An app that needs a connection to record a set is broken at the exact moment it's needed. This should be non-negotiable and it repeatedly isn't.

Analysis is per-exercise, not per-muscle

Hypertrophy responds to weekly sets per muscle group, but almost every app charts individual lifts. Your chest volume is spread across bench, incline, dips and flyes, and nothing sums it for you. Alpha Progression is the lone exception.

The jargon wall

Science-based apps open with MEV, MRV, RIR and stimulus-to-fatigue ratio, then explain them later or never. Beginners bounce. The knowledge is real; the sequencing is backwards.

You can't answer "is this working?"

The actual question every lifter has. It requires comparing a block against the block before it — volume, intensity, and outcome together. No app frames its data around the training block as a unit.

What to build differently

Ten seconds, one thumb, no connection

Hard budget for logging a set: under ten seconds, one-handed, offline. Pre-fill last session's weight and reps so the common case is a single tap to confirm. Rest timer starts itself. If any interaction pushes past ten seconds, that interaction is the bug — people either rush their logging or blow out their rest periods, and both corrupt the data you're collecting.

Weekly sets per muscle is the headline number

Make muscle-group volume the primary view, with per-exercise charts secondary. Every exercise maps to primary and secondary muscles; a set of incline press counts fully to chest and partially to front delts and triceps. Show the week's running total per muscle against a target band — the commonly cited hypertrophy range is roughly 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week, with individual variation. This one decision puts you ahead of every free app.

Show the math, and show its error bars

Estimated 1RM is the honest measure of strength progress, because it normalizes across rep ranges. Compute it, but be honest about where it breaks:

Epley   1RM = w × (1 + r / 30)    best at 2–5 reps Brzycki 1RM = w × 36 / (37 − r)  best at 6–10 reps

Both land within roughly 2–10% of a tested max inside the 2–10 rep window, and degrade badly above it. So pick the formula by rep count, and visually de-emphasize any e1RM derived from a set above 10 reps rather than plotting it as though it were equally trustworthy. Nobody else does this, and it's the difference between a number and an honest number.

Teach at the moment of use, never in a manual

The first time the app asks for RIR, it explains RIR — inline, in one sentence, with a tap for more. Not in an onboarding carousel, not in a help section. Every recommendation carries its "why" behind a disclosure: "Suggesting 3 sets not 4 — your chest volume is already at 18 sets this week." This is how you serve a new lifter and an advanced one from the same interface, and it's exactly what RP gets backwards.

The block is the unit of analysis

Build around training blocks, not infinite scroll. At the end of a block, produce a real summary: volume by muscle, e1RM change per lift, sessions completed, where you stalled. Then let people compare this block to the last one. That single screen answers "is this working?" — the question nothing on the market answers.

Model the set flexibly from day one

The reason spreadsheets survive is that apps hardcode weight × reps. Give a set optional fields for RIR/RPE, tempo, and a type flag (straight, drop, myo-rep, cluster, AMRAP, backoff). Retrofitting this later means a painful migration; allowing for it now costs a few extra nullable columns.

Build order

Sequenced deliberately — each tier is usable on its own, and each one earns the right to build the next.

1

Beat the spreadsheet

This alone clears the bar you set, and it's the tier most apps never get truly right.

  • Exercise library with custom exercises, each mapped to primary/secondary muscles
  • Fast offline set logging with last-session pre-fill and an auto-starting rest timer
  • Body weight tracking with a trend line, not just raw daily points
  • Per-exercise history and e1RM chart with the rep-range honesty above
  • Full data export — your permanence guarantee, and the promise no competitor makes
2

Programs and analysis

Where it stops being a log and starts being a training tool.

  • Program builder on desktop, execution on mobile
  • Progression rules — start with linear and double progression, leave room for expression later
  • Weekly volume per muscle group against target bands
  • Block summary and block-over-block comparison
  • Stall detection: flag a lift whose e1RM hasn't moved in three-plus weeks, and say what that usually means
3

Teach and guide

The differentiator, and the part that serves the new lifters you mentioned.

  • A small set of well-explained starter programs rather than a library of thousands
  • Just-in-time concept explanations wired to first use
  • Post-session check-in feeding next week's volume, in plain language — no landmark jargon
  • Deload prompting driven by accumulated fatigue and performance decrement
  • Equipment-aware exercise substitution

The one-sentence version

The logging loop is a solved, commoditized problem — copy Strong's and move on. The unsolved problem is turning a year of logged sets into an answer to "is this working?", and it's unsolved because the honest answer sometimes costs a subscription its renewal.

That's your opening, and it's also the reason to build rather than install: the analysis layer is the part everyone paywalls, and it's the part you actually want.
