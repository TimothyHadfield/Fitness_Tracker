# Q&A: Rest Times, Cardio Interference, Stretching, Drop Sets and Muscle for Health

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Are Drop Sets Dumb? Does Cardio Kill Gains? Stretch for Gains? Motivation? Career in 5 Years (Q&A)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OxGLA4cUezU)) · 2019-02-09 · 18 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **Rest 1-2 min** between hypertrophy sets of 6-15 reps; **3-5 min** for strength work under 6 reps; never under 1 minute even on high-rep work.
- Cardio interference is real at the molecular level but manageable at the human level. It scales with **frequency, duration and mode** — running interferes more than cycling or swimming.
- Drop sets are best justified on efficiency grounds; use them sparingly, late in a session, on small isolation muscles.
- Static stretching and mobility drills are only worth the time if a specific restriction stops you reaching full ROM on the lifts that matter.
- More natural muscle is essentially always good for health; the health risks come from excessive leanness, steroid abuse, or overtraining — not from muscle itself.

## Motivation on bad days
Jeff's rule: he simply doesn't skip the gym for having a bad day. A written program you can adhere to matters here — freestyling makes skipping far more tempting, whereas building trust in yourself to follow a program makes skipping progressively less likely. Accept that progress oscillates. Don't demand peak performance on a bad day and don't beat yourself up when the lifts are down; you'll bounce back. How you feel walking in is a poor predictor of the session — his worst-feeling days often produce his best workouts.

## Muscle mass and health
Most health benefits of lifting come from the resistance training and strength gained, not from muscle mass per se (Jeff flags this as opinion, not research-backed): improved bone mineral density, joint and ligament health, protection against age-related strength and muscle loss, insulin sensitivity, cardiovascular health.

There's likely no point at which *naturally* built muscle turns harmful. The cardiac problems seen in long-term steroid abusers come from carrying far more mass than a natural lifter can build. Rule of thumb: the more muscle you can carry naturally, the better, unless it comes with a very high body fat percentage.

The real health risks in fit-looking people:
- **Excessive leanness.** In men, below roughly **8-10% body fat**, expect low testosterone and a high rate of body image dysmorphia. In women it's worse: amenorrhea, reduced bone mineral density, increased osteoporosis risk.
- **Overtraining** to the point of injury, common in people fixated on maximising growth.

Looking fit is a decent general proxy for being healthier than a sedentary person, but it isn't the same thing as being healthy.

## Rest periods [05:36]
Frame rest around the variables that actually drive growth: volume (dose-response with hypertrophy, ceiling unknown), progressive overload, and a threshold of effort — roughly **RPE 7-8** — below which volume and overload don't matter much.

- **Too short** → not recovered enough to handle the same load, so volume drops.
- **Too long** → sessions drag out and intensity fades toward the end.

Recommendations:
- **6-15 reps (most hypertrophy work): 1-2 minutes.**
- **Under ~6 reps (strength): 3-4 minutes, up to 5 on a genuinely heavy set.**
- **High-rep work: still don't go under 1 minute** — the research consistently favours longer-rest groups for hypertrophy.

## Cardio and the interference effect [08:08]
**Baseline health guidelines (American Heart Association, 2015):** at least 30 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity 5 days/week (150 min total), or 25 min of vigorous activity 3 days/week. These are aimed at an average sedentary population and don't account for individual context.

Alan Aragon wrote a 2010 piece on the overlap between resistance training and cardiometabolic outcomes. Key takeaway: how much cardio you need depends on lifestyle. Someone in agricultural work has enormous NEAT — one study cited found over **2,000 kcal/day** from non-exercise activity — while a desk worker might get 200-300 kcal from fidgeting and will need more formal cardio for the same benefit. High-volume, high-rep resistance training also delivers cardiometabolic benefit on its own.

Jeff's own routine: ~30 min of walking 2-3x/week (which also helped his lower back and his ability to handle volume), plus pickup basketball 1-2x/week.

**Interference:** there's a documented molecular-level conflict between the pathways driving endurance adaptation and those driving hypertrophy. At the whole-human level it depends on many factors — genuine hybrid athletes with elite endurance and strength exist (he names Alex Viada). The more advanced you get in both modalities, the more intricate programming has to be. Three factors determine interference:
- **Frequency** — more often, more interference. Jeff keeps it to 2-3x/week.
- **Duration** — longer sessions, more interference.
- **Mode/impact** — running interferes more than cycling or swimming.

Keep cardio to an effective minimum, short, and low-impact.

## Flexibility and physique [12:14]
Jeff isn't aware of studies on flexibility and muscle growth. The old **FST-7** approach — pump the muscle full of blood then stretch it between sets to stretch the fascia and let more nutrients in — he considers bro science with no supporting research.

The real benefit of mobility is enabling a **full range of motion** on the basic movements, which is what optimises hypertrophy. Bodybuilding has a low mobility requirement: if you can reach depth on a squat, get into position on a bent-over row, and get into a sumo stance, you have enough.

Address specific deficits with specific drills — e.g. if you can't get wide enough for sumo, train adductor flexibility and foam roll hamstrings and inner thighs before deadlifting. Absent a specific restriction, don't spend time on general static stretching and mobility work.

## Drop sets [15:49]
Brad Schoenfeld published a review (Jeff recalls it as a systematic review, roughly 2017) on advanced techniques. Jeff's read: the evidence on drop sets is **underwhelming**, and the clearest benefit is **efficiency** — cramming volume into a short session.

There is a reasonable mechanistic case: if you take a set of six to failure, some slow-twitch motor units may not have been fully recruited; dropping the weight and continuing should recruit a fuller spectrum of motor units, potentially yielding more growth. This has not been fleshed out in training research.

Practical use:
- Reserve for **advanced lifters** as an intensity technique.
- Best fit in a **hypertrophy/metabolic-stress block** where you're training to failure, not a pure strength block.
- Best exercises: **leg extensions, preacher curls, lateral raises** — smaller muscles that tolerate the beating.
- Be conservative: they're harder to recover from, and doing them early in a session impedes volume accumulation on the heavy work that follows.

## Practical application
- Time your rests by rep range: 1-2 min for 6-15 reps, 3-5 min under 6 reps, never below 1 min.
- Train hard enough to matter — RPE 7-8 or above.
- Do 2-3 low-impact cardio sessions per week, kept short. Adjust for your NEAT: a desk job needs more, an active job needs less.
- Skip general stretching; fix only the specific restrictions blocking full ROM on your main lifts.
- Add drop sets late in a session, on isolation work, during hypertrophy-focused blocks only.
- Don't cut below ~8-10% body fat (men) for health reasons — leaner is not healthier.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Jeff explicitly flags his "benefits come from strength not mass" position as opinion with no supporting research.
- The volume dose-response ceiling is unknown.
- The motor-unit-recruitment rationale for drop sets is mechanistic theory, untested in training studies.
- No studies on flexibility and hypertrophy that Jeff was aware of.
