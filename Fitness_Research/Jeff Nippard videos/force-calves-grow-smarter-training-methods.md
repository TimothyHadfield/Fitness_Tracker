# Calf Training: Technique, Tempo and Rep Ranges That Actually Force Growth

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To FORCE YOUR CALVES To Grow With Smarter Training Methods" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-qsRtp_PbVM)) · 2019-03-19 · 7 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Train both a straight-leg and a bent-leg calf raise. The gastrocnemius crosses the knee, so it's better hit with the leg straight; the soleus crosses only the ankle, so it's better hit seated/bent-leg.
- Split rep ranges: at least one heavy day at 6-12 reps and one light day at 12-20+ reps. Seated (soleus) work skews even higher rep.
- Tempo is the whole exercise: 1-2 second pause at the top, ~2 second negative, and a mandatory 1-2 second pause in the fully stretched bottom position.
- The bottom pause exists to stop the elastic Achilles tendon from bouncing the load for you. Bouncing calf raises are the worst way to train calves.
- If your technique is currently a bounce, drop to bodyweight only for a few weeks and rebuild the tempo before adding load.

## Anatomy and mechanics
Every calf raise is ankle plantarflexion — pointing the toes down. That trains the two-headed gastrocnemius and the underlying but actually larger soleus.

The gastroc is biarticular: it crosses the ankle *and* the knee, so it also flexes the knee. That's why it's active during leg curls. Squats and deadlifts involve some plantarflexion so the calves get some work there, but the range of motion is small — direct calf training is necessary to maximise growth.

Because the soleus crosses only the ankle, it's better targeted with a bent-leg (seated) raise, and the gastroc with a straight leg. Both muscles work on any plantarflexion regardless of knee angle, so using both positions makes the most sense.

## Variations
- **Standing calf raise machine** — the demo movement, but what matters is the straight leg, not standing per se. Leg press and Smith machine work equally well.
- **Donkey calf raise** — easy to set up on a Smith machine; in some independent EMG data it showed the highest muscle activation of six variations tested.
- **Single-leg calf raise** — good for preventing one calf taking over and creating asymmetries.
- **Seated calf raise** — soleus emphasis, same principles, higher reps.
- **Calf jumps** — explode out of the stretched position where the calves are strongest, exploiting the natural strength curve and targeting the less abundant fast-twitch fibres. Far more room for form inconsistency, so keep slow controlled raises as the bread-and-butter work.

## Rep ranges [01:32]
There's merit in training across 6 to 20+ reps. The most recent evidence suggests both the gastroc and soleus are type I dominant, which favours higher reps — but the ankle moves through a full range in a biomechanically strong position, so excluding low-rep strength work would be negligent. Hence: one heavier day at 6-12, one lighter day at 12-20. Seated calf raises deserve slightly higher reps still, since the soleus is even more slow-twitch dominant.

## Setup and execution
1. **Priming sets.** One or two bodyweight-only sets through a full range to establish the mind-muscle connection and loosen the ankle joint.
2. **Full stretch available.** On a Smith machine, stand on plates or a riser so the heels can drop below the toes.
3. **Foot position.** Straight ahead or a slight flare. A 2011 study found that externally rotating the toes may increase medial (inner) gastroc activation, but later studies found no significant difference between foot positions — so use whatever is most comfortable.
4. **Balls of the feet on the edge** of the platform for a true full range. Barefoot is fine if the stretch or contraction feels better; shoes are fine provided they don't restrict ankle mobility (high-top Chucks are a bad choice).
5. **Flex the quads before the raise.** This fully extends the knees, keeping the emphasis on the gastroc, and prevents cheating by "squatting" the weight up with the quads.
6. **Bottom position** should feel like the calves are being pulled apart, similar to the hamstrings at the bottom of a Romanian deadlift.
7. **Concentric:** press the toes down into the ground as the shoulders rise against the pads, without shrugging. Knees and hips stay perfectly straight and locked.
8. **Top:** pause a full 1-2 seconds, forcefully squeezing.
9. **Eccentric:** ~2 seconds, keeping tension and resisting on the way down.
10. **Bottom pause:** 1-2 seconds — count "one Mississippi, two Mississippi" out loud or in your head so you don't get lazy. Springing out of the stretch lets the highly elastic Achilles tendon carry momentum and reduces the calf muscle's contribution.

**Seated calf raise specifics:** don't use your grip to help boost the weight, and don't create momentum by leaning back at the hips on the way up. Nippard leans slightly back and grips the sides of the seat to resist the temptation to cheat.

## Common errors
**Partial range / bouncing.** Most people bounce between concentric and eccentric, letting the Achilles reflex take the ankle about halfway up and then dropping without active resistance. It looks hard but the tendons, not the muscles, are doing the work. Fix: slow down, pause in the full stretch, count every phase.

**Going too heavy.** Especially problematic here because calf training is so technique-sensitive. If you aren't already doing the above, start with bodyweight only for at least a few weeks to groove a controlled tempo before adding any load — and once you do load, never at the expense of tempo and technique.

## Practical application
- Two calf sessions per week: heavy day 6-12 reps, light day 12-20 reps, with at least one straight-leg and one bent-leg movement across the week.
- Prescribe tempo explicitly on the calf raise: 2s down, 1-2s pause stretched, controlled up, 1-2s squeeze at top.
- Use a riser so the heels can drop fully; flex the quads to lock the knees before each rep.
- Consider donkey calf raises and single-leg work as variations; keep calf jumps as an accessory only.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The EMG data ranking donkey calf raises highest is described as "independent," not a named published trial, and EMG activation is not the same as growth.
- The foot-position question is unresolved: the 2011 finding for toes-out and medial gastroc was not upheld by later studies.
