# Constant Tension Training and Cryotherapy vs Cold Water Immersion

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Is Constant Tension Training Legit? | Cryotherapy Completely Debunked" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WLMIfMewXgI)) · 2017-07-01 · 5 min
**Topic:** Recovery

## Bottom line
- Whole-body cryotherapy showed **no benefit at any time point on any recovery measure** — cold water immersion beat it outright.
- Cold water immersion itself is questionable: a plain **10-minute bike cooldown** matched or beat it, and CWI *blunted* long-term lean mass and strength gains.
- A 2017 skull crusher study found the partial-ROM constant-tension group gained **nearly twice the triceps cross-sectional area** as the full-ROM group over 8 weeks.
- Constant tension is best applied to exercises with a circular resistance path (pec flyes, skull crushers) by stopping just shy of lockout.
- Both pieces come from the July 2017 issue of MASS (Monthly Applications in Strength Sport).

## Cryotherapy vs cold water immersion [00:31]
Summarised by **Dr. Mike Zourdos** from a 2017 paper ([Abaïdia 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/27396361)). Ten men were split into two groups. Both performed muscle-damaging exercise — five sets of eccentric-only leg curls — then completed recovery tests (perceived muscle soreness, perceived recovery, creatine kinase). They then received either:

- **Cold water immersion (CWI):** bath at 10°C for 10 minutes, or
- **Whole-body cryotherapy:** chamber at −110°C for 3 minutes.

Recovery tests were repeated at 24, 48 and 72 hours.

**Result:** CWI outperformed cryotherapy overall. Cryotherapy had **no effect size in its favour at any time point for any measure**.

Proposed mechanisms for how body cooling might aid recovery: vasoconstriction reducing blood flow and therefore inflammation, and reduced cytokine activity dampening nociceptor (pain receptor) signalling.

### But is cold water immersion even worth doing?
Two further findings undercut CWI:

- A 2017 paper in the *Journal of Physiology* found CWI gave **no additional inflammatory reduction** over a simple 10-minute cycling cooldown after training ([Peake 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/27704555)).
- **Roberts and colleagues (2015)** found the 10-minute bike cooldown produced **more lower-body lean mass and strength gains** than CWI ([Roberts 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/?term=26174323)) — likely because cold water attenuates the anabolic signal and reduces muscle protein synthesis.

Zourdos's conclusion: cold exposure may have short-term value for athletes who accumulate heavy muscle damage and train multiple times per day (strongman competitors, CrossFitters). For most lifters, a simple **10-minute cooldown is the most practical recovery method**.

## Constant tension training [02:34]
Summarised by **Greg Nuckols** from a 2017 paper by **Goto and colleagues** (name from auto-captions as "goo" — spelling uncertain).

**The gap this filled:** earlier ROM literature favoured full range of motion, but it compared full to *truncated* ranges — e.g. a full squat vs a half squat, both starting from the same bottom. That isn't how constant tension is actually used in the gym. In practice, lifters keep tension by working the *middle* of the range: stopping just shy of parallel and never fully locking out at the top.

**Design:** skull crushers. One group trained full ROM, the other trained a partial range covering roughly the **middle third** of the full range. Eight weeks.

**Result:** the constant-tension group gained **nearly twice the triceps cross-sectional area** as the full-ROM group ([Goto 2017](http://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/Abstract/publishahead/Partial_range_of_motion_exercise_is_effective_for.95936.aspx)).

**Proposed mechanism:** hypoxia — reduced oxygen in the muscle can signal for growth. Nuckols's objection: if hypoxia alone explained it, you'd expect to see comparable differences in the blood flow restriction literature, and you simply don't.

**Nuckols's alternative explanation:** the way the exercise was executed may have had the partial-ROM group training harder or with higher relative loads, because of how rep cadences were standardised.

## Practical application
- **Recovery:** default to a **10-minute low-intensity bike cooldown** after training. Skip cryotherapy chambers entirely. Reserve cold water immersion for high-damage, multiple-sessions-per-day scenarios, and avoid it when hypertrophy is the goal.
- **Constant tension:** apply it selectively to exercises with a **circular resistance path** — pec flyes and skull crushers are the named examples. Stop slightly shy of lockout to keep the muscle loaded. This is not a blanket argument for partial reps on all exercises.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The cryotherapy/CWI study used only 10 men and measured a single training bout — nothing is known about repeated use over time.
- The constant-tension result is a single 8-week study on one exercise. The near-twofold difference is impressive but the confound (relative load / training effort from standardised cadence) is unresolved.
- The researcher name for the constant-tension paper is garbled in the auto-captions; treat the spelling as uncertain.
