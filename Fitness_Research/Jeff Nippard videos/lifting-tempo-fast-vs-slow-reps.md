# Lifting Tempo: How Fast Should You Move the Weight?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Fast or Slow Reps for Muscle Growth? | Lifting Tempo | Fundamental Series Ep 6" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TQxMvpe2lQ8)) · 2018-10-21 · 11 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Tempo is the **lowest-priority** variable in the hypertrophy hierarchy — below volume, intensity, frequency and rest. Don't overthink it.
- Anything from **0.5 to 8 seconds per rep** produces statistically similar hypertrophy. There is no magic tempo.
- Reps taking **longer than 10 seconds are worse** for hypertrophy, because you have to strip the load so far that mechanical tension drops.
- Default to **1-0-1-0 or 2-0-2-0** with full range of motion and controlled (not gravity-dropped) eccentrics.
- Use deliberate tempo prescriptions only when you have a specific goal: power, eccentric overload, technique repair, or metabolic stress.

## What the research says
[01:32] Schoenfeld, Ogburn and Krieger (2014) ran a meta-analysis pooling studies across different lifting tempos ([Schoenfeld 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/25601394)). Across the range from **0.5 s per rep up to 8 s per rep**, hypertrophy was statistically equivalent. An 8-second rep might be a 4 s eccentric plus a 4 s concentric — that grew muscle about as well as very fast reps did.

[02:34] The one clear finding against tempo extremes: reps lasting **over 10 seconds** were worse for growth ([Schoenfeld 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/25601394)). The likely mechanism is load reduction — super-slow positives and negatives force the weight down so far that the tensile stimulus falls off.

The practical implication is permission to be creative. Since a wide band of tempos works, tempo becomes a tool you can vary by exercise and by training goal rather than a number you must obey.

## Reading tempo notation
[03:06] Four digits, each a phase of the rep. Using a dumbbell chest press and **2-0-2-0**:
1. First digit — **eccentric** (lowering): 2 seconds down.
2. Second digit — **pause at the bottom** (stretched position): 0, so reverse immediately.
3. Third digit — **concentric** (lifting): 2 seconds up.
4. Fourth digit — **pause at the top** (shortened position): 0, no squeeze.

An **X** in the concentric slot means "move as explosively as possible."

## Tempo prescriptions by goal

**Power / getting stronger** — [05:09] Squat at **1-0-X-2**: 1 s down, no pause in the hole, explode out of the bottom, then 2 s at the top to reset your breath. Nippard says this is almost without exception what he uses for heavy squats. Slowing the eccentric too much on the squat kills the explosive concentric; a bottom pause is useful for technique but is not very transferable to powerlifting and also blunts speed out of the hole. Bench equivalent: **1-1-X-2** — 1 s down, 1 s pause on the chest, explode, 1-2 s at the top.

**Eccentric overload** — [06:40] He notes this is ahead of the literature, so treat it as conceptually sensible rather than proven. Lat pulldown at **3-0-1-0**, where the 3 s is the return phase (the eccentric on a pulldown is the bar travelling up). Rationale: you are stronger eccentrically than concentrically, so slowing that phase — or adding resistance from a partner — loads a phase that normal loading under-stresses.

**Technique repair** — [07:43] Slow eccentrics on compounds force positional discipline. A 3 s squat eccentric can reinforce staying upright if you tend to pitch forward; a bottom pause kills momentum and forces an upright torso. He recommends slow eccentrics mainly on **squat and bench**, and explicitly does **not** recommend them for the deadlift.

**Metabolic stress / pump** — [08:14] Whether metabolic stress genuinely drives hypertrophy is controversial, but he sees merit and no downside to a pump. Calves at **3-1-3-1**: 3 s down, 1 s pause in the stretch (he says always pause at the bottom on calves), 3 s up, 1 s squeeze at the top. Note this totals 8 seconds — **about as slow as he would ever prescribe**, since past that mark hypertrophy suffers.

## Why tempo ties back to form
[00:31] Tempo connects directly to the highest-priority item in the series: safety and form. Uncontrolled lifting raises injury risk and reduces what you get out of the exercise. The two things that define proper form here are **full range of motion** (chest press: bar to the chest, then to full extension) and **control on the eccentric** — actively resisting gravity rather than letting the weight fall.

## Practical application
- For the great majority of your sets, run **1-0-1-0** or **2-0-2-0** and stop thinking about it.
- You may shave the concentric or eccentric to 0.5 s if you want to hunt a squeeze at the top or a stretch at the bottom — control on both phases is the only non-negotiable.
- Heavy squats and bench: eccentric under control, explosive concentric (X), longer top pause for breathing.
- Reserve 8-second total reps for isolation/pump work such as calves. Never program reps beyond ~10 seconds.
- If a technique fault keeps showing up on squat or bench, add a 3 s eccentric or a bottom pause for a block.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Eccentric-overload tempos are, by his own admission, ahead of the evidence — the conceptual case is stronger than the data.
- Whether metabolic stress is a genuine hypertrophy driver is unsettled in the field.
- Tempo sits near the bottom of the priority list; gains from manipulating it are small relative to volume, load and effort. Its main value is making training more interesting and more specific.
