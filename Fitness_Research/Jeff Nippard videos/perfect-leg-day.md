# The Perfect Leg Day: Six Movements for Quads, Hamstrings, Glutes and Calves

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Perfect Leg Day (According To Science)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8zWDuWKdBZU)) · 2023-08-07 · 7 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- One heavy deadlift top set of 5 at RPE 8-9, then back off to two sets of 8 stiff-legged deadlifts at roughly 50-60% of the top-set load.
- Quad volume comes from 4x10-12 leg press with strict rest caps of two minutes, plus 3x8-10 leg extensions with a 3-second eccentric to catch the rectus femoris.
- Hamstrings get 3x8-10 glute ham raises; calves 4x15-20 seated; abs 3x10-20 Roman chair leg raises.
- Do one calf exercise with a straight knee and one with a bent knee, though the soleus evidence is now shakier than it used to be.

## Session structure
5-10 min treadmill or StairMaster, then lower-body-specific dynamic stretching for knees and hips.

1. Deadlift — 1 top set of 5 (after a warm-up pyramid)
2. Stiff-legged deadlift — 2x8
3. Leg press — 4x10-12
4. Glute ham raise — 3x8-10
5. Slow-eccentric leg extension — 3x8-10
6. Seated calf raise — 4x15-20
7. Roman chair leg raise — 3x10-20

## Deadlift top set [00:31]
Take it to RPE 8-9 — no more than one or two reps left. Because the volume is a single set, it has to be pushed reasonably close to failure, but not so hard that it wrecks the remaining leg work.

Substitutions if you dislike conventional pulls: trap bar deadlift (more upright torso, more quad), barbell hip thrust (glute-dominant), or Bulgarian split squat (quad-dominant with a forward lean shifting some emphasis to glutes and hams).

If you want deadlift strength, run linear periodization across six weeks: 5 reps week 1, 4 in week 2, 3 in week 3, 2 in week 4, a 1RM in week 5, then deload in week 6 with 4 reps at a lighter load, adding weight each week. If you only care about hypertrophy, stay at 5 reps and progress load or technique week to week.

## Stiff-legged deadlift [01:32]
Nippard drops from around 405 lb on the top set to 225 lb here — about 50-60% of working weight is the right ballpark. Treat it as a conventional deadlift with high hips: keep hips high, knees mostly straight, initiate by hinging. Start light and stay away from failure until the technique feels natural.

## Leg press [02:02]
Medium stance on the platform. Chase depth on every rep — as deep as you can go without the lower back noticeably rounding. He keeps a slight knee bend at the top rather than locking out (locking out is not actually dangerous — he defers to a Mike Israetel video on that — but the constant-tension approach lets him feel the quads better). Smooth consistent cadence, no pauses between reps.

Cap rest at two minutes. Not because shorter rest grows more muscle, but because long rests drag the session out and focus falls apart by set three or four.

## Glute ham raise [03:03]
He uses a GHR machine with a counterbalance weight to reduce the load, which makes the movement more controllable than bodyweight. Alternatives: set up on the back of a lat pulldown machine using a stick, broom handle, or lat bar as a counterweight, or have a partner pin your ankles and do Nordic ham curls — control the negative, push yourself up with your hands at the bottom.

He stays in the "active range," cutting out the top quarter where hamstring tension goes to zero.

## Slow-eccentric leg extension [04:03]
The leg press already grew the quads, but the rectus femoris is the only quad head crossing both knee and hip, so it never reaches full contractile potential in compounds — and squats are worse still, because the hips reach full extension at the top. He recommends leg extensions at least once per week for this reason.

Use a 3-second count on every negative. Two payoffs: the eccentric is likely more hypertrophic than the concentric, and slowing it forces lighter loads, which spares the knees without costing stimulus.

## Seated calf raise [05:04]
He is more ambivalent about seated calf raises than he used to be. A 2020 study found the soleus responds poorly to both high-rep and low-rep training, so the old "target the slow-twitch soleus with high reps" logic looks outdated. The soleus appears substantially less responsive to training than the gastroc.

Arguably you would be better off putting that volume into straight-leg work to involve the gastroc. He thinks swapping seated for standing calf work is defensible, but wants a long-term hypertrophy study comparing soleus and gastroc growth on seated vs standing before dropping seated entirely. Until then: include both, one bent-knee and one straight-knee. If you have no seated machine, you are probably not missing much.

Rep range for calves does not appear to matter much; he uses higher reps here purely for variety after lower-rep work earlier in the week.

## Roman chair leg raise [06:06]
The 10-20 range is wide because proximity to failure varies enormously — some people are near failure at 10, others not until 20. Stop when you can no longer bring your legs to chest level without excessive swinging. Cue: curl the lower back and squeeze the abs, don't just lift the legs.

If you cannot manage 10 straight-leg reps in good form, bend the knees and transition to straight legs as core strength builds. If 20 is easy, slow the negative to a 2-3 second count.

## Practical application
Run this as the second leg day of a push/pull/legs week (it pairs with a lower-rep first leg day). Track the deadlift top set and the leg press load; those are the progression drivers. Keep rest tight on leg press. Add the leg extension even if squats and presses feel like enough — the rectus femoris case is the reason.

## Caveats & unknowns
The seated calf raise recommendation is explicitly provisional: the soleus data undercuts the rationale, and the decisive standing-vs-seated hypertrophy study does not exist yet. The active-range cue on glute ham raises he calls a minor preference, not a big deal.
