# Is Dorian Yates-Style HIT (One Set To Failure) Good For Size?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Is Dorian Yates HIT (Low Volume) Training Good For Size?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gd0zm91f9nk)) · 2019-03-10 · 3 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- Very high effort and very high volume don't mix; pick one per training block.
- If you go low volume, sets must be taken to or very near failure to make them count.
- If you go high volume, cut back on failure training and run lower RPEs.
- Nippard leans toward higher volume for hypertrophy but treats the low-volume/high-intensity literature as legitimate, not fringe.
- Best answer is periodisation across the year rather than committing permanently to one camp.

## The question
Dorian Yates-style HIT — roughly one all-out working set to failure per exercise, in the lineage of Mike Mentzer and Arthur Jones. Yates himself credits that low-volume, high-intensity style for the hard, grainy look of 90s-era bodybuilders.

## Does HIT explain the "grainy" 90s look?
Nippard isn't convinced. Too many confounders: drug protocols have changed considerably since then, and modern competitors tend to look more bloated for reasons unrelated to set counts. There are also very grainy, dense-looking lifters (he names Branch Warren) who don't train in a Yates-like style. He doesn't rule it out, but there's no clean case for training style being the cause.

## The two research camps
- **Higher-volume camp:** researchers such as Brad Schoenfeld and James Krieger, whose work generally supports a dose-response relationship between weekly volume and muscle growth.
- **Lower-volume camp:** James Fisher and James Steele (Nippard says on camera he can't recall their first names). They argue you capture most of the hypertrophic return from a single all-out set to failure — far closer to what Yates advocated.

Nippard says he's open to both bodies of literature and interviews researchers from the volume side, but doesn't treat the low-volume work as refuted.

## How to reconcile the conflicting results
The practical resolution is that intensity of effort and volume trade off against each other:

- Choose the HIT route and you are more or less forced into low volume — and then failure training is essential, because with so few sets each one has to deliver a full stimulus.
- Choose high volume and you must back off effort: train to failure less often and keep RPE relatively lower.
- Stacking both — many sets, every set to failure — is where injury and overtraining risk appears.

## Practical application
- Low-volume block: few working sets per exercise, each taken to failure.
- High-volume block: more sets, most stopped short of failure, lower average RPE.
- Alternate the two across a training year as a macrocycle — periods of higher-volume/lower-intensity work followed by Yates-style low-volume/high-intensity blocks.
- Don't pigeonhole yourself as "only HIT" or "only high volume"; locking into one style is unlikely to maximise long-term potential.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This is a short Q&A answer with no specific set/rep prescriptions or study citations beyond naming the researchers involved.
- The claim that training style drives the "grainy" bodybuilding look is untested and confounded by drug protocols and genetics.
- No guidance is given on how long each block should last — only that cycling them intelligently over a calendar year is the aim.
