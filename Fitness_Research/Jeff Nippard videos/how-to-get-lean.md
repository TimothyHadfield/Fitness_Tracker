# How To Get Lean: Five Experts on Fat Loss That Sticks

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Smartest Way To Get Lean (Shredding Science Explained)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d8V9ZaSq9Oc)) · 2021-02-01 · 18 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Fat loss is an energy deficit, full stop — but the goal is losing *fat*, not weight, so lift, keep the deficit moderate, and eat plenty of protein.
- Target a deficit of **0.5–1.0% of body weight per week**, reserving the top of the range (up to ~1.5%) only for people who are already high in body fat.
- Protein: **above 2 g/kg/day**; a quick alternative rule for high-body-fat people is grams of protein = your height in centimetres.
- Cap cardio at roughly **half your resistance-training volume** — no more than 30 min low-intensity per day and 1–2 HIIT sessions per week.
- No supplement causes fat loss. The useful ones (creatine, whey, fish oil, multivitamin/vitamin D) are the same ones you'd take anyway.

## Eric Helms' five keys, in priority order
[00:30] Helms has published peer-reviewed work on physique-athlete recommendations, protein during caloric restriction, and binge eating. His ranked list:

1. **Energy deficit.** Non-negotiable. (Edge cases exist — recomping at maintenance, or a novice losing fat in a small surplus — but energetically the deficit is what drives fat loss.)
2. **Progressive resistance training.** The single best tool for signalling the body to keep muscle rather than burn it.
3. **Appropriate deficit size.** 0.5–1.5% body weight per week; only go near the high end at high body fat.
4. **Sufficient protein.** >2 g/kg/day, or use height in cm as a gram target so you don't overshoot relative to lean mass.
5. **Appropriate cardio volume.** Don't take the whole deficit from cardio. Avoid high-impact/high-eccentric modalities (lots of running is a poor choice) and don't do it right before lifting.

## Food quality vs. macros
[03:02] Both positions are partly right. Calories and macros decide the outcome, so it does come down to hitting numbers. But highly processed foods have a **lower thermic effect of food (TEF)**, so their energy-output side is smaller — that just means you'd have to lower macros further to compensate. It still resolves to energy balance.

The real argument for food quality is adherence: mostly single-ingredient foods (fruit, vegetables, lean protein, a carb source) improve satiety, preserve energy output and keep hunger signalling under control. Helms warns against making foods off-limits — a hard ban triggers the "rebellious teenager" response. Keep an "adult card": consciously choose to fit in a treat when it helps you follow the diet.

## Getting beach lean vs. stage shredded (Cliff Wilson)
[04:35] Wilson coaches natural bodybuilders (clients: 125 pro cards, 87 pro titles, 13 world championships). His view: the methods for beach-lean and contest-shredded are the same, only the **degree and duration** differ. You can be somewhat inconsistent and still get beach lean; you cannot be inconsistent and get shredded. Diet duration plus diligence is the single biggest factor. He does not recommend contest leanness for health-oriented people.

**Stubborn fat:** two things matter. First, a better-developed muscle group looks leaner because the same fat is spread over a larger area — one reason muscular people look leaner overall. Second, stubborn fat distribution is largely genetic and sex-linked, and there is no trick: keep dieting and the body eventually has to pull from that area. The catch is that reaching that point means significant discomfort, and the first time someone gets that lean they are almost bound to lose some muscle and look "stringy." Subsequent attempts are less traumatic mentally and physically because the person knows what to expect.

## Psychology and habits (Sohee Lee)
[08:08] Lee's master's thesis was on eating-behaviour psychology. Common failure pattern: people want to hear "cut out dairy, go keto, do intermittent fasting" — none of which are *wrong*, but none of which are *required*. They pick the plan that looks perfect on paper instead of the one that fits their actual lifestyle, over-rely on early motivation, underestimate the difficulty, quit at two or three weeks, and move to the next thing ("shiny object syndrome").

The fix is habits over willpower. Research shows self-control is very useful for *forming* habits but is a poor long-term strategy; the defining feature of a habit is that it requires no cognitive effort. Practical version — **choice architecture / "designing for laziness":**
- Put calorie-dense foods somewhere annoying (top of the pantry, back of a cupboard).
- Move a visible bowl of sweets into an opaque container, a drawer, or across the room — intake drops substantially without conscious effort.

## Why most diets fail (Layne Norton)
[11:10] No diet type wins when calories are equated — Mediterranean, low-carb and plant-based perform about the same. Adherence is poor across the board and more people regain weight than keep it off.

Five behaviours common to successful long-term maintainers:
- **Cognitive restraint** in some form — calorie counting, time-restricted eating, points systems, carb or fat restriction.
- **Regular self-monitoring** — frequent weigh-ins. Not for day-to-day noise; for spotting a weeks-to-months trend and correcting it.
- **Regular exercise** — good evidence it sensitises you to satiety signals.
- **Structured programs** — a coach or dietitian, but even commercial programs beat winging it with no plan.
- **Low recency** — valuing long-term data over short-term feeling. In one recent study this was the strongest characteristic associated with maintenance: choosing the goal over the doughnut.

Norton adds that after reaching goal weight you should be able to maintain on **more calories than you ate while losing**, and that restraint gets easier as habits build. Nippard's own experience: flexible dieting (eat what you want if it fits macros) fixed his adherence permanently — but tracking macros is highly stressful for some people, who do better with low carb. Find the method of restriction that feels *least* restrictive to you, and don't assume what's easy for you is easy for anyone else.

## Fat-burner supplements (Eric Trexler)
[15:17] Trexler has published on caffeine, creatine, omega-3s and more. The categories marketed for fat loss:
- **Fat-oxidation enhancers:** carnitine, CLA.
- **Energy-expenditure increasers:** caffeine, capsaicin, ephedrine, yohimbine.
- **Hunger reducers:** most thermogenics also blunt hunger; also ginger, 5-HTP.
- **Nutrient blockers:** fat or carbohydrate absorption blockers.

Every one has a flaw: caffeine habituation with consistent use; quality-control problems (yohimbine); side effects (yohimbine, ephedrine); nutrient blockers that work too well and cause GI distress; and for most, an outright lack of strong conclusive evidence. The best-studied ones produce effects you could get more cheaply and easily by other means.

## Practical application
- Set a deficit of 0.5–1% body weight per week and expect it to take longer than you'd like.
- Keep lifting progressively throughout the cut — this is what makes the lost weight fat rather than muscle.
- Protein >2 g/kg/day (or grams = height in cm if you carry a lot of fat).
- Cardio: ≤30 min low-intensity daily, ≤1–2 HIIT sessions weekly, never immediately pre-lifting, and total cardio volume under half your lifting volume.
- Build most meals from single-ingredient foods; deliberately schedule a treat rather than banning it.
- Restructure your environment so the easy option is the compliant one.
- Weigh yourself regularly and read the multi-week trend, not the daily number.
- Supplements: same list cutting or not — creatine monohydrate, whey if protein is short, fish oil (fat is usually restricted on a cut, making essential fatty acids harder to get), and a multivitamin or vitamin D as food intake falls.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Relapse statistics remain poor no matter which diet is used; nobody in this video claims a solution to that.
- Getting truly shredded is not a sustainable or health-oriented goal for most people, and the first attempt is likely to cost some muscle.
- Trexler recommends no fat-loss supplement as causal — the evidence isn't there, and the well-studied options are replaceable with cheaper methods.
- Processed-food TEF differences are real but small enough that they're handled by adjusting macros, not by avoiding foods on principle.
