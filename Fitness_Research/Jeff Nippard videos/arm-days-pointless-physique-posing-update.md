# Are Dedicated Arm Days Necessary? (Weak-Point Prioritisation)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Are Arm Days Pointless? / Physique Posing Update" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A8hEonfElkc)) · 2016-06-17 · 7 min
**Topic:** Programming

> Note: this is a vlog with roughly 90 seconds of training discussion in it (from about [03:40] to [05:15]). The note is short because the educational content is thin — there are no studies, numbers or set/rep prescriptions in the video.

## Bottom line
- A separate arm day is not required for growth; it is a way of *reorganising* the same work, not adding a new stimulus.
- Jeff runs it because arms are his own judged weak point, not because the structure is superior.
- The two legitimate ways to prioritise a lagging body part are: train it first in a session, or give it its own day.
- Training arms first in a pull session compromises the back work that follows, so he prefers the separate day.
- Beyond that, he treats the argument as theoretical — programme around your weak point, not around a template.

## Why he has an arm day at all
His split is a normal push/pull/legs with the direct arm work pulled out and parked on its own day. The reason is competition feedback: across all six shows he has competed in, judges have consistently told him his arms need to come up — usually biceps and traps — with the only other note being that he could be a bit more conditioned. So arms get prioritised.

## The two ways to prioritise a weak point
1. **Train it first**, when you are freshest.
2. **Give it its own session.**

Option 1 has a cost he does not want to pay: fatigued biceps blunt the back training that comes afterwards in the same workout. Pulling the arm work out of the pull day sidesteps that interference entirely, which is why he chose option 2.

## Would it actually matter? [04:19]
He poses it as a thought experiment: run a randomised controlled trial where group A does push / pull / legs / rest and group B does push / pull / legs / arms, and he does not expect *any* difference in hypertrophy between them. The volume is the same volume; only its placement changed. Past a certain point, he says, the debate is purely theoretical.

## The secondary reasons
He also keeps the day because it is low-cost and he enjoys it. He treats it almost as an active rest day: everything else recovers, it is not especially taxing or fatiguing, he still gets to the gym, burns some calories and gets a pump — while nudging the weak point along.

## Practical application
- If your arms are not lagging, you do not need an arm day. Fold the arm work into push and pull as usual.
- If arms *are* your weak point, prioritise them by position: either first in a session, or on their own day.
- Prefer the separate day if the alternative is doing biceps before back — pre-fatiguing the elbow flexors will cost you on rows and pulldowns.
- Judge a split by whether it prioritises your specific weak point, not by whether it looks like the "correct" bodybuilding template.
- A light arm day can double as a de facto rest day when the rest of the week has been hard.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Nothing here is evidence-based; the RCT he describes is hypothetical and, as of the video, had not been run. He is explicit that it is his opinion.
- No volume, rep range or exercise prescriptions are given for the arm day itself.
- This is an early (2016) video and reflects his competitive-bodybuilding context — the weak-point logic assumes you have judged feedback or a clear visual imbalance to act on.
