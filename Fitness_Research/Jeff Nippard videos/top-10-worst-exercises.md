# Top 10 Worst Exercises: The Ones Not Worth Prioritising

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "My Top 10 WORST Exercises (Maybe Avoid These)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1GsYg9YG0Xk)) · 2019-11-12 · 11 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- His framing: there are few if any inherently good or bad exercises, only better and worse choices for a given goal. He quotes Dr. Bret Contreras — injuries in the weight room have more to do with poor form than the exercise itself; exercises are tools, and a good carpenter doesn't blame his tools.
- Most entries fail on **redundancy, wrong direction of resistance, or reduced overloading potential**, not danger.
- Two entries are gym-etiquette complaints rather than biomechanical ones (rack pulls, curling in the squat rack).
- The only entry he actively says most size/strength trainees should avoid outright is unstable-surface training.

Ranked 10 (least objectionable) to 1 (least favourite).

## The list

**10. Standing dumbbell external rotation.** Not harmful, just ineffective. External rotation happens in the transverse plane while gravity pulls the dumbbell down in the sagittal plane, so the load provides essentially zero resistance to the rotation itself — it only loads the biceps isometrically. Rotator cuff prehab (rear delts, infraspinatus) is worth doing; do it with **cables or bands** instead. Bodyweight external rotations would be no worse than the dumbbell version.

**9. Above-the-knee rack pull.** Not hated, just inefficient for its usual purposes. For deadlift strength, a **lower block pull** carries over better. For lockout/glute strength, the **hip thrust** loads the glutes better in full hip extension. He agrees it overloads the traps isometrically, but shrugs or cable shrugs take the traps through a full range without needing that much load. Main objection: slamming huge loads around damages gym equipment — poor etiquette unless you have a private gym or bring your own bar.

**8. Cable squat.** Another case of tension applied in the wrong direction — the cable pulls you forward into the machine, so it mostly challenges balance rather than loading quads or glutes. It amounts to a bodyweight squat with a balance component. Some use for teaching beginners to stay upright, but past rank-beginner level move to axially loaded variations: barbell back squat, front squat, goblet squat, machine squat or hack squat.

**7. The ass-to-grass squat.** Nothing wrong with squatting very deep *if you can do it safely*, but many lifters lack the mobility or skeletal structure. For size and general strength, **parallel is perfectly acceptable**. Competitive powerlifters must get the hip crease below the knee for the lift to count, but for everyone else the risk-to-reward ratio escalates the deeper you go. Squatting deeper than your anatomy allows usually adds no extra range at the knee or hip — it just rounds the lower back and raises injury risk. Recommendation: squat as deep as you comfortably can.

**6. Hack squat machine shoulder press (and shoulder press machine shrug).** Makes the list for being unnecessary rather than dangerous. Convoluted uses of machines outside their design intent rarely add anything a basic variation doesn't already deliver. The hack squat press locks his shoulders in uncomfortably; a dumbbell press or a purpose-built machine shoulder press does the job. Same reasoning for standing on the seat of a shoulder press machine to shrug — dumbbells, cables, a trap bar or a barbell all set up more easily.

**5. Landmine T-bar cheat row with a very upright posture.** To maximise lat range of motion on any row, the torso should be close to perpendicular to the line of pull. Sitting back and staying upright cuts range of motion for no benefit other than heavier loading — a trade-off he doesn't think is worth it for size or injury avoidance. He concedes many bodybuilders have built impressive backs on cheat rows, but from a cost-benefit view you get more from reducing the weight, leaning further forward, and controlling the load.

**4. Curling in the squat rack.** Not an exercise fault — an etiquette fault. A barbell can be set up for curls anywhere; a squat can only be done in the rack. Especially bad when the load is heavier than you can handle with good form. If the gym is empty or there are several free racks, it's fine — but yield to someone showing up with a belt and knee sleeves.

**3. Front raise.** Fine and safe, but almost guaranteed to be redundant in a typical bodybuilding program. Most bodybuilders already have front delts overdeveloped relative to side and rear delts, new lifters gravitate toward mirror muscles, and all horizontal and vertical pressing already taxes the front delts heavily. At best redundant, at worst it worsens an existing imbalance. **Fix: swap front raises for lateral raises, bent-over raises or reverse flyes** to balance the three deltoid heads.

**2. Behind-the-neck pulldown and behind-the-neck press.** Not everyone doing these will get hurt, and plenty of lifters have the mobility to perform them fine — they have never given him trouble in his own experimentation. But at least one study shows the behind-the-neck lat pulldown produces **lower lat activation** than pulling to the front ([Signorile 2002](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/12423182)). For the behind-the-neck press, being able to force pure shoulder abduction confers no particular advantage; a standard overhead press plus lateral raises would be more effective. They allow less overloading, feel awkward for most lifters, carry more injury risk, and offer no noticeable hypertrophy benefit. Unless you clearly have the mobility and respond well to them, pull and press to the front.

**1. BOSU ball curls and BOSU ball squats.** For a lifter chasing muscle and strength, a curl on a stable surface allows more overload, better movement control and a better mind-muscle connection — the same for squats. The usual justification is "functionality," but as Brad Schoenfeld argued in a 2010 ACSM article, calling an exercise functional or non-functional is misleading: for a very unfit trainee, a routine of only "non-functional" machines would be highly functional in terms of daily living. Functionality is a continuum ([Schoenfeld](http://www.lookgreatnaked.com/articles/is_functional_training_really_functional.pdf)). That same 2010 position stand concluded that **unstable devices should not be used when hypertrophy, strength or power is the goal**, because force generation, power and velocity are all impaired ([Behm 2010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/20130673)). His verdict: most size/strength trainees should avoid them — they hinder performance while increasing injury risk.

## Practical application
- Swap dumbbell external rotations for cable or band versions.
- Swap above-the-knee rack pulls for low block pulls (deadlift strength), hip thrusts (glutes/lockout), or shrugs (traps).
- Swap cable squats for any axially loaded squat variation.
- Squat as deep as your structure allows; parallel is enough for size and strength.
- Use machines for their designed purpose.
- Row with the torso near perpendicular to the line of pull; drop the weight if you can't.
- Replace front raises with lateral, bent-over or reverse flye work.
- Pull down and press in front of the neck.
- Keep hypertrophy and strength work on stable surfaces.

## Caveats & unknowns
- He stresses that with enough biomechanics and anatomy knowledge you can build a case against literally any exercise, so this list is about priorities, not prohibitions.
- If one of these works for you, he says keep it — modify it, substitute it, or continue as-is.
- Cheat rows and deep squats both have legitimate contexts (bodybuilding tradition, powerlifting rules).
