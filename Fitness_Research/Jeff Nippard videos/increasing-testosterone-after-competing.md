# Post-Contest Recovery and Peri-Workout Protein Timing

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Increasing Testosterone After Competing | Anabolic Window Is Real? (New Research)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F8TFfAM5t8Q)) · 2017-06-13 · 5 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- In trained men cutting, **28 g of whey pre- and post-workout** spared lean mass and drove fat loss; a carb-matched group lost lean body mass instead — despite both groups already eating high protein.
- This suggests protein **timing** may matter for trained lifters in a deficit, even when total daily intake is sufficient.
- Post-contest, **metabolic rate recovers once weight and fat are regained** — regaining is the fix, not a problem.
- There is **no post-contest anabolic window**. The early weight gain is glycogen and water; after that you just gain fat.
- Testosterone recovery is **impaired by regaining fat too fast *and* too slow**. Both extremes blunt it.

Both pieces are summaries by **Eric Helms** from the June 2017 issue of MASS (Monthly Applications in Strength Sport).

## Peri-workout whey in a deficit [00:00]
A 2017 paper on **16 trained young males in a caloric deficit**, split into two groups:
- **Protein group:** 28 g whey pre-workout and 28 g post-workout
- **Carb group:** 28 g carbs pre- and post-workout

**Result:** body fat was lost **only in the protein group**, while the carb group **lost lean body mass**.

### Why this is surprising
Previous research on protein sparing muscle in a deficit compared a genuinely high intake (~2.3 g/kg) to a low one (~1 g/kg). Here, both groups were already high:
- Carb group: **~2.6 g/kg bodyweight**
- Protein group: **~3.0 g/kg bodyweight**

Both are high by every current standard in the literature, so it seems unlikely that going from 2.6 to 3.0 g/kg is what preserved lean mass.

**Helms's hypothesis:** this is a **protein timing effect**. For trained individuals in a caloric deficit, putting protein in the pre- and post-workout meals may be a viable lean-mass-preservation strategy.

### Squaring this with Schoenfeld's 2013 meta-analysis
The famous 2013 paper by **Schoenfeld and colleagues** concluded that as long as total daily protein is sufficient, timing around the workout doesn't matter much. Helms points out that **19 of the 25 studies** in that meta-analysis were conducted on **untrained subjects**, and highlights other research suggesting timing may matter more specifically for **trained individuals in a caloric deficit**.

## Post-contest recovery [02:31]
A 2017 paper by **Trexler and colleagues** followed **15 natural bodybuilding, physique, figure and bikini athletes for 4-6 weeks after competition**. Until this study, the evidence base was one previously-reviewed study plus a single case study on one male natural bodybuilder.

**Finding 1 — Metabolism recovers with weight.** Metabolic rate recovered fine once weight and fat were regained. The best way to restore metabolism after a prolonged cut, especially contest prep, is simply to **regain the weight and fat back into a healthier range**.

**Finding 2 — No post-contest anabolic window.** At the **1-week mark, 2 kg of bodyweight had been gained with no body fat gained** — body fat percentage was unchanged. On the surface that looks like a primed anabolic state, but it is most likely **water gain accompanying a large glycogen refill** (which counts as lean body mass). By the **4-6 week mark, 2 kg of body fat had been gained and no further lean mass**. So you are primed for **glycogen storage**, not muscle growth — and once glycogen is topped up, you gain fat as normal.

**Finding 3 — Testosterone recovery is non-linear.** Testosterone predictably craters during contest prep and rose over the 4-6 weeks as weight and fat were regained. The counterintuitive part: the **magnitude of testosterone recovery was *smaller* in men who gained more fat**. There appears to be a competing mechanism — regain too much or too fast and the testosterone rebound is impaired. Regain too slowly and it also fails to recover as quickly as it could.

Supporting this, Helms cites **2014 research** in which men fed a **1,200 calorie surplus for 4 weeks saw testosterone *decrease***.

## Practical application
- **Cutting while trained:** place protein deliberately in the pre- and post-workout meals rather than relying on daily total alone. ~28 g whey either side of training is the tested dose.
- **After a contest or extended cut:** regain weight and body fat deliberately — this is what restores metabolic rate and testosterone.
- **Don't chase a post-competition anabolic window.** The first week's gain is glycogen and water; beyond that, surplus becomes fat.
- **Moderate the rebound rate.** Both aggressive overfeeding and overly cautious slow regain blunt testosterone recovery. Specific rate recommendations were given in the MASS issue but are not stated in this video.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The whey study had only 16 subjects; the timing explanation is Helms's hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism.
- The Trexler study followed 15 athletes for only 4-6 weeks — a short window on a long recovery process.
- The exact "how fast is too fast, how slow is too slow" thresholds for post-contest weight regain are not given here.
