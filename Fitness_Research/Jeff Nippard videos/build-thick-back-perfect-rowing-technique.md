# Pendlay Row and Helms Row Technique for Back Thickness

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Build a Thick Back With Perfect Rowing Technique (Pendlay Row/ Helms Row)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=axoeDmW0oAY)) · 2018-12-11 · 8 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Load the Pendlay row in the **8-12 rep range** and treat just under **1× bodyweight** as a high-end strength standard for that rep zone.
- Pull with the elbows travelling back at **45-65 degrees** from the torso; more flare = more traps, more tuck = more lats, and 45-65 hits everything reasonably evenly.
- Keep the **chest roughly parallel to the floor** and the lumbar spine in a neutral isometric hold throughout — no leg drive, no hip extension.
- The most common error by far is **going too heavy**; fix that and most other faults self-correct. Adding weight only counts as overload when form is unchanged.
- If you can't stop cheating with leg drive, substitute the **Helms row** (chest pinned to an incline bench, dumbbells, **12-15 reps**).

## What the movement trains
The Pendlay row — named after American Olympic weightlifting coach **Glenn Pendlay**, originally used for explosive power off the floor — is modified here as a strict barbell row for back development.
- **Shoulder extension** — mainly lats, with some long head of triceps.
- **Transverse shoulder abduction** — rear delts.
- **Scapular retraction** — mostly mid traps.
- **Isometric lumbar extension** — spinal erectors hold the bent-over position throughout. This matters twice over: stronger erectors support the deadlift, and because they run the length of the spine into the lats and traps they make the whole back look bigger.
- Biceps assist with elbow flexion, but this is a back builder, not an arm builder.

## Setup
1. Load the bar evenly and **use the safety clips** — an unclipped bar lets plates shift progressively on each rep.
2. Approach as for a conventional deadlift: roughly **shoulder-width stance**. Longer legs may need a slightly wider stance or feet turned out so the knees stay out of the bar path.
3. Push the hips back to reach the bar, shins vertical, bar over **mid-foot to toes** when viewed from the side.
4. **Double overhand grip, slightly wider than shoulder width** — pick a width where the upper and lower arm form a **90-degree angle** at the top of the pull.
5. Chalk or straps are fine (they let you focus on pulling with the back), but the loads shouldn't be heavy enough to need them.
6. **Anteriorly rotate the pelvis** — "pop the butt out like a bikini pose" — to pull the spine into true neutral. A totally flat lower back is actually slightly flexed, which is undesirable under load in this position.
7. If you can't reach the floor without rounding, **elevate the bar on bumper plates or aerobic risers** to shorten the range and preserve a neutral spine.

## Execution
- Start each rep with the scapulae **slightly protracted**, then initiate by pulling the shoulder blades together and driving the elbows up and slightly back at **45-65 degrees** relative to the torso (viewed from above). Over-tucking blocks the traps; over-flaring removes the lats and puts the shoulder in a worse position.
- The bar should touch the **lower sternum, around nipple level**.
- Finish the concentric by squeezing the shoulder blades together to nail the mid traps.
- Lower under control with the scapulae retracted until the plates are a few inches off the floor, then protract as the plates come to a stop.
- **Dead stop vs touch-and-go:** a true Pendlay row brings the weight to a full stop on the floor each rep, resetting the stretch-shortening cycle. A controlled touch keeps more constant tension. Both are acceptable.
- Maintain the isometric lumbar contraction through the whole concentric and eccentric. Keep the head neutral — a little neck flexion or extension is fine, but jerking the head up or down makes full scapular retraction harder.

## Loading and fatigue management
Moderate reps, **8-12**. Because the Pendlay row deliberately places the back in a mechanically disadvantaged position, too much load or too many reps accumulates lower-back fatigue that can interfere with deadlifting later in the week.

Reference point [02:11]: Dr. Mike Israetel, around 250 lb bodyweight with outstanding lat development, uses **235 lb** with perfectly controlled, identical-looking reps. The lesson is not "why is someone this big so weak" but "why is someone this big using less weight than me."

## Common errors
1. **Too heavy** — the dominant fault.
2. **Leg drive / hip extension to heave the bar.** Full range of motion is better for size and strength in the best studies, and maximising lat range requires the torso close to perpendicular to the line of pull, i.e. chest near parallel to the floor. Test: if you can read the lettering on your shirt at the top of the rep, your chest is rising too much.

## Helms row substitute
Upper chest against the top of an **incline bench** (padded with a towel or knee sleeves), back angle still near parallel to the floor, rowing **dumbbells**. Because the chest is pinned, leg drive is impossible — ideal for people who can't stop cheating the Pendlay row.
- Best in the **12-15 rep range**.
- More **lat-dominant** than trap-dominant, since you row back rather than out; deliberately focus on scapular retraction to still hit the mid traps well.
- Downside: you lose the **isometric spinal erector** work, which matters if you don't squat or deadlift regularly.

## Practical application
- Program the Pendlay row for 8-12 reps as a core back movement; Jeff calls it about as close to an absolute must as anything for complete back development.
- Aim toward just under bodyweight for reps as a long-term strength target.
- Rotate elbow angle within 45-65 degrees depending on whether you want more trap or more lat emphasis.
- Swap in the Helms row at 12-15 reps if form breaks down, and add direct erector work if you use it as a full replacement.
