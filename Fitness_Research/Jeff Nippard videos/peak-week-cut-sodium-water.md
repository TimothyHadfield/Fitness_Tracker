# Peak Week: Why Cutting Sodium and Water Is a Bad Idea

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "PEAK WEEK - Should I Cut Sodium and Water?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P98AM29NjsM)) · 2014-11-07 · 31 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The only water layer worth targeting is the **dermal layer: 0.5 mm thick on the eyelids to 3 mm on the back**, about **70-75% water**. Best case, manipulation shifts roughly **2 mm at the thickest point**. Competitors are being hospitalised and dying over that.
- **Depleting dietary sodium is always a bad idea** — it doesn't lower blood sodium, it impairs glucose uptake into muscle, and combined with high water intake it risks hyponatremia.
- **Prolonged heavy sodium loading is also bad** (causes oedema), but a **short acute sodium load before showtime is good** — it drives vasodilation and fullness without spilling extracellular fluid.
- **Limiting water intake is a bad idea** because there's no known way to selectively lose dermal water without also losing intramuscular and plasma water, which flattens you out.
- The whole peak week is **massively overrated**. If you're genuinely lean at 7 days out, you'll look good — you'd have to do something drastic and unintelligent to look worse.

## Scope
This discussion is limited to **natural bodybuilders**, because anabolic steroids and growth hormone have a known side effect of causing water retention, which changes the problem entirely. Nippard notes two schools of thought in the natural community: that water shouldn't be cut at all and cutting is detrimental, versus that water and/or sodium must be manipulated to peak.

## The anatomy: which water actually matters [02:35]

**Body water compartments.** About **two-thirds is intracellular fluid** (inside muscle, heart, liver and all other cells); the remaining third is **extracellular**, subdivided into:
- **Interstitial space** — fluid between cells and blood vessels, bathing the organs.
- **Plasma water** — the water in the blood, inside the vessels.
- **"Third space"** — mostly cerebrospinal fluid, small enough to ignore.

As a bodybuilder you want **intracellular fluid high** (full, round muscle) and **plasma water high** (vascularity). So the target is interstitial fluid — except most interstitial fluid isn't under the skin at all. **Muscle itself has a high volume of interstitial fluid**, including the fluid running into the T-tubules, and you don't want that low. Nor does interstitial fluid around organs affect your appearance. **Only the specific layer under the skin matters.**

**Skin layers [05:36].** Four layers above muscle/bone:
- **Stratum corneum** — dead cells, only **20-30 cells thick**; pre-contest exfoliation removes it. Ignore.
- **Epidermis** — about the thickness of a single strand of hair. Ignore.
- **Dermis** — **0.5 mm on the eyelids up to 3 mm on the back**, possibly with a genetic component. This is the layer that matters. It is **~70-75% water**, the rest connective tissue.
- **Subcutaneous fat** — what you shed by dieting, attaching to underlying muscle or bone.

So even at the thickest point on the back, water manipulation can only address **around 2 mm**.

**The diagram that doesn't exist [07:08].** The blue "subcutaneous water layer" printed on water-pill packaging and in magazine ads is not a real anatomical structure. Open a physiology textbook and what you find is the dermal layer, roughly three-quarters water by composition. The layer only appears as drawn in advertisements selling diuretics.

## Sodium [08:10]

**Depleting sodium is futile.** A **1990 Harvard study** (the caption garbles the lead author — possibly "Ragadal" or similar; treat the name as uncertain) reduced dietary sodium to essentially zero for **6 days** and measured blood and urinary sodium. **Blood concentrations stayed essentially unchanged.** The body regulates sodium so tightly that dietary depletion has no meaningful effect on your physiology.

Three concrete harms from depleting sodium:
1. **Hyponatremia risk** — especially when combined with the high water intake bodybuilders typically pair it with.
2. **Impaired glucose uptake** — sodium depletion reduces the effectiveness of the **SGLT1 transporter** that shuttles glucose into muscle, directly undermining the fullness you're chasing.
3. It doesn't change blood sodium anyway (see above), so there is no upside to weigh against these.

**Why depletion fails mechanistically [19:34].** Two hormones govern this. **Antidiuretic hormone (ADH)** retains water; **aldosterone** retains sodium. When you drop dietary sodium, the body simply secretes more aldosterone, the kidneys retain more sodium, and blood levels stay constant. Critically, the **timescales are hourly, not daily or weekly**: ADH is synthesised, transported and stored within **1-2 hours** and has a **half-life of 10-35 minutes**; aldosterone's half-life is **under 20 minutes**. This badly undermines the popular protocol of water-loading to suppress these hormones before a sudden cut — **within an hour or two of cutting water, the hormones have already spiked and you're retaining water again.**

**Chronic sodium loading is also bad [10:15].** A **2005 paper on the treatment of oedema** notes that sodium chloride intake generally equals output over a few days: when intake rises there's a compensatory rise in excretion after a lag. But if the fluctuation is **too wide and too long**, intake exceeds the kidneys' clearance rate, the sodium excess stimulates ADH release, and water is retained — some in the vessels, then leaking into interstitial spaces including the dermal layer, which is exactly where you don't want it. This is what you see in competitors who binge for days after a show: swelling and fluid pockets in odd places like the ankles.

**Acute sodium loading is good [11:47].** A paper on the acute effects of sodium ingestion (Nippard says 2018, which postdates this video — treat the year as a caption error) concluded that a **high-sodium meal may cause a transient blood pressure increase, but in healthy people acute sodium ingestion does not cause sustained hypertension or hypotension**. Blood pressure normalises because **blood vessels dilate**, increasing volume and reducing pressure — and vasodilation is exactly what a bodybuilder wants for vascularity. A **1995 study** on sodium intake and vasodilation concluded that animal studies suggest **nitric oxide production is increased under salt loading**, and that this increase protects against salt-induced hypertension.

This is part of why competitors often look better the **day after** the show, having eaten nachos and pizza: they filled out and got the vasodilation.

**Anecdote:** at the Muscle Mayhem, his friend Jose ate slice after slice of pizza backstage and looked full and vascular with no extracellular fluid retention — because he spiked sodium sharply **on show day** while keeping it balanced in the preceding days. Nippard himself ate several high-sodium ham and cheese sandwiches.

## Water [14:22]

Traditionally lifters just stop drinking, hoping to shed a subcutaneous water layer that doesn't exist as advertised. The problem: **reducing water intake loses water from every compartment**, and there's far more water in the intracellular fluid than in the dermal layer. Cutting water — especially carelessly, and especially alongside sodium reduction — is an excellent way to **flatten out and look stringy and depleted on stage**. He knows of no clear way to selectively target the dermal layer.

**Two possible exceptions:**

**1. Dry carb-loading [15:57].** Reduce water intake roughly a day out while **drastically increasing carbohydrate intake** at the same time. The rationale: rising muscle glycogen pulls water into the muscle, keeping intracellular volume high while extracellular volume flushes. Nippard attributes the idea to **Lyle McDonald** and says it makes intuitive sense; he hasn't used it himself. Conditions for trying it: **carbs jacked very high, sodium kept high, and only a modest water cut — around 50-75% reduction at 24 hours out.**

**2. Cutaneous transpiration [17:01].** Distinct from sweating — it's water moving through and off the dermal layer (the steam that forms around your hand on a cold mirror). It accounts for about **300 mL of water loss per day** and is the one loss route that specifically drains the layer under the skin. Nippard has no proven way to increase it deliberately, but observes he looks drier and more vascular after a tanning bed or time in the sun. His practical inference: **stay warm before the show.** If competing in a cold climate, wear slippers backstage, wear warm baggy clothing, and consider bringing a small space heater. If competing somewhere warm, spending time in the sun may help — he credits this with part of his good peak in California.

## Recommendations [21:04]

**Water — two conservative protocols:**
1. **Keep water intake identical through peak week and on show day.** This is the one he favours.
2. Keep water the same through peak week, then **reduce it slightly on Friday evening**, timed with heavy carb loading (the dry-loading approach). He warns this can backfire and flatten you out more easily, but sees a case for it in people with a genuinely thick dermal layer, especially on the back — and only done very conservatively.

**Sodium:**
- Keep sodium **constant throughout peak week**, at roughly your intake in the preceding weeks and months.
- Then load acutely: a **very high-sodium meal on Friday night, or at minimum early Saturday morning**. Pizza, ham and cheese sandwiches, sushi — or just salt your normal food heavily, which achieves the same thing without changing macros.
- **Only use foods you know you digest well.** If you haven't eaten pizza in 6 months, show day is a risky time to find out.
- Don't measure sodium with great precision during peak week — the body already regulates it far more precisely than you can. The point of constancy is avoiding weird swings in scale weight that make you panic.
- **Test it in advance:** hold sodium at around **3,000 mg/day** for a week, spike to **~8,000 mg** on a Friday night, and see how you look Saturday morning. His personal response: increased vascularity for **1-2 days**, with spillover only after **2-3 days** of very heavy loading. His own protocol is to start ramping sodium **around 5 pm Friday** and keep it very high all through Saturday.

## Perspective
Peak week is largely overrated. Carbs and sodium can make a real difference — he shows himself 3 days out looking tight but flat, versus filled out after carbs and sodium — but people over-worry to their own cost: **anxiety drives cortisol up, which itself is a sufficient stimulus for water retention and can make you look significantly worse on show day.** A more nonchalant approach is better, because the body does most of the regulation for you. He shows examples of competitors who do essentially nothing on peak week except raise carbs — Lane Norton, Alberto Nunes, and his own lineup at Muscle Mayhem where none of the three cut water and two were drinking around **8 litres on show day**.

The safety framing he closes on: people are taking diuretics and being hospitalised or dying in pursuit of taking a **3 mm layer down to 2 or 1 mm**. He'd rather see competitors drinking water backstage than on stage with their lips stuck to their teeth. He's depleted water himself and describes it as feeling like death.

## Practical application
- Keep water and sodium **constant** all through peak week.
- Acute sodium spike Friday evening or early Saturday — familiar, well-tolerated high-sodium foods, or heavy salting.
- Never deplete sodium. Never combine low sodium with high water.
- If experimenting with dry loading: max carbs, high sodium, water down only 50-75% at 24 hours out — and rehearse it in a prep week first.
- Stay warm backstage (slippers, baggy warm clothing, space heater) to exploit cutaneous transpiration.
- Rehearse any sodium protocol weeks in advance so show day holds no surprises.
- Arrive at 7 days out already lean — a male competitor should already have glute striations. If you're relying on water manipulation to save the physique, you're going to drown.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The lead author of the 1990 Harvard sodium-depletion study is garbled in the auto-captions and should not be quoted without verification.
- The "2018" acute sodium ingestion paper postdates this 2014 video; the year is almost certainly a caption error.
- Dry carb-loading is presented as intuitively plausible, not evidence-backed, and Nippard has not used it himself.
- There is no established practical method for selectively increasing cutaneous transpiration — the tanning-bed and sunlight observations are personal anecdote.
- Dermal thickness may vary between individuals and possibly genetically; the 0.5-3 mm figures are the commonly cited literature range.
- Everything here excludes enhanced athletes, whose water retention profile differs because of anabolics and growth hormone.
