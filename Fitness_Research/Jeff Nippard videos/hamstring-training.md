# How to Train Hamstrings: Hip Extension, Knee Flexion, and a Two-Day Template

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Effective Way to Train HAMSTRINGS | Training Science Explained" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0a_fVS2s4Ho)) · 2017-12-10 · 9 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Hamstring work should roughly **match quad volume** in a complete lower-body program — it matters for injury prevention and athletic performance, not just looks.
- You need **both** hip-extension exercises (deadlift variations) and **knee-flexion** exercises (leg curls). Deadlifting alone is not enough.
- **Squats are a bad hamstring exercise** — a 2009 EMG paper (Ebben et al.) ranked the squat worst of six movements tested for hamstring activation.
- Hamstrings are **not** fast-twitch dominant — newer, better-methodology data shows a fairly even type I / type II mix, so use both heavy and high reps.
- Cap hamstring volume at **under 20 sets per week** (per Dr. Mike Israetel, this is where recovery problems usually start).

## Anatomy and why it dictates exercise choice
Four muscles. From medial to lateral: **semimembranosus** (most medial), **semitendinosus** (middle), and **biceps femoris**, which splits into a **long head** and a **short head**.

- Semimembranosus, semitendinosus and biceps femoris long head all originate on the pelvis and insert below the knee (tibia for the first two, fibula for the BF long head). These are **biarticular** — they cross both the hip and knee, so they perform **hip extension** (deadlift) and **knee flexion** (leg curl).
- The **biceps femoris short head crosses only the knee**. It has no hip action at all, so a program of only hip-extension work never trains it.

Net: four muscles flex the knee, three extend the hip. Effective training needs hip extension, knee flexion, and ideally movements doing both at once.

## Why squats fail and deadlifts work
[01:33] On a squat the hips and knees extend simultaneously, so the hamstrings shorten at the hip while lengthening at the knee. Total muscle length stays roughly constant through the range, which means very little tensile stimulus for growth.

The deadlift shares the simultaneous hip-and-knee-extension pattern, but the hips start much higher and the knees are less flexed, putting the hamstrings in a better position to contribute to hip extension. A **2002 study from Escamilla and colleagues** found high hamstring activation in both conventional and sumo deadlifts, and in the EMG comparison referenced from his quad video the deadlift ranked **number one** for hamstring activation while the squat ranked worst.

Research hasn't shown much activation difference between **stiff-leg / Romanian deadlifts** and conventional deadlifts, but he still prefers a straight leg for hamstring emphasis because it puts the hamstrings under a greater stretch, which may matter for anabolic signaling.

Other pure hip-extension movements — the **lower back extension** and the **reverse hyper** — also hit the hamstrings well and are worth including.

## Knee flexion work
[03:36] Almost any knee-flexion exercise gets high hamstring involvement, and he doesn't think the differences are worth nitpicking. That said, a **2012 study** comparing EMG across 14 exercises found the **slide leg exercise** came out on top. Practical alternatives to the awkward study version:
- **Brett Contreras' gliding leg curl** — hang from a barbell, hips fully extended, curl your body in toward a high plyo box.
- A **seated row machine variant** — feet on the seat, upper back on the floor, curl the seat in toward you.
- The **glute-ham raise**, which also trains both functions at once.

These likely work well because the hamstrings hold the hips extended isometrically while concentrically flexing the knees.

He has no strong preference between **lying and seated leg curls** — EMG activation is similar — so use whichever you feel best, or rotate both.

## Regional targeting
**Foot position on leg curls** shifts which region works hardest (data from Lynn & Costigan): **toes pointed out (external rotation)** biases the **lateral** hamstrings, **toes pointed in** biases the **medial** hamstrings. Memory cue: point your toes toward the region you want to hit.

A **2015 paper from Schoenfeld and colleagues** found the **lying leg curl beat the stiff-leg deadlift for distal (lower) hamstring activation**, with no significant difference between the two for proximal (upper) activation. Combined with the biceps femoris short head's lack of a hip attachment, this is the case for including a dedicated knee-flexion exercise rather than only deadlifting.

## Practical application
**Recommended program — hamstrings twice per week, on lower-body days:**
- **Day 1:** heavy stiff-leg deadlift for **4–8 reps** + a light leg-curl movement for **8–20 reps**.
- **Day 2** (2–3 days later): heavy leg-curl movement for **4–8 reps** + a light hip-extension movement for **8–20 reps**.
- Add one supplemental exercise on either day if hamstrings are lagging.
- **Stay under 20 sets per week.**

Execution notes:
- Go heavier / lower reps on hip-extension movements like the stiff-leg deadlift — but **going too heavy is a common mistake** that lets the lower back and glutes take over. Build a mind-muscle connection and feel a strong stretch under complete control.
- High-rep deadlifts are fine, but he prefers the **dumbbell** version for pump work since its loading capacity is lower anyway.
- Leg curls work at both high and low reps.
- Rotate toe-in / toe-out on curls for medial vs lateral emphasis.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Stiff-leg/RDL vs conventional deadlift: the stretch advantage is his preference and a "may be important" mechanism, not a demonstrated growth difference — EMG shows little difference.
- Most of the evidence cited here is EMG activation data, not long-term hypertrophy outcomes.
- He explicitly declines to rank knee-flexion variations against each other beyond the slide-leg finding.
- This is a 2017 video; the fiber-type claim rests on "new data with improved methodology" that isn't named.
