# Forearm Training: Anatomy, Exercise Selection and Volume

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Build Huge Forearms | Training Science Explained" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3xHrOLzTLYI)) · 2017-08-13 · 6 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Heavy compound pulling builds forearms, but likely isn't enough to **optimise** their growth — direct work adds more.
- Cover three functions: **wrist flexion**, **wrist extension**, and **grip**, plus a **reverse-grip curl** for the brachioradialis.
- Prescription: **2-3 exercises per workout, 2-3 sets each, 10-20 reps** with relatively light loads, done at the **end** of an upper body session.
- Frequency: **2-3x per week**. Volume-equated research found training forearms **10x/week is no better than 3x/week** for strength or size.
- Reverse-grip curls beat hammer curls for isolating the brachioradialis, and finishing with **partial reps in the top half** biases it further.

## Anatomy [00:31]

The forearm contains roughly **20 muscles** operating between the elbow and wrist, split into two compartments:

- **Anterior (front)** — flexion of the wrist and the fingers/phalanges
- **Posterior (back)** — wrist and finger extension

The muscles that move the fingers are located in the forearm and operate the fingers through long tendons, like a puppet on strings, so the fingers themselves can stay slender for precise tasks. This is why grip-intensive exercises like **farmer's walks** train the forearms effectively even though the wrist never moves through flexion or extension — the anterior muscles are contracting **isometrically** to keep the fist clenched.

About **five of the 20** muscles perform elbow flexion (curling) and forearm pronation. The most prominent is the **brachioradialis**, which — unlike other visible forearm muscles such as the flexor digitorum superficialis — **does not cross the wrist joint**, so it cannot be trained by wrist flexion or extension at all.

## Targeting the brachioradialis [01:01]

- A paper titled *The Function of Brachioradialis* found it is **equally active in neutral, supinated and pronated positions**. Since the biceps are strongest when supinated (palms up), the way to shift emphasis toward the brachioradialis is to curl with a **pronated / reverse grip** — reducing biceps contribution rather than increasing brachioradialis contribution.
- **Murray et al.**, *Journal of Biomechanics*: peak torque for the brachioradialis occurs between **100 and 120 degrees of elbow flexion**, while peak biceps torque is nearer **80 degrees**. So **partial reps in the top end of the range** bias the brachioradialis over the biceps.
- Nippard's top brachioradialis builder: the **reverse-grip barbell curl, finished with partial reps in the top half of the range**.
- **Hammer curls** are a good exercise but, like most curls, hit the biceps substantially and aren't great for isolation.

## Does direct forearm work matter? [02:04]

A **2004 study** on high school baseball players ran 12 weeks of training in two groups:

- **Group 1:** a typical full-body 3x/week program including grip-intensive exercises (Romanian deadlifts, bent rows).
- **Group 2:** the identical program **plus six forearm exercises**, including wrist curls and wrist extensions.

At 12 weeks, only one forearm strength measure reached statistical significance — but **every single strength measure favoured the direct-forearm-training group**. Given that strength gains tend to track hypertrophy, Nippard expects a longer study would show size differences too. Worth noting the other side: the resistance-training-only group still made significant strength gains on nearly every forearm task, confirming heavy compounds do build forearms — just probably not optimally.

## Exercise selection [03:06]

Include at least one wrist flexion and one wrist extension movement.

- **Wrist flexion:** dumbbell wrist curl with the forearm braced against a bench. Preferred over the barbell version, which strains the wrists more.
- **Wrist extension:** bench-braced dumbbell extension, and the **standing dumbbell extension** — the standing version requires more grip strength and can be loaded heavier, making it his **number one for the back of the forearm**.
- **Grip:** especially important if you don't do much heavy pulling. Favourite is the **plate pinch**, progressively adding **10 lb plates** to increase both weight and thickness — a form of double progression. **Farmer's walks** and **heavy barbell holds** also work.
- **Fat grips** or similar can build grip, but he advises **against** adding them to exercises you already do — they cut the load you can use and let back and shoulder work suffer for the sake of forearm strength. Same warning for farmer's walks and heavy holds: they can cause recovery problems unless you correspondingly reduce trap and back workload.

## Practical application

- **2-3 forearm exercises per workout, 2-3 sets each.**
- **10-20 reps** with relatively lighter weight. Heavy loads risk joint issues and may fatigue the larger muscles before maximally stimulating the small forearm muscles.
- Place forearm work at the **end of an upper body workout** so grip strength doesn't compromise the rest of the session.
- Train forearms **2-3x per week** — more frequency isn't better once volume is matched, and carryover from other lifts is already high.
- Include a **reverse-grip curl at least once per week** in your regular arm training for the brachioradialis.
- Consistency and progression dominate. Pick an exercise you enjoy and feel working, and stay on it.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The 2004 baseball study was 12 weeks, measured strength not size, and only one measure hit statistical significance.
- Optimal volume is individual; the 2-3 exercises / 2-3 sets figure is a general starting point.
