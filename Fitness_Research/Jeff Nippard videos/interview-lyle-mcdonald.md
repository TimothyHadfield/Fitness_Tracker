# Lyle McDonald on Set Point, Metabolic Damage, Reverse Dieting and Training Volume

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Interview With Lyle McDonald | Part 2 | Reverse Dieting, Metabolic Damage & Training Volume" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Iqw-RJzrOjI)) · 2016-04-04 · 53 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Set point is real but doesn't reset downward. Post-obese subjects show adaptation up to at least seven years out with no sign it resolves. It seems able to move *up* (pregnancy, obesity), never down [03:16].
- The adaptation is smaller than believed and mostly NEAT. Dieting obese subjects 10% below start weight drops total expenditure ~150 kcal/day, only ~50 kcal of it RMR; 75–90% is non-resting [05:47].
- Metabolic damage is, in McDonald's words, "a bunch of crap" [15:26].
- Reverse dieting: take no more than ~two weeks to return to maintenance. Any gain beyond that comes from more training and more NEAT, not raised RMR [07:48].
- "Volume is the most important factor" is a silly framing. If one factor must be named it's progressive tension overload [26:42].
- Practical volume: 40–70 reps per muscle per session (Wernbom's figure, also Eric Helms'), 2–3×/week — roughly 4–8 hard sets at 8–12 reps.

## Set point vs settling point [00:42–04:46]
A **set point** is defended: overfeed an animal, metabolism rises and hunger falls; underfeed it, the reverse (his thermostat analogy). A **settling point** is environmental. The 30-year argument over which exists parallels a 20-year argument over whether metabolic adaptation exists at all, now settled that it does. His answer is **both**, with settling operating within a narrow range before defence kicks in; the only studies failing to find adaptation were in the extremely obese, where there's nothing to defend. Obesity persists despite a set point because cheap, palatable food overwhelms any biological system — animals on a "cafeteria diet" of essentially cookie dough eat themselves obese.

Does it reset lower? **Probably not.** Post-obese adaptation persists ≥7 years. Staying lean getting easier over time he attributes to **behaviour** — entrenched habits, consistent training, catching slips early — not physiology.

## What actually changes [05:16–14:23]
- Obese dieted to 10% below start: RMR down ~50 kcal, total down ~150 kcal/day, 75–90% of it non-resting.
- Differences look bigger *while dieting* because leptin is lower than at the same weight at maintenance; hormones return toward normal at maintenance.
- Offset it with activity: if aerobic exercise burns 15% fewer calories, do a bit more. More exercise also lets you eat physically more food. Exercise's demonstrated value is in weight-loss *maintenance*, partly because studies rarely give enough of it — 600 kcal six days a week is a lot.
- **NEAT is the dominant individual variable**: total daily expenditure varies by up to **2,000 kcal/day** between highest and lowest NEAT — enough to double someone's expenditure. Partly cultural (he cites a country where women's NEAT is roughly triple men's from farm and house work; roughly equal in Europe, Austria, America), partly genetic and neurochemically mediated via dopamine, and largely subconscious. He suggests Nippard interview **James Levine** ("Dr LaVine") as the NEAT researcher.
- The arithmetic: **+1 kcal/minute over an eight-hour workday = 500 kcal/day**.
- "Slow metabolism" is not an RMR phenomenon — little between-person RMR variance absent real thyroid disease, little TEF variance. The male/female gap is ~10% for body weight and **disappears normalised for lean mass**; women are slightly higher in the luteal phase from progesterone.

## Reverse dieting [07:48–11:51]
Don't take more than about two weeks to reach maintenance; people get "nutty about the water weight," but extra weeks are extra deficit. On pushing past maintenance:
- RMR contribution is trivial — overfeeding **1,000 kcal/day** raises RMR maybe **100 kcal**.
- What genuinely happens: more calories permit harder and more training, and NEAT rises.
- If weight *is* gained: a Leibel study ("liel") overfeeding lean and obese subjects to 10% above start weight produced the same **~15%** adaptive rise in expenditure that dieting produces downward — but at the cost of gaining 10% of bodyweight, defeating the purpose.
- Variability is enormous. In the classic 1,000 kcal overfeeding study some gained ~12 kg and others ~2 kg, driven by spontaneous movement, insulin and nervous-system output.
- **The depressing finding**: people who raise expenditure most on overfeeding lower it *least* on dieting, and vice versa — genetically blessed or screwed at both ends. The four worst NEAT responders in that study were women.

## Metabolic damage [15:26–22:33]
Nippard's framing: adaptation happens during the diet, damage claims permanent carryover. McDonald: it's crap.
- **Minnesota starvation experiment**: metabolisms returned to normal.
- **Anorexics**: up to **30% decrease in RMR**, cortisol **2–4× normal** — the most provably wrecked physiology available — with full recovery on normal refeeding.
- **Post-obese**: full recovery. Yo-yo dieting studies show no major changes.
- One woman followed through three diet-and-regain cycles: no fundamental change in body composition or anything else.
- A male bodybuilder case study: expenditure normalised as soon as he returned to his initial body fat.

**The two anomalies he flags honestly**: two Arctic explorer case studies left permanently ~5% below normal expenditure, and the **Biosphere** experiment (a self-sustaining trial that became caloric restriction when crops failed) where NEAT stayed ~5% reduced after weight restoration. He has no explanation and calls ~100 kcal/day trivial.

His alternative explanation for claimed damage: **underreporting** — people claiming to eat X often eat X plus 30–80%, and when measured "their metabolism is never impaired."

On Lane Norton's cellular-memory rat model (fat cells sensing prior deficit): not done in humans, rat models often differ, and even granting fat-cell memory he sees no route to impaired metabolism when leptin returns to baseline. He's explicitly open to changing his mind. Drugs are a carve-out he won't claim expertise on: thyroid abuse at high dose long enough may permanently affect thyroid metabolism (attributed to Scott Abel — "Scott ael," uncertain), and male steroid abusers may end up hypogonadal.

## Regaining after a diet [21:03–25:39]
The Minnesota fat overshoot is explained by a **muscle signal**: fat signals via leptin, but muscle sends a still-unidentified hunger signal. Subjects regained fat fast and muscle slowly, and appetite stayed elevated until muscle was restored.

Corollary: limit muscle loss on the diet (sufficient protein, resistance training), then keep protein high and train while refeeding, so you end at the same body composition. Even in the post-obese, simply keeping protein high means more of the regain is lean mass — overfeeding is anabolic in its own right. It took obesity researchers 30 years to arrive at this. Nippard supplies the rebuttal to using Minnesota against fast regain — those subjects weren't resistance trained on the way down *or* back up, so physique athletes get a **muscle memory** response they couldn't. McDonald accepts it.

**Targets**: at 4% (male) or 10% (female) you'll feel terrible with nothing near normal hormones until roughly **8–10% male / 16–18% female**. A female who dieted to 10% should regain some fat over the **first four to six weeks**, then build gradually; nothing recovers until calories are back toward maintenance. This is not licence to get fat — a small female is looking at ~1 lb of fat per week and only lost ~15 lb of fat anyway.

**Menstrual dysfunction**: any lost cycle must be reversed because bone density is being lost. It can take **three to five months** to resume, and in monkey models more calories return it faster.

## Training [26:42–50:46]
**On "volume is king."** Context: Schoenfeld's volume-equated ~7×3 vs 3×10 study found similar hypertrophy, feeding the conclusion that a specific 6–15 rep hypertrophy range doesn't exist. McDonald's objection is the next step: without sufficient intensity no amount of volume grows anything; with sufficient intensity low volume grows *something*. If one factor must be named it's **progressive tension overload and getting stronger** — known since 1970s work-induced-hypertrophy research. The biggest natural bodybuilders are always strong; guys who aren't strong don't get big without drugs. His reductio: if only volume mattered, do 36 sets once a month. Parallel complaint about "time under tension" — the original claim was time under *high* tension, and he was disappointed to see Mel Siff make the sloppy version.

**Failure** [29:48–34:21]. HIT advocates (framing traced to Arthur Jones) define intensity as training to the limit rather than as % load. "You can't know how hard you're working unless you go to failure" is BS given RPE scales, and nobody's failure is true failure anyway.
- Required for growth? Absolutely not — people grew without it for decades. It's useful for calibration: some time at failure teaches you what "one to two reps short" actually means.
- Costs: exhausting, and rarely achievable in form on big lifts. He asks how many have taken squats to true failure — stuck mid-rep and pinned. People progress fine without it because it isn't safe.
- **It interacts with volume**: with one set, go to failure. With multiple, taking the first or second to failure often crushes the rest (10, then 6, 5, 4 instead of four eights). A training partner of 20 years' experience would have an entire workout wrecked by one set taken too deep.
- Research is mixed — he recalls a well-designed study using a training block plus peaking/testing phase where failure worked better for bench and worse for squat.
- **Preferred use**: with four sets of eight programmed a couple reps shy, take the last to failure occasionally as a **progress metric** — 12 reps means raise the weight.
- Early DUP work in which nearly every session hit a maximum saw overreaching and overtraining in **four to six weeks**.

**Reps in reserve** [37:25]: he agrees with Nippard's RPE 8–9 proposal. Five reps in the tank is "a good warm-up set," not a way to train for hypertrophy — **1–2 RIR maximum**. Powerlifting differs: RPE 5–6 work is speed work, deload, or technical submaximal volume (he mentions Mike Tuchscherer's waving).

**Drop sets / reverse pyramid** [34:51]: dropping 10% after failure raises the open question of whether the stimulus falls. Straight sets, Berkhan's reverse pyramid (crediting Poliquin earlier) and drop sets are all potentially valid, depending on the lifter's ability to repeat sets — a nervous-system trait — and on biomechanics. His own case: built to squat, but long arms and limiting triceps collapse any compound chest movement by set three, so he splits it (2 compound / 2 isolation / 2 compound / 2 isolation).

**Volume prescription** [38:26]: Wernbom's ("wor bomb's") **40–70 reps** per session, 2–3×/week ≈ 80–210 reps/week; at 8–12 reps that's **4–8 sets**. He notes it's an intermediate prescription and Helms raises it when strength progress stalls — "he and I argue about this constantly." Empirical systems cluster there: Bill Starr's 5×5 plus up to 40 reps of assistance = 65; Lee Haney's 8×8. Most people do too many redundant exercises ("flat bench, flyes, dumbbell bench, dumbbell flyes, incline bench, incline flyes — those are all the same exercise"); women do endless leg work at sub-threshold intensity. Pick 2–3 quality exercises: chest maybe two, legs three, back often three. Studies in Olympic lifters and swimmers show moderate volumes beating both high and low.

**The volume trap / MRV** [41:58]: find the lowest volume and frequency producing the gains you want. Elite athletes take ten years to reach current volume — swimmers go three days a week, then four, then doubles, reaching 12 sessions a week by high school. Jump volume and you have nowhere to go on accommodation. Beginners: one set 2–3×/week, build to three, hold, increase around six months. He suspects high-volume claimants aren't working hard — his anecdote is putting one through a set of 20-rep squats and asking what else he wanted to do for legs; the answer was nothing.

**Frequency** [45:02]: the real question is distributed vs concentrated volume (10 weekly sets as 2×5, 5×2, or 10×1). There's a minimum stimulus threshold and nobody knows it. On the informal Norwegian powerlifting project where the elite team gained more on six days a week than three, he notes the coach later **still said he'd train less frequently with more volume despite those results**. His most distinctive objection: **connective tissue**. Muscle recovers relatively quickly; tendons and ligaments take forever, which is why runners going three to six days a week get hurt without extremely gradual progression. He also notes the reversal argument — if protein-synthesis duration per session shortens as you advance, advanced lifters might need *more* frequency, inverting bodybuilding tradition — and cites Häkkinen ("hakan") showing female Olympic lifters gain better splitting volume across two daily sessions, while flagging Olympic lifting as a different sport.

**MPS research** [49:10]: the link to hypertrophic outcomes is "pretty scant." He mentions a paper tracking protein synthesis over six hours that didn't correlate with long-term growth, defers to Schoenfeld, and frames it as a genuine open question — growth is long-term remodelling, so focusing on one acute pathway may be a mistake.

## Points of disagreement
- **Against the evidence-based crowd's "volume is primary" consensus** — a rabbit hole where intensity and frequency stop being considered [26:42].
- **Against Lane Norton's cellular-memory framing** of metabolic damage: rat data only, no human demonstration, no mechanism given normalised leptin [19:01].
- **Against slow multi-week reverse diets** — two weeks maximum. Nippard notes his own hosted debate reached the same conclusion.
- **With Eric Helms** he agrees on 40–70 reps but says they argue constantly about volume; Helms is more flexible at advanced levels.
- Nippard, not McDonald, supplies the correction that Minnesota subjects weren't resistance trained — McDonald accepts it [23:05].

## Practical application
- Return to maintenance within ~two weeks post-diet; don't creep.
- After a contest-lean diet expect to need ~8–10% (M) / 16–18% (F) before hormones normalise; regain over 4–6 weeks, then build gradually. Restore a lost menstrual cycle — bone density is at stake, and it can take 3–5 months.
- Keep protein high and keep training on both the way down and back up.
- Attack NEAT, not RMR. +1 kcal/min over an eight-hour day is 500 kcal.
- Train at 1–2 RIR; use failure occasionally as a progress check on a final set.
- Target 40–70 reps per muscle per session, 2–3×/week (4–8 hard sets at 8–12), raised only when progress stalls, across 2–3 non-redundant exercises per body part.
- Add volume and frequency in steps over years. Connective tissue is the limiter nobody accounts for.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Nearly all set point and adaptation research is in obese or post-obese populations — "researchers don't care about lean people." Direct metabolic measurement in very lean bodybuilders mostly doesn't exist.
- He'd change his opinion on metabolic damage given human data, and a physiological "crack" in some individuals is conceivable. Drug effects on thyroid and the gonadal axis are outside his expertise. MPS-to-hypertrophy translation is unresolved and he declines a confident answer.
- Names garbled by auto-captions, flagged as uncertain: "Dr LaVine" (almost certainly James Levine), "liel" (almost certainly Rudolph Leibel), "wor bomb" (Wernbom), "hakan" (Häkkinen), "Mike tare" (Mike Tuchscherer), "Martin Burkin" (Martin Berkhan), "Scott ael" (probably Scott Abel), "shonfeld" (Schoenfeld), "meno henselman" (Menno Henselmans).
- Recorded April 2016; several of his caveats ("I haven't kept up with that literature") are time-stamped to then.
