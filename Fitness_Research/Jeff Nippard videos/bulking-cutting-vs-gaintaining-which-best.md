# Bulking and Cutting vs "Gaintaining": Body Recomposition Revisited

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Bulking/ Cutting vs 'Gaintaining': Which Is Best?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ubHlOQnmnd0)) · 2018-11-24 · 29 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Building muscle in a caloric deficit is genuinely possible** and applies to far more people than the usual "untrained, obese, or on steroids" list.
- Two forgotten recomposition populations: **detrained lifters** (muscle memory is real) and **people who got lazy with training** and are now ramping intensity back up.
- **Losing fat in a caloric surplus is real in principle but practically useless.** Jeff would never coach it — for recomposition, prescribe a **slight deficit (5-20% below maintenance)** or maintenance, never a surplus.
- If you want maximum muscle, bulk and cut. If you'd rather stay lean year-round and accept slightly slower gains, **gaintaining** (maintenance-ish calories, aggressive progressive overload, maybe **~1 lb of bodyweight per month**) is perfectly reasonable.
- **Nobody has run the study** comparing 12 months of gaintaining against 6 months bulk + 6 months cut. Jeff would bet on bulk/cut, but says it isn't clear.

## The polls and what they showed
Thought experiment from the previous video: a 5-year lifter, intelligent and dedicated, who has never trained forearms directly, starts **20 sets/week of forearm work** during a 12-week cut. Will he gain forearm muscle? **90% of YouTube viewers said yes** — Jeff agrees, and notes he deliberately rigged the setup (stressing net body mass loss) to trick people, and they didn't fall for it.

On Instagram the same questions did worse: **24% said building muscle in a deficit is impossible**, and **40% said losing fat in a surplus is impossible** (~5,000 and ~8,000 people respectively).

Altered version: the same lifter *has* trained forearms directly for five years. Now **71% said he'd at best maintain**, 28% said he'd still add size. Jeff sides with the majority — maintaining would be a good outcome.

## Evidence on lean mass loss during contest prep
Case studies of natural bodybuilders getting very lean almost without exception show lean mass loss. Lean mass isn't identical to muscle mass (water shifts count), but Jeff thinks real muscle loss is likely at very low body fat.

He cites **Kistler and colleagues (2014)** ([Kistler 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24901578)), with **Peter Fitschen** a contributor, and Fitschen's 2015 summary of the three or four natural bodybuilding prep case studies then available. Across those three studies, **21%, 32% and 43% of total weight lost was lean mass**. The **43% figure (Robinson et al.)** came from the case where weight was lost fastest — hence Fitschen's argument that slower dieting preserves more muscle, and hence the trend toward longer preps.

Jeff's caution: other factors (cardio volume, training quality, macronutrient breakdown) confound this, so don't over-weight the finding — but the trend across the three studies is real.

## When recomposition actually applies
The common comment is that recomposition only works if you're **(1) untrained, (2) obese, or (3) using anabolic steroids**. Jeff accepts all three but says the list is incomplete, and explains the shared mechanism: **muscle has a much lower energy density than fat**, so if you build muscle fast enough it skews the energy-out side of the equation and the simple calories-in/calories-out arithmetic stops mapping cleanly onto body composition.

- **Untrained** — fastest muscle gain there is.
- **Obese** — not fast muscle gain, but abundant stored fat serving as the energy source. Jeff's thought experiment: a morbidly obese person doing light resistance training on a total fast with only essential amino acids, vitamins and minerals would still build muscle. He also thinks you don't need to be that obese — cutting from **20% to 10% body fat** while recomposing seems plausible to him (though he's not aware of data establishing a threshold).
- **Steroid users** — same as untrained: muscle gain fast enough to change the energy dynamics.
- **Detrained lifters** (the forgotten fourth) — post-injury, post-layoff. **Muscle memory** is a scientifically legitimate phenomenon; regain is fast almost regardless of prior status.
- **Lifters who let training slide** (the forgotten fifth) — very common. Motivation and intensity naturally wax and wane; someone who coasted for months and then starts pushing again can definitely build muscle in a deficit.

**The most broadly useful case:** you came back from holiday fatter and slightly smaller. Don't bulk (more fat) and don't just cut (accept the muscle loss). Instead run a recomposition: a **slight deficit of 5-20% below maintenance**, with the focus on progressively rebuilding strength and training intensity. You'll almost certainly build muscle, lose fat, and get your old physique back quickly.

## Fat loss in a surplus, and the thermodynamics
Much more obscure and much less likely than muscle gain in a deficit. The surplus would have to be tiny and the muscle gain sustained over so long that it isn't practically meaningful. Jeff set it up purely as a thought experiment to make the conceptual point: **there is no switch in the body that flips at maintenance and starts storing fat.** Thermodynamics only requires net energy storage, not fat storage specifically.

He is explicit that energy balance holds — dozens of metabolic ward studies show surpluses cause weight gain and deficits weight loss. The point is to think at the **net scale, not the tissue scale**: where the weight is gained or lost depends on training, and the disparity in energy density between muscle and fat is the x-factor.

## Numbers and sources
- The **3,500 kcal per pound** rule: Jeff recommends **Lyle McDonald's** article "The 3500 Calorie Rule," which explains why it works as a rough ballpark for most people but breaks down once training is in the equation, and ignores water weight (water carries no energy).
- The **~9,400 kcal per kilogram of fat mass** figure comes from **Hall (2008)**, who calculated the required energy deficit per unit of weight loss ([Hall 2008](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2376744/)). It's an average — the true value shifts with body composition as hydration status and tissue energy density change.
- **Eric Helms'** November 2015 article on Shredded By Science (with co-authors/contributors, one named in the captions as Lawrence Judd — spelling uncertain) covers the same territory. It lists the flawed assumptions people make, starting with **homogeneity of the human system** (treating all tissues as having equal metabolisable energy). It also summarises lean body mass and fat mass changes across many studies, showing recomposition occurring in both surpluses and deficits — including in **trained subjects with 9+ years of experience** in the **Jose Antonio** papers, and in elite athletes.
- Helms' opening example: a woman 12 weeks into training whose clothes fit far better and who looks visibly leaner, but whose scale weight hasn't moved — classic beginner recomposition. Jeff's balance: don't stress the scale early in a diet, but don't use recomposition as a cop-out for a stalled fat loss phase either. Look for visual and measurement change.

## Bulking/cutting vs gaintaining
For intermediate-to-advanced lifters whose goal is **maximum muscle**, Jeff still recommends separate bulk and cut cycles.

But he questions whether most people actually have that goal. Marginally more muscle isn't worth carrying extra fat and disliking your physique for months if you'd rather stay lean. For those people, **gaintaining** — stay lean, hover near maintenance, chase progressive overload, gain roughly a pound a month — makes sense. He credits **Lane Norton** and **Alberto Nunez** as having popularised the approach in natural bodybuilding a few years earlier (noting he may be misattributing).

The unanswered question he'd like tested: a **12-month study**, one group doing 6 months bulk + 6 months cut, the other gaintaining for the full year. Since cutting reliably costs some muscle, does that loss offset the faster gain rate of bulking?

**His hypothesis (bulking wins):** a surplus gives a more anabolic environment — slightly higher testosterone, more anabolic signalling — and being heavier lets you lift more weight, meaning more volume and tension. If you then cut slowly with sufficient protein, muscle losses stay small, so you finish 12 months ahead. But gaintaining has demonstrably worked for many people and keeps you lean year-round, which may be the better outcome for you.

## Practical application
- **Recomposition goal (returning lifter, detrained, beginner, or overweight):** slight deficit, 5-20% below maintenance, and prioritise rebuilding training intensity and strength.
- **Maximum muscle goal (intermediate/advanced):** separate bulk and cut phases.
- **Want to stay lean year-round:** gaintain — maintenance-ish calories, hard progressive overload, ~1 lb/month weight gain.
- Never program a surplus for someone whose goal is simultaneous fat loss and muscle gain.
- **Cut slowly** and keep protein high to preserve lean mass; fast weight loss cost 43% of lost weight as lean mass in the worst case study.
- Don't judge early diet progress by scale weight alone — use measurements and appearance.

## Caveats & unknowns
- No study compares gaintaining to bulk/cut over a matched period. The recommendation is reasoning, not evidence.
- The three prep case studies are a very small sample with many confounds (cardio, training quality, macros).
- The 9,400 kcal/kg figure is an average that changes with body composition; the 3,500 kcal/lb rule is a rough approximation only.
- No data establishes how overweight you need to be for fat stores to fuel recomposition; Jeff says so explicitly.
- The attribution of gaintaining to Norton and Nunez is offered with an explicit "I may be recalling this incorrectly," and one article contributor's name is uncertain from the captions.
