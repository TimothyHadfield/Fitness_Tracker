# Four Science-Based Ways to Add Weight to Your Squat Fast

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Fastest Way To Blow Up Your Squat (4 Science-Based Steps)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sdeQjm7avi8)) · 2021-03-24 · 11 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Prime the nervous system with **heavy walkouts at 95–105% of 1RM** and **pin squats**, once every couple of weeks.
- Fine-tune three technique variables: try **low bar** (most people are 5–10% stronger with it), find your ideal **depth**, and go about **2 inches wider per side** with slightly more toe flare.
- Use gear properly: a belt is worth roughly **5%**, knee sleeves **2–5%**, and a hard-soled shoe beats a running shoe.
- Set **regular PRs** at a cadence matched to your training age — every session as a novice, every other week as an intermediate, and periodic near-PR top sets as an advanced lifter.

He notes the basics still apply and are important: squat **2–3× per week**, squat early in the workout while fresh, and eat enough calories and protein.

## 1. Prime the nervous system with acclimating drills
[00:31] Nothing mystical — you're getting the brain and nervous system used to firing under heavy loads, like shooting a weighted training ball so the real basketball feels light afterwards.

**Heavy walkouts.** Load **95–105% of 1RM**, walk it out, brace as normal, then walk it back in without squatting. If you're new to walkouts, start with a weight you've actually squatted and work up to supramaximal loads. Set the safeties higher than usual and use a spotter if available. Do these **once or twice a month before your planned squat work**: later sets feel lighter, and it builds confidence and familiarizes the upper back and stabilizers with supporting heavy loads.

**Pin squats.** Set the bar on safety pins at the bottom and start with the concentric. Useful for lifters intimidated by a lift that begins with the lowering phase — with a deadlift, failing to lift means the bar just stays on the floor, but many experienced lifters fear unracking and descending and then getting buckled. Starting with the positive removes that fear. Progression: set the pins at about **half-squat depth** and gradually lower them over time toward parallel or just below. If new to pin squats, start with **65–75% of 1RM for 4–6 reps** at parallel; with the pins set higher you can load much closer to max and lower them progressively.

If confidence is what's limiting your squat, pick one of these drills and do it **once every couple of weeks**.

## 2. Fine-tune technique
[02:34] Three variables: bar position, depth, stance width.

**Bar position.** Only about a quarter of his audience squats low bar, despite most people being **5–10% stronger** with it. A **2017 study** comparing the two found the low bar back squat is a more efficient way of squatting large loads — but with an influence of expertise, meaning there's an adjustment period before the strength boost appears. The mechanical change is small: shift the bar a couple of inches lower on the back, which causes slightly more forward lean; the bar should still travel in a straight line over mid-foot. He personally slightly favours high bar for hypertrophy (similar stimulus at lighter loads), but if the goal is squat numbers, low bar is a no-brainer to try. You don't have to switch exclusively — run both concurrently, low bar for heavy top sets and high bar for paused technique and hypertrophy work, which makes you a more well-rounded lifter.

**Depth.** Two schools of thought, both workable, so test both.
- *Cut depth to the minimum your goal requires.* Less depth = less ROM = more weight moved. For powerlifting you must get the hip crease below the knee joint; for general strength and size, parallel or just below is enough, and going deeper is wasted energy.
- *Squat as deep as you comfortably can* to maximize the bounce out of the hole (the stretch-shortening cycle). Nippard falls in this camp himself — squatting with the intention of limiting depth makes him weaker.

Highly individual. General pattern: **good ankle mobility → squat more by going deeper and using the bounce; poor ankle mobility → squat more by cutting to minimum required depth.**

**Stance width.** If you currently squat at or just outside shoulder width, go about **2 inches wider on each side** with slightly more toe flare. Per a large article by **Greg Nuckols**, a wider stance lets you hit the stretch reflex without going unnecessarily deep, increases adductor involvement, and eases the load on the back — collectively allowing most people to squat more. Anthropometry varies, so it may not work for you.

## 3. Use your gear better
[06:08] Three items with reasonable empirical support.

**Belt.** Not a passive tool and not a licence to get lazy with bracing — it exists so you can brace harder. Actively brace against it: breathe down into the belt before descending, pushing the midsection out in a full **360 degrees** rather than only inflating the gut forward. That creates a rigid column of support and reduces energy leaks. Research repeatedly shows belts do **not** reduce core activation, contrary to popular belief. His estimate: about a **5% boost**, especially once you learn to use it — a 405 lb squatter should reach ~425 lb fairly quickly.

**Knee sleeves.** More optional. They appear to work by bunching behind the knees for extra spring out of the bottom, and add comfort and stability that reduces perceived difficulty at the same load, allowing more volume tolerance. In his coaching experience, about a **2–5% absolute strength boost**, a figure supported by a new study covered in a recent issue of the MASS research review.

**Shoes.** A solid sole increases stability and force transfer far better than a squishy running shoe. On a budget: Chuck Taylors or Vans. With limited ankle mobility and trouble reaching depth, consider a heeled squat shoe — though this is largely personal preference.

## 4. Set regular weight or rep PRs
[07:41] How often depends on training age.

**Novice or detrained (first 6 months of training or retraining).** Set a PR essentially every session. Pick a single rep target — say **5 reps** — and add **5 lb every week**. Sustained, that's over **100 lb on the squat in under six months**. This is linear single progression: sets and reps fixed, weight climbing weekly. When the sets get grindy or form breaks down while adding weight, move on.

**Beginner to intermediate** (also useful for more advanced lifters who've trained unstructured). Linear **double** progression. Pick a narrow rep range, e.g. **4–5 reps**, and alternate adding a rep and adding weight. Week 1: 3×4 @ 200 lb. Week 2: 3×5 @ 200 lb. Week 3: back to 4 reps @ 205 lb. Week 4: 5 reps @ 205 lb. Weight PRs now come every other week.

**Intermediate to advanced.** PRs become infrequent and progress non-linear, but you still need regular PR signposts — which can be as simple as hitting a familiar weight and rep count at lower exertion or with better technique. In his power building program you work up to a near-PR or actual PR top set **every second week**, with exact load auto-regulated, then perform a **max test at the end of the 10-week block** aiming for a new PR.

The principle: as you advance, PR signposts get less frequent but must not disappear — they're your confirmation that you're still trending in the right direction.

## Practical application
- Add a heavy walkout at 95–105% of 1RM, or a pin squat session, once every 2 weeks before squat work; safeties high, spotter present.
- Test low bar for at least a training block; expect an adjustment period before the 5–10% shows up.
- Test both depth strategies (minimum-required vs deepest comfortable) and keep whichever gives more weight; let ankle mobility guide the first guess.
- Move stance 2 inches wider per side with more toe flare and see if the lift improves.
- Brace 360 degrees into the belt; add knee sleeves and a hard-soled shoe.
- Match your progression scheme to training age: 5 lb/week single progression → 4–5 rep double progression → biweekly near-PR top sets with an end-of-block max test.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The 2017 low bar study showed an expertise effect, so the strength advantage may not appear until the technique is learned.
- Depth strategy is explicitly individual — both schools work, and Nippard's personal preference (deep) is not a universal recommendation.
- The wider-stance recommendation depends on anthropometry and may not apply to you; he had not yet tested it on himself at the time of filming.
- The 5% belt figure is his own estimate; the knee sleeve figure comes from coaching experience plus one new study.
- The audience poll on bar position may be skewed by his own high-bar demo video.
