# Muscle Protein Synthesis Explained: What It Is, What Training Does to It, and Whether It Predicts Growth

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Everything You Need To Know About Muscle Protein Synthesis ft. Jorn Trommelen" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_otSunLL8AU)) · 2018-03-24 · 62 min
**Topic:** Physiology

Part one of the Jorn Trommelen interview — PhD researcher at Maastricht University, a leading muscle metabolism lab known for metabolic tracer and muscle biopsy work. This part is mostly training and mechanism; part two covers diet.

## Bottom line
- Muscle is a wall; amino acids are bricks. **Muscle protein synthesis (MPS)** adds bricks, **muscle protein breakdown (MPB)** removes them. Both run constantly; net balance decides whether you grow.
- **MPS is the variable that matters.** It can swing 3-5 fold. MPB stays in a narrow range and is largely predictable in healthy people — and blocking it entirely is harmful, not helpful.
- Training makes the muscle better at using dietary amino acids, and that sensitisation lasts **1-2 days (up to 72 hours)** — which is why the post-workout "window" is not urgent.
- Training guidelines that are actually clear: **multiple sets beat one set; ~10+ sets per muscle per week; at least 2 sessions per muscle per week; ≥2 minutes rest; train close to failure; load and rep range barely matter if you do.**
- MPS **does** predict long-term hypertrophy — when the measurement window matches what you are measuring.

## What MPS and MPB actually are [06:07]

Both processes run all the time. If synthesis exceeds breakdown you are in positive balance and growing; if breakdown exceeds synthesis you are shrinking.

MPS dominates the equation for three reasons:
1. **MPB moves within a narrow range** — up slightly with exercise, down slightly with feeding. MPS can be manipulated 3-5 fold. So net balance is almost entirely determined by MPS.
2. **The only lever on breakdown is eating**, and only a minimal amount of insulin is needed for the maximal inhibitory effect. Any time you are not fasted, you have already done what you can.
3. **Breakdown is functional, not the enemy.** Rats genetically engineered to be unable to break down muscle protein end up **smaller and weaker** than normal rats. Cracks in a wall should be torn out and rebuilt before you build higher. The ideal state is high turnover — lots of breakdown clearing old, misfolded proteins while synthesis runs higher still. (Trommelen draws the parallel to intramuscular fat: endurance athletes and diabetics both carry a lot, but high turnover makes it healthy in one case and inflammatory in the other.)

Practical consequence: most studies measure MPS only. In healthy trained subjects, breakdown rates are simply not that interesting.

## Does training "tear the muscle down"? [10:18]

Not the point. Brad Schoenfeld's three proposed hypertrophy mechanisms — mechanical tension, metabolic stress, muscle damage — are not equal; damage and metabolic stress are heavily debated, with some arguing they are net negative. **You do not need to induce muscle damage.** Likely an inverted-U: a little may help, too much hurts. If damage were the goal, getting punched in a boxing gym all day would make you the most jacked man alive.

Also: eating protein before a workout means you can be in **positive protein balance during training itself** — Trommelen's lab has shown this. Any time you have eaten some protein, MPS exceeds MPB.

## Why training is required [14:56]

Eating protein alone raises MPS above MPB, but you cannot eat 500 g of protein a day and become huge — the body will not hold onto muscle protein without a reason to. Exercise supplies the reason.

Tracer studies show that even untrained, some ingested amino acids get built into muscle — but **after training you utilise far more of them**. Crucially, that sensitisation is **long-lasting: at least 1-2 days, with the longest documented MPS elevation after exercise being 72 hours.** This directly devalues the "eat protein immediately after training" window.

## Training variables where the science is clear [17:32]

Trommelen frames programming as science setting the boundaries and art filling them in.

- **Sets:** multiple sets are clearly superior to one. Old-school HIT ("one all-out set, anything more is overtraining") is simply wrong. **~10 sets per week beats fewer than 10**, divided however your split allows. Volume likely needs to rise with training age — after a year at 10 sets your body is used to it.
- **Load / rep range:** Stu Phillips's research line showed **you can build muscle with essentially any load as long as you train to muscular failure.** The goal is to recruit and fatigue the fibres. Jeremy Loenneke's blood flow restriction work supports this — light loads fatigue the muscle early, and fatigue is what the muscle senses. Loenneke has even shown growth from **contracting the muscle with no weight at all** (essentially posing). Actual failure is probably not required; **1-2 reps shy is likely fine.**
- **Rest:** at least **2 minutes** between sets. Nothing magic about 2 minutes — it is about recovering enough to use more load on later sets. If you do not want to wait, superset with something unrelated (bench press, then leg extensions). Caveat on the research: studies comparing 1-minute to 5-minute rest favour long rest, but the long-rest workout takes five times as long — you could have used that time for more sets.
- **Frequency:** at least **2 sessions per muscle per week** is clear. Whether more is better is not. This fits the 72-hour MPS elevation. Note that a "bro split" often trains muscles more than once anyway once you count close-grip bench hitting triceps, pressing hitting delts, etc.

**Why the "sexy" questions never get answered:** it is easy to show 3x/week beats 1x/week, but distinguishing 4x from 5x per week would need something like 10,000 subjects because of diminishing returns. Null results in small studies are a statistical artefact, not proof that a variable does not matter.

## Does the MPS response shorten as you get more trained? [30:55]

Less established than commonly claimed. Studies typically measure **mixed** muscle protein synthesis — everything in the muscle. In an untrained person *every* protein type responds, including mitochondrial protein, which stops adapting after ~3 months of lifting. So mixed MPS looks like it declines — but that is the irrelevant fractions dropping out. Looking specifically at **myofibrillar** protein (the contractile proteins that give strength and size), it is **not clear** that the response shortens. Trommelen would not be surprised if it does, but the evidence is weaker than the high-frequency crowd suggests. And even if true, doubling session volume might equally prolong the MPS response.

## Progressive overload [36:09]

Trommelen absolutely believes it is the basis of everything — but the common definition is too narrow. Adding weight while dropping reps (10RM this week, more weight for 8 reps in two months) is **not** progressive overload, it is trading. Real progression means **more weight at the same reps, or more total weekly volume** (or more frequency, or higher intensity — the tanning analogy: to keep getting darker you need more frequency, longer exposure, or higher intensity).

Ideal world: keep volume low and add weight weekly at fixed reps — linear progression. When that stops working, add weekly sets. Practically, as weekly volume climbs you almost always have to raise frequency to handle it (30 sets of legs in one day is not viable).

Jeff raises bodybuilders who use "muscle confusion" and grow anyway. Trommelen's read: **they are progressing without tracking it.** Different exercises recruit different fibres, so rotating movements maintains stimulus across regions; they just do not log workouts, so they never notice their bench went up over a year. He notes that if truly no progression occurred, someone would still bench their first-ever weight after five years of training despite carrying far more muscle — evolutionarily nonsensical.

Jeff's practical point: **whether your bench (or any rep max) is going up is a very good indicator that everything else is working.** If volume is climbing but strength is not, you are either overtraining or still undertraining.

## The big methodological argument: does MPS predict hypertrophy? [44:20]

This section is the core of the interview.

The criticism (partly triggered by EMG-validity papers with Andrew Vigotsky among the authors) attacked the final link in the chain: that acute MPS predicts long-term hypertrophy.

Three studies, in order:
1. **Untrained subjects, MPS measured for 4 hours after one session, then 10 weeks of training.** No correlation between acute MPS and muscle gain. This is the study everyone cites.
2. **Six-week training study measuring MPS across the entire six weeks.** Good correlation with muscle mass gains.
3. **Same authors as study 1, improved design: MPS measured over 24 and 48 hours after a session.** MPS **did** predict muscle gains — except in completely untrained subjects.

Trommelen's explanation: **the measurement window must match the response you are studying.**
- The exercise-induced MPS response lasts 24-48 hours (up to 72). Measuring only the first 4 hours is like judging a marathon by the first 5 km.
- Conversely, a 4-hour window is *correct* for measuring the response to a protein shake. Measure that same shake's effect over 3 months and you will see nothing — the window is far too long.
- The untrained exception: in completely untrained people MPS is artificially high and directed at **repairing** damaged non-contractile proteins rather than building myofibrillar protein. After about four weeks of training the response specialises toward myofibrillar synthesis and the correlation appears.

So of three studies, two show a good correlation and the one that does not is exactly the condition where a correlation would be surprising.

## Why Trommelen trusts MPS studies over small training studies [53:34]

Muscle gain is slow. Take a controversial intervention — say foam rolling — with 10 subjects per group over 6 weeks. Even if it genuinely helped by 10%, one group gains 1.0 kg and the other 1.1 kg: not statistically detectable. Over six months it would be 4.0 vs 4.4 kg — a 400 g difference, potentially significant. Properly powered you would need six times the duration and roughly three times the subjects: an **18x bigger study**. Or you could run 18 bad studies, get 18 publications, and become famous for "busting myths" while reaching the wrong conclusion every time.

His filter: **a training study under 10 weeks with fewer than ~20 subjects per group commands very little faith** — not worthless (it feeds meta-analyses) but deceiving on its own.

Concrete illustration: **about 80% of protein supplementation studies find no significant increase in muscle mass**, purely because they are too small and too short. Had we not had MPS studies showing a clear anabolic response, the field would have abandoned protein 50 years ago. It took his lab's meta-analysis (nine years before this interview) to convincingly show protein works, and only by pooling all those small trials.

MPS studies are also **more expensive** than most long-term training studies, not easier — the ideal is doing both, with MPS measured across a training study.

His methodological principle: dismissing any line of evidence — mechanistic, animal, in vitro, observational — means you do not understand it. Where studies conflict, you owe an explanation (e.g. "the long-term study was underpowered"). "That doesn't translate" usually means "I don't get it."

## Why the criticism arose at all [59:41]

People confuse MPS with **molecular signalling markers** (gene expression, phosphorylation). Gene expression says the body *might* grow — and if you do not eat, nothing happens. **MPS is not a signal; it is literally amino acids being added to the wall. It is the definition of getting bigger.** If adding bricks did not correlate with wall size, something very strange would be going on.

Jeff's corroboration from another angle: milk protein produces greater MPS spikes than soy protein acutely, **and** chronic trials show more hypertrophy with milk than soy — the two lines agree.

Trommelen's caution on interpreting magnitude: a 50% higher MPS does not mean 50% more muscle gain. You have to account for duration. If whey beats soy by 50% for only the 3 hours after exercise, divide by eight to express it over 24 hours.

## Practical application
- Train each muscle **at least twice a week**, **10+ sets per muscle per week**, increasing volume as you advance.
- Take sets **close to failure (0-2 reps in reserve)**; load and rep range are secondary once effort is sufficient.
- Rest **at least 2 minutes**, or superset unrelated muscles to save time.
- Track your lifts. If your strength on a fixed rep target is not rising every couple of months, adjust volume.
- Do not chase muscle damage or soreness, and do not rush post-workout protein — sensitisation lasts 24-72 hours.
- When reading research: check whether the MPS measurement window matched what was being measured, and treat null results from small, short training studies with scepticism.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Whether frequency beyond 2x/week matters, and whether the MPS response truly shortens with training age, are both unresolved.
- Whether your 20th set of legs in a session is worthless or as valuable as your first is genuinely unknown — performance is wrecked, but the muscle only senses fatigue.
- Trommelen explicitly declines to comment on EMG validity (not his field); his lab colleagues are sceptical of the EMG-to-MPS step.
- Much of the fitness debate, he argues, is really about **perspective**: minimalists want the 20% of effort that gives 80% of results; competitive athletes want everything. Neither position is wrong — the error is claiming a marginal variable "doesn't matter" when the data are merely underpowered.
