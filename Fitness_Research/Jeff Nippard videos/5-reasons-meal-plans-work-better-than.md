# Five Reasons a Fixed Meal Plan Can Beat IIFYM (Late-Prep Case)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "5 Reasons Why Meal Plans Can Work Better than IIFYM" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=anAC-x2I6Fw)) · 2015-11-23 · 9 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Three weeks out from his pro debut Jeff abandoned flexible dieting for a fixed five-meals-a-day plan: same foods daily, with portion sizes adjusted whenever his coach changed his macros.
- The research trend favouring flexible over rigid dieting for weight control is real but **population-level, not universal**. Some people are highly reactive to food cues and are better served by simply not having the trigger food available.
- The advantages he claims are **adherence, time, performance, measurement precision and psychology** — not any metabolic property of the foods.
- Nothing about chicken, rice and sweet potato is magic. The plan was a behavioural tool for the hardest three weeks of a contest prep.
- A middle option: **bracket the day** — fixed breakfast and fixed pre-bed meal, flexible in between.

## 1. Fewer cravings and less hunger [00:30]
This looks counterintuitive against the flexible-vs-rigid literature, where more flexible approaches generally track with better weight-control outcomes. But the literature also describes real individual variation in **food-cue responsiveness**:

- Some people who deprive themselves of a specific craved food crave it **more** for being deprived.
- Others get a small taste and find that taste is a **sufficient reward to want more** — the taste escalates rather than satisfies.
- Either can be compounded by the **"to hell with it" effect**: having eaten the Reese's cup, the day is deemed blown, so overeating continues.

Jeff places himself in the second camp, and thinks most of the bodybuilding population is there too. For him, outright restriction controlled cravings better than moderation did — particularly at very low body fat, where the pressure is highest. Hunger improved as a side effect, because the foods he wanted most were the **least satiating** ones.

## 2. Time freed up [02:34]
Late in prep, a growing share of his day was going into logging food in MyFitnessPal and engineering meals to hit exact numbers. A fixed structure removed that decision and data-entry load entirely.

**Partial version:** keep the same breakfast and the same bedtime meal every day, fill the middle flexibly. You get a consistent start and end to the day plus room to fit foods you want.

## 3. Better gym performance [03:35]
Nutrient timing around training is minor **relative to** total daily macronutrient intake and training quality — but "minor" is not "zero". Jeff argues the effect of pre-workout nutrition likely **grows as you get leaner**, because there is simply less energy circulating and the pre-workout meal becomes most of what you have to train on.

You do not need a meal plan to eat well pre-workout — but a plan **guarantees** it happens. His candid point about flexible dieters: self-restraint is often overestimated, and the food that gets eaten is sometimes unsatiating or gives no sustained energy for training.

Priority framing: if the goal is lifestyle, general muscle gain or fat loss, peri-workout nutrition can be ignored entirely. It is a tool for people trying to extract the last increment of muscle and fat loss.

## 4. Cleaner weigh-in data [05:41]
He tells clients — correctly — to watch the **trend**, not daily fluctuations. In practice, near the end of prep, an unexplained 2-3 lb spike is demoralising. A fixed plan reduces the noise in the signal:

- **Sodium stays roughly constant** day to day.
- **Food weight and volume stay constant.** High-volume, high-residue foods sit heavier on the next morning's scale than low-volume, low-residue foods; if that varies daily, scale weight varies daily.

Important qualifier: none of this changes fat loss. Sodium swings and food volume have **no long-term effect on fat loss** — they may shift how you look through water shifts, but the benefit here is psychological and measurement-related, not physiological.

## 5. It felt like bodybuilding [07:14]
The personal reason. Eating the foods bodybuilders historically eat — chicken and rice, sweet potato — was motivating, a daily reminder that he had done a hard prep before and could do it again, and a source of nostalgia for past preps that he deliberately used as fuel.

## Practical application
- Judge flexible vs rigid by **your own** cue-responsiveness, not by the average finding. If a small taste reliably escalates, restriction may genuinely serve you better.
- Reserve full meal plans for the final weeks of a prep or any phase where adherence is the binding constraint — not for offseason or general lifestyle dieting.
- If a full plan feels excessive, bracket the day: fixed first and last meals, flexible middle.
- Standardise sodium and food volume if you want daily weigh-ins to be interpretable late in a diet.
- Fix a specific pre-workout meal when leanness makes training energy scarce; ignore peri-workout timing when goals are general.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Entirely a personal-experience video from Jeff's pro debut prep; the flexible-vs-rigid research and food-cue-responsiveness work are described but **no studies or researchers are named**.
- The claim that pre-workout nutrition matters more as you get leaner is presented as reasoning, not as a tested finding.
- Adherence is individual: for a person who spirals under restriction, a fixed plan is the worse tool. The whole argument turns on knowing which type you are.
