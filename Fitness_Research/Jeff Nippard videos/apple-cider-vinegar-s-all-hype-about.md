# Apple Cider Vinegar: What's All the Hype About?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "APPLE CIDER VINEGAR: What's All The Hype About? (Science-Based)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bmGVlIaqTZk)) · 2018-05-14 · 9 min
**Topic:** Supplements

## Bottom line
- Mostly busted. The only well-supported effect — blunting blood glucose and insulin response — comes from the vinegar (acetic acid), not anything unique to apples, so plain vinegar works just as well.
- Only one decent weight-loss study exists: 12 weeks in obese subjects produced roughly 2 kg loss at the highest dose, and all groups regained it within 4 weeks of stopping.
- The anti-glycemic effect works by inhibiting starch digestion, so it helps with complex carbs and does essentially nothing for simple sugars — the opposite of when you'd want it.
- The "mother" has no research behind it, and its enzymes would be digested in the stomach anyway.
- Do not use it for tooth whitening — the acid erodes enamel.

## What it actually is [00:32]
Apples are crushed and the liquid pressed out. Yeast ferments the natural sugars to alcohol, then bacteria convert the alcohol to acetic acid — vinegar. Nothing about this is apple-specific; vinegar can be made from grapes, coconuts, potatoes, maple syrup, wine, and essentially any fermentable carbohydrate source.

## The "mother" [01:02]
The stringy goop in unfiltered ACV, credited by advocates with containing health-giving enzymes. Nippard is sceptical:
- No research exists separating the mother's effects from those of the acetic acid itself.
- Physiologically, proteolytic enzymes in the stomach would chop up any enzymes in the mother, rendering them inactive.
- Even if something else in there were beneficial, it would almost certainly be present in greater quantity in an actual apple.
- Most ACV people actually buy doesn't contain the mother at all.

## Weight and fat loss [02:04]
One high-quality study exists: a **2009 trial in 175 obese Japanese subjects**, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, over **12 weeks**, three groups:
- placebo (made to taste like vinegar),
- **15 ml** (~1 tablespoon) vinegar daily,
- **30 ml** (~2 tablespoons) vinegar daily,

split between breakfast and supper. Both vinegar groups ended significantly lower in body weight and body fat than placebo, despite no difference in reported calorie or macronutrient intake. The authors concluded daily vinegar intake might help prevent metabolic syndrome by reducing obesity, proposing **AMPK-mediated inhibition of lipogenesis** as the mechanism.

Reasons for scepticism:
- Subjects were obese; results may not extrapolate to lean, healthy people at all.
- The effect size is meagre — even the high-dose group only went from about **73 kg to 71 kg**, roughly **2 kg (4.5 lb) in 12 weeks**.
- At a **4-week follow-up after the study ended, all three groups had regained the weight**.
- Energy intake was tracked by **self-reported diet diary**, universally recognised as unreliable and inaccurate for calorie reporting.

A **2013 study** did show increased satiety when vinegar was taken with breakfast — but the effect was driven by vinegar making people **nauseous**, which led the author to discourage its use as an appetite suppressant.

## Blood glucose — the strongest claim [04:37]
A **2017 meta-analysis of 11 studies** found a significant effect in favour of vinegar for both glucose and insulin response. Five of those comparisons did use apple cider vinegar specifically. However:
- Effects are generally larger in subjects with **diabetes or insulin resistance** — that's where the practical significance lies, not in healthy individuals.
- The effect is **carbohydrate-type dependent, and ironically backwards**: it works by inhibiting **starch digestion**, so it helps meaningfully with complex/starchy carbohydrates (where the glycemic response is already lower) and **doesn't seem to work at all with simple sugars** — precisely the situation, like sugary drinks and processed food, where you'd most want it. A catch-22.

## The other claims [05:40]
- **Acne:** it can apparently dry out pimples, but because of the smell and the risk of chemical burns, experts recommend safer and more effective skincare methods.
- **Digestion:** possible if the mother is prebiotic, but an actual apple contains far more prebiotics, plus more nutrients and fibre. On probiotics, the Vinegar Institute states the mother is produced by and contains **acetobacter** — a line of bacteria unlikely to be probiotic. Digestive benefits are not well supported, though Nippard stays open to individual benefit for reasons not yet understood.
- **Tooth whitening:** actively unwise. The acid clearly erodes enamel.
- **Cancer and cardiovascular disease:** no convincing human evidence.
- **House cleaning:** chemical disinfectants are simply more effective.

## Verdict
Busted. The credible glucose and insulin benefits aren't unique to apple cider vinegar — plain vinegar does the same — and mainly matter to people with or at risk of disordered glucose control. Weight loss is determined by calorie balance; Nippard has yet to find an effective, legal shortcut around diet and exercise.

## Practical application
- If you want a glycemic benefit and have insulin resistance or diabetes, any vinegar will do; take it with starchy, complex-carbohydrate meals, not with sugary ones. Doses studied for weight were 15-30 ml (1-2 tbsp) daily, split across meals.
- For appetite control, prefer higher-fibre foods, more water with meals, or simply eating more slowly — the vinegar route works by making you queasy.
- For everything else claimed of it, eat an apple.
- Don't put it on your teeth.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The weight-loss evidence rests on a single study with obese subjects, self-reported intake, a small effect, and complete regain at follow-up.
- No research exists isolating the mother's effects.
- Whether the glucose benefit translates to meaningful health outcomes in healthy people is unaddressed.
