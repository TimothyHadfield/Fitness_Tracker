# Menno Henselmans on Bro Splits, Training Frequency and the Anabolic Window

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Interview With Menno Henselmans | Bro Splits, Training Frequency & Anabolic Window" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xB6EHn4H5LY)) · 2016-01-06 · 83 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **Menno's central model:** the "anabolic window" is not a two-hour post-workout slot — it is the whole period a muscle sits in positive protein balance after training, and it **shrinks as you approach your genetic ceiling**. Beginners: up to 72 hours (so 1x/week works). Advanced: hours, so frequency has to rise.
- **Menno:** beginners get no volume-equated benefit from training a muscle more than once a week; intermediates should lean toward **3+ sessions per muscle**; advanced lifters can go to **daily, or even twice-daily**, at roughly 1–2 exercises per muscle per session.
- **Menno on the Norwegian Frequency Project:** the national powerlifting team, identical percentage-based programs, **3 vs 6 sessions per body part per week**. The 6-day group made large gains (many lifters ~20% strength increases over 8–12 weeks) where the 3-day group made the little you'd expect of elite lifters.
- **Menno:** the frequency literature *understates* frequency's benefit, because in the real world spreading work out also raises the load you can use on every exercise after the first.
- **Their key disagreement:** when progress stalls, **Nippard would drop frequency and raise per-session volume, then rebuild frequency**; **Menno would never use reduced frequency to break a plateau** — he'd add volume, variety or a more complex periodisation model.
- **Menno on metabolic stress:** "a great idea that didn't live up to the evidence." Every pump-based prediction (short rests, moderate reps, partials) failed when tested. Mechanical tension dominates.

## How often to train a muscle [06:03]
Menno's answer depends entirely on training age.

**Beginners.** Most research shows **no benefit to training a muscle more than once weekly** when weekly volume is equated. The traditional bro split works well for beginners.

**Why** — the anabolic window. Elevated muscle protein balance above sedentary baseline persists for a long time in beginners; Menno cites **over 72 hours documented**, and argues that in a genuinely untrained person the window effectively **lasts all week**, which is exactly why frequency studies in untrained subjects come up null.

**Intermediates.** The evidence-based consensus is ~2x/week; **Menno goes higher — three or more sessions per muscle** for most intermediates. He counts **three good studies** showing 3 sessions per body part beating 1, volume- and frequency-matched overall.

**Advanced.** Daily per body part, in a full-body rotation. He cites **one study on highly advanced female athletes showing benefits of twice-daily over once-daily training**, and believes there's a second.

**Nippard's counter [07:36]:** Rippetoe's *Practical Programming* argues the opposite — that the stress-recovery-adaptation cycle is *shorter* in beginners, hence squatting three times a week on a novice linear progression. Menno's reply: he admires Rippetoe's work but Rippetoe is a self-described non-science-based coach; the logic is plausible but the research doesn't support it.

**Menno's mechanism for the reversal** [08:37]: the **repeated bout effect**. Advanced lifters aren't recovering faster so much as **inflicting far less damage** — beginners are clumsy and very good at damaging themselves. He credits a Norwegian researcher whose name the captions render as **"to Rastat" / "Tulsat" — most likely Truls Raastad, flagged as uncertain**, as the leading authority on post-strength-training recovery, whose work shows recovery from a given session gets quicker as you advance. Central fatigue exists but is well self-regulated and usually taken out of context.

## The Norwegian Frequency Project [11:09]
The study Menno leans on hardest, unpublished at the time and known to him through Norwegian contacts (he says he has seen the actual programs).

- Subjects: the **Norwegian national powerlifting team** — elite.
- Design: **identical percentage-based programs**, elaborately periodised down to specified sets, reps and percentages (e.g. box squats at 70% — five reps, then eight reps, then three reps at 80%). **Training volume exactly equated.**
- Only variable: **6 sessions per body part per week vs 3**.
- Result: the 3-day group progressed as you'd expect elite lifters to — barely. The 6-day group progressed well, with **many lifters gaining ~20% in strength** across an **8- or 12-week** study.
- Hypertrophy was also measured (quadriceps). It **did not reach significance — p ≈ .06** — which Menno characterises as a strong trend.
- The team initially didn't want to publish, treating it as a competitive secret; it leaked and went viral, and was under review by the time of recording.
- Data plots appeared in an article by **Børge Fagerli** *(captions: "Berg/Burger Fagerly" — flagged)*, roughly titled "Reignite your progress with new science", on EliteFTS; Menno notes some sites had the data pulled.

**Nippard's drug objection [13:12]:** if the subjects were enhanced, a 6x/week response is more plausible. Menno's answer is genuinely two-sided:
- No literature exists on how anabolic steroids interact with optimal frequency.
- **Argument for higher frequency on drugs:** increased work capacity.
- **Argument for lower:** drugs push your genetic ceiling further away, so an advanced natural lifter on drugs is functionally an *intermediate* again — which by Menno's own model would *reduce* optimal frequency.
- Historical support for the second reading: pre-steroid-era bodybuilders overwhelmingly ran **full-body programs at ~3x/week per muscle**, and the shift to once-weekly body-part training tracked the rise of drug use from Arnold's era onward. He cites a modern pro — captions render **"Big Remy", almost certainly Big Ramy — flagged** — who reportedly trains some body parts only once every two weeks to avoid growing too much.
- On Norway specifically: Menno believes this team is genuinely drug-free. Norway shifted to a radical anti-doping policy after PEDs were legal there until roughly 5–10 years prior, with lawsuits and middle-of-the-night door-knock testing. In his own client intake form he asks everyone about drug use; Norwegians answer "never have, never will" almost without exception.

## Does frequency help independently of volume? [18:23]
Menno: yes, definitely — and he thinks the literature **understates** the benefit.

The argument: every frequency study in advanced lifters **equates volume**, but in practice higher frequency *raises* volume for free. If incline press, decline press, flat bench and flyes all sit in one session, everything after the first exercise is done fatigued. Split across the week and you're **stronger on every one of them**, so the same number of sets moves more total load.

The failure mode he warns about [19:54]: people take their existing program, multiply the training days, keep the same per-session volume, and **massively overshoot weekly volume** — then get injured and conclude high frequency is inhuman. **Frequency and volume must not be scaled up in proportion.**

## Is there a minimum stimulus per session? [20:57]
Nippard's objection: a single set of flyes in isolation doesn't feel like enough — you're not warmed up, you don't feel stimulated. Splitting 5x5 squats into five single sets across five days must generate a different stimulus than doing all five at once.

Menno says he **used to hold exactly that position**, and was talked out of it by Børge Fagerli's client results. His current view:
- The minimum effective per-session stimulus is **very low**. Even in trained lifters, **one hard set at 30% of 1RM significantly raises muscle protein synthesis** — enough to open the adaptation window.
- Muscle tissue is highly **plastic** — far more adaptable than, say, bone. There is no high threshold to clear.
- On warming up: it's largely habituation. In multi-exercise sessions the earlier exercises *are* the warm-up (especially with pyramid schemes), so full-body training needs more deliberate warm-up. But after a few sets of Romanian deadlifts you're warm enough that **one set at 70% brings chest to near-max performance**.
- He cites a **Brazilian study** finding **no effect of warming up on all-out bench press performance at 70%** — injury risk aside, performance was unchanged, suggesting warming up is largely a feeling. **His own caveat**: that study was run in Brazil, where he arrives at the gym already soaking wet; training in Norway he needs 20 minutes in a sweater before he starts sweating. Climate matters.

## The hybrid Nippard proposes, and Menno accepts [25:06]
Nippard's structure: put the **bulk** of a muscle's volume on one day, taking advantage of the warm-up and per-session accumulation, then add a **minimal-threshold hit** on another day. His actual program: chest and shoulders one day; arms another day with **close-grip bench press** added, so chest and triceps get a second exposure.

**Menno agrees this can work well.** His analogy: blood-flow-restriction research (again attributing it to the Raastad-type group) uses low-damage, low-fatigue BFR sessions to *supplement* traditional training and raise frequency without raising fatigue — structurally the same idea as "one big volume bolus plus multiple low-volume pulses." He expects broadly the same result as evenly distributing the volume.

## How Menno actually programs high frequency [28:41]
**Intermediate template — a two-out-of-three-day rotation per muscle:**
- Day A: heavy, strength-oriented, lower reps (e.g. heavy bench press)
- Day B: lighter, higher reps (e.g. flyes)
- Day C: rest for that muscle — while it's working other muscles (e.g. heavy Romanian deadlifts for hamstrings and back on C, leg curls at higher volume on D)

Each muscle rotates through this across the week.

**Advanced template:** effectively **full body every day**, seven sessions a week.
- He tried simply rotating a fast A/B full-body split. It **worked for people with muscle memory and people on steroids, and blew out recovery and caused injuries for many others**.
- What he uses instead: **three or four completely different day templates**, each technically full-body, with different exercises and different rep targets. More variety, not more of the same.
- **1–2 exercises per body part per day**, no more — limited by session length and by how fast weekly volume accumulates.
- **Sets per muscle per day range from 1 to about 8**, and optimal volume varies dramatically between people. He notes **women generally tolerate more volume and higher frequency** (referring to his article "nine reasons women should not train like men").
- His highest-volume case: a client the captions render as **"Nina Ross" (spelling uncertain)** who earned an IFBB Pro card doing **8 sets per muscle group per day, split over two daily sessions, every day**. He is explicit that she is a best-case profile — good genetics, female, very low-stress life, training as her job, sleep and nutrition dialled in, sponsors preparing her meals.

## The novelty effect [33:19]
Nippard's objection: frequency-study subjects are almost universally bro-split trainees at baseline (he recalls a survey where the figure was near 100%), so the higher-frequency arm gets a *novel* stimulus and the control arm keeps doing what it always did.

**Menno partly concedes — but only for strength.** His decomposition: strength adaptation = hypertrophy + neural adaptation. Hypertrophy is roughly **linear** within a program block and diminishes slowly over a career. Neural adaptation is **exponentially diminishing** — logarithmic — rising fast and flattening fast, because the nervous system is even more plastic than muscle tissue. So novelty inflates *strength* readings sharply.

He then attacks two related ideas:
- **"Muscle confusion"** — which he calls "muscle confusion confusion." The premise is to change everything constantly to prevent adaptation. But **adaptation is the goal**; hypertrophy *is* the adaptation. The concept rests on a misreading of the adaptation process.
- **Block periodisation** — the serious version of the same idea. His read of the literature: block periodisation **does not outperform a continuous program**, and a recent study found **undulating periodisation superior to block periodisation for both strength and hypertrophy**. He allows genuine merit for **mixed athletes** balancing strength, power, endurance and mass, and says he isn't an expert there because he doesn't train mixed athletes.

He also accuses coaches of exploiting this: **change everything every four weeks and your clients are permanently novices at what they're doing**, so progress always looks great — while the year-over-year progress photos show nothing. He says he lost years as an intermediate to this himself. In his own practice, client progress is **quite linear over months and years**, and he sees no benefit from program changes made for their own sake.

One more Menno claim [39:01]: **what you trained like before barely matters.** He works with pro bodybuilding, functional, low-carb and CrossFit backgrounds alike; what determines optimal volume, frequency and intensity is **how advanced you are relative to your genetic capacity**, not your training history's style.

## Where they actually split: how to change a program [44:36]
**Nippard's position.** He weights psychology more heavily. He takes clients' boredom at face value and sends a new program. He himself can't stay on a routine longer than 3–4 months. He invokes the periodisation review by **John Kiely** *(captions: "John Kylie" — flagged)* that Greg Nuckols referenced, whose recurring theme is that changing the stimulus tends to produce new or stronger adaptations. So he'd **periodise frequency itself**: run high frequency for a block, drop back, then rebuild.

**Menno's position.** Programs *should* change — systematically as you advance (frequency up, volume up), based on measured progress, and unconditionally when something isn't working. What he rejects is **wholesale revamping every four weeks at an arbitrary interval**. His changes are small and targeted: if someone's chest is stagnating, he changes the chest work and leaves the legs alone. He notes people are often at different training ages in different body parts — someone who trained chest and biceps for two years longer than legs needs a more advanced bench program than squat program.

**The plateau question [47:39] — the sharpest disagreement.**
- **Nippard:** once you're training everything 5x/week and per-session volume can't rise further, **reduce frequency, raise per-session volume, then bring frequency back up** and harvest the frequency response again.
- **Menno:** *"I wouldn't use frequency as a means to break a plateau"* — at least not a reduced one. Frequency stays the same or goes up. He'd instead add **volume, exercise variety, a more complex periodisation model (e.g. triple-undulating), or different exercises entirely**. The only thing that would lower his prescribed frequency is a **lifestyle change that impairs recovery** — a more stressful job, for instance.
- Nippard names it explicitly as their fundamental disagreement and attributes it to Menno weighting the frequency data more heavily than he does.
- **Menno's escape hatch [50:13]:** by the time you have genuinely maxed out both frequency and volume — years of training, elite level — you are simply at your **genetic limitations**, and nothing you do gets you much more jacked; the remaining changes are nervous-system fine-tuning.

## The natty ceiling, measured [50:43]
Menno is not sure a hard "natty max" exists, versus gains slowing asymptotically. His evidence is a year of meticulous self-measurement: identical life circumstances and diet for two weeks at his best estimate of maintenance, two-week averaging, **weighing his gym clothing**, morning fasted weight and gym weight averaged, **seven-site caliper measurements**. Result: **one pound of muscle gained in a year**, at identical caliper readings. Structure of that year: roughly an **eight-month bulk followed by a four-month cut**. He was pleased — any measurable progress counts. He has trained **~14 years**.

Nippard's parallel: identical stage weight every year since competing at Canadian Nationals in 2012, with the look maturing but muscularity flat. Both agree this is not a reason for fatalism — a pound of muscle on stage is meaningful, and Menno says he'd be ecstatic to be five pounds heavier in five years.

## Nippard's three defences of bro splits, and Menno's replies

**1. Enjoyment [54:49].** Nippard: lifters, including elite ones, find blasting a body part more fun than one exercise per muscle.
Menno: **highly subjective**, and no literature exists on enjoyment across frequencies in strength athletes. His clients are usually sceptical of his programming at first and come to like it. From motivation theory he'd predict higher frequency might be *more* enjoyable — more variety per session, heavier weights on every lift, and **progress itself is the most motivating factor**. But he concedes it can legitimately be a reason to run a body-part split.
His coaching approach [56:51]: **never take a client's preference at face value** until they've given both a genuinely honest try — one to two weeks minimum for any lifestyle change (his analogy: people who "hate keto" after two days). If they've honestly tried both, he finds a middle ground — one all-out body-part day supplemented with blood-flow-restriction work through the week to sneak frequency back in.
**Both explicitly agree** against the claim that enjoyment shouldn't matter for elite athletes. Menno: *"I think that's bunk."* His example is a client the captions garble as **"Sikfar Garforce" (name uncertain)**, a Norwegian national powerlifting champion who cannot tolerate more than four weeks on anything — and who, Menno is confident, would not have become champion had he been forced onto the "optimal" program. Menno adds an aside that having powerlifters take a colon cleanse to make weight is one of his weight-cutting tactics: no performance detriment, several pounds easily lost.

**2. Better pumps [60:29].** Menno grants the pump is real but attacks the whole metabolic-stress case [62:03]. The pump story went: bodybuilder folklore (Arnold), then science debunking it as mere lactate/blood-flow accumulation, then **Brad Schoenfeld's metabolic-stress work**, which the bros took as vindication. Menno's point is that **every practical prediction metabolic stress made has failed**:
- *Optimal time under tension* for pumps — doesn't exist on most research.
- *Shorter rest intervals* → bigger pump → more growth — **not true**. He co-authored a review with Schoenfeld on this, and a newer study suggests **significant hypertrophy benefits to resting longer**, simply because you do more work.
- *8–12 reps (or the sets of 15 seen on programs like Simply Shredded)* as the growth range — **not true** volume-equated; heavy weights match or beat it.
- *Partial range of motion* keeps tension on and gives the sickest pumps — but **full ROM is superior in most cases**. The striking case is bench press, where the top has a large strength-resistance gap so the weight feels light. **Two studies** show strength gains roughly **60–70% greater with full ROM**, consistently — **even against a group doing two partial sets (one bottom-range, one top-range) for every one full-ROM set**, and even though the partial group's total training load was much higher because they could use far more weight on the top range.
- **Blood-flow restriction** is the one place metabolic stress earns its keep: when intensity is too low for mechanical tension to do the job, added metabolic stress can come close to maximising hypertrophy. But **no study he knows of shows BFR beating traditional high-intensity training** — so mechanical tension is primary and metabolic stress is a **potentially redundant add-on**.
Nippard's pushback: granting tension outranks stress doesn't mean stress contributes nothing; he'd keep it on the table as a small factor. Menno agrees on the psychological side and mentions **citrulline** as a supplement that plausibly increases pumps (and arginine/"NO" products, which he's unsure about) — worth taking purely because a client wants better pumps, with no expectation of performance or gains.

**3. Arm prioritisation [69:13].** Nippard: hitting arms fresh on their own day fixes a weak point; putting arms first in a push/pull/legs would compromise the subsequent back or chest work.
Menno: **nothing wrong with starting a workout with barbell curls** — exercise order should follow priority. If arms lag, open with close-grip bench, overhead extensions, barbell curls or Bayesian curls.
But he's wary of dedicated arm days [70:13] because men systematically overdo arms and chest. Tally the volume in almost any program with an arm day and **arms and chest dwarf hamstrings** — despite hamstring development being a high-scoring item in bodybuilding judging.
His evidence: **two studies adding arm isolation work on top of compound exercises — one in advanced trainees, one in untrained — both found no significant benefit for hypertrophy or strength.** He is careful not to over-read this: there was a **trend** favouring the arm training, and he would *not* say compounds are all you need. His framing is that curls are **icing on the cake** relative to getting genuinely strong at chin-ups.
His alternative explanation for why arm days seem to work: an arm day quietly **increases arm volume and frequency**, and it's the volume and frequency doing the work, not the "dedicated day" concept.
**He accepts Nippard's specific case** [73:49]: if the comparison is four days a week with arms folded in versus five days a week with an arm day, the arm day is a net positive. What he'd object to is Monday chest / Tuesday back / Wednesday legs / Thursday shoulders / Friday arms — structurally a different thing.

## The anabolic window and nutrient timing [74:20]
Menno's model, referring to his own presentation/article "Workout nutrition does matter: high frequency bodybuilding and more":

- The anabolic window **isn't** the one-or-two-hours-either-side folk version. It **starts as soon as fatigue accumulates in a muscle** and can last a long time.
- **Beginners: documented over 72 hours** — effectively all week for a truly untrained person. That is why untrained subjects show no benefit from frequency above 1x/week.
- **As you advance the window shortens** — you become resistant to muscle damage and recover faster. That is *why* frequency has to rise.
- The blunt corollary [76:23]: **"If you're an advanced trainee and it's your second rest day in a row, you are not gaining muscle mass that day."** For an elite trainee, net positive protein balance may only last a few hours after training — which is his explanation for why elite natural lifters gain so little.
- Consequence for nutrition: the shorter your window, the more the meals around training matter. He mocks the "IIFYM crowd" version where you squat heavy and grow all week.
- **Untrained lifters:** workout nutrition is nearly irrelevant, provided total protein is adequate and there are at least three whole meals a day. Shake in the locker room vs a meal at home — doesn't matter.
- **Advanced/elite:** it does matter, and becomes **a matter of hours** — he cites "a really nice study from Japan" in advanced trainees. His prescription is not to slam a shake immediately but to **sandwich the workout sufficiently with meals**. How wide that sandwich can be depends on the meal — mixed meals with fibre and fat digest slowly; a **whey shake** is fast, and with whey specifically the research suggests **more frequent protein pulses** help and **timing it tightly does matter more**. In practice **very few of his clients take a post-workout shake**; he just ensures the surrounding meals are adequate.

**Nippard's position:** a shake won't hurt, especially in an off-season with calories to spare, and it aids consistency. Menno's only reservation is that Nippard isn't a high-daily-protein advocate, so total intake could get unnecessarily high.

**The meta-point both land on [79:53]:** the researchers most famous for debunking the anabolic-window myth — **Alan Aragon, Brad Schoenfeld, Eric Helms** — reportedly all still take post-workout shakes themselves. Menno reads that as the gap between **questioning something in the literature** and **what you actually recommend**: you can doubt the benefit exists, but the benefit might exist and the effort is trivial, so cost-benefit says take it. He names the opposite stance **"scientific nihilism"** — refusing to act until three randomised peer-reviewed trials say so. Nippard notes Schoenfeld has said in interview that for a normal gym-goer it makes little difference, but an elite bodybuilder chasing every last morsel should get post-workout nutrition in within roughly half an hour.

## Points of disagreement
- **Breaking a plateau (the explicit one).** Nippard: cycle frequency down and per-session volume up, then back. Menno: frequency stays or goes up; never reduce it to break a plateau. Both name this as their fundamental split.
- **Program change and psychology.** Nippard takes client boredom at face value and swaps programs; Menno refuses to take preferences at face value until both options have had an honest trial, and changes only the specific thing that's stagnating.
- **Block periodisation.** Nippard uses it and finds it beneficial as a powerlifter/bodybuilder hybrid; Menno says its track record in the literature is poor and undulating periodisation beat it, while granting merit for mixed athletes.
- **Beginner frequency.** Menno directly contradicts Rippetoe's stress-recovery-adaptation rationale for high-frequency novice programs, calling the logic plausible but unsupported.
- **Metabolic stress.** Nippard wants it kept on the table as a small contributor; Menno regards essentially all of its practical predictions as falsified.
- **Where they agree, notably:** enjoyment matters even for elite athletes ("that's bunk" to the contrary); Nippard's one-big-day-plus-small-pulses hybrid is legitimate; a dedicated arm day is fine if it's genuinely adding a training day; post-workout shakes are worth taking on cost-benefit grounds even if the evidence is weak.

## Practical application
- Set frequency by **training age**: beginner ~1x/week per muscle is fine; intermediate **3+ sessions per muscle**; advanced can go daily with 1–2 exercises per muscle per session.
- When raising frequency, **do not scale volume up in proportion** — that is the standard way people injure themselves and conclude high frequency doesn't work.
- If you like body-part blasting, use the hybrid: one **high-volume day per muscle**, then **small pulses** (even one hard set, or a light/BFR session) on other days.
- Even **one hard set at 30% 1RM** is enough to raise protein synthesis and open the adaptation window — don't assume a minimum session needs to feel big.
- On full-body days, **warm up deliberately** — you no longer get a free warm-up from earlier exercises for the same muscle.
- Advanced-lifter template: **3–4 distinct full-body day templates** with different exercises and rep targets, not one A/B split rotated fast.
- Change programs **systematically and locally** — fix the stagnating body part, leave the rest — rather than revamping everything on a four-week timer.
- **Full range of motion**, longer rests, and heavier loads all beat their pump-maximising alternatives. Don't program for the pump.
- If arms lag, put arm work **first in a session**; a dedicated arm day is worth it mainly because it adds volume and frequency, so audit whether hamstrings are getting comparable attention.
- Nutrient timing: **sandwich the session with meals**. Only elite lifters need it tight. A post-workout shake is a low-effort bet worth taking.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The **Norwegian Frequency Project was unpublished** at recording; Menno knows it through personal contacts and having seen the programs. Its hypertrophy result was **not statistically significant (p ≈ .06)**, and the strength data is on elite powerlifters, not bodybuilders.
- **No literature exists on steroids × optimal frequency**, and Menno argues both directions himself.
- **No literature on enjoyment across training frequencies** in strength athletes — his motivational argument is inference.
- The twice-daily claim rests on roughly **one, possibly two studies**, in advanced female athletes.
- The arm-isolation null result comes from **two studies** with a non-significant trend *favouring* the added arm work — a weak null, and Menno says so.
- The Brazilian warm-up finding is **one study**, and Menno immediately flags the climate confound.
- The **novelty confound** is conceded for strength; Menno's separation of neural from morphological adaptation is a model, not a measurement.
- Menno's one-pound-per-year figure is **n=1 self-measurement by calipers**, however carefully controlled.
- His high-frequency prescriptions come largely from **coaching observation** (his own and Børge Fagerli's) ahead of controlled trials, and he is candid that the elite examples had exceptional recovery circumstances.
- **Name flags from auto-captions:** the Norwegian recovery researcher rendered "to Rastat"/"Tulsat" is most likely **Truls Raastad**; "Berg/Burger Fagerly" is **Børge Fagerli**; "John Kylie" is **John Kiely**; "Greg/rack Knuckles" is **Greg Nuckols**; "Brad Showfeld/Sheld/Shenfeld" is **Brad Schoenfeld**; "Big Remy" is likely **Big Ramy**; "Eric Elms/Helen's" is **Eric Helms**; "Ripto" is **Rippetoe**; the military-study author rendered **"HKA"** and the clients **"Nina Ross"** and **"Sikfar Garforce"** could not be resolved and are left as-is.
