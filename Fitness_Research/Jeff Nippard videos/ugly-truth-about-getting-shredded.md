# The Real Cost of Staying Shredded, and Why to Aim for the Bottom of Your Range

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Ugly Truth About Getting Shredded (Science Explained)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=larBqTjA7-g)) · 2022-08-24 · 10 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Contest-level leanness looks impressive for about one hour a day under good lighting; the other 23 hours you look small and depleted.
- The "natty triangle": you can be **shredded and natural, big and natural, or big and shredded — pick two.** Only genetic outliers get all three.
- Body dysmorphia gets worse, not better, past "beach lean" — most people stop being able to see their own physique objectively.
- Getting extremely lean tanks hormones, mood and sleep. In a 2013 case study of a natural bodybuilder, testosterone fell to about a quarter of baseline and mood disturbance rose 617%.
- **Target the lower boundary of your own body-fat range and maintain there** rather than pushing below it. Everyone's range is different.

## The three harsh realities

**1. You look best for a fraction of the day.** [00:32] In the posing footage from his pro-card win at the California Natural Muscle Mayhem, Nippard was **150 lb (68 kg) at 5'5"**. He looks more jacked in those photos than usual, and much less jacked than usual walking around. Chasing shredded means trading everyday size for a photo condition.

**2. You may still not be happy with how you look.** Confidence and body image improve as people get leaner — but only up to a point. Once past beach lean and into shredded territory, a switch tends to flip: people fixate on every remaining bit of fat and lose objectivity. It is common to only appreciate how lean you were months later, looking back at photos.

**3. The health cost is real, even natural.** [02:03] A **2013 case study** followed a natural male bodybuilder through **26 weeks** of dieting. He lost **30 lb**, going from **14.8% to 4.5% body fat by DEXA**, and stepped on stage with visible glute striations and no low-back fat. The physiological damage:
- **Testosterone fell from 922 ng/dL to 227 ng/dL** — from about as high as naturals get down to a clinically low level, comparable to a man in his 80s or 90s.
- **Ghrelin up, leptin down** — constant, punishing hunger.
- **Cortisol doubled.**
- **Total mood disturbance rose 617%.**
- Severe sleep disruption is another very common effect — the body actively resists sleep because it wants you to eat first. Nippard calls this one of the most brutal parts.

**Women face the same effects plus menstrual disruption**, with downstream consequences including infertility and bone mineral loss. A case study of a natural figure competitor found she **still had not regained her period 1.5 years after the show**. Recovery varies: another study found **72% of bikini and figure competitors combined had their period back within 3-4 months.** Some of that variability likely reflects the different leanness required for bikini versus figure, and whether the competitor was willing to regain healthy body weight.

**Odd side effects.** Competitors are commonly told they smell of ammonia or nail polish — energy balance is so negative that the body burns protein for fuel, producing ammonia excreted in sweat and urine. Food behaviour gets strange too: watching Food Network compulsively, hoarding junk food, and finding genuinely unappetising things appealing.

## Why some influencers seem fine
Three plausible explanations, none of which means it's easy:
- They are suffering as much as everyone else, but looking lean is worth it for their business or social media.
- They use performance-enhancing drugs, which make low body fat somewhat easier to hold — though **most of the side effects still apply to enhanced lifters, apart from the testosterone crash.**
- They genuinely have a lower body-fat set point. Nippard guesses someone like David Laid can hold 6-8% without much suffering. This is not common.

## Set point vs the dual intervention point model
[05:37] Set point theory — the idea that the body defends one specific body-fat level, pulling you back up with hunger and lethargy when too low and back down via appetite suppression and raised metabolism when too high — dates to the 1950s and has been updated.

The prevailing modern version is the **dual intervention point model**: instead of one set point there are **two boundaries defining a range** the body is comfortable within. Nippard's example: below **8-9% body fat** his body punishes him with skyrocketing hunger, a tanked metabolism and the side effects above; above roughly **25%** it fights back with reduced appetite, raised metabolism and excessive sweating. But he can sit at the **lower end of the range without much resistance.**

The range differs hugely between people — one person might sit fine at 6%, another might feel like death below 12%.

**Staying below your range also costs progress.** People who hold a shredded look year-round struggle to gain size or strength, and the longer they stay under the range the more likely they are to binge, rebound, and end up **less lean than if they had been more conservative in the first place.**

## Practical application
- Find the lower boundary of your own range and treat that as the target physique — lean enough to look good, sustainable enough to keep training and progressing.
- Dip below it only for a specific reason such as a competition, and **get back out of it promptly** to limit the side effects.
- Expect hunger, cravings and even some strength loss even inside your range — that is normal and does not mean you're too lean.
- The signal to re-evaluate: when fat loss goes from **challenging to all-consuming**. You will know.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Body-fat ranges are individual, so no universal percentage target is given — the boundaries quoted (8-9% / 25%) are Nippard's own.
- The dramatic hormonal figures come from single-subject case studies, not controlled trials; the period-recovery data show wide variation between individuals.
- Set point / dual intervention point is a model, not a settled mechanism.
