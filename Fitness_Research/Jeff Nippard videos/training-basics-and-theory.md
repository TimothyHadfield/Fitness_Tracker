# Training Fundamentals: The Ladder of Training Theory

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Training Basics & Theory | Chapter 1: The Fundamentals Series" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TN9i9Ni0Xr4)) · 2018-01-21 · 22 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Priorities stack in order: sustainability (built on safety + enjoyment) → effort → progressive overload + prioritisation → the acute training variables (volume, intensity, frequency, rest, tempo, splits).
- Don't leave more than 3-4 reps in the tank on most working sets. Failure is not required, and should be rare.
- Progressive overload is the single biggest driver of hypertrophy — add weight or reps over time, or overload rest/tempo instead.
- Sleep 7-8 hours a night; 5-6 hours measurably hurts performance and body composition.
- Chase a *practical* routine you'll stick to, not the theoretically *optimal* one.

## The ladder model
[01:31] Rungs from bottom up: **sustainability** (with safety and enjoyment as the two side rails) → **effort** → **POP** (progressive overload + prioritisation) → **acute training variables**. The variables at the top only pay off if the rungs below them are in place.

## Safety
[02:32] Three components:

1. **Good form.** Control the eccentric — roughly a 2-second negative, or at minimum lowering under your own control rather than gravity's. Use full range of motion; partials have a place but not for beginners.
2. **Appropriate load.** Not too heavy, not too light. Use the **2-for-2 rule**: if for two consecutive weeks you can get two extra reps on your last set of an exercise, add weight. Example: 3×10 curls with 20 lb, and you hit 12 reps two weeks running — increase the load.
3. **Adequate recovery.** Beginners: one day on, one day off (e.g. upper / rest / lower / rest, repeating). This lets joints, ligaments and soft tissue recover, not just muscle. People with an athletic background can tolerate more frequency. Listen to unusual soreness or aches; if pain feels injurious, take the extra rest day or do light cardio and think long-term.

**Sleep:** the literature strongly favours 7-8 hours over 5-6 for both performance and body composition. Some evidence that 9-10 hours further improves athletic performance (notably in sprinters), though whether that translates to strength, hypertrophy or body composition is unresolved. More sleep is a cheap way to speed progress.

Nutrition is the fourth recovery lever (covered in later chapters).

## Enjoyment
[07:10] People who enjoy training adhere better and push harder. If you genuinely dislike lifting, design a routine that "sucks less":
- Minimise exercise count by leaning on compounds (squat, bench, press, deadlift) that cover a lot of muscle per movement.
- Consider full-body training so you're only in the gym 2-3× a week rather than 4-5.

**Variety vs consistency:** some people need frequent change to stay engaged; others thrive on a fixed plan they can track. Most do best with a blend — a stable core of main lifts you track week to week, plus flexible accessories you rotate.

**"Bro" methods** — drop sets, supersets, forced reps — get criticised because they can obscure progression, but they have some empirical support and make training more fun. Fine as seasoning, not as the centre of the program.

## Effort
[11:45] Most trainees simply don't push hard enough. He cites research (discussed in a video with Stephanie) finding roughly 20% of trainees were leaving 10 or more reps in the tank on bench press — nowhere near enough exertion for meaningful hypertrophy.

**Recommendation: leave no more than 3-4 reps in the tank on most sets.** (If you can grind out a 15th rep but stop at 12, that's 3 in the tank.)

**When to train to failure:**
- Save it for the *last set of the last exercise* for a body part.
- Reserve it for isolation / single-joint work — curls, triceps extensions, lateral raises — and especially machines, where failing is low-risk.
- Do not take heavy compounds (squat, bench, overhead press, deadlift) to true failure; if you're going near failure on them, use a spotter.
- Failure training is optional — an intermediate technique. Staying within ~3 reps of failure is enough.

No program is so well designed that it substitutes for hard work.

## POP: progressive overload and prioritisation
[16:19] **Progressive overload** = doing more over time. The usual forms:
- More weight (100 lb × 10 → 105 lb × 10)
- More reps (100 lb × 10 → 100 lb × 11)

Alternative overload levers:
- **Efficiency** — same weight and reps with shorter rest (e.g. 2 min → 90 s), making the session harder and shorter.
- **Exertion** — slow the tempo, e.g. a 1-second negative becoming 1.5-2 seconds. Hard to measure precisely, and you don't need to.

Without overload you won't optimise growth — this is the core flaw in "muscle confusion", where constant change destroys any trackable progression.

**Prioritisation** ties to specificity: keep the goal the goal. If you want a bigger bench, program for it. Practical tactics:
- Train the priority muscle **first in the session** — research shows you're stronger earlier in a workout.
- Train it **earlier in the week**, after a rest day, and spaced away from a heavy leg day so you arrive fresh.

## Practical application
1. Pick a split you can sustain (upper/lower with rest days between is his beginner suggestion).
2. Control the eccentric ~2 s, full ROM, apply the 2-for-2 rule to add load.
3. Take working sets to within 3-4 reps of failure; save true failure for the final isolation set of a body part.
4. Log your main lifts and add weight or reps every week.
5. Put your weak point first in the workout and early in the week.
6. Sleep 7-8+ hours; take an extra rest day when pain looks injurious.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Recovery needs, tolerable frequency and preference for variety are highly individual.
- Whether 9-10 hours of sleep improves body composition, strength or hypertrophy (as opposed to sprint performance) "remains to be seen".
- The threshold for "close enough to failure" isn't clearly defined in the literature — ~3 reps in reserve is his estimate.
- Optimal ≠ practical: he repeatedly favours the adherable program over the theoretically best one.
