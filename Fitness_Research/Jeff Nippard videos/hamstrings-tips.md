# Targeting Hamstring Regions: Knee Flexion, Hip Extension and Foot Position

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Science-Based Hamstrings Tips | Weird Hamstring & Shoulder Workout" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kXnRqoW6Alw)) · 2016-10-19 · 6 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Program **both** a knee-flexion movement (leg curl) and a hip-extension movement (RDL / stiff-leg deadlift) — they hit the hamstrings differently.
- Lying leg curls produce **greater distal (near-knee) hamstring activation** than the stiff-leg deadlift; there was no significant difference at the proximal (upper) end.
- Inner vs outer hamstring bias is **not** reliably driven by exercise choice, but it *is* driven by **foot rotation**: point your toes in the direction of the fibres you want to hit.
- "You can't target regions of a muscle" is outdated — muscles do contract along their full length, but EMG evidence shows regions can be activated to greater or lesser degrees.
- To specialise a muscle group: train it **first in the session** (priority principle) and **add extra volume on other days** to raise frequency.

## Specialisation approach [02:02]
He was specialising hamstrings by two means:
1. **Hitting hamstrings first in the session** — research shows exercises performed earlier in a workout are performed better.
2. **Increasing frequency** by tacking hamstring volume onto unrelated days (e.g. a shoulder-focused push day).

## Anatomy [02:33]
Four muscles: **biceps femoris long head, biceps femoris short head, semitendinosus, semimembranosus.** The group can be split **medial** (nearer the midline) vs **lateral** (further from it), and **proximal** (upper, hip end) vs **distal** (lower, knee end).

Note that the **short head of biceps femoris does not cross the hip joint**, so it contributes nothing to hip extension — it is knee-flexion only.

## Can you bias regions? The EMG evidence [03:35]
- **Schoenfeld, Contreras et al. 2015:** the **lying leg curl produced greater distal hamstring activation than the stiff-leg deadlift.** This makes mechanical sense — knee flexion happens only in the leg curl, and the short head of biceps femoris is knee-flexion only.
- Surprisingly, the **same study found no significant difference in proximal (upper) hamstring activation** between the leg curl and the stiff-leg deadlift, contrary to the intuition that a hip-extension movement should preferentially load the hip end. He flags that this intuition had no empirical basis.
- **Medial vs lateral:** that same paper found **no difference** between leg curl and stiff-leg deadlift. A **2013 paper (author name garbled in the captions — sounds like "Zebis et al.", treat as uncertain)** found the **RDL** activated more **inner (medial)** hamstrings while the **lying leg curl** hit more **outer (lateral)**. Other studies conflict. Overall the data does not support a clear inner/outer split between these two exercises.
- **Foot position does work.** **Lynn and Costigan 2009** found that **internally rotating the feet (toes in) increased medial hamstring activation**, while **externally rotating (toes out) increased lateral hamstring activation.**

## Practical application
- **Base programming:** include at least one **knee-flexion** movement (lying/seated leg curl) and one **hip-extension** movement (Romanian or stiff-leg deadlift). This is described as the smartest way to program hamstrings.
- **For distal / lower hamstring emphasis:** favour the lying leg curl.
- **For inner (medial) hamstrings:** point the toes **in**.
- **For outer (lateral) hamstrings:** point the toes **out**.
- Simple rule: **point your toes in the direction of the fibres you want to activate.** This applies to lying leg curls, single-leg glute bridges and single-leg deadlifts.
- **Specialising a lagging muscle:** move it to the front of the session, and add extra volume on non-specialised training days to raise weekly frequency.

## Block periodisation aside [00:30]
From a guest lecture clip: one way to organise training is **block periodisation** — blocks of roughly **4–6 weeks**, each targeting a specific adaptation, exploiting the **training residual effect**. An adaptation you have already built can typically be **maintained on 50% to two-thirds of the work it took to build it**, freeing volume to be spent elsewhere.

## Caveats & unknowns
- All of the regional findings are **EMG activation data**, not hypertrophy outcomes — greater activation is a proxy, not proof of greater regional growth.
- The medial/lateral exercise comparisons are **directly contradictory across studies**; only the foot-position finding is presented as reasonably consistent.
- The 2013 medial/lateral paper's author name is garbled in the auto-captions and should be verified before citing.
