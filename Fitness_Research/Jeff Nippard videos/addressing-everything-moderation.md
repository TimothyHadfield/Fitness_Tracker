# Why "Everything in Moderation" Still Smuggles In a Good Food / Bad Food Split

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Addressing: 'EVERYTHING in Moderation'" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WsdRTbdPk-k)) · 2014-09-19 · 4 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- "Everything's fine in moderation" sits very close to the **appeal to moderation** (false compromise) — the fallacy that the right answer always lies between two extremes.
- Some things are harmful at any dose, and some are fine at essentially any realistic dose, so "moderation" is not a general-purpose answer to whether a food belongs in a diet.
- Saying food X is fine but food Y is fine *only in moderation* still constructs a good-food/bad-food dichotomy — it just does it more politely.
- The better framing is **inclusive** dietary prescription: hit calories, macros, produce, dairy and fibre targets, then fill the remainder with foods you like.
- The argument is against a framing, not against restraint — Nippard still thinks some things (alcohol, for example) genuinely are best used in moderation.

## The reasoning error [00:44]
The appeal to moderation, or false compromise, holds that the truth must lie between two stated positions. The Wikipedia illustration Nippard borrows: one party says hydrogen cyanide is a delicious and necessary part of the human diet, another says it is toxic and dangerous. Splitting the difference — "a little cyanide is fine" — is obviously wrong. Cyanide is bad in any amount.

The mirror case matters just as much: some foods are simply fine, in moderation or otherwise. "In moderation" only does real work when a substance actually has a dose-dependent harm curve. Applied indiscriminately to food, it doesn't answer the question; it just sounds balanced.

## Why the phrasing still creates a dichotomy [01:47]
The everyday use is: "sure, you can have some high-glycemic carbs on a diet, as long as it's in moderation," or "you can treat yourself to ice cream once in a while." Notice the structure. Food X needs no qualifier. Food Y gets one. That asymmetry is a moral ranking of foods dressed up as pragmatism — the "treat yourself" wording concedes that Y is bad and is being permitted as an exception.

**Exclusive framing:** this food is not good, but if you must have it, keep it small.
**Inclusive framing:** here is what the diet needs to contain; whatever calories and macros are left over are yours.

Nippard prefers the second because it defines the diet by what goes in rather than by what is being tolerated.

## The inclusive template he offers [01:47]
- Hit your daily calorie and macronutrient targets.
- Three to four servings of vegetables per day.
- Two to three servings of fruit per day.
- One or two servings of dairy.
- Hit your fibre goal.
- Fill in the remainder with foods you enjoy.

## Owning the tension in his own earlier advice [02:17]
He had previously suggested roughly 20% of the diet could come from junk food, which looks inconsistent with the argument here. He concedes the number was **arbitrary** — drawn from personal experience and the psychological effects he noticed when eating a lot of highly palatable food, not from data. He also flags the definitional hole underneath any such rule: there is no clear answer to what counts as "junk food," which is precisely the problem with prescriptions built on food categories.

## What he is not claiming [03:18]
He is not saying moderation talk is always wrong. Many things really are best used in moderation — alcohol is his example. The narrow claim is that dietary prescriptions should be built on inclusive statements rather than exclusive ones, and that this particular phrase, common and well-meant as it is, quietly reintroduces the good/bad food split it appears to dissolve.

## Practical application
- Write your diet as a list of things to include and targets to hit, not a list of foods to limit.
- If a food-rule you use relies on the category "junk food," notice that the category has no clear boundary and your rule inherits that vagueness.
- Discretionary calories are what's left after the targets are met — a structural consequence, not a moral allowance.
- Flag your own arbitrary numbers as arbitrary. A percentage from personal experience is a heuristic, not evidence.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The 20% junk-food figure is explicitly self-reported and arbitrary, with no supporting data.
- "Junk food" is undefined, by his own admission, which limits how precisely any such guideline can be applied.
- This is a claim about how to frame advice, not an empirical finding about outcomes; no evidence is presented that inclusive framing produces better adherence.
