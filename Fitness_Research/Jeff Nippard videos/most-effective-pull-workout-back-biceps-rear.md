# The Science-Based Pull Workout: Back, Biceps and Rear Delts

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Effective Science-Based PULL Workout: Back, Biceps & Rear Delts (Science Applied Ep. 2)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f2JDJV0AnyY)) · 2018-03-17 · 14 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- A full pull day built for a push/pull/legs split: lat pre-activation, one heavy vertical pull, one heavy row, a multi-grip pulldown, a rear-delt tri-set, direct biceps, and a heavy rack pull.
- Effort target across the heavy work is RPE 8-9 (1-2 reps in reserve), with the rack pull kept to 2-3 reps in reserve.
- Grip variety matters more than any single "best" grip — individual variability in lat recruitment is large, so cycle wide → medium → reverse within the pulldown.
- Sequence grips so the lats work fresh under the wide grip and the biceps absorb more of the load as fatigue accumulates.
- If you include below-the-knee rack pulls, restructure the split to pull → push → legs → rest so the lower back gets a recovery day before squatting or deadlifting.

## The routine

**1. One-arm cable pullover (straight-arm pulldown) — 2 × 15-20, light, well short of failure**
Lat pre-activation. Not a term from the literature, but built on sound principles: **Fisher and colleagues (2016)** found glute pre-activation drills carried activation over to heavier compounds like the hip thrust, and the same neural logic should apply to lats — a muscle many lifters struggle to feel. A study published the month of filming found that using an **internal focus** (consciously thinking about the working muscle) produced greater biceps thickness across an 8-week programme. At minimum it warms up the tissue, raises blood flow and improves the pump.

Execution: treat it as the opposite of a lateral raise. Instead of shoulder abduction, perform pure **shoulder adduction** — drive the elbow down and in toward your side, in the mid-frontal plane (the plane that splits your body evenly front and back). You can crunch laterally toward the pulling side for a stronger contraction, since the lats weakly contribute to lateral spinal flexion. Light weight, mindful contractions, no failure.

**2. Wide-grip pull-ups — 3 × 6-8, RPE 8-9**
**Doma and colleagues (2013)** found no significant difference in latissimus dorsi activation between the lat pulldown and the pull-up, but the pull-up won for **biceps and erector spinae** — better value on a day targeting all the pulling muscles. Pull-ups also travel: pulldown machines are loaded differently gym to gym, whereas a bar is a bar, which makes long-term overload easier to track.

Load with a dip belt if bodyweight doesn't get you close enough to failure in 6-8 reps; use the assisted machine if you're newer or heavier. Optionally take the final set to failure.

Execution — range of motion and tempo:
- Descend until the elbows are nearly fully extended, but **don't relax into a dead hang** — keep some tension at the bottom without shortchanging the range.
- Pull as high as you comfortably can: at minimum eyes clearing the bar, aiming to bring the chest toward the bar even if it doesn't touch.
- Tempo: roughly **1 second up, 1 second down**, control being the criterion. In a follow-up article to his 2015 systematic review and meta-analysis, **Dr Brad Schoenfeld** wrote that a wide range of tempos can maximise hypertrophy, but concentrics shouldn't exceed 3 seconds (slower may be detrimental) and eccentrics must be controlled against gravity rather than dropped, since dropping fails to provide sufficient tension. For heavy compounds, prioritise progressive load at a normal tempo.
- Between sets: 5-6 second lat stretches. Holds that short won't hinder subsequent performance and improve the pump.

**3. Meadows row — 3 × 10-12**
The main heavy row. With the arm out to the side, the plane of motion lines up directly with the **mid-trap fibre orientation**, maximising mid-trap and rhomboid recruitment through scapular retraction, while also hitting the **rear delts** via transverse shoulder adduction.

No research exists on the Meadows row specifically, but **Lehmann and colleagues** found seated cable rows produced greater lat activation than lat pulldowns, plus more trap activation — the caveat being that seated rows use a neutral grip and shoulder extension while the Meadows uses a pronated grip and horizontal shoulder adduction. The general point holds: rows hit essentially the whole back musculature including the lats.

Setup: load with **25 lb plates** for extra range of motion at the bottom. Stagger the stance and get fairly bent over with a flat back so you row directly out to the side — best mid-trap feel. To bias the lats instead, stand more side-on to the bar and row backward, closer to a dumbbell row done with a barbell.

**4. Omni-grip lat pulldown — 3 × 12-15, one set per grip**
Set 1 wide, set 2 medium, set 3 reverse (underhand). The literature shows no strong trend favouring wider or narrower grips for lat recruitment, but **large individual variability** — so using a variety is the practical answer. **Lusk et al. (2010)** (surname uncertain — captioned as "lusca Tao") found a supinated grip was worse for lat recruitment, plausibly because the biceps take over and leave less for the lats.

That's exactly why the **sequence** matters: start wide while the lats are freshest, move to a closer grip so the biceps contribute more as lat fatigue builds, then finish reverse-grip with the biceps most involved. This lets you keep the same load across all three sets while hitting the lats from slightly different angles.

**5. Rear delt tri-set — rope face pull → reverse pec deck → band pull-apart, minimal rest**
Packs rear-delt volume into a short window.
- **Rope face pull:** the multi-joint movement of the three — treat it as a **high row** and go fairly heavy. Brace a foot against the upright so balance isn't a limiter. Target everything back there: rear delts, traps, rhomboids. Squeeze the shoulder blades hard every rep.
- **Reverse pec deck:** lighter, isolation-focused. Drive the arms out and back **using the rear delts** rather than squeezing the shoulder blades — minimise trap involvement here. Experiment with pronated (palms down) and neutral grips: a **2013 study** found the neutral grip produced higher average EMG activation, though some subjects did better pronated (Nippard is in that camp himself).
- **Band pull-aparts:** burn out the rear delts, generate local metabolic stress, and provide a different tension profile — the pec deck gives near-constant resistance while the band peaks at the top of the range. Externally rotate slightly, since the rear delts assist external rotation.

**6. EZ-bar biceps curl — 3 × 6-8**
Given the pulling volume already accumulated, direct biceps work isn't strictly required for growth, but it optimises progress: the biceps only assist in the other movements and never reach fatigue, so hit them heavy at the end. EZ bar is easier on the wrists than a straight bar. Grip width: little activation difference between medium and wide, but a **very close grip may reduce biceps involvement and strain the wrists** — avoid it. Since the long (outer) head crosses the shoulder and contributes to shoulder abduction, a **wider grip** may involve it more. Moderate cheating on the final few reps is fine as long as eccentric control is maintained.

**Optional: incline dumbbell curl — 2 × 15-20** if arms are a priority. The point is high-rep fatigue plus a greater biceps stretch from the arm being behind the torso. Keep elbows back, upper back against the bench, both arms simultaneously to save time.

**7. Below-the-knee rack pull — 3 × 6-8, 2-3 reps in reserve**
Provides a heavy isometric stretch on the upper traps and loads the spinal erectors up the whole spinal column while keeping the spine extended and stabilised. The above-the-knee version spares the lower back more and allows heavier loading of the upper traps isometrically; Nippard chose below-the-knee to target more of the total back musculature and for better carryover to floor deadlifts (which he classes as a lower-body exercise, not a back exercise, and so excludes here).

Straps and a belt are both endorsed — they aid safety and let you move more weight for a greater tension stimulus. Contrary to popular belief, a belt doesn't meaningfully impede core musculature recruitment.

Technique: pull the tension out of the bar before lifting rather than walking up and yanking it off the rack. Keep the bar over mid-foot through the full range, chest and hips rising simultaneously, and simply lift the chest to lock out.

## Practical application
- Slot this into push/pull/legs. **If you include rack pulls, run pull → push → legs → rest** so the lower back has a recovery day before heavy squatting or deadlifting.
- Session order: pre-activation → heaviest compound (pull-up) → heavy row → pulldown variety → rear-delt tri-set → biceps → rack pull.
- Total: roughly 17-19 working sets plus the light pre-activation pair.
- Keep the heavy compounds at RPE 8-9, only the last pull-up set optionally to failure, and rack pulls always short of failure.

## Caveats & unknowns
- "Pre-activation" isn't an established term in the literature; the supporting evidence comes from glute research and is extrapolated to the lats.
- No research exists on the Meadows row itself — the rationale is biomechanical inference plus adjacent row studies.
- Grip-width effects on lat recruitment show large individual variability, so any single recommendation is weak.
- Two cited surnames (the 2010 pulldown grip study; the seated-row EMG study) are garbled or imprecise in the auto-captions and should be verified.
