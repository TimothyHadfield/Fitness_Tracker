# Nutrition

Everything in the library that concerns food, distilled to what you'd actually act on. Part one covers
**energy balance and body composition** — bulking, cutting, recomposition, metabolic adaptation,
reverse dieting, cardio, adherence and contest prep. Part two covers **macros, food quality, timing and
specific foods**. Organised by decision, not by video; each claim group links to the note with the full
detail.

The videos span roughly a decade, **2014 to 2025**, and his position moved — most visibly on **reverse
dieting** (whose logic he interrogated in 2016 and now tells people to skip entirely) and on **how lean
is worth staying** (from "get under 10%" to "find your range and sit at the lean edge of it"). Where old
and new conflict the newer view is his current one, and the
[shifts section](#where-his-position-moved-in-this-half) at the end lays them out rather than papering
over them.

## Part one — energy balance and body composition

### Energy balance is the frame

Body weight is decided by energy in versus energy out. The refinement that matters: the equation governs
**net energy storage, not tissue**. Nothing flips at maintenance and starts depositing fat; where the
weight goes depends on training, and because muscle is far less energy-dense than fat, calorie
arithmetic doesn't map cleanly onto body composition — which is what makes recomposition possible
([bulking vs gaintaining](bulking-cutting-vs-gaintaining-which-best.md)). Energy out has four parts and
only one is the gym: **RMR**, **TEF**, **EAT**, and **NEAT** — everything else you move, which does most
of the adapting and most of the varying between people
([get lean & stay lean](get-lean-stay-lean-forever.md)).

**Weight gain is not fat gain.** Three pounds of *fat* in a week needs an excess of about **10,800 kcal**
(3,600/lb × 3) *and* that none went to glycogen or was burned. Run that arithmetic before reacting: if
you didn't cheat it's water, sodium, cycle phase, stress or gut content. Judge the trend, not the point —
peaks-and-valleys is the typical male pattern while female dieters more often show a **staircase**, which
makes monthly comparisons more useful than weekly. A multi-week stall is a different problem from a
fluctuation ([weight gain ≠ fat gain](weight-gain-not-equal-fat-gain.md)).

**One enormous day does very little.** **10,000 kcal on a 3,000 kcal maintenance** yields an estimated
**0.5–0.6 lb of actual fat**, not the 5–8 lb the scale shows next morning: about **19%** goes to the
thermic effect of food, a conservative **+200 kcal of NEAT per extra 1,000 eaten** takes more, and
roughly half the remainder partitions to fat. Horton 1995 cross-checks it — a one-day pure-carb surplus
stored ~**10%** as fat versus ~**60%** for pure fat ([10,000 calorie challenge](10-000-calorie-challenge.md)).

**Between-person variation is the underrated part.** At **80 kg**, total daily expenditure across 6,000+
subjects ranged **~1,400 to ~5,700 kcal/day** ([metabolism](metabolism-and-energy-expenditure.md)).
Bouchard fed 24 subjects **+1,000 kcal/day for 100 days** under supervision and saw gains of **~10 to
~30 lb**; Levine found NEAT responses to the same surplus from **−98 to +692 kcal/day**. Two people can
sit **~400 kcal/day** apart at rest before either moves. None of that contradicts energy balance; it
explains why avoiding a surplus is much harder for some people, and why willpower-shaming is useless
([is being fat a choice](being-fat-choice.md)).

### Bulking

**Rate of gain determines fat gain** — the number to hold is **1–2% of bodyweight per month** as a
beginner, **0.5–1%** as an intermediate or advanced lifter ([how to bulk](how-to-bulk.md); an earlier
video gives the wider **0.5–1.5%/month**, [eat big to get big](eat-big-get-big.md)). At 170 lb with two
years of training that's **1–2 lb/month**, so a year yields **12–24 lb** — close to all-lean near
beginner status, roughly **half lean, half fat** near your ceiling, which he treats as the acceptable
price of not spinning wheels.

**A 5–10% surplus is enough**: his own maintenance is **2,800 kcal**, bulking at **~3,000**, adjusting on
observed rate; ballpark maintenance is **bodyweight in lb × 14–18**. Dirty bulks run **50–100% above
maintenance** and, without drugs, just fill fat stores ([how to bulk](how-to-bulk.md)). **Garthe 2013**
gave elite athletes an extra **~600 kcal/day** and found **no significantly greater muscle** but **over
three times the fat** ([bear mode](going-bear-mode-worth.md), [eat big to get big](eat-big-get-big.md),
[body recomposition](body-recomposition.md)); the counterweight, **Rozenek 2002**, favoured a **4,300
kcal** group over a **2,500 kcal** one for fat-free mass with no fat gain at all, which he attributes to
novice subjects. The framing that survives both: **nutrition is permissive to training, not causative.**

**Three ways to run a gaining phase**, each defensible for a different goal:

- **Lean bulk** — 5–10% surplus at the rates above. The default.
- **Gaintaining** — maintenance-ish calories, aggressive overload, roughly **1 lb/month**, lean
  year-round. He credits Lane Norton and Alberto Nuñez and calls it perfectly reasonable
  ([bulking vs gaintaining](bulking-cutting-vs-gaintaining-which-best.md)).
- **Bear mode** — a deliberate heavier look capped at **15–20% body fat**, neck and traps trained
  specifically, optimising for how you look *in clothes*. He ran it ~8 months, **160 → 183 lb at 5'5"**,
  gaining strength and losing hunger — but looked *smaller* shirtless and lost enough work capacity to
  hurt high-volume leg days ([bear mode](going-bear-mode-worth.md)).

**Nobody has run the study** comparing 12 months of gaintaining against 6 months bulk plus 6 cut; he'd bet
on bulk/cut but says outright it isn't clear. And don't slack in the gym — eating more while training
less guarantees fat over muscle. Intermediates should hold **~8–15 sets per muscle per week** and keep
**2–3 moderate cardio sessions/week**, since poor conditioning caps the volume you can complete
([how to bulk](how-to-bulk.md)).

### Cutting

**Lose 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week**, from a **15–25%** deficit. That figure is stable across every note
touching it ([starvation mode](starvation-mode-real-thing.md), [how to get lean](how-to-get-lean.md),
[get abs in 60 days](get-abs-60-days.md)). Trexler's version: **~1%/week** average, tapering to
**~0.5%/week** late, slightly above 1% early if you start high in body fat
([Trexler](we-get-slow-metabolisms-reverse-diet-science.md)). His 2023 position goes further — **go
slower still**. His own cut ran **40 weeks for 24 lb** (187 → 163 lb), slow enough that he ate out and
stayed relaxed, on the argument that if you never felt deprived on the way down you aren't desperate to
end the diet ([get lean & stay lean](get-lean-stay-lean-forever.md)).

**Three things make the lost weight fat rather than muscle**: a sustained deficit, progressive resistance
training throughout, and enough protein (**1.6–2.2 g/kg**, or **>2 g/kg** in Helms' version; macro detail
is in Part two). Cardio is fourth, capped at roughly **half your lifting volume**
([how to get lean](how-to-get-lean.md)). **Speed costs muscle**: across three natural bodybuilding prep
case studies, **21%, 32% and 43%** of total weight lost was lean mass, the 43% coming from the fastest
cut ([bulking vs gaintaining](bulking-cutting-vs-gaintaining-which-best.md)), and Garthe 2011 shows it
prospectively — matched weight loss, but the slower group **kept more muscle**
([refeeds & diet breaks](refeeds-and-diet-breaks.md)). **Sleep decides the split**: in Wang 2018 the
well-slept group lost **83% of their weight as fat** while a group sleeping **one hour less on five
nights a week** lost **85% of it as lean mass** ([body recomposition](body-recomposition.md)).

**Mini cuts** are short deficit phases of **2–8 weeks** against a full prep of 24–36. Two hard rules: not
within **6 months of a show**, and not when already **under 10% body fat**
([minicutting 101](minicutting-101.md)).

| Purpose | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Appetite reset after a long bulk | **20–25% deficit, 2–6 weeks** |
| Easy-conditioning lifter (offseason ceiling 12–15%) | **10–15% deficit, 5–10 weeks**, target 5–10 lb |
| Hard-conditioning lifter (ceiling 10–12%) | **20–25% deficit, 2–3 weeks**, after 1–2 month bulks |

His counterexample matters: **two straight years with no mini cut** took him from 140 to 151 lb on stage
with better conditioning, and he credits most of his long-term development to that uninterrupted surplus.
Take the deficit from **food rather than cardio**.

#### What each body fat percentage actually looks like

Every level online is misrepresented; DEXA numbers look far leaner than the charts suggest. If you think
you're 15%, you're probably higher ([every body fat %](every-body-fat-looks-like.md)).

| Men | What you see | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **~30%** | "dad bod"; outline, no separation | leaner than ~40% of American men |
| **~20%** | arm and shoulder definition, abs when flexed | sustainable and healthy for many |
| **~15%** | clear separation, some vascularity | maintainable year-round; only ~2% of US men are leaner |
| **~12%** | full six-pack, striations flexed | the floor without active tracking |
| **~9–10%** | separation between muscle heads | his own latest DEXA: **9.0%** |
| **~8%** | shredded, deep striations | testosterone drops, brain fog, food obsession |
| **~5%** | dense and grainy | borderline dangerous; below ~6–8% the body eats muscle |

Women carry **10–12% more essential fat**: **40% female ≈ 28–30% male**, **30% ≈ 20%**, **20% ≈ 10%**.
Healthy ranges are **men 10–20%, women 20–30%**; below ~12% (men) and ~15% (women) the side effects
outweigh the aesthetics for almost everyone. In a report of over 9,000 American men, exactly **one** was
under 12%.

#### The real cost of staying shredded

Rossow 2013 followed a natural bodybuilder through **26 weeks**, **30 lb** lost, **14.8% → 4.5%**:
**testosterone fell from 922 to 227 ng/dL**, cortisol **doubled**, ghrelin rose, leptin fell, **mood
disturbance rose 617%**. Women add menstrual disruption — one figure competitor **still had no period
1.5 years post-show**, though **72% of bikini and figure competitors** in another study recovered within
3–4 months ([the ugly truth](ugly-truth-about-getting-shredded.md)).

His framing device is the **"natty triangle"**: shredded and natural, big and natural, or big and
shredded — **pick two**. He prefers the **dual intervention point** model over classic set point theory:
not one defended level but **two boundaries defining a range**. His own runs from about **8–9%** — below
which hunger skyrockets — to about **25%**, and inside it he can sit near the lean edge with little
resistance. **So target the lean boundary of your own range and maintain there.** Below the range you
stop progressing, and the longer you stay under, the more likely you are to binge, rebound and end up
**less lean than if you'd been conservative**. Pick the endpoint before you start: **10–20% for men**,
**18–28% for women** ([get lean & stay lean](get-lean-stay-lean-forever.md)) — the 2025 video puts the
women's band at **20–30%**. History matters: someone who sat at 40% for a decade may find 20% harder than
a genetically lean person finds 8%.

### Recomposition — who can and who cannot

Three notes cover this and **the position broadened visibly across them**.

**2017** ([build muscle & lose fat](build-muscle-lose-fat-same-time.md)): meaningful recomp happens in
**four groups** — beginners, detrained lifters (**muscle memory**: myonuclei persist through detraining),
the overweight/obese, and steroid users. Advanced naturals get a **temporary window** straight after a cut
and should then stop chasing it. His own case: ~10 lb of lean gain in six months after ten years of
training, which worked because he cut first, finished at **8–10% body fat** (not leaner — the body needs
some regain to recover), was detrained off an injury, and gained at **1–3 lb/month**.

**2018** ([bulking vs gaintaining](bulking-cutting-vs-gaintaining-which-best.md)) adds **detrained
lifters** and **lifters who let training slide**; the most broadly useful case is coming back from holiday
fatter and slightly smaller, where a **5–20% deficit** plus rebuilt strength beats either bulking or
straight cutting. **2019** ([body recomposition](body-recomposition.md)) is broadest: possible for
**nearly everyone**, with a fifth category — the **sub-optimised trainee**, intermediate by training age
but never actually trained or fed hard. The only impractical group is advanced lifters already optimised
and near their genetic ceiling.

**Reconciling them:** the mechanism never changed — the energy-density gap between muscle and fat makes it
work, and training is the engine. What changed is his estimate of how many people are optimised enough to
be excluded, and by 2019 the answer is: very few. The protocol is constant — pick a lead goal (muscle if
lean, fat loss if not), set calories **+5 to 25%** or **−10 to 20%** accordingly, keep protein high, and
**never program a surplus** for someone chasing simultaneous fat loss.

### Metabolic adaptation

**Starvation mode is not real; metabolic adaptation is.** In the Minnesota Starvation Experiment (Keys,
1945) 32 men held a **55% deficit for 24 weeks** and **nobody stopped losing** — average loss **25% of
starting bodyweight**, most ending near **5% body fat**. Metabolic rate fell **40%**, but ~**25
percentage points** was simply less metabolically active tissue, leaving a true **adaptive component of
about 15%**. That is the largest adaptation anywhere in the literature, it took getting to 5% body fat,
and it still didn't stop fat loss ([starvation mode](starvation-mode-real-thing.md)).

**NEAT is the adaptive lever, not RMR.** Obese subjects dieted to 10% below start weight drop total
expenditure only **~150 kcal/day**, of which just **~50 kcal** is RMR — **75–90% is non-resting**
([Lyle McDonald](interview-lyle-mcdonald.md)). NEAT can differ by up to **2,000 kcal/day** between two
people matched for body composition, sex and age, and Camps 2013 found it down-regulates **more the
harder and faster you diet**. The tractable arithmetic: **+1 kcal/minute across an eight-hour workday =
500 kcal/day**.

**When the scale stalls it's almost never your metabolism.** In order: under-reported intake (Mahabir
2006 — 65 women under-reported by **37% on average**, logging 1,600 while eating ~2,500), water retention
(often released as a sudden 3–4 lb "whoosh"), then an over-aggressive deficit
([starvation mode](starvation-mode-real-thing.md)). Trexler's fix for a stalled dieter doing everything
right is counter-intuitive: **a few days at maintenance, away from the gym**.

**Metabolic damage — permanent carryover — is not supported.** McDonald calls it "a bunch of crap":
Minnesota metabolisms normalised, anorexics with **up to 30% RMR reduction** recover fully on refeeding,
post-obese subjects recover, and yo-yo dieting shows no major change; he flags two anomalies honestly
(Arctic explorer cases and **Biosphere**, both ~5% low) and calls them trivial
([Lyle McDonald](interview-lyle-mcdonald.md)). Henselmans' reanalysis, in review at the time, corrected
measured RMR for body composition across Minnesota, Biosphere, anorexia patients and both bodybuilder
prep papers and found **no damage anywhere**
([the debate](reverse-diet-debate-eric-helms-menno-henselmans.md)). Trexler's non-damage explanation for
why the *second* prep is harder: suppressed expenditure persists until **both lean tissue and fat** are
regained, and many people overshoot fat before recovering muscle, so prep two starts fatter, lighter in
muscle and already suppressed. Substantial recovery takes **4–6 months**; menstrual regularity can take
over a year ([Trexler](we-get-slow-metabolisms-reverse-diet-science.md)).

**Set point** is both set point and settling point in McDonald's account, settling operating in a narrow
band before defence kicks in — and it appears able to move **up** (pregnancy, obesity) but **not down**,
with post-obese adaptation persisting at least seven years. Staying lean getting easier over time he puts
down to **behaviour**, not physiology ([Lyle McDonald](interview-lyle-mcdonald.md)).

**Can you speed metabolism up?** Only four things clearly work: **building muscle** (**30 lb over five
years ≈ +180 kcal/day**), **cardio** (partly self-cancelling — **100 kcal burned yields ~72 kcal** of
extra daily expenditure), **dieting slowly**, and **NEAT smuggling**. Green tea, sauna (**+5 kcal**/10
min), cold plunges (**+14 kcal**) and meal frequency probably don't; most "boosters" are worth 5–30
calories. The punchline sits in his caveats: **metabolic rate is far less predictive of long-term
weight-loss success than people assume**
([metabolism](metabolism-and-energy-expenditure.md)).

### Reverse dieting and diet breaks

The most contested area in this half, and he has hosted both sides.

**The 2016 debate.** He defines reverse dieting as slow, methodical calorie reintroduction after a cut to
raise metabolic rate and limit fat regain — then notes both teams attacked the definition rather than the
practice ([the debate](reverse-diet-debate-eric-helms-menno-henselmans.md)).

*Against (Helms, Henselmans).* Post-show the goal is fast recovery, and **fat regain is the mechanism of
recovery, not its cost**; Helms wants **~5% over stage weight within 2–4 weeks**. His evidence is coaching
data — 3D Muscle Journey ran a conservative reverse-diet approach and dropped it at roughly a **10%
success rate**, and even the successes didn't later diet on more food or start leaner. Physiologically
most of the TDEE drop is **NEAT**, with RMR at best **20–30%** of it, so manipulating food to "correct
adaptation" targets the wrong variable. Henselmans supplies the timing: adaptive thermogenesis is
measurable within **a day** and thyroid and sympathetic activity recover in **at most 8–14 days**, so
**two weeks at maintenance clears the adaptations**. The one real error reverse dieting fixes is returning
to your *old* maintenance — an 80 kg man who dieted from 3,000 to 2,000 kcal no longer has a 3,000 kcal
maintenance.

*For (Norton, Fitschen).* Norton's actual protocol is "**quickly jump calories up pretty high, get
somebody feeling really good**," then add slowly — much closer to the other side than the label implies.
His fear is that rapid regain triggers binge-restrict cycling, and his set-point case rests on **one rat
study** (animals refed after dieting overshot original body fat, added ~**50% more fat cells**, and kept
gaining until individual cells returned to pre-diet *size*) plus Brownell's weight-cycling animal work.
He concedes the thinness himself and regrets popularising "metabolic damage." Fitschen splits by context:
stage-lean athletes should **not** be reversed slowly; a general-population dieter who isn't suffering
can go slower.

They converged more than they diverged — all four agree the drop is mostly NEAT, that psychology sets the
rate, and (Fitschen's line, endorsed by everyone) that **you make progress when you are not dieting**.
**Still unresolved:** whether fast fat regain permanently raises a body-fat set point, and whether
building offseason calories carries into the next prep, where Helms and Norton have directly opposed
client cases and neither can generalise.

**His own position, and where it went.** The immediate follow-up
([my take](reverse-diet-debate-follow-up-take.md)) is blunt: creeping back in increments makes no sense,
because **at your new maintenance you cannot, by definition, gain fat** — people who "lose weight while
reverse dieting" are simply still in a deficit. Jumping straight to maintenance is the *minimum*; for a
post-show competitor he'd prefer straight into a **400–1,000 kcal surplus**, managed with a **weight cap
of roughly 10% over stage weight in the first four months** rather than a calorie schedule, backed by
soft structure — discrete solid meals, **25–40 g protein each**, stop when full. He grants that fat
returns faster than muscle so you look *less* lean at a given weight for a while, but **fast and slow
regain land in the same place 3–6 months later**, making it a choice between a short ugly period and a
long uncomfortable one.

By **2019** he splits it in two ([Trexler](we-get-slow-metabolisms-reverse-diet-science.md)): post-contest
or very lean athletes need a **recovery diet**, not a slow reverse; someone moderately lean and *staying*
there (~8–10% for a man) can reasonably go to maintenance then creep up, with reverse dieting buying at
most **100–200 kcal/day**. By **2023** it hardens into don't: after having no plan at all, the
second-commonest post-diet failure is **meticulous reverse dieting**, which drags the diet out and
eventually breaks willpower. **If you're no longer trying to lose weight, there's no reason to still be
in a deficit.** Go to your new maintenance immediately — **200–600 kcal above your end-of-cut intake** —
then treat maintenance as a **dynamic range** and push toward its top while the trend stays flat
([get lean & stay lean](get-lean-stay-lean-forever.md)). McDonald's 2016 number agrees: **no more than
about two weeks** to reach maintenance ([Lyle McDonald](interview-lyle-mcdonald.md)).

**Refeeds and diet breaks are a separate question.** Distributing a deficit differently changes body
composition even at matched net calories. **Refeeds should run 48 hours, not 24** — one day is too short
to raise metabolic rate (**at least 48 hours**) or refill glycogen (up to **10 g carb/kg**, i.e. **800 g**
for an 80 kg lifter, far more practical as two days at 400 g). The only trial in trained lifters is
**Campbell 2020** (n=27, 7 weeks): a **35% deficit for 5 days plus 2 at maintenance** versus a continuous
**25% deficit**, matched weekly. The refeed group **lost less fat-free mass and preserved RMR better**;
**fat loss was identical**. Refeeds are a muscle-retention tool, not a fat-burning trick
([refeeds & diet breaks](refeeds-and-diet-breaks.md)).

| Diet-break study | Population | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Wing & Jeffery 2003** | obese, non-exercising | breaks inserted *expecting* regain; **all three groups lost the same** |
| **Byrne 2018 (MATADOR)** | 36 obese men | **50% more fat lost**, no extra muscle loss, REE dropped **half as much**; at 6 months the gap widened to **80–90%** ([note](diet-breaks-improve-fat-loss-metabolism.md)) |
| **Peos 2021 (ICECAP)** | trained/active, 12 weeks | **no difference** in fat loss, muscle retention or metabolic slowdown — but **every psychological outcome favoured breaks** |

The catch in Byrne is structural: matching total deficit meant **30 weeks instead of 16**. He also raises
the possibility it's partly an **adherence artefact**, and notes that on lower maintenance calories a
"break" may be only **400–500 extra kcal/day**. So: refeeds have the better case for lean trained lifters
(one study), diet breaks for the overweight (in a population unlike yours), and for getting properly
shredded there's little direct research and the best coaches use both. Trexler's hybrid is the honest
answer — **diet linearly until progress genuinely grinds, then introduce breaks**. His 2024 rule of thumb:
past **3 months** of continuous dieting, take **2–3 weeks at bodyweight × 16–18 calories**
([get abs in 60 days](get-abs-60-days.md)).

### Cardio and fat loss

**Cardio is a supplement to the diet, not the engine.** At **170 lb**, burning 1,000 calories takes
roughly **three hours of brisk walking** — calories you could eat in under a minute. Worse, real-world
weight loss from cardio is only **20–50% of what the maths predicts**, through compensatory eating and
reduced NEAT: burn 2,500 kcal/week with no dietary change and you lose **0.14–0.36 lb**, not 0.7. It's
still worth doing — cardiorespiratory fitness is a major predictor of all-cause mortality, conditioning
lets you complete your lifting volume, and activity is one of the few things reliably associated with
keeping weight off ([cardio mistakes](cardio-for-fat-loss.md)).

**Dosage**: **HIIT 1–2 sessions/week, 15–30 min**, at least a day away from leg day; **LISS 0–5
sessions/week, 20–40 min**; total cardio under half your lifting volume; low-impact modes preferred; and
**lift first, cardio last** in a shared session, with advanced lifters separating them by hours or days.
Interference is real but is an advanced-lifter problem — a 2021 meta-analysis found no strength effect in
untrained and moderately trained people and a significant one in the well-trained
([cardio mistakes](cardio-for-fat-loss.md), [how to get lean](how-to-get-lean.md)).

**HIIT is not better for fat loss.** After **80 minutes at 80% max heart rate** afterburn added only
~**80 calories** against the 700–800 burned during the session, and a 2017 review of 28 studies concluded
EPOC can't explain any greater fat-loss potential. Wewege's meta-analysis found **no significant
difference on any body composition measure**, with HIIT needing ~**40% less time** — though those subjects
were obese with no dietary control, and he expects the gap to shrink in lean trained people. HIIT's real
costs are recovery, interference, injury risk and partial redundancy with hard lifting, which is already
interval-shaped ([HIIT vs LISS](hiit-liss-which-better-fat-loss.md)).

**Fasted cardio** gets two notes seven years apart and the position is stable. It genuinely **does** raise
fat oxidation during the session — real physiology, from low insulin, low glycogen and elevated cortisol
and epinephrine ([the truth about fasted cardio](truth-about-fasted-cardio.md)). But burn more of a
substrate during a session and you burn less over the next 24 hours, which Paoli 2011 measured directly.
Schoenfeld's 2014 trial — 20 women, matched **500 kcal deficit**, 1 hour of cardio 3×/week for 4 weeks —
found **no difference**, and a 2017 review of five trials agrees while finding **no detriment** either
([does fasted cardio burn more fat](fasted-cardio-burn-more-fat.md)). The muscle-loss counter-claim is
equally unsupported. **Do cardio whenever you'll adhere to it.** Two caveats stay open: McDonald's
mechanistic **alpha-2 receptor** argument for fasted work on stubborn fat at the tail end of a cut, and
the fact that fasted cardio in very lean physique competitors is simply unstudied.

### Behaviour and adherence

**No diet type wins when calories are equated.** Johnston's 2014 meta-analysis of **48 RCTs across 10
named programs** found the differences small and unimportant; the best diet is the one you stay on longest
([get lean without tracking](get-lean-without-tracking-macros-dieting.md)). Norton's five behaviours
common to long-term maintainers: **cognitive restraint** in some form, **regular self-monitoring**,
**regular exercise**, a **structured program**, and **low recency**
([how to get lean](how-to-get-lean.md)).

**Flexible dieting beats rigid clean eating.** "Clean" has no coherent definition — protein powder is
highly processed and always makes the list, and there's no defensible reason sweet potatoes are clean and
white potatoes aren't, when white potatoes tested as the **most satiating** common carbohydrate source. A
2012 trial forbidding **bread** produced identical fat loss over 16 weeks but **far more dropouts**.
Westenhoefer found flexible control associated with **lower BMI, less binge eating and better one-year
weight loss**, with the three-year follow-up showing better *maintenance* too; rigid dieting — but not
flexible dieting — tracks with eating disorder symptoms. Eliminate enough foods and you get deficiencies:
Kleiner found elite bodybuilders at **46% of the RDA for vitamin D** and women at **52% for calcium**. The
reframe he takes from Helms: **include nutrient-dense foods rather than exclude bad ones**
([why you shouldn't eat clean](shouldn-t-eat-clean-lose-fat-more.md)).

**The one intervention study is a null result.** Laurin Conlin's thesis put **27 completers** through **10
weeks** on a 25% deficit, macro tracking versus a dietitian-written meal plan: **no difference on any
measure**. The single exception came in the uncontrolled **10-week post-diet period**, where the flexible
group gained **1.53 kg lean mass** and the meal-plan group lost **0.59 kg** — a finding she couldn't
explain and warned against over-reading. **26 of 27 said they'd choose macros** if repeated. Her most
important contribution is definitional: **"flexible dieting" in the research literature does not mean
IIFYM** — there it means a moderate *mindset* versus all-or-nothing rigid restraint, so the older
questionnaire evidence doesn't transfer automatically. And her hard line against popular IIFYM claims:
**flexible dieting does not eliminate cravings** at low body fat and low calories. Jeff pushed back with
Westenhoefer's data and Meule's finding that chocolate cravers told not to eat chocolate for two weeks had
cravings *increase*; they resolved it by population — inclusion helps general-population dieters and stops
helping stage-lean competitors ([the flexible diet discussion](flexible-diet-discussion.md)).

He has argued the meal-plan side himself: three weeks from his pro debut he swapped flexible dieting for a
fixed five-meal plan, claiming gains in adherence, time, gym performance, **weigh-in data quality**
(constant sodium and food volume means less scale noise) and psychology. It turns on **food-cue
responsiveness** — some people crave a restricted food *more*, others find a small taste escalates rather
than satisfies, and he puts himself and most of the bodybuilding population in the second camp. A
continuum, not a binary; a middle option is to **bracket the day** — fixed breakfast and pre-bed meal,
flexible in between ([5 reasons meal plans can work](5-reasons-meal-plans-work-better-than.md)).

**Losing fat without tracking** works fine — the three pillars (deficit, enough protein, weight training)
are the whole game. Four behavioural levers create a deficit passively: rotate carbs and fats between
meals (it's the *combination* that makes food easy to over-consume), extend fasting windows (a 12-week
**8-hour eating window** pilot produced ~**3% bodyweight** loss with no other restriction), cut liquid
calories (subjects consumed **30% more** of a liquid than a semi-solid version of an identical drink), and
eat out less (US restaurant meals average **1,205 kcal**, range **113–3,008**). Pick one or two, not four
([get lean without tracking](get-lean-without-tracking-macros-dieting.md)).

**Habits over willpower.** Self-control is useful for *forming* habits and poor as a long-term strategy;
a habit by definition costs no cognitive effort. The practical form is choice architecture — sweets into
an opaque container across the room, calorie-dense food somewhere annoying, phone in another room
([how to get lean](how-to-get-lean.md)) — plus **temptation bundling**. And **keep weighing yourself**:
self-monitoring is one of the few behaviours consistently associated with long-term maintenance, **2–3
days a week** is enough, and skip it if it causes real distress
([get lean & stay lean](get-lean-stay-lean-forever.md)).

**Spot reduction: not busted, not confirmed.** Three studies found nothing — 1971 tennis players, 2007
single-arm training (n=104, MRI), and 2013 single-leg training, the better test since it produced real
total fat loss. Then a 2018 University of Rome study split subjects into upper-body-only and
lower-body-only groups and followed **every session with 30 minutes of light cycling**, finding strong
regional effects with identical whole-body fat loss — the cardio may be the deciding variable. Jeff stays
sceptical and doesn't recommend chasing it; in his coaching experience stubborn areas are simply the last
to lean out ([spot reduction](lose-bodyfat-from-specific-bodyparts.md)).

**Obesity is not a simple choice.** Given the metabolic, NEAT and hunger variation above — plus
appetite-raising medication, shift work (**23% higher overweight risk**), sleep duration (dose-response,
**7–8 hours** optimal), stress, income and food environment — the most defensible version of "choice" is
"a complex series of choices intertwined with many other contributing factors." Fat loss stays achievable
via a sustained deficit for almost anyone; blame is misplaced and useless
([is being fat a choice](being-fat-choice.md)).

### Contest prep

**Peak week is massively overrated, and the asymmetry is the point.** The upside is small; the ability to
wreck yourself is large — a botched week can make a stage-ready athlete look six weeks out. And there is
essentially **no research**: "if anyone is talking about hard science behind peak week, they're
overstating the evidence" ([peak week roundtable](peak-week-roundtable.md)).

**The anatomy argument is the one to keep.** The only water affecting how you look is in the **dermis —
0.5 mm on the eyelids to 3 mm on the back, about 70–75% water** — so manipulation shifts roughly **2 mm at
the thickest point**, and the blue "subcutaneous water layer" on water-pill packaging is not a real
anatomical structure. **Never deplete sodium**: a 1990 Harvard study took dietary sodium to essentially
zero for six days and blood concentrations **stayed unchanged**, because aldosterone rises and the kidneys
retain more; depletion also impairs the **SGLT1 transporter** shuttling glucose into muscle, and paired
with high water intake it risks hyponatremia. The hormone half-lives kill the water-load-then-cut protocol
outright — **ADH's half-life is 10–35 minutes, aldosterone's under 20** — so within an hour or two of
cutting water you're retaining again. Prolonged heavy sodium loading causes oedema, but a **short acute
load before showtime is good** ([peak week: sodium and water](peak-week-cut-sodium-water.md)).

The roundtable's three coaches converge on a low-variance default: **hold water constant, hold sodium
constant through the week, then 1–3 g of sodium immediately before the pump-up**. They diverge on
carbohydrate — Wilson's **rapid backload** (deplete to 40–100 g/day Monday–Thursday, then **850–1,600 g in
one Friday**, most male bodybuilders at 1,000–1,250 g) versus Helms' **front load** anchored to the
athlete's own refeeds (**90–120% of the highest refeed**) versus Theobald's case-by-case, including fat
loads. Two unanimities: **nobody supports cutting water while loading carbs** — if roughly 3 of every 4
grams stored is water, restricting water removes three-quarters of the fullness — and everyone targets
**slightly blurred Friday night, not razor sharp**. Load formulas fail because glycogen capacity expands
and contracts with habitual carb intake ([peak week roundtable](peak-week-roundtable.md)). Two more
things: **anxiety itself raises cortisol enough to cause water retention**, and **arrive lean at 7 days
out** — a male competitor should already have glute striations
([peak week: sodium and water](peak-week-cut-sodium-water.md)).

**Auditing his own 2010 prep** shows what a decade changed; his verdict on the old sheet is **4/10**. It
was a **rigid meal plan** with calories and macros never calculated, so it taught him nothing and
collapsed the moment he stopped following it; **fat was 13% of calories**, the number he calls most
concerning, against a floor of **15–20%** (~40–60 g/day on prep); **protein at 265 g/day** was overkill;
the **pre-workout meal was chicken and broccoli with no usable carb source** — the biggest violation; the
immediate post-workout shake was redundant given an anabolic window of roughly **4–5 hours**; and fasted
cardio, flax seeds, tilapia-dries-you-out and no-carbs-before-bed were all folklore
([everything I wish I knew](everything-wish-knew-about-dieting-10-years.md)).

### Where his position moved in this half

- **Reverse dieting** — 2016: hosts the debate, concludes the slow ramp buys nothing and jumping straight
  to maintenance is the *minimum*. 2019: splits it, allowing a slow reverse for someone moderately lean
  intending to stay there. 2023: **recommends against it outright**
  ([2016](reverse-diet-debate-follow-up-take.md),
  [2019](we-get-slow-metabolisms-reverse-diet-science.md), [2023](get-lean-stay-lean-forever.md)).
- **How lean to get** — 2015: "if you're at 8%, eat your way up to 10–12%," with under-10% as the working
  range for a serious lifter ([minicutting](minicutting-101.md)). 2022: find the lean boundary of your own
  range and stay there ([the ugly truth](ugly-truth-about-getting-shredded.md)). 2025: he explicitly
  challenges the line that 10–12% is the sweet spot every man should aim for — the men who cruise at 10%
  are usually genetically lean ([every body fat %](every-body-fat-looks-like.md)).
- **Recomposition** — 2017: four groups, advanced lifters should stop chasing it. 2019: "nearly everyone,"
  with the sub-optimised trainee added as a fifth and much larger category
  ([2017](build-muscle-lose-fat-same-time.md), [2019](body-recomposition.md)).
- **Diet breaks** — 2017: MATADOR reads as a major physiological win, **50% more fat lost**. 2021: the
  Peos trial in trained subjects finds **no physiological difference at all**, and he reframes breaks as
  primarily psychological for lifters, with 48-hour refeeds carrying the better evidence there
  ([2017](diet-breaks-improve-fat-loss-metabolism.md), [2021](refeeds-and-diet-breaks.md)).
- **Rate of loss** — the 0.5–1%/week figure never changed, but his own practice drifted to the slow end
  and below it: a 40-week cut for 24 lb, argued on adherence rather than physiology
  ([get lean & stay lean](get-lean-stay-lean-forever.md)).
- **Meal plans** — 2015: five reasons a fixed plan can beat IIFYM, from his own prep. 2020: he audits that
  era's plan and names rigidity as its core structural mistake. Reconcilable — the 2015 case is about the
  final weeks of a prep, not a way to live — but the emphasis moved hard toward flexibility
  ([2015](5-reasons-meal-plans-work-better-than.md),
  [2020](everything-wish-knew-about-dieting-10-years.md)).

## Part two — macros, foods and timing

Calories decide the direction; this half decides what fills them. One macro has a target worth
defending to the gram. Almost everything else resolves to "it matters less than you were told."

| Variable | Number |
|---|---|
| Protein, bulking | **1.6–2.2 g/kg (0.7–1.0 g/lb)** |
| Protein, cutting | **1.8–2.7 g/kg (0.8–1.2 g/lb)** |
| Protein, vegan | **above 1.8 g/kg** |

| Anabolic window | **4–6 hours** spanning the session |
| Caffeine cap | **400 mg/day**, everything counted |

### Protein

**Bulking 1.6–2.2 g/kg (0.7–1.0 g/lb); cutting 1.8–2.7 g/kg (0.8–1.2 g/lb); recomp the bulking
numbers or slightly higher** ([protein for muscle growth](protein-for-muscle-growth.md)). You need
less in a surplus because a well-fed body burns carbs and fat before it comes for muscle. The anchor
is the **Morton meta-analysis: gains optimise around 1.6 g/kg, confidence interval reaching
2.1–2.2** — where the old 1 g/lb rule lands. Trommelen is blunt that **1.2 g/kg is definitively
insufficient** and **1.6 g/kg is the floor in a deficit**
([protein dose per day and per meal](protein-dose-per-day-and-per-meal.md)). Two edge cases: high
body fat breaks bodyweight scaling, so use Helms's **1 g per centimetre of height**; and **older
adults clearly need more**, while **women respond the same as men**.

**His number has drifted down and he says so.** The 2019 micros video still gave "at least 0.8 g/lb,
1 g/lb as the easy number" ([how much do micros
matter](much-micros-matter-fat-loss-muscle-gain.md)). By 2023 excessive protein was one of his four
worst mistakes, citing Morton's **break point at 0.7 g/lb** — above which extra protein does very
little, and below which **you would still grow at 0.5 g/lb, just slower**
([worst nutrition mistakes](worst-nutrition-mistakes-every-lifter-makes.md)). He cut at 165 lb on
**150 g/day (0.9 g/lb)**. The reason is mechanistic: protein gives short blips of muscle protein
synthesis; training gives a prolonged elevation an order of magnitude larger.

#### Per meal, distribution, pre-sleep

**Absorption is a red herring** — humans absorb virtually all ingested protein, even 300 g, and the
"20–30 g cap" confuses absorption with utilisation ([how much protein in one
meal](much-protein-absorb-one-meal.md)). Of a meal, **~95% is absorbed, ~50% is retained by gut
tissue, and about 20% of the remainder is taken up by muscle — roughly 10% of what you ate.**

The usable ceiling is a dial, not a switch ([protein dose](protein-dose-per-day-and-per-meal.md)):
**5 g, the smallest dose ever tested, already significantly stimulates MPS**; **~20 g gives
near-maximal MPS for 4–5 hours** (~3 g leucine; **~30 g plant-based**); the response is **linear to
20 g then declines sharply**; **40 g gives about 10–20% more**, often not significantly. Three things
raise the ceiling: **body size** (Phillips proposes **0.4 g/kg, ~0.18 g/lb per meal** — 22 g at
120 lb, 36 g at 200 lb), **how much muscle you trained** (Macnaughton's 40 g beat 20 g after
*full-body* work; the earlier null studies used legs only), and **age**. Trommelen rejects the
**muscle full effect** for lifters — every supporting study was done **without exercise** — and
rejects Layne Norton's **refractory period**, never demonstrated. Don't overreact the other way:
**~101 g in a single 4-hour window** preserved lean mass no differently from a spread pattern.

On distribution, the key experiment is **Areta**: 80 g of whey over 12 hours post-training as
**2 × 40 g, 4 × 20 g, or 8 × 10 g. The 4 × 20 g pattern won**
([intermittent fasting](science-behind-intermittent-fasting.md)). Trommelen's read on his own field's
most-cited study is deflating — proof of concept, not a real-world result: everything fed was fast
whey, the 40 g doses wasted everything above 20 g, and **repeated with casein he expects no
difference**. So the recommendations spread: **4 meals** (Trommelen), **3–5**
([protein for muscle growth](protein-for-muscle-growth.md)), **3–5 high-protein meals** per Phillips
([response to Hyman](5-simple-steps-won-t-burn-fat.md)), and by 2023 **1–6 is fine for fat loss,
3–6 probably slightly better than 1–2 for growth**. That last claim rests on thin data — Taguchi
found **no significant difference between 3 meals and 6**, Yasuda found 3 trended above 2 **without
reaching significance**, and a 2009 review across **60 studies** found strong evidence of **no
association between eating frequency and weight status.** Six meals does not stoke the metabolism:
the thermic effect of food scales with total calories, not plate count.

**Pre-sleep is the underused lever. 30 g did not significantly raise overnight MPS across ~8 hours
in Trommelen's study; an earlier study with 40 g did** — the 20 g rule holds only when the next meal
is 4 hours out, and the night is 7–8+. **So: 40 g before bed.** A **12-week study using 30 g
increased muscle gains with no increase in body fat.** The strongest part of the case is field data:
across 300+ national- to Olympic-level athletes, **total protein averaged ~1.5 g/kg but over 80% came
from breakfast, lunch and dinner**, leaving a ~12-hour gap. **The counter-evidence is honest and he
includes it**: two trials comparing casein before bed against casein in the morning found **no
significant difference after 8–10 weeks** — but both had subjects at ~2 g/kg total protein. That is
the pattern across this whole domain: timing details fade when the daily total is already high.

#### Quality, and whether MPS predicts growth

**On a mixed diet hitting your total, quality takes care of itself.** Leucine triggers mTOR and
**~3 g per meal** is the ballpark: **whey needs 29 g of powder (~145 kcal), chicken breast 40 g of
protein (~200 kcal), vegan isolates under 200 kcal, whole wheat bread over 2,000 kcal.** The DIAAS
tables ranking animal protein on top assume proteins eaten *in isolation*, which is why **leucine,
BCAA and EAA supplements are usually unnecessary.** Two exceptions: **collagen has essentially zero
leucine** and "beef protein" is often just collagen; and plant proteins carry the **limiting amino
acid** problem — fix it by **mixing sources and adding 10–20% to your total.**

Whether acute MPS predicts hypertrophy at all is the methodological core of the library
([muscle protein synthesis](muscle-protein-synthesis.md)). MPS swings **3–5 fold** while breakdown
moves in a narrow range, and **blocking breakdown entirely is harmful** — rats engineered unable to
break down muscle protein end up **smaller and weaker**. On the famous criticism, three studies:
untrained subjects with MPS measured **4 hours** after one session showed **no correlation** with
10-week gains; a six-week study measuring MPS **across the whole six weeks** correlated well; and an
improved design measuring **24 and 48 hours** found MPS **did** predict gains, except in the
completely untrained. Trommelen's resolution: **the measurement window must match the response.**
The exercise MPS response lasts 24–48 hours, so measuring the first four is judging a marathon by the
first 5 km — while four hours is exactly right for a protein shake. His filter: **a training study
under 10 weeks with fewer than ~20 subjects per group commands very little faith**, which is why
**about 80% of protein supplementation studies find no significant muscle gain.**

#### Safety

**Both scare stories are busted in people with healthy kidneys**
([bone loss and kidney damage](high-protein-diets-cause-bone-loss-kidney.md)). The bone myth is the
**acid-ash hypothesis**, built on a 1970s–80s error: protein does raise urinary calcium, and because
the era's balance studies said protein didn't affect intestinal absorption, the calcium "must" have
come from bone. **They were wrong about absorption.** The 2007 National Osteoporosis Foundation
review found higher protein **significantly increases intestinal calcium absorption** — enough to
account for nearly the entire rise in urinary calcium, **with no increase in skeletal catabolism** —
plus **positive trends between protein and bone density at most sites**. The kidney myth generalises
from diseased kidneys to healthy ones: in normal renal function more protein **increases GFR**, a
marker of improved function. **The kidney-disease exception is genuine.** The ISSN reports no
evidence of risk in healthy exercising people at intakes **as high as 4.4 g/kg (2 g/lb)**.

The one dissent is Kamal Patel's, and it isn't mechanistic
([Kamal Patel interview](interview-kamal-patel-examine-com.md)). He ran **~2 g/lb of lean body mass**
himself for a decade and agrees the renal and skeletal fears are unfounded; his worry is
**accumulated dose across decades**, which no study will ever measure. Nippard's rebuttal: with no
mechanism, Occam's razor applies. They agree to disagree; Patel names **3 g/lb** as definitely too
high.

### Carbs and sugar

Four videos across eleven years, including one framed as a self-correction — which repays reading
carefully, because it corrects less than the title promises.

**2014 — [11 anti-sugar claims picked apart](11-weird-things-sugar-probably-isn-t.md).** A widely
shared article listed 11 harms and cited **zero references**; he found the underlying studies and
most didn't say what was claimed. Four recurring errors: dose extrapolation, correlation-as-causation,
hasty generalisation from one disease to another, and reductionism. The headline "sugar makes your
organs fat" study gave **~150 g pure fructose/day (25% of calories) on top of an ad libitum diet in
overweight subjects already gaining weight**. "Tense blood vessels" pointed the *opposite* way:
isocaloric fructose *lowered* diastolic and mean arterial pressure. For "type 3 diabetes" he searched
the cited paper's full text for "sugar" and "fructose" and got **no hits for either**. He does not
refute all eleven — **the AGEs/skin claim he leaves open**. Reference point at the time: **~60 g
fructose/day shows no negative effects on any measured marker.**

**Jan 2018 — [is sugar making you fat](sugar-making-fat.md).** The direct evidence: a **2013
systematic review of 68 studies** found swapping sugar for other macros at constant calories produces
**no change in body weight**. The correlational case is weak — sugary drink consumption rose with
obesity, but so did **bottled water consumption**, and in **Australia 1980–2002 refined sucrose
consumption fell 23% while obesity tripled.** The WHO's **10% of calories** cap is driven mainly by
dental caries, and the WHO itself calls the sugar-to-weight relationship "moderate to low." Aragon's
ballpark: **~50 g/day fructose**, implying **~100 g/day total sugar** — a round number for
orientation, not a threshold. His conclusion is narrower than the title: sugar does not appear to
play a *central causal* role, not that intake is irrelevant.

**Three days later — [was I wrong about sugar](was-wrong-about-sugar-insulin-addiction-fructose.md)**
is titled as a self-correction. The honest reading is that **it is a defence with concessions, not a
retraction** — he takes the four strongest objections and holds his position on all four, adding
mechanism:

- **Insulin.** Sugar isn't special: all carbohydrate raises it, and **protein is a powerful insulin
  stimulator too** — a high-protein/low-carb meal produced insulin release slightly *higher* than a
  high-carb meal. The elevation is **transient**: at energy balance lipogenesis and lipolysis cancel
  over 24 hours, and in a deficit net fat balance goes negative regardless. And on zero carbs a
  surplus still stores fat via **acylation-stimulating protein**.
- **Addiction.** Here he genuinely softens: sugar has **addictive-*like* qualities**, not addiction
  in the heroin sense. The rodent evidence cuts against the addiction reading — **knock out the taste
  buds and rodents stop overeating sugar**, and under **devaluation** rats made sick on sugar become
  averse, while rats keep taking cocaine or heroin despite being made sick.
- **Insulin sensitivity.** Under **isocaloric** conditions, high- vs low-sucrose diets showed **no
  difference over six weeks** in healthy subjects. He concedes the individual case fully: someone
  drinking two litres of Pepsi a day would improve by dropping it.
- **Fructose.** Not "toxic" — toxicity is about effects and dose, not where something is metabolised.
  Labelling work attributed to Brad Dieter has **roughly half of ingested fructose converted
  immediately to glucose, roughly half to lactate, and only about 1% converted to fat**, holding to
  around **100–150 g**.

What moved was the framing, not the verdict: he now treats hyper-palatable processed food as an
**environment and availability problem** rather than an addiction problem, and says moderating sugar
is reasonable for some people on those grounds. The numbers also drift across the three videos —
**60 g fructose benign** (2014), **~50 g fructose / ~100 g sugar** as an upper bound, **1% conversion
holding to 100–150 g** — and the note flags the last as recalled with an "I think."

**2019 — [Guyenet on what's really making us fat](s-making-us-fat-carbs-sugar.md)** supplies what the
earlier three lacked. Guyenet agrees energy balance is the main driver — **Kevin Hall's meta-analysis
of ~20 metabolic ward studies found carb vs fat made almost no practical difference at matched
calories**, with a small significant edge **favouring carbohydrate**. But **intake is not a passive
choice**: it's regulated by brain circuits governing appetite, cravings and food-cue reactivity,
largely non-consciously, and **about 75% of differences in body fatness are attributable to
genetics** — Bouchard's twin overfeeding showed near-identical gains within pairs and huge variation
across them.

He also supplies the correlation problem in its strongest form: **US sugar intake is down 15–23%
since 1999 and UK intake down ~22% over half a century — spanning the entire UK obesity epidemic —
while obesity rose.** He's careful this isn't ironclad and doesn't show sugar plays no role; what it
refutes is sugar as the **primary** cause. And here is what the earlier videos underplayed: **sugar
makes us eat more**, by increasing the seductiveness of food. Craving and addiction-like eating track
the **combination of carbohydrate and fat** plus salt, glutamate and texture — chocolate, ice cream,
baked goods, and sugar-free savoury ones like fries and pizza. His hierarchy: **control your food
environment → eat simple whole foods → raise protein → exercise, sleep, manage stress → only then
restrict carbs or fat.**

**Carbs at night — busted** ([carbs before bed](carbs-before-bed-they-making-fat.md)). Metabolism
doesn't slow at night: Zhang's pooled **sleep:awake ratio is 1.0011**. Carbs aren't readily converted
to fat anyway — de novo lipogenesis is rare; what happens is **fat oxidation is suppressed**. And
evening insulin sensitivity isn't uniquely poor: Biston's morning meal won, but it followed an
overnight fast, and in the *same paper* a mid-afternoon meal showed **no difference** against a night
meal. Direct evidence: **Fong 2017 screened 121 studies to 5 calorie-matched trials; four of five
individually favoured a smaller evening meal, but pooled results were not significant** — thin, and
worth saying. The one legitimate cut-off is **behavioural**.

### Fats, fibre, micronutrients, water

**Fat has a floor, not a target.** Patel brackets the testosterone question as **definitely more than
5–10 g/day, definitely less than 50 g**, with a stronger link to *total* fat than to saturated fat.
**Nippard's practical floor is 30–35 g/day, and only at the very end of a cut**
([Kamal Patel interview](interview-kamal-patel-examine-com.md)).

On saturated fat the honest position is **nobody knows** ([vegan diet
science](vegan-diet-science.md)). Across RCTs it has an **extremely strong effect on blood lipids**,
yet **RCTs of hard outcomes — heart attack, heart failure, stroke, cardiac mortality — show no
effect.** Patel is blunt that nobody has explained the gap; Nippard agrees. Two corrections survive
regardless: **LDL-C is the wrong number** (particle count, **LDL-P**, is what matters, and **size
doesn't rescue you**), and **HDL isn't "good"** — a large HDL-raising trial lifted it **20–40
percentage points and was stopped because mortality rose.** Practical version: **don't avoid
saturated fat unless you have heart disease or familial hypercholesterolemia.** On omega-3 the two
disagree without resolving it: Patel's evidence review found the few borderline-significant studies
were **always supplementation, never fish**. **Nippard's protocol: fatty fish 2–4× per week, failing
that 3 g combined EPA+DHA daily.**

**Fibre: 10% of your daily carb grams, floor 20 g/day** — 500 g carbs → 50 g fibre; the Academy of
Nutrition and Dietetics line is **20–35 g/day for adults, age + 5 g for children**
([how much fibre](much-fiber-eat-per-day.md)). **Source matters as much as the number**: high-fibre
foods are usually micronutrient-dense, and hitting 35 g with fibre bars gets you the absorption
penalty without the nutrient payload. He also flags a surprise: **the satiety claim is shakier than
advertised.** A 2011 review of **104 RCTs found only 43% detected any appetite effect, and only by as
little as 10%**; a 2013 review found **59% showed no effect at all**. The studies that did show one
used **more viscous fibre**, so bias toward **oats, beans and passion fruit** rather than raising
grams. (Note the tension: the keto note recommends raising fibre for appetite control, on evidence
weaker than that advice implies.)

**Micronutrients matter much less than macros for body composition, and deficiencies are more common
than people think** — both halves true ([how much do micros
matter](much-micros-matter-fat-loss-muscle-gain.md)). Cliff Wilson's answer on whether getting below
6% is macros or food choices surprised him: it really is the macros, with clients reaching **4–5%
body fat eating cereal the whole way down** — and Nippard declines to say whether that's *healthy*.
If the diet is bad enough the deficiencies bite: **calcium, magnesium, zinc, vitamin D**, with
**zinc and magnesium deficiency able to downregulate testosterone.** **Carb-to-fat ratio is
individual** — some do better lower-carb, some far better higher-carb, and for a third group it makes
no difference once protein and calories are fixed.

**Water: drink to thirst** ([you don't need 8 glasses](don-t-need-8-glasses-water-day.md)). The rule
traces to a 1974 book that said six to eight glasses per 24 hours **and explicitly said it could come
from coffee, tea and milk**. **Coffee and alcohol both count** — caffeinated drinks don't raise urine
output in habitual consumers, and alcohol is a net diuretic only for about three hours, actually
*increasing* retention from 6 to 12. **Around training, thirst is not enough**: you're ~2% dehydrated
by the time you feel it, and **Savoie's meta-analysis found 3% dehydration significantly reduced
strength, power and endurance, averaging a ~5% drop across 28 studies.** Aragon's numbers: **6–10 oz
before, ~7 oz every 15–20 minutes**; daily check, **five to seven clear urinations.** Water doesn't
boost metabolism — it helps by **displacing caloric drinks and adding fullness**.

### Nutrient timing

The library contains a correction *and* a correction to the correction, which is the useful part.

**The myth** entered the peer-reviewed literature, not just bro science: Ivy and Ferguson (2013)
recommended nutrients **within 45 minutes**, citing 134 papers
([anabolic window](post-workout-anabolic-window.md)).

**The correction.** Schoenfeld, Aragon and Krieger's 2013 meta-analysis of 20 studies found a
small-to-moderate timing effect — but a **sub-analysis showed the differences could be explained
entirely by total daily protein intake.** Training elevates MPS for **at least 24–48 hours**, with
the initial amino-acid sensitivity spike lasting **2.5–3 hours**. So the window is **4–6 hours
centred on the session**, and the model is a **dial, not a switch**.

**The correction to the correction.** By 2021 he argued the science crowd had overcorrected
([nutrient timing around training](nutrient-timing-around-training.md)). Aragon and Schoenfeld's own
paper says pre- and post-exercise meals shouldn't be separated by more than **3–4 hours**, or
**5–6 hours** after a large pre-workout meal — split the difference and call it a **4–5 hour
bracket**, which matters more for **leaner lifters, people on lower calories, and high-volume
bodybuilding-style training.**

| Meal | Target |
|---|---|
| **Pre-workout**, 1–1.5 h out | **1 g/kg carbs** (low-GI **plus a serving of fruit**), **0.5 g/kg protein**, **0–20 g fat** |
| **Intra-workout** (optional) | **0.5 g carbs per minute of training from ~30 min in** — 30 g across 90 minutes |
| **Post-workout** | **0.5 g/kg protein**, **1–1.5 g/kg carbs** (**~0.5 g/kg** cutting) |

**Fat is the timing lever** — less the closer the meal sits to the session. Intra-workout carbs are
only worth it if you **train fasted, are lean and in a deficit, train over an hour, or are elite**.
Post-workout carbs are least urgent: typical resistance training depletes glycogen by only
**36–39%**, replenished within **24–48 hours** as long as you eat carbs at some point.

**One study pushes back on all of this and deserves flagging rather than burying**
([post-contest recovery and peri-workout protein](increasing-testosterone-after-competing.md)).
Dudgeon 2017, 16 trained males in a deficit: **28 g whey pre- and post-workout versus 28 g carbs.
Body fat was lost only in the protein group; the carb group lost lean body mass** — despite both
groups already eating **2.6 and 3.0 g/kg**. That gap is implausible as the cause, so Helms's
hypothesis is a genuine **timing** effect, and he notes **19 of the 25 studies** in the 2013
meta-analysis were on **untrained** subjects. Small study, unproven mechanism — but if you are
trained, lean and cutting, placing protein around training is cheap insurance.

The same note covers **post-contest**, which people get wrong in the opposite direction. Trexler
followed 15 natural competitors for 4–6 weeks. **Metabolic rate recovered once weight and fat were
regained** — regaining is the fix. **There is no post-contest anabolic window**: at one week, **2 kg
gained with no change in body fat percentage** (water accompanying a glycogen refill); by 4–6 weeks,
**2 kg of fat and no further lean mass.** And **testosterone recovery is impaired by regaining too
fast *and* too slow** — recovery was *smaller* in men who gained more fat, and a **1,200 kcal surplus
for 4 weeks made testosterone decrease.**

### Diets as systems

Four named systems, one shared conclusion: they work by controlling calories, and the best diet is
the one you adhere to.

**Keto — busted as a fat-loss advantage**
([the ketogenic diet](ketogenic-diet-science-behind-low-carb-keto.md)). Both mechanisms fail: the
**insulin hypothesis** has been experimentally falsified as too simplistic, and the **metabolic
advantage** has no direct experimental support. What keto does is **suppress appetite and eliminate
food categories**. A **2014 JAMA meta-analysis of 48 diets** found differences small and likely
unimportant; **Bueno's meta-analysis** of sub-50 g studies found a significant edge of **about 1 kg**,
which the authors called clinically insignificant. The confound is that keto groups usually eat more
protein — and **Soenen's 132-subject, four-arm, calorie-matched trial settles it: at one year, no
difference between normal-carb and low-carb when protein was matched, and high protein beat normal
protein in both carb conditions.** With calories and protein matched, Wilson found similar strength
and power gains, though Burke's work suggests fat-adaptation **may impair shorter high-intensity
performance.** Macros if you run it: **~5% carbs / 20% protein / 75% fat, under 50 g carbs/day.**

**Intermittent fasting — valid but not superior**
([intermittent fasting](science-behind-intermittent-fasting.md)). The literature studies
**alternate-day fasting** and **time-restricted feeding** (16–20 h fast, 4–8 h window). The
spectacular results are **rodent** results, unreplicated in humans. **The largest systematic review,
40 studies, found fasting suppressed hunger better than continuous restriction but produced no
significantly better weight loss or body composition.** Muscle-loss fears are unfounded — **40 hours
of fasting produced no significant atrophy**, and a **24 h fast reduces liver glycogen by less than
half while sparing muscle glycogen entirely.** The one real argument against it is **protein
distribution**. Obese populations respond better than lean, and the **Canadian Paediatric Society
classifies fasting and meal-skipping as unhealthy for adolescents.**

**Vegan diets — a bioavailability problem, not an impossibility**
([vegan diet science](vegan-diet-science.md)). Neither Nippard nor Patel claims veganism is
health-superior: **Epic-Oxford found no mortality difference**, the **Adventist study found ~10–15%
lower mortality in vegetarians**, and the flaw in the 24-study review favouring vegan is **the
control group** — these compare "eats meat" to "doesn't," and vegetarian-vs-Mediterranean has never
been run. For the lifter: **above 1.8 g/kg protein**, **pea + rice** blends, **rotate brands**
(contamination, not phytoestrogens), and whole-foods-only means **four to six meals a day** —
**100 g quinoa is 120 kcal for only 4.4 g protein**, so matching a 20 g animal dose takes **over
1,000 calories** in one feeding. Supplement **B12** unconditionally; **zinc is Patel's number-one
deficiency** in vegans and vegetarians; check **iodine** if you swapped iodised salt for pink salt.
**Calcium is overrated** — reading ~300 studies for the 2009–10 vitamin D guidelines he found **no
link between calcium alone and osteoporosis prevention or bone density.** **Iron is the opposite
problem**: eat as little as you can while staying non-anaemic.

**Detox diets — busted, and occasionally dangerous**
([detox diets](detox-diets-work-fat-loss-health.md)). Three strikes: **the toxins are never named**
(which is exactly what keeps the claims untestable); "chemicals" is not a category of harm, and the
liver, kidneys, skin, lymphatic system and GI tract are already self-cleansing; and a review of
commercial detox diets found **no compelling evidence** for toxin elimination or weight management,
recommending health professionals **discourage** them. The concrete danger is **forced
overhydration** — a documented case of seizures from hyponatremia, with some plans prescribing up to
**60 litres**. Worth keeping: **POPs and heavy metals are the one class not readily cleared by the
liver and kidneys**, and so the only plausible candidates for a real dietary detox — but that work is
mostly animal data.

### Specific foods and food quality

**Milk — busted as harmful** ([the science of milk](science-milk.md)). A **2017 meta-analysis of 29
studies and over 700,000 participants found no association between dairy intake and all-cause
mortality, coronary heart disease or cardiovascular disease.** He handles the funding problem
properly: that study was dairy-industry funded, so he checks replication — **a 2015 meta-analysis
with no conflicts found the same.** On cancer, a 2016 review found **four cancers positively
impacted, several neutral, only prostate cancer showing a possible adverse signal.** All of it
correlational. Both weak anti-dairy arguments fail: "unnatural" is an appeal to nature, and milk
isn't acid-forming — systemic pH isn't influenced by diet. **Lactose intolerance is the legitimate
one: 65–75% of people have some degree.** Acne evidence is thin and points at fluid milk, **not
yogurt or cheese**.

**Red and processed meat — dose and heat, not the category**
([Kamal Patel interview](interview-kamal-patel-examine-com.md), [vegan diet
science](vegan-diet-science.md)). The WHO figure is **relative**: **50 g processed meat daily = 18%
higher colorectal cancer risk**, against a ~4–5% baseline, so it moves to about 5–5.9% — **an
absolute increase of about 1%**, for 50 g every day across a lifetime. The mechanisms are
**N-nitroso compounds** from heating nitrate-preserved meat and **heterocyclic amines** from
open-flame cooking — **it's not processing per se, it's usually the high heat.** Nitrates themselves
aren't the villain; they're likely why leafy greens and beets are good for you. Mitigation:
**cruciferous vegetables, herbs and spices** alongside meat. Patel's line: **"eat meat as much as you
want; eat processed meat on a special occasion — just don't make a habit of it."** The speculative
worry is **Neu5Gc**, highest in red meat, absent in chicken and fish, with **Hashimoto's patients
showing 7× higher levels** but rheumatoid arthritis patients not — narrow recommendation, consider
avoiding red meat **with a family history of autoimmune conditions.**

**Eggs — genuinely unresolved, and he says so** ([vegan diet science](vegan-diet-science.md)).
Favourable: a large 2018 RCT in **diabetics, 12+ eggs/week vs 1–2, found no difference in any
cardiometabolic risk factor.** Conflicting: a meta-analysis two years earlier found **more heart
failure at higher intake** — observational, but large, and a **hard outcome** where the RCT measured
only intermediate markers. **The reconciliation is dose: neither study had a moderate group.** Five
or ten a week is almost certainly safe; three or four a day he isn't sure about. **Dietary
cholesterol was debunked decades ago**, and **only ~1–2% of yolk cholesterol oxidises**. Patel
concedes the sharpest criticism of egg defenders: **don't argue dietary cholesterol, argue saturated
fat.**

**Superfoods — the word is marketing, some of the foods still earn their place**
([kiwi fruit](kiwi-fruit-one-true-superfood.md), [5 superfoods](5-superfoods-recently-added-diet.md)).
"Superfood" has no scientific definition, and building meals around hero ingredients makes eating
**more** complicated, not healthier. The kiwi video is a deliberate joke at superfood hype whose
nutrition data happens to be accurate: kiwi beats apples, oranges, bananas, strawberries and
blueberries on **total fibre, vitamin C, vitamin E and vitamin K** and carries **~2× the vitamin C of
oranges** — yet it's **~0.2% of global fruit consumption.** Best-supported uses: **constipation**
(fibre plus **actinidin**) and **sleep — 2 kiwis before bed for four weeks improved sleep onset,
duration and quality.** Eat the peel. The other four: **blueberries 0.5–1 cup/day**; **garlic**,
liberally or **600–1500 mg/day** (lower total cholesterol over 2+ months across 39 trials);
**mussels or scallops 1–2×/week** (**18 g protein per 150 g at 129 kcal, ~7% leucine — on par with
chicken and beef**, plus ~1 g omega-3 and zinc); and **leafy greens daily**, where **400 mg of
beetroot nitrates for six days increased total reps and weight lifted on bench press.** One rule
across all: **take antioxidants from food, not pills.**

**Fresh vs frozen, and cooking method — both mostly busted**
([fresh vs frozen](fresh-vs-frozen-fruits-vegetables.md), [does cooking affect
nutrition](cooking-affect-nutrition-food.md)). Fresh-versus-frozen comparisons mostly show no
significant difference, and where they do they run **both ways**; the finding that matters is that
**five days of refrigerated storage lowers nutrient content**, so "fresh" that's sat in your fridge
can be worse than frozen. In the US, **canned and frozen produce collectively contribute more to
total nutrient intake than fresh.** On cooking, results across **13 frozen vegetables** were
nutrient- and vegetable-specific — spinach beta-carotene **rose 24% with steaming**, sliced carrots'
**12% with microwaving** — with **one consistent finding: boiling retained the least vitamin C in
every vegetable examined.** Microwaving is safe. Rotate methods; put the effort into **variety**.

**Apple cider vinegar — mostly busted**
([apple cider vinegar](apple-cider-vinegar-s-all-hype-about.md)). The one well-supported effect —
blunting glucose and insulin, per a **2017 meta-analysis of 11 studies** — comes from the acetic
acid, so **plain vinegar works just as well**, and it's **backwards**: it inhibits **starch**
digestion, so it helps with complex carbs and does essentially nothing for simple sugars. The one
decent weight study — **175 obese subjects, 12 weeks, 15 or 30 ml daily** — found **about 2 kg lost
at the high dose, all regained at 4-week follow-up**, on self-reported intake. The 2013 satiety
finding was driven by vinegar making people **nauseous**. **Don't put it on your teeth.**

**Energy drinks — the scary ingredient list is not the problem**
([are energy drinks bad](energy-drinks-bad.md)). Dose by dose, most ingredients do nothing: **B
vitamins** supply none of the claimed energy, **guarana** is caffeine at too low a dose,
**L-carnitine's** effective dose is **60× what's in a can**, and you'd need **~32 cans to reach the
Ace-K ADI, ~6 for sucralose**. **Taurine's** lifetime upper limit is **3 g/day** against ~1 g per can.
Two ingredients matter: **sugar**, avoidable by buying diet, and **caffeine — cap total daily intake
at 400 mg (Health Canada)**, counting the drink *and* your pre-workout and coffee, when one can
carries **150 mg+**. **Over 79,000 emergency room visits between 2005 and 2011** were attributed to
these products, **many involving alcohol** — never combine them. **Pure caffeine powder is the
genuinely dangerous form: two teaspoons can be lethal.**

**Breakfast — busted**
([is breakfast the most important meal](breakfast-most-important-meal-day.md)). The claim traces to a
1917 *Good Health* article with no evidence offered, edited at the time by **John Harvey Kellogg**,
inventor of corn flakes. Betts's data: **resting metabolic rate is identical** between eaters and
skippers; skippers burn **fewer calories through spontaneous activity**, and overeat later by only
**~161 calories after skipping a 700-calorie breakfast — leaving them 539 calories/day lower.** The
epidemiology is thoroughly confounded. **The one clear case: if you train in the morning and that
day's performance matters, a carb-rich breakfast genuinely is the most important meal.** **Children
and adolescents should not be skipping meals.**

**"Everything in moderation" smuggles the good-food/bad-food split back in**
([addressing "everything in moderation"](addressing-everything-moderation.md)). Look at the structure
of its everyday use: "you can have some high-GI carbs *as long as it's in moderation*", "*treat
yourself* to ice cream." Food X needs no qualifier; food Y gets one. That asymmetry is a moral
ranking dressed as pragmatism. His alternative is **inclusive** framing: hit your calories and
macros, **3–4 servings of vegetables, 2–3 of fruit, 1–2 of dairy, your fibre goal — then fill the
remainder with foods you enjoy.** He then flags his own earlier "20% of the diet can be junk food"
rule as **arbitrary**, noting the hole under any such rule: **there is no clear definition of "junk
food."**

**What a rat obesity study can and can't tell you**
([junk food makes rats fat](junk-food-makes-rats-fat.md)). The headline said junk food makes rats
lose their appetite for a balanced diet; the finding was narrower — a **cafeteria diet impaired
sensory-specific satiety.** Chow-fed rats stopped responding to cues for a food they'd eaten to
satiety; after two weeks on cookies and cake, they **kept responding.** **But even granting full
human transfer, this changes only the calories-in side — there is not a shred of evidence that any
food causes fat gain above and beyond its excess calories.** The correction is environmental, not
prohibitionist: **a 2002 study found rigid dieters had worse mood disturbance, higher body weight and
more eating-disorder symptoms**, and later work found **rigid control inversely related to dieting
success while flexible control was positively associated with it.** So keep junk food in the house to
a minimum, **buy single-serving packages**, and treat flexibility as a feature.

**Budget — $8.92 a day builds the same muscle as $10,000**
([getting jacked for $10/day](get-jacked-10-day.md)). Cheapest protein: **lentils, milk, chicken
breast** — protein powder ranks sixth. Cheapest carbs: **white rice, brown rice, oats**; cheapest
fats: **sunflower seeds and peanut butter**. Buy vegetables **frozen or canned**. **5 g of creatine
costs 15 cents** in bulk, and **plain coffee at ~25 cents works as well as a multi-ingredient
pre-workout** — a 2022 study found caffeine alone matched a full formula. Against a Michelin-trained
chef, a $700/hr coach and a spa: **identical macros produce identical gains.** The chef's benefit was
that macro-friendly food tasted better, which is an **adherence** benefit. The one place he saw real
value was **coaching**.

### The worst nutrition mistakes lifters make

Four, from the 2023 video ([worst nutrition mistakes](worst-nutrition-mistakes-every-lifter-makes.md)).

1. **Thinking you can force-feed muscle growth.** Garthe split **47 elite athletes** into large- and
   small-surplus groups on the same program for 10 weeks. The large-surplus group ate **621 more
   calories per day**, gained more total weight and **a tiny bit more lean mass that did not reach
   statistical significance** — and substantially more fat. **Nutrition is permissive: training causes
   growth, food merely permits it.** Targets: **10–20% surplus for faster gain with some fat, 5–10%
   for a leaner bulk, a slight deficit to prioritise fat loss while still building.**
2. **Overrating supplements.** **Creatine is worth roughly 2–3 lb of lean mass** — real, cheap, worth
   taking, and then compare it to a steroid cycle adding 10–20 lb in a month. **Caffeine's** biggest
   effect may be behavioural. **Protein powder is a convenient food, not magic.**
3. **Thinking you need very high protein.** The break point is **0.7 g/lb**, and this is a position he
   revised **downward**.
4. **Misunderstanding meal frequency.** Don't force six to eight meals to speed up your metabolism —
   it doesn't — and don't grind through fasting because it burns belly fat — it doesn't.

### What actually matters, in order

Nutrition's hierarchy is far shorter than the volume of argument around it suggests.

1. **Calories**, set to your goal — the whole of part one, and the mechanism every named diet above
   secretly operates through.
2. **Protein**, **0.7–1.0 g/lb** in a surplus, **0.8–1.2 g/lb** in a deficit. The one macro with a
   target worth defending to the gram.
3. **Adherence** — why keto, fasting and flexible dieting all tie in matched trials, and why the diet
   you'll still be running in six months beats the optimal one you'll quit.
4. **Food environment and food quality.** Not moral categories, just the practical fact that
   hyper-palatable, always-available, energy-dense food gets eaten in quantities you didn't choose.
   Whole foods, enough produce, enough fibre, single-serving packages.
5. **Protein distribution** — roughly **3–5 feedings of 20 g+**, plus **40 g pre-sleep** if your
   overnight gap is long. Worth a few percent.
6. **Nutrient timing** — keep pre- and post-training meals inside **4–5 hours**. The only timing rule
   that has to hold, and it matters most if you're lean, cutting or training hard.
7. **Everything else** — cooking method, fresh versus frozen, superfoods, vinegar, detoxes,
   breakfast. Rounding errors, or nothing at all.

The failure mode is the same as in training: people litigate items 5–7 while 1–3 are still unfixed.
And the errors in the material they're litigating from are consistent enough to check mechanically —
extrapolating from extreme doses, mistaking correlation for causation, and blaming one nutrient for a
multi-factor outcome. **Ask what dose, ask which population, and ask whether calories were
controlled.** Most nutrition arguments die on one of those three questions.
