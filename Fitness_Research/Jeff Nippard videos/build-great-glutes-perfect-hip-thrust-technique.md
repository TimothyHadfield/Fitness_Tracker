# Hip Thrust Technique for Glute Growth

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Build Great Glutes with Perfect Hip Thrust Technique (Fix Mistakes!)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xDmFkJxPzeM)) · 2019-02-05 · 7 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- **8-15 reps for hypertrophy; 4-8 reps if the goal is strength.**
- The hip thrust loads the glutes at and beyond neutral hip position — where squats and deadlifts lose tension — which is likely why it outperforms the squat for glute growth.
- Lock the hips out fully every rep; hips must finish **above the knees**. Failure to lock out is the most common error.
- Posteriorly tilt the pelvis at the top by crunching the abs slightly. Extending the lower back instead of the glutes is the second most common error.
- Control the eccentric to about **2 seconds** — the temptation to drop the weight is unusually strong here.

## Why the hip thrust
It trains almost pure **hip extension** (hips moving from flexed to extended). Adding a hip circle or glute loop around the knees also brings in **hip abduction**, hitting the upper and side aspects of the glutes.

Two qualities make it unusual: a huge capacity for overload, and an excellent basis for building a mind-muscle connection with the glutes. Overload capacity is illustrated by Bret Contreras hip thrusting over 700 lb and Katy Sonnier doing over 500 lb for reps at 120 lb body weight.

**Research:** Contreras and colleagues (2015) found the hip thrust produced higher glute EMG activation than the back squat. This was followed by the first hip thrust twin case study: over six weeks, the twin who hip thrusted gained significantly more upper *and* lower glute growth than the twin who squatted. The likely mechanism is that the hip thrust loads the glutes at and past neutral hip position, whereas vertically loaded lifts like the squat and deadlift shed tension exactly where the glutes reach full hip extension.

## Setup [01:33]
The main downside of the exercise is the setup. Specialty equipment (the Hip Thruster) makes it easier; without it, three steps:
1. Find a bench matching your **tibia height** — a decline bench often works — and brace it against a rack, wall, or other stable support so it can't slide back.
2. Wrap a thick pad or towel around the bar.
3. Load the bar in front of the bench.

Position yourself between bench and bar with the bar centred on your hips and the pad centred on the bar. Upper back against the bench. Stance slightly wider than shoulder width with about **15 degrees of foot flare**. While warming up light, adjust foot and hip placement so that tibia and femur form a **90-degree angle at the top of each rep**.

Start light and progress gradually — don't let form slip chasing PRs.

## Execution [02:35]
- **Before the thrust:** flex the glutes, then focus on squeezing them to drive the weight straight up. Unlike the squat, don't think about driving the feet into the ground — just think about fully extending the hips until the weight can't go higher. You should feel an extremely strong glute contraction.
- **Gaze straight ahead, not up.** Chin and rib cage tucked down.
- **At the top:** squeeze the glutes together as hard as possible — as if holding a coin between your butt cheeks. Simultaneously **posteriorly rotate the pelvis** by contracting the abs slightly. This pulls the glutes into full hip extension and prevents the lower back from hyperextending instead.
- **Hands** stabilise the bar against side-to-side wobble only — don't use the arms to jack the weight up.
- **If you feel it in the quads:** try shifting the feet further forward. For some people this shifts emphasis onto the hamstrings instead, so experiment with stance width, hip position and foot flare to find what suits your proportions.
- **Tempo:** for hypertrophy you can use a **1-3 second pause** at the top as an intensity technique. When training for strength, a quick squeeze at lockout, then straight into the negative. Lower under control over roughly **2 seconds** as the glutes stretch under load.

## Common errors [04:07]
**1. Failing to fully lock out the hips.** Can come from using too much weight, but more often people avoid lockout simply because it burns. If you aren't feeling that burn in the glutes at the top, you almost certainly aren't fully extending. Hips must be above knees on every rep.

**2. Extending the lower back instead of the glutes.** Fix with posterior pelvic tilt: crunch the abs, keep the chin down.

**Self-test [05:09]:** deliberately arch your lower back and squeeze your glutes as hard as you can. Then contract your abs to flatten the lower back and squeeze again. The glute contraction is noticeably stronger with a flat lower back. That's the position you want at lockout.

## Alternatives and variations
Jeff isn't sure a truly equivalent substitute exists.
- **Leg extension machine hip thrust** — workable if using higher reps.
- **Glute bridge** — same basic movement with the upper back on the floor rather than a bench, which limits the range of motion. Good when short on time; probably the next best thing.
- **Single-leg hip thrust** and **knee-banded hip thrust** — worth including; the band challenges the glutes through hip abduction, hitting the side and upper glutes more.

## Practical application
- Hypertrophy: 8-15 reps. Strength: 4-8 reps.
- Bench at tibia height, braced; feet set so tibia and femur are at 90 degrees at lockout; stance slightly wider than shoulder width with ~15 degrees of flare.
- Every rep: pre-flex glutes → drive up by squeezing glutes → hips above knees → abs crunched, pelvis posteriorly tilted, chin down → squeeze glutes together → lower over ~2 seconds.
- Add a 1-3 second top pause as an intensity technique on hypertrophy work.
- Rotate in banded and single-leg variations to bias the upper/side glutes via abduction.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The twin case study is a single-pair, six-week case study — suggestive rather than conclusive.
- The tension-at-lockout explanation for the hip thrust's advantage is Jeff's proposed mechanism, not a tested finding.
- Foot placement fixes for quad-dominant feel are individual and require trial and error.
