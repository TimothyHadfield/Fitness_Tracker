# Front Squat Technique: Bigger Quads and a Stronger Back Squat

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "HOW TO FRONT SQUAT: Build Bigger Quads & A Stronger Squat" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v-mQm_droHg)) · 2019-03-26 · 8 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- The front squat's defining feature is the upright torso forced by the bar sitting in front of the body; that shifts work toward the quads and dramatically onto the upper back.
- You will usually fail a front squat because your traps and rhomboids can no longer hold the upper spine extended — not because the legs gave out. That makes it the single best accessory for raw back squat strength.
- Load it in the 6-8 rep zone. Heavier and upper back failure ruins form; much lighter/higher reps taxes the cardiovascular system more than the muscles.
- Olympic (clean) grip is the first choice; straps are a temporary workaround, cross grip a distant third.
- Sit straight down, not back. Keep elbows up, chest up, and lead with the chest and elbows on the way out.

## Why it differs from the back squat
Any squat trains simultaneous knee extension (quads) and hip extension (glutes). Using a figure from Bret Contreras's biomechanics book, Nippard points out that in both variations the bar must stay centred over the mid-foot. With the bar racked in front, the only way to keep that balance is a much more upright back angle — the back squat lifter leans forward, the front squat lifter stays vertical. That single difference produces slightly more quad demand and much greater upper back demand.

A 2015 study from Contreras and colleagues found the front squat delivers a similar training stimulus to the back squat despite requiring lighter loads. Practical upshot: comparable hypertrophic stimulus at lower absolute loading, which may reduce cumulative injury risk.

## Rack position and grip
**Olympic grip (recommended).** Bar rests just behind the shoulders, actually touching the front of the neck — if it feels like you're being slightly choked, it's probably right. Don't hold it in the palm; let it sit on the first two or three fingers, hands just outside the shoulders. Drive the elbows up until the humerus is parallel to the floor; from the front the elbows point straight ahead.

**Strap technique.** Loop straps over the bar and grip the tails, letting the elbows stay high with limited wrist/shoulder mobility. Treat as short term — work on wrist and shoulder mobility so you can eventually drop the aid.

**Cross grip.** Same bar position against the neck, arms out front, crossed over the top of the bar. Workable, but the bar is less balanced and stable and elbows are harder to keep up as fatigue accumulates, which can pull you forward. Fine as long as you're not training close to failure.

## Execution [04:07]
1. Exaggerate cranking the elbows up and forward during setup until the upper arms reach parallel.
2. Unrack by driving the hips forward, elbows staying up. Take three steps back.
3. Feet slightly wider than shoulder-width with 15-30 degrees of toe flare — wide enough to be comfortable, not so wide that depth suffers or the hips shoot back.
4. Big diaphragmatic breath, held on the descent. Before descending, push slightly up on the bar and extend the upper back by thinking "chest up."
5. Initiate by sitting straight down, breaking at knees and hips together (or knees very slightly first). Sitting back drops the elbows and destroys balance.
6. Push the knees forward and slightly out in line with the toes. Expect more forward knee travel than in a back squat — that is fine; "knees past toes" is a myth (he has a separate video on it).
7. Hit at least parallel — the front squat makes depth easier, so there's no excuse not to break parallel.
8. No pause at the bottom: use the stretch reflex, reversing immediately. Press big toe, little toe and heel evenly into the floor while driving the bar up, leading with chest and elbows.
9. Drive the hips forward on the ascent. At a sticking point, think about forcing the hips under the bar while driving the knees out — without pushing the knees past the toe direction.
10. Re-establish the upright rack position at the top, re-brace, repeat.

## Common errors
**Elbows dropping.** Feels natural when new and gets worse with fatigue. Fix: elbows up before you unrack, forward or slightly upward gaze, and a deliberate effort to hold them up during the descent (when they're most likely to fall).

**Heels lifting.** Can come from chest/elbows dropping and the centre of gravity shifting forward, or from poor ankle mobility. Fixes: light foam rolling and dynamic stretching for calves/ankles before squatting, static calf stretching after; more toe flare (especially if you point straight ahead); a heel-elevated lifting shoe, which reduces the required ankle plantarflexion range. Caution: don't over-correct into "weight on the heels," which makes you sit back. Aim for even pressure triangulated across all three foot contact points.

## Practical application
- Program front squats in the 6-8 rep range as a quad-focused main lift and as squat-specific upper back assistance.
- Prioritise the Olympic grip; use straps only while mobility catches up.
- Cue: elbows up, chest up, sit straight down, break parallel, bounce out of the hole, lead with the chest.
- If you can't front squat at all: machine hack squat is the best hypertrophy substitute (upright torso, quad dominant) but has far less carryover to the back squat because the upper back isn't loaded. Goblet squats are another substitute but will need higher reps once dumbbell weight becomes the limiter.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The Contreras 2015 comparison shows a similar stimulus, not a superior one — the front squat's edge is mostly in its transfer to squat weak points and lower absolute loading, not raw hypertrophy.
- The claim that lighter loading "might reduce injury risk over time" is offered as a reasonable inference, not a demonstrated finding.
