# The Most Scientific Way to Train Back: Grip, Rows, Shrugs and the EMG Evidence

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Scientific Way to Train Back (10 Studies Explained) | Training VLOG" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NDmJNX9JrLs)) · 2016-09-07 · 13 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- The EMG-optimal lat pulldown is a **pronated (overhand) grip, pulled to the front of the neck, at roughly 1.5× biacromial width**, with a small (~11°) lean back.
- Rows belong in every back routine: they match pulldowns for lat activation and beat them for traps and rhomboids.
- Train the lat through both of its functions — shoulder **adduction** (wide pulldowns) and shoulder **extension** (close neutral-grip pulldowns, rows).
- Shrugs are worth keeping despite the anatomical argument against them; upper-trap EMG data supports them.
- Biceps are heavily pre-fatigued by a long back session — train them first, on their own day, or accept reduced output at the end.

## Warm-up and pre-activation [02:56]
The session opens with **one-arm lat pull-ins** as a pre-activation movement: light work to warm the lats and establish a mind-muscle connection so the lats are more active in the heavier exercises that follow. Jeff deliberately **laterally flexes the spine** (crunching to the side) during these, because the lats act synergistically to laterally flex the spine. He recommends experimenting with how much side-crunch lets you feel the lats most.

## Lat pulldown: what the studies say about grip [04:00]
Three papers, taken together, define the optimal setup.

**Lusk et al., 2009** — four conditions: wide pronated, wide supinated, narrow pronated, narrow supinated. (Auto-captions render the name as "Lal"; likely Lusk — treat as uncertain.) Finding: **regardless of grip width, the pronated/overhand grip produced more lat activation** than supinated. Conclusion: overhand beats underhand for the lats.

**Signorile et al., 2002** — compared several pulldown variations: in front of the neck, behind the neck, neutral grip, and others. Finding: the **wide grip to the front of the neck** was best for lat EMG. Conclusion: pulling in front of the neck beats behind the neck.

**Andersen et al., 2014** — three pronated grip widths defined by **biacromial distance** (the distance from the outer edge of one shoulder to the outer edge of the other): 1×, 1.5×, and 2×. Findings:
- All three widths were **similar on the concentric**.
- **1.5× and 2× were best on the eccentric** for lat activation.
- **Biceps were most activated at 1.5×.**
- Conclusion: the **medium grip (1.5× biacromial)** optimises lats and biceps together.

**Synthesis:** pronated grip, ~1.5× biacromial width, bar pulled to the front of the neck.

## Cheating and torso lean [06:02]
An article Jeff drew these studies from referenced a paper finding that an **11° lean back** recruited the lats better than staying completely upright. So a slight amount of "cheating" on the pulldown is actually beneficial — provided you aren't generating the rep with momentum.

## Close neutral-grip pulldown: the other lat function [06:32]
The wide pulldown trains the lat primarily through **shoulder adduction**. The close neutral-grip version brings the elbows down toward the front of the body, training **shoulder extension** — the lat's other primary function. Extra benefits: slightly more range of motion, and more trap and rhomboid involvement, especially if you squeeze the shoulder blades together.

## Rows: chest-supported T-bar [07:03]
Chest support gives the back extra support and mechanically prevents cheating.

**Lehman et al., 2004** (captions: "liem") — found rows to be **just as effective as lat pulldowns for lat recruitment, and better for trap and rhomboid activation**. Jeff's takeaway: for bang-for-buck, having a row in your routine is an absolute must.

## Cable kneeling row variation [07:33]
Cables set low, forearm braced against the knee. He reports a strong lat stretch and a large range of motion, and notes the cable gives **more constant tension through the curve** than a dumbbell. Execution: ~10 reps half-kneeling, then finish the set **lying down** for another 6-10 reps, which produces a very strong lat contraction.

## Shrugs: the anatomical objection and the rebuttal [08:33]
Smith machine shrugs, which also take pressure off the lower back.

**The objection — Johnson et al., 1994:** upper trap fibres run **transversely**, not vertically as previously assumed, and don't attach at the scapula, so they can't function as scapular elevators. The inference some people draw is that shrugs barely involve the upper traps.

**Jeff's rebuttal:** he doesn't find this a strong argument. **Andersen et al., 2008**, citing three other studies showing the same, found **shrugs produced the greatest upper-trap EMG activity of all the exercises tested**. Also: even if shrugs weren't hitting the upper traps specifically, they still hit other scapular elevators such as the **levator scapulae**. Given that programs already include rows and lateral raises, adding a low-risk shrug makes sense — and he can plainly feel his traps contracting when he shrugs.

## Full-range lateral raises for traps [09:33]
An unorthodox movement Jeff picked up from Ian McCarthy, who he believes got it from Menno Henselmans (uncertain). Taking the lateral raise through a full range of motion produced one of the best upper-trap "burns" he'd felt in a long time, with a very strong squeeze at the top. **Go very light and be careful** — he flags shoulder impingement as a real risk.

## Chest-supported incline shrug [10:34]
A movement Jeff says he invented: chest braced on an incline support, shrugging **up and back** simultaneously. He finds it effective for mid and upper traps from a different angle than the upright shrug, and it completely eliminates the cheating that's endemic to shrugs — going up on the toes, or ducking under the bar to get it moving.

## Biceps placement [11:05]
Rows and pulldowns already work the biceps heavily, so by the end of a long back session it's hard to give arm work full effort. Jeff's usual solutions are to train biceps **at the beginning of the session** or **on their own day**. In this workout he deliberately put back first, because he was prioritising back after a couple of months off from training it due to injury, then finished with **4 sets of 10-12 alternating dumbbell curls per arm**.

## Practical application
A back session built from this video's logic:
1. One-arm lat pull-ins — pre-activation, light, with slight lateral spine flexion.
2. Lat pulldown — pronated, ~1.5× biacromial, to the front of the neck, ~11° lean back.
3. Close neutral-grip pulldown — shoulder extension, squeeze the scapulae.
4. Chest-supported T-bar row — lats plus traps and rhomboids, no cheating.
5. Kneeling cable row — 10 half-kneeling, then 6-10 lying.
6. Smith machine shrug.
7. Full-range lateral raise — very light.
8. Chest-supported incline shrug — up and back.
9. Biceps last only if back is the priority; otherwise move them earlier.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Nearly all of this rests on **acute EMG activation**, not measured hypertrophy. Higher EMG is a proxy, not proof of more growth.
- Several researcher names are garbled by auto-captions: "Lal" (likely **Lusk**, 2009) and "liem" (likely **Lehman**, 2004) — both flagged as uncertain. Signorile 2002, Andersen 2014, Johnson 1994 and Andersen 2008 are stated clearly.
- The 11° lean-back finding is second-hand: Jeff cites an article that referenced the paper, not the paper itself.
- The full-range lateral raise carries impingement risk and comes from a secondhand attribution chain (Ian McCarthy → possibly Menno Henselmans).
- The chest-supported incline shrug is Jeff's own invention with no supporting data.
