# Is Training Volume Killing Your Gains? Per-Session Volume Thresholds

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Is Workout Volume Actually Killing Your Gains? (Athlean-X Response)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mja2fDwYA5s)) · 2019-11-30 · 9 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- Weekly volume and hypertrophy follow a dose-response curve up to a point, with diminishing returns for most people somewhere around **10-20 hard sets per muscle per week**.
- A study by Barbalho and colleagues found the *opposite* — 5 sets/week beat 10, 15 and 20, and the 20-set group actually lost pec size — but the design crammed every set into a single weekly session, all taken to failure.
- The likely explanation is a **per-session** volume ceiling, possibly around 5 sets per muscle per workout, on top of the weekly one.
- Volume is only "junk" when effort is too low, or when it's piled into one session and overwhelms recovery.
- His preferred setup: moderate-to-high weekly volume, spread over 2+ sessions per muscle, at 1-3 reps in reserve with occasional failure.

## What the evidence said before this study
A 2017 meta-analysis pooling 34 treatment groups across 15 studies established a clear dose-response relationship: more volume produces more hypertrophy. But there is a point of diminishing returns where extra sets buy very little, and it is highly individual — probably in the region of 10-20 tough working sets per muscle per week for most people and most muscles. Past that, there is a further point where you may lose muscle to overtraining. That curve is the prevailing model.

Jeff Cavaliere's argument, which Nippard partly endorses, is that fixation on volume has let some trainees stop exerting themselves — optimising set counts as a way of avoiding hard effort.

## The Barbalho study
40 young men with at least 3 years of training experience, split into four groups doing 5, 10, 15 or 20 sets per muscle per week. Same compound exercises for every group; the only variable was set count. Push/pull/legs, but only **three days per week**, so each muscle was hit once weekly (push Monday, pull Thursday, legs Friday) — effectively a bro split. All sets were taken to failure. After six months, the 5-set group grew the most and the 20-set group grew the least, with the 20-set group losing some pec size.

Two things reconcile this with the existing model:

1. **The subjects weren't advanced.** Despite three years of training, their lower-body strength suggests otherwise. Research indicates more advanced lifters benefit from more volume, and less-trained lifters need less.
2. **The per-session threshold.** In the MASS review article that prompted the video, Greg Nuckols references rodent research on muscle protein synthesis within a single workout, where the response appears to cap out around **five sets**. Assume something in that region per body part per session: doing 15 hard chest sets in one workout means work quality collapses partway through — sloppier form, less weight, less focus — while splitting the same 15 sets across three weekly sessions preserves the quality of every set and leaves you less wrecked.

The 20-set group in Barbalho was doing all 20 sets in one session, to failure. So the study doesn't overturn the volume model; it adds a second axis to it — how much volume per *session*, not just per week.

## Four volume × effort scenarios
- **High volume (20+ sets/week), low effort (5+ RIR):** kills gains. Most of it is junk volume — you never recruit the highest-threshold fibres, so you don't grow.
- **High volume, high effort (every set to failure), no volume adjustment:** also bad. Overtraining is very likely. This is the 20-set Barbalho group.
- **Low volume, very high effort (sets to failure):** works. A handful of all-out sets per muscle per week is a legitimate strategy for busy people wanting short workouts. Not optimal in his view, but it gets you most of the way and won't kill gains — provided intensity really is high.
- **Moderate-to-high volume, moderate effort (1-3 RIR, occasional failure), tuned to progress and recovery:** his preferred approach and, he argues, the best way to optimise growth.

The logic behind preferring the fourth: pulling back slightly on effort costs almost nothing in growth (the difference between failure and a couple of reps in reserve is very small either way), but it buys the capacity to do more *high-quality* volume, which — spaced and managed across the week — produces the best result.

## Practical application
- Aim for roughly 10-20 hard sets per muscle per week, adjusted down if you're less trained and up if you're advanced.
- Cap per-session volume for a given muscle — think in the region of ~5 sets per body part per workout — and split the weekly total across **at least two sessions**.
- Run most sets at 1-3 reps in reserve, with failure used occasionally rather than by default.
- If you must train low-volume (time constraints), intensity has to be near-maximal to compensate.
- Adjust volume based on assessed progress and recovery, not a fixed number.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Nobody knows exactly where the diminishing-returns point sits, and it may be too individual to ever pin down.
- The ~5-set per-session ceiling comes from rodent MPS data, and Nippard explicitly frames it as an assumption "for the sake of argument."
- Barbalho's subjects were nominally experienced but strength data suggests they weren't, limiting generalisation to advanced lifters.
- He acknowledges the topic is complex and the video may raise as many questions as it answers.
