# Is the Sumo Deadlift Cheating? What Competition Data and Biomechanics Say

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Is The Sumo Deadlift Cheating? (Response To CBum)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yRYkhPBljYk)) · 2022-04-25 · 10 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- Sumo is legal in every major powerlifting federation, tested and untested — there is no stance-width rule.
- If sumo were a genuine shortcut, elite lifters would all use it. They don't: most 93 kg IPF world-championship men pull conventional, whereas 100% of them arch on bench (an actual universal advantage).
- Sumo cuts barbell range of motion by only about 7–15% depending on limb length, and it does not skip the hardest part of the lift the way a half squat does.
- Biomechanically the two lifts are far more similar than assumed: identical hip extension demands and identical glute/hamstring activation. Sumo taxes the quads more; conventional taxes the spinal erectors more.
- Pick whichever you're stronger and more comfortable with, after giving each a genuine 1–2 month trial.

## The rules and the real claim
[01:33] Conventional = legs inside the hands (usually just inside shoulder width). Sumo = legs outside the hands. Both are executed via knee extension (quads) plus hip extension (glutes and hamstrings), with high forearm activation from gripping and high spinal erector activation resisting spinal flexion.

Under the IPF rulebook there is no stance-width rule and sumo is not cause for disqualification; this is standard across essentially all federations. So the real claim is not literal cheating but that sumo makes the lift *easier* — a shortcut analogous to a partial-range squat.

## The competition data argument
[02:03] At the IPF World Championships — the most incentivized elite lifters on earth — the majority of 93 kg (205 lb) men pull **conventional**, not sumo. Compare bench arching, another accused "cheat": **100%** of lifters at that meet used some arch, because arching genuinely helps almost everyone. Sumo shows no such universal adoption.

[03:05] Expanding across weight classes reveals a clear trend for men: roughly **75% pull sumo in the second-lightest class**, and that flips in the superheavyweights to about **25% sumo / 75% conventional**. Heavier lifters do worse with sumo. Female data is murkier — closer to 50/50 in the middle classes — but the same directional pattern holds at the extremes. Proposed explanations are mobility limitations, or a skeletal one: the biggest lifters are also the tallest, and taller lifters may have less favourable limb ratios for sumo.

## Range of motion
[05:11] Nippard measured bar travel himself: conventional **19 in**, sumo **16 in** — a 3 in / ~15% reduction. For a taller lifter at his gym: conventional **22.5 in**, sumo **21 in** — 1.5 in / ~7%. Less of a difference than expected.

Three counterpoints to the ROM objection:
1. **Shorter ROM ≠ easier.** Any small ROM reduction gets offset by loading more weight. His sumo ROM is 15% shorter but he lifts about 15% more weight sumo, so RPE ends up the same. If you don't get a strength boost from sumo, you simply don't load more.
2. **Which part of the range gets cut matters.** A half squat removes the hardest portion — nobody fails a squat in the top half. Sumo removes neither of the deadlift's two sticking points: people fail either off the floor or at lockout past the knees, and sumo still requires both.
3. **The logic isn't applied consistently.** Any bench grip wider than shoulder width shortens ROM; nobody calls anything but a close grip cheating. Same for wide-grip lat pulldowns. As long as you get sufficiently close to failure, a wider grip isn't considered cheating there.

Nippard adds that the latest evidence suggests hitting the hardest part of the range matters more than maximizing total range.

## Joint-level biomechanics
[07:48] Bar ROM is not joint ROM. With conventional, the knees start much less bent and go through relatively little knee extension — more like a half squat. With sumo the starting knee angle is far deeper, producing a **larger knee extension ROM**, so the quads work harder.

From a 2D view, conventional appears to use more hip ROM. But the hip is a ball-and-socket joint moving in three dimensions, so it needs 3D analysis. **Escamilla and colleagues** ran a 3D kinematic study and found **no significant difference in hip extension demands between sumo and conventional at any point in the range**. This is likely why EMG research shows **no difference in hamstring or glute activation** between the two.

The only real differences: quads work somewhat harder in sumo; spinal erectors work somewhat harder in conventional. That erector loading may explain why conventional *feels* subjectively harder — but "feels harder because your back is loaded more" is not evidence that it's objectively harder in terms of biomechanics, relative intensity, or total muscle activation.

## Practical application
- Rough starting heuristic: if you're **under ~180 lb / 81 kg as a male** or **under ~140 lb / 65 kg as a female**, you may well be better at sumo; heavier than that, conventional is more likely your stronger pull.
- Don't take the heuristic as gospel — try both. Technique takes time to learn, so give each style **at least 1–2 months** before judging.
- Then simply use whichever you're stronger with or more comfortable with. Or train both and get gradually stronger at each.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Why sumo suits lighter lifters is theorized (mobility, limb proportions, height) rather than established.
- Female competition data is less clear-cut than male data.
- The ROM measurements are two anecdotal cases (Nippard and a tall gym-goer), not a study.
