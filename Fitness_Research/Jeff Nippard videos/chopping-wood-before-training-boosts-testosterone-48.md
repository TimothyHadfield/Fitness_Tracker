# Acute Testosterone Spikes Don't Build Muscle (The Wood-Chopping Study)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Chopping Wood Before Training Boosts Testosterone 48% | Can We Apply This For Gains?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ymih3w7dtg0)) · 2019-03-11 · 5 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- No — don't chop wood before leg day. The study behind the claim isn't transferable, and the underlying premise is wrong anyway.
- Acute post-workout hormone spikes (testosterone, growth hormone, IGF-1) are too transient and stay within the physiological range, so they don't meaningfully drive muscle growth.
- What drives growth is progressive tension and progressive overload over time — not designing sessions around hormone spikes.
- Chronically raising a *low* baseline testosterone (say 300 to 500-600 ng/dL) via lifestyle likely helps. Nudging 600 to 800-900 probably doesn't do much.
- The dramatic body composition changes come from injected testosterone taking you well above 1000 ng/dL — i.e. supraphysiological, not from acute training-induced blips.

## The study in question [00:00]
A viewer asked about a study in the Journal of Evolution and Human Behavior reporting a **48% rise in testosterone after one hour of chopping wood**, versus roughly **20%** from lifting weights — should you chop wood before leg day?

Nippard looked it up. It was published in **2013**, not new. The subjects were forager-horticulturalist men from a community in Bolivia; the auto-captions garble the group's name ("Simon forager farmers"), so the exact population name shouldn't be trusted as transcribed. Key details:
- Subjects ranged from **age 16 to 80**.
- Their **baseline salivary testosterone was already one third lower** than age-matched US males.
- The paper itself notes that non-industrialised populations facing energetic constraints and pathogenic stress show lower testosterone across all adult ages compared with men in industrialised nations.

Nippard's objection: comparing deprived men in that setting with men living in Western abundance is apples to oranges. What raises their testosterone may not raise yours. Take the finding with a grain of salt.

## The bigger point: the hormone hypothesis is dead
The broader question — should you care about acute hormonal changes around a training session? Probably not.

The idea was popular a decade or more ago. Recent literature has converged on a consensus: focus on progressive tension and progressive overload. Building a program around acute spikes in testosterone, growth hormone or IGF-1 is futile because those spikes are (a) extremely transient and (b) don't take you far enough outside the normal physiological range to affect muscle growth. Nippard credits work by **Brad Schoenfeld** (transcript garbles this as "Bradshaw infilled") and **Stuart Phillips**, and says essentially every expert in the field now agrees.

## What testosterone actually does matter for
Testosterone is unambiguously anabolic — but what counts is **prolonged elevation** and where your **baseline** sits.

The natural physiological range runs roughly **300 to 1000 ng/dL**. Reasoning through it:
- If you're at the low-but-healthy end (~300) and lifestyle changes — more sleep, for instance — take you to 500-600 chronically, that likely improves training performance and possibly muscle mass.
- If you're already around 600, going to 800-900 probably makes little difference. Nippard says plainly he doesn't know, and the literature doesn't establish how much movement within the normal range is needed to produce visible physique changes.
- Injected testosterone pushing you **well above 1000 ng/dL** is where the large changes in body composition, muscle mass and performance appear.

## Practical application
- Don't add exercises, rearrange training order, or pick a time of day with the goal of spiking testosterone or any other hormone.
- Program for progressive overload and mechanical tension.
- If you suspect low testosterone, address baseline levels through sleep and general lifestyle (and, implicitly, medical assessment) rather than acute training tricks.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Nippard explicitly says he doesn't know how much within-range testosterone change is needed for physique-level effects — the literature hasn't answered it.
- The Bolivian population's name is garbled in the auto-captions and is not reproduced here.
- The 48% and 20% figures come from the viewer's framing of the 2013 paper; Nippard doesn't dispute them but argues they don't generalise.
