# Q&A: Fibre Types, "Dangerous" Exercises, Cutting Without Losing Strength

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Fast vs Slow Twitch Fibers? Dangerous Exercises? Current Split? Train With A Mask?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2d4eRqVYJXw)) · 2020-08-31 · 22 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- **Fibre type barely matters for rep selection.** Almost every muscle we care about is roughly a 50/50 type I / type II split. Train everything with a mix of low and high reps. The one exception is the **soleus**, up to ~90% type I — include sets of **15-25 reps** for calves.
- **You should mostly not lose strength while cutting.** Losing it signals a problem with diet or training, not an inevitability.
- Cut at a maximum of **1% of body weight per week** (~2-2.5 lb for a 200 lb lifter), don't slash carbs, don't abandon strength training, and consider **halving volume** if that's what it takes to hold your loads.
- **No exercise is inherently contraindicated** — only certain people are contraindicated for certain exercises. Fear-mongering triggers a nocebo effect that's more dangerous than the lifts.
- **Creatine: 3-5 g of monohydrate daily, forever**, including while cutting. No cycling, no desensitisation.

## Muscle fibre type and rep ranges
[10:06] The common belief: type I (slow twitch) responds better to high reps and light weights, type II (fast twitch) to heavy weights and low reps. He used to believe it too — he was told hamstrings are type II dominant and therefore need heavy low-rep work.

Reality: **almost every muscle we care about developing is close to an even split**. Quads are roughly a perfect **50/50**. Hamstrings are slightly type II dominant, but only about **60/40** according to one study — so even by the fibre-type logic you'd still want mixed rep ranges there.

More importantly, the mechanism doesn't survive testing: research shows **type I fibres respond well to both high and low reps**, and **type II fibres also respond well to both**. Using a combination is almost always best.

**The exception — soleus.** Sitting under the gastrocnemius, some studies put it as high as **90% type I**. If the higher-reps-for-type-I theory has any merit anywhere, it's here. Training calves exclusively in the 6-12 range may cost you growth. His own practice: high reps close to or at failure on **seated** calf raises; standing works too (both target the soleus). Make sure some calf sets fall in the **15-25 rep** range.

## Cutting without losing strength
[02:32] Most people wrongly accept strength loss as a given. Exceptions where loss is genuinely expected: getting **contest lean (below ~8% body fat)**, or very heavy lifters dropping several weight classes so their leverages change. For anyone just getting "beach lean", strength loss is a sign something is wrong.

**Diet fixes:** lose slowly — **no more than 1% of body weight per week** (about 2-2.5 lb at 200 lb). Don't cut carbs completely or do anything extreme; that's where strength loss starts, and muscle loss follows strength loss.

**Training fixes:** don't overhaul everything into machines and high reps, abandoning the strength training that built the muscle. Consider **reducing volume — even to half** of what you were doing — because recovery capacity shrinks as you get leaner. Direct that capacity toward maintaining a tensile stimulus (keeping the loads you used before the cut) rather than toward accumulating volume.

**RPE drift while cutting:** sessions feel worse when dieting, so an old RPE 8 or 9 sensation may now correspond to a true RPE 6. Relying on RPE alone becomes a slippery slope into progressive under-effort and strength loss. **Recalibrate** what RPE 9 and 10 feel like in the deficit, and track both RPE and your actual numbers against pre-cut loads.

## Advice for a first-time lifter
[06:04] Focus on **the basics** and on **balance**.

Pick a handful of fundamental movement patterns, master the technique on them, and progressively get stronger over time. There are **six main movement patterns**; a very good beginner routine is one exercise per pattern, **2-3 sets each**, as a full-body workout performed **2-3 times per week**. Extra calf and ab work is fine to sprinkle in.

Don't copy Instagram or YouTube routines — the people posting them are advanced and their content isn't appropriate for you. Use regressions where needed: goblet squat if the squat intimidates you, Romanian deadlift or back extension before the deadlift.

Save fancy variations, intensity techniques and higher volume for later. If you start at high volume, you have nowhere left to add when you eventually plateau.

**Balance:** men gravitate to arms and chest, women to glutes and abs; both produce disproportionate physiques. Train the whole body first and specialise later.

**Be patient:** hypertrophy is commonly **delayed by one to two months** because **neurological adaptation precedes hypertrophic adaptation**. Getting stronger without visible size early on is normal and is a good sign.

## Are any exercises dangerous?
[15:10] No exercise is inherently so harmful that it must be avoided. There are **no contraindicated exercises, only contraindicated people** — e.g. someone with a history of knee pain probably shouldn't do lots of leg extensions.

The bigger problem is trainers labelling exercises dangerous. The current consensus in pain science and medicine is that **instilling that fear is more likely to cause harm than the exercise itself** — the **nocebo effect**, the inverse of placebo: told something will hurt you, you become fearful enough that it becomes more likely to, or you perceive harm that isn't there.

Two genuine caveats:
- **"Functional" unstable-surface work** (Bosu ball squats, Bosu curls) adds risk without meaningful benefit for people training for size and strength. Real functionality comes from getting stronger on basic compound movements on **stable surfaces**.
- **Ego lifting** is the actual danger, and it can attach to any exercise — often bench press. People chase numbers, let technique slip, and handle weights they can't control. That's when exercises become problematic.

Individual biomechanics vary, so self-experimentation matters, but going into the gym with a mental blacklist is a bad mindset. The common exercises are safe and belong in a complete routine.

## Creatine while cutting
[12:39] Yes. Creatine is stored almost exclusively in **muscle tissue**, and to a lesser extent the brain, so any water retention is **intramuscular, not subcutaneous** — it won't make you look bloated or watery. If anything the fuller muscles push against the skin and produce a *tighter* look.

**Dose: 3-5 g creatine monohydrate per day, every day, indefinitely.** No need to ever come off. Decades of safety research, plus cognitive, general metabolic, strength and muscle mass benefits. **You do not desensitise to it** the way you do to caffeine — treat it like a nutrient or protein powder.

## Training in a mask
[00:31] He found essentially no performance impact. Reason: his compound work is mostly low reps (rarely above 8-10 on squats, bench, deadlifts) so breathing isn't the limiter, and high-rep isolation work isn't hard enough to matter. The one exception was a set of 8 on squats, where getting a big Valsalva breath in on reps 7-8 was brutal. Masks would plausibly matter more for cardio or CrossFit-style high-rep compound work.

## Fitting a cardio-intensive sport around lifting
[18:42] Three variables determine interference: **frequency, intensity and mode (impact)**. Low-impact modes like swimming and cycling tolerate higher frequency and intensity without interfering. Basketball is **high impact**, so keep frequency lower — his own limit is **2-3 times per week**, played at low intensity (mostly shooting around).

Then audit your own recovery. Still progressing week to week on a linear program means you're probably fine. Slowing progression, feeling banged up, extra soreness, achy joints, worse sleep, lower motivation = too much total demand; pull back cardio by a session a week, reduce its intensity, or switch to a lower-impact mode.

## Practical application
- Train every muscle across low, moderate and high reps; don't allocate rep ranges by fibre type. Add **15-25 rep** sets for calves.
- On a cut: max **1%/week** loss, keep carbs in, keep training heavy, cut volume rather than loads, and recalibrate your RPE scale.
- Beginners: one exercise per movement pattern, **2-3 sets**, full body **2-3x/week**, whole-body balance, patience through the 1-2 month lag.
- **3-5 g creatine monohydrate daily**, permanently.
- Stop treating specific exercises as forbidden; stop unstable-surface work and ego lifting instead.

## Caveats & unknowns
The hamstring 60/40 figure comes from a single study. The soleus recommendation is explicitly hedged — it's the only place he thinks the fibre-type theory earns any merit. Strength maintenance during a cut doesn't apply to contest-lean dieters or heavy lifters dropping multiple weight classes. Exercise safety comes down to individual biomechanics and self-experimentation. His mask observations are personal and specific to low-rep compound training; he defers to a linked article by Dr Carl Juneau for the actual science.
