# Are Energy Drinks Bad For You? Ingredient-by-Ingredient

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Are Energy Drinks Bad For You? (What The Science Says)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_i0nJQG162A)) · 2018-02-26 · 14 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The scary-sounding ingredient list is not the problem. The two ingredients worth watching are **caffeine and sugar**.
- Keep total daily caffeine under **400 mg** (Health Canada). One energy drink can carry **150 mg+** — four to five times a can of Coke — so a drink plus a pre-workout can blow past the limit.
- Choose **diet / sugar-free** versions; sugar here is empty calories driving total intake up with no nutritional return.
- For a healthy active adult, roughly one per day is low-risk. Jeff drinks them himself.
- Avoid or be cautious if: pregnant or nursing, a child or teenager, or with personal/family cardiovascular history. Never combine high caffeine with alcohol.

## The "chemicals" objection
Long unpronounceable ingredient lists drive most of the fear, but this is an appeal-to-nature fallacy [01:33]. Water is a chemical (dihydrogen monoxide sounds alarming too); an all-natural blueberry has a huge ingredient list; sharks are natural and will kill you; antibiotics are synthetic and will save you. There's no clear line between "natural" and "synthetic," and natural origin says nothing about safety. Labels also look scarier when the chemical name is used — vitamin B6 listed as pyridoxine hydrochloride is still just vitamin B6.

The right approach is dose-by-dose evaluation of each ingredient. Jeff works through Examine.com's breakdown of the ten most common energy drink ingredients. Overarching principle: **the dose makes the poison** — slam 50-100 cans of Monster and water toxicity would likely kill you before anything else did.

## Ingredients that do essentially nothing (good or bad) [04:41]
- **Niacin, B6, B12, inositol** — water-soluble, so overdose risk at typical doses is basically nil. But they also don't deliver the claimed "energy": vitamins have no calories, aren't acute energy sources, and aren't stimulants.
- **Guarana** — just a natural caffeine source, but the dose in these drinks is almost always too low to matter either way.
- **L-carnitine** — over-hyped. It does improve sperm quality, but the effective dose is around **60× higher** than what's in a can. It doesn't increase fat burning except in carnitine deficiency, which is very rare in adults without metabolic disorders.

## Taurine
Potential upsides: antioxidant and cardioprotective effects, improved insulin sensitivity. A **2008 toxicology report set a lifetime daily supplemental upper limit of 3 g**. A can of Monster has about **1 g**, so habitually hitting or exceeding three cans a day is worth pausing over. That same paper noted much higher intakes have been tested without adverse effects and may be safe — the data above 3 g just isn't strong enough for a confident conclusion [05:42].

## Ginseng
Sold for focus, and it may deliver. A 2010 study found positive cognitive effects at **400 mg**, but *slower arithmetic* at **200 mg** versus placebo ([Reay 2010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/20737519)) — so if you're taking ginseng for focus, take more than 200 mg. A **2011 systematic review of 57 clinical trials** concluded ginseng has a good safety profile with low adverse-event incidence; 16 of 57 trials reported side effects, mostly GI (diarrhoea, cramps) ([Lee 2011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/21704950)) [06:43].

## Artificial sweeteners
Usually sucralose and acesulfame potassium (Ace-K). You'd need roughly **32 cans of diet soda** to reach the acceptable daily intake for Ace-K, or about **6 cans** for sucralose — and the ADI is itself a deliberately conservative lifetime-exposure figure [07:13].

## Sugar
Not toxic, and not the villain it's made out to be at these doses — but over the long term it's the most plausibly harmful component, because it **unnecessarily raises total daily calories with no nutritional value**. That matters most for the teenagers and youths drinking several a day, and for anyone without regular exercise or daily activity [08:15]. Entirely avoidable by choosing the diet version.

## Caffeine
The actual active ingredient, and it works — strong evidence for increased alertness, wakefulness, performance and energy. The question is only harm [09:16].

- Popular drinks can carry **upwards of 150 mg** per can, 4-5× a can of Coke.
- **Health Canada (May 2017) safety alert: healthy adults should cap caffeine at 400 mg/day.** A 2017 review on caffeine safety mirrored this.
- That same review reported **over 79,000 emergency room visits between 2005 and 2011** attributable to overconsumption of high-caffeine energy products in patients aged 12 and up — typically tachyarrhythmia (very fast heart rate). **Many of those cases also involved alcohol.**
- Lethal dose of caffeine is around **10 g** — roughly 50 cans, so not a realistic route. **Pure caffeine powder is far more dangerous: two teaspoons can be lethal.**

## Practical application
- **Track total daily caffeine and stay under 400 mg** — count the energy drink *and* the pre-workout, coffee and anything else.
- **Pick diet / sugar-free** whenever available; sugary versions are best skipped entirely.
- **Don't mix energy drinks with alcohol.**
- Don't buy or dose **pure caffeine powder** — the margin for error is lethal.
- Keep taurine in mind if you're drinking three or more cans daily (~3 g).
- If taking ginseng for focus, dose above 200 mg.
- **Avoid:** pregnancy and nursing (caffeine), children and teenagers (more susceptible to cardiovascular effects). **Be very careful:** existing cardiovascular problems or family history of heart problems.
- One per day for a healthy active adult is, in Jeff's assessment, low risk.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Verdict is "kind of busted" — harm depends on who's drinking (age, body weight, health status) and how much.
- Ingredient *interactions* haven't been well studied. Examine notes a possible sugar-caffeine interaction that acutely raises cardiac workload; the evidence is weak, and severity would scale with dose and health status.
- Whether the residual risk is acceptable is partly a personal risk-tolerance call, not purely an evidence one.
- 2018 video; market and formulation data are dated (energy drink market cited at $12 billion US, projected to double by 2025).
