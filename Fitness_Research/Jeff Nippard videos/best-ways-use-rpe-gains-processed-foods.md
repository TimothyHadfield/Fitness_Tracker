# MASS Review Roundup: RPE, Isolation Work, Processed Foods, Alcohol, Muscle Confusion, Sodium Bicarb, IF

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Best Ways To Use RPE For Gains? Processed Foods Bad? Rows Good For Biceps? Muscle Confusion? IF?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7cTkVBpeCMk)) · 2019-10-01 · 37 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- RPE isn't just for picking load — it can autoregulate **reps per set** and **number of sets** too. Cap autoregulated sets at **5-6 per exercise**.
- Rows alone don't grow biceps; **direct isolation is needed past the beginner stage**. Pulldowns may be a partial exception in beginners.
- Ultra-processed diets caused **~500 extra calories per day** in a metabolic ward study (Kevin Hall's group), calorie- and macro-matched.
- "Muscle confusion" fails: identical vs constantly varied programs produced identical growth over 8 weeks. Vary between blocks, not workouts.
- Sodium bicarbonate: 66% of 35 studies showed benefit, but almost all endurance. Dose ~0.3 g/kg, split into three, taken ~60 min pre-training.

## RPE and RIR: a complete guide (Dr. Mike Zourdos) [00:31]
**RPE** = rating of perceived exertion, a subjective measure of effort. **RIR** = reps in reserve, its inverse. Failure = RPE 10 = 0 RIR; stopping one rep short = RPE 9 = 1 RIR.

**History:** the RPE scale dates to the 1970s in endurance sport, originally scored 6-20 because a rating of 6 mapped to a heart rate of 60 bpm and 20 to 200 bpm. The reps-in-reserve version used in lifting today is credited to powerlifting coach **Mike Tuchscherer** (garbled in captions as "Mike Tasharer").

**Scale:**
- RPE 10 — maximal, no more reps
- RPE 9 — 1 rep in reserve
- RPE 9.5 — no more reps possible, but you could have used slightly more weight for the same reps
- RPE 6-8 — greyer; roughly 2-3 reps left

**Why not always RPE 10:** hypertrophy is similar anywhere in the RPE 7-10 zone, while failure creates far more fatigue and limits recovery. So leaving reps in the tank makes sense in most contexts.

**Use 1 — autoregulating load.** Instead of "3x8 bench with 225", the prescription is "3x8 at RPE 8" and you pick the weight based on the day. Autoregulation has been shown to produce greater strength gains than fixed loading.

**Use 2 — a whole macrocycle.** A four-month, 3-day-per-week example from the article:
- Month 1: high volume (3x10, 4x8, 5x6), low RPE 5-7
- Month 2: ~2 reps cut from every set, RPE up
- Month 3: volume lower again, heavier loads, **RPE 7-9**
- Month 4: peaking block — pure strength work, triples, and heavy singles at **RPE 9.5**

This is linear periodization: volume down, load and effort up.

**Use 3 — correcting a missed RPE.** If a target of RPE 6-8 comes in at an actual RPE 4, add roughly **8% load** for the next set (table from an **Eric Helms** paper). Generally: RPE too low, add weight; too high, drop it; in-zone, lifter's choice.

**Use 4 — autoregulating reps.** Fix the exercise, the sets, and the load (e.g. 4 sets of squats at 70% 1RM), then **stop each set when you hit RPE 6-8** with no target rep count. This is how **Eric Helms** trained when Nippard trained with him in Australia — three sets at RPE 8, whether that came out to 6, 10 or 14 reps. It avoids both stopping far short of a real stimulus and grinding to hit an arbitrary number.

**Use 5 — autoregulating sets.** Fix exercise, load and reps, then keep adding sets until the target RPE is reached (e.g. set 1 RPE 7, set 2 RPE 7.5, set 3 RPE 8 → stop). Some people, women especially, tolerate so much volume that this runs away — so **cap it at about 5-6 sets per exercise**.

**Bonus — last-set RPE tracking.** An idea Nippard got from **Bryce Lewis** and uses in his programs: log the actual RPE of your final set. If the same weight and reps felt like RPE 9 last week and RPE 8 this week, you got stronger — time to add load.

## Multi-joint vs single-joint for biceps (Greg Nuckols on a study by "Manurro" et al. — surname garbled in captions) [12:15]
Design: 8 weeks, **within-subject unilateral** — each subject did dumbbell curls with one arm and dumbbell rows with the other, acting as their own control. A stronger design than group-splitting at low sample sizes.

Findings:
- Strength was specific: rows improved rows, curls improved curls, with essentially no crossover.
- No meaningful **cross-education effect** (usually the untrained limb gains up to 40% of the trained limb's strength), which was unexpected.
- **Biceps hypertrophy was greater with curls than rows.** Rows alone weren't enough to grow the biceps.

Contrast: a study from **Gentil and colleagues** found lat pulldowns produced biceps hypertrophy similar to curls — in beginners. Likely reason: on a pulldown the biceps can be the limiting factor, especially for people who pull with their arms. On a dumbbell row, the lats almost always fatigue first.

**Framework for whether a compound covers an assistance muscle:**
1. Is that muscle likely to limit performance on the lift?
2. How much range of motion does it get? (Wide-grip bench = little elbow extension for triceps; close grip = much more.)
3. Is it **biarticular**? The main ones from a physique standpoint are the **long head of the triceps, the hamstrings, and the rectus femoris**. Biarticular muscles need isolation work covering both functions — e.g. skull crushers with the arms angled back overhead to add shoulder extension for the triceps long head; leg curls **plus** a hip hinge like the RDL for hamstrings (squats are poor for hamstrings).

## Processed foods (Eric Helms on Kevin Hall's group) [19:58]
Metabolic ward study, **20 adults**, 2 weeks each on unprocessed vs ultra-processed diets **matched for calories, sugar, fat, macronutrients and fibre**, eating ad libitum. If everyone ate everything served, both would have been ~3,900-4,000 kcal.

Result: the ultra-processed group ate about **500 kcal more per day**. Reasons: processed foods are eaten faster, suppress appetite less, and require more total calories to reach the same protein intake. That last point ties to **protein leverage theory** — satiety requires hitting a protein threshold, and processed foods are lower in protein per calorie.

His take: neither extreme is right. Tracking macros is temporary for 99% of people, so knowing that a whole-food diet makes eating less effortless is a valuable non-tracking heuristic. The "if it fits your macros" line has merit mainly for people who are afraid of processed food — an occasional Snickers derails nothing. Helms' summary: unprocessed diets produce higher satiety, less hunger, lower intake and easier fat loss. Think fruits, vegetables, lean protein, single-ingredient foods.

## Alcohol (Eric Trexler) [24:32]
A systematic review found **no meaningful effect of fairly heavy drinking (~8 standard drinks for an 80 kg person) on recovery from resistance exercise** — but the recovery measures were isokinetic strength, creatine kinase (a muscle damage marker) and countermovement jump, which may not reflect bodybuilding-style training. Nippard's own experience with 8 drinks: dehydration, lethargy, ruined sleep, worse performance — he recommends taking the next day off, rehydrating and refilling glycogen.

Effects depend on dose, and there's a **sex-specific effect**: heavy drinking impairs recovery and muscle protein synthesis much more in men than women, probably because estrogen is protective. Alcohol blunts MPS to a degree that extra protein cannot fully rescue. It badly degrades sleep quality and duration even though you may fall asleep faster. It raises testosterone acutely but decreases it at higher doses over longer time frames. Drinking also lowers inhibitions, leading to binges that aren't compensated for the next day.

Verdict: an occasional drink is fine and fits into macros; consistent high intake is bad for health, body composition and performance.

## Muscle confusion [29:05]
Another **within-subject unilateral** study: one leg did an identical unilateral leg extension and leg press program for 8 weeks; the other leg had load, rest periods and set numbers constantly varied. **Identical growth in both legs.** Progressive tension is what matters; variety neither helps nor hurts.

Caveat he adds: if you've trained the *same style* for a very long time, novelty can produce new growth — powerlifters doing years of heavy low-rep work grew better once **20+ rep work** was introduced. So vary training **from block to block over months, not workout to workout**. Much of the periodization literature amounts to exactly this periodic alteration of variables.

## Sodium bicarbonate (Dr. Mike Zourdos) [32:40]
Baking soda is chemically basic and buffers acid accumulation in fatiguing muscle. A systematic review of **35 studies** found **66% showed a performance benefit** — but nearly all were endurance; **only one was resistance training**. Zourdos concludes the benefit requires **high-volume sessions with short rest intervals**.

Dose: **0.3 g/kg** (stated as "mg/kg" in the captions, which is a slip), taken **~60 minutes pre-training**, **split into three separate doses** because it causes significant GI distress at the recommended dose. Trial it on a non-training day first. Not in the same tier as creatine, caffeine or whey.

## Intermittent fasting [34:42]
Nippard isn't a fan of IF for maximising the 24-hour anabolic response — mechanistic research favours spreading protein more evenly. But plenty of very muscular people practise it successfully. Helms' position: an **8-hour eating window** is a suitable strategy for lifters with no adverse effects, provided **total daily protein is high enough** and **training occurs in the fed state**. Nippard's advice: don't let the window get too narrow, train fed, pay attention to peri-workout nutrition.

## Practical application
- Prescribe by RPE, not fixed load. Most work in the **RPE 7-9** zone; save 9.5-10 for peaking or final isolation sets.
- Try autoregulating reps (stop at target RPE) or sets (add sets until target RPE, capped at 5-6).
- Log the last set's actual RPE each week as a progression signal.
- Isolate biceps directly; isolate biarticular muscles (triceps long head, hamstrings, rectus femoris) through both of their functions.
- Build the diet from single-ingredient foods; expect ~500 kcal/day of unintentional overeating from ultra-processed diets.
- Keep alcohol occasional; take a rest day after heavy drinking.
- Keep exercises stable within a block; change between blocks.
- Sodium bicarb only for high-volume, short-rest sessions: 0.3 g/kg split in three, ~60 min pre-workout, tested first on a rest day.

## Caveats & unknowns
The biceps study surname is garbled in the auto-captions and should not be cited without checking. The alcohol review's recovery measures may not generalise to hypertrophy training. The sodium bicarbonate evidence is nearly all endurance-based. The muscle confusion study ran only 8 weeks — nothing is known about longer timescales.
