# Sumo Deadlift Technique for Muscle and Strength

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Effective Way to Deadlift for Muscle and Strength (Sumo Technique Explained)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XsrD5y8EIKU)) · 2019-02-12 · 10 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Sumo vs. conventional is largely a comfort/leverage choice — but rotating between them periodically helps master both patterns.
- Sumo is harder on the **quads**; conventional is harder on the **spinal erectors** (attributed to Escamilla and colleagues — the auto-captions garble the second name as "cola wiki").
- Train it heavy: **5-10 reps for hypertrophy, 1-5 for strength**, no more than **1-2x/week** even for advanced lifters.
- Pull the slack out of the bar before every rep. Do not "grip and rip."
- Finish by driving the hips forward, not by pushing the feet down — that turns the deadlift into a squat and shoots the hips up.

## Muscles trained
The deadlift combines hip extension and knee extension, but hip extension massively dominates (the squat is the knee-dominant lift of the pair). Hip extension is handled by the **glutes and hamstrings**, knee extension by the **quadriceps**, and there is a strong **isometric spinal extension** contraction from the erectors running the length of the back. Additional isometric stabilisation at the shoulders and scapulae means **lats and traps** work hard too. Jeff still treats it as a lower-body exercise and programs it on leg day.

Because it's multi-joint and moves large muscle masses, it's highly conducive to progressive overload — and it's more physically and mentally demanding than the squat, which is why frequency stays low.

## Setup [02:36]
- **Footwear:** flat-soled shoes (Chuck Taylors) or deadlift slippers. High socks to avoid shin scrapes. Optional 10 mm lever belt to increase torso rigidity and force transfer.
- **Bar position:** shins about half an inch behind the bar, bar roughly centred over the tongue of the shoe when looking down.
- **Stance:** feet as wide laterally as possible while keeping heels in line with knees. Toes flared out about **30-40 degrees** in full sumo — pointing toward the front end of the plates.
- **Working into the stance:** if full sumo isn't accessible, start conventional, move to semi-sumo (hands just inside the legs), and widen over several weeks. Stretch and foam roll the inner thighs and groin to build the adductor mobility needed.

## Getting to the bar [03:38]
Think of a stiff-leg deadlift: push the hips back first rather than squatting down to the bar. Once you can't hinge further, push the knees slightly forward and out toward the toes as the hips drop the remaining distance.

**Grip width:** arms hanging straight down, roughly shoulder width. Some sumo pullers prefer slightly wider so the hands clear the quads at lockout; Jeff is stronger at shoulder width.

**Grip options:**
- *Alternated (over/under)* — most common, allows the most weight. Switch which hand is supinated periodically to avoid imbalances. Slight injury risk if it causes twisting at the top; bicep tendon tears are anecdotally more common on the underhand side, though Jeff has never seen one. This is what he uses.
- *Double overhand* — safest, but grip strength becomes the limiter and deadlift strength may never reach potential.
- *Hook grip* — solves the grip limit but has a painful adjustment period.
- *Straps* — allow easy double overhand, but do **at least one heavy working set per session without straps** so grip doesn't lag.

Dig the bar into the base of the hand and drive the pinky into it to reduce grip fatigue.

## Bracing and the pull [05:39]
1. **Pack the lats** by thinking about pulling the bar in close — this stops the bar drifting forward.
2. Pull the lower back into **neutral**, creating a stretch in the hamstrings, and hold that flat lumbar position for the whole lift. Slight thoracic rounding is more acceptable and less dangerous, but it must not round *further* during the lift.
3. Without dropping the hips, breathe deep into the gut and lean your body weight slightly in front of the bar. From here you should be able to draw a straight line through scapula, mid-bar, and mid-foot.
4. **Pull the slack out:** apply light upward tension so the bar rises a millimetre and comes softly up against the inside of the plate holes before the plates leave the floor. Jeff calls this a crucial step most inexperienced lifters miss. Grip-and-rip means you collide with the weight before the plates move, making the pull harder, wasting energy, and throwing you out of position.
5. Lift off the floor **explosively, leading with the chest**, then finish by driving the **hips forward**. You can think about driving the feet into the floor, but over-focusing on that turns the movement into a squat and makes hips shoot up. Treat it as a hip hinge.

**Three isometric holds throughout the concentric:** lumbar spine (prevents lower back rounding), retracted shoulder blades, and shoulders pulling the bar tight into the legs. From the side the bar travels straight up over mid-foot, dragging up the shins.

**Lockout:** straighten the knees, squeeze the glutes to lock the hips, lift the chest to lock the shoulders. Do not lean excessively backward, hyperextend the lower back, squeeze the shoulder blades together, or shrug.

## The eccentric [08:10]
Many powerlifting coaches call the deadlift concentric-only — just guide the bar down without resisting — because actively resisting the eccentric makes the lift much harder to recover from both within the session and in the following days.

Jeff's compromise: if you're new to the lift or relearning it, **control the negative over about one second** so each rep is consistent and you start the next rep in position.

Either way, lower by **setting the hips back first**, stiff-leg style, until the bar clears the knees, then let the knees bend and the bar reach the floor in a straight line. Initiating the negative by bending the knees forces the bar forward around the knees, ruining the start position and wasting energy.

Let the plates come to a **full dead stop** on the floor between reps — no bouncing — then reset: breath, slack out, hips forward, lock out, hips back, follow the bar down.

## Practical application
- Program sumo deadlifts on leg day, 1-2x/week maximum.
- 5-10 reps for hypertrophy; 1-5 reps for strength.
- Rep sequence: set feet → hinge to the bar → set grip → pack lats → neutral lumbar → breathe and shift weight slightly ahead of the bar → pull slack → explode leading with the chest → drive hips through → lock knees, hips, chest → hips back, then knees, bar down in a straight line → full reset.
- Rotate conventional and sumo across training blocks rather than only ever pulling one.
- Build adductor and groin mobility before chasing a maximally wide stance.

## Caveats & unknowns

- The alternated-grip bicep-tear risk is explicitly anecdotal, not from data.
- Whether to resist the eccentric is a coaching judgement call, not a settled question — Jeff and many powerlifting coaches differ.
