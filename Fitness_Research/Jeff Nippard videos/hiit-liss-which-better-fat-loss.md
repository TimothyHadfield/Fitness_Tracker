# HIIT vs LISS: Which Is Actually Better for Fat Loss?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "HIIT OR LISS: Which Is Better For FAT LOSS? (What The Science Says)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ir83IWRVTOw)) · 2018-03-19 · 11 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The claim that HIIT is better for fat loss than steady-state cardio is largely busted. Body composition outcomes come out the same.
- The afterburn (EPOC) effect is real but trivially small — roughly 80 extra calories after an 80-minute high-intensity session, versus the 700-800 burned during it.
- HIIT's genuine edge is time efficiency (one meta-analysis found ~40% less time for equivalent results), adherence, and possibly appetite blunting — but the time advantage is smaller than advertised once you control the conditions.
- HIIT costs more recovery, can interfere with weight training, and is partly redundant if you already lift hard.
- **Cap HIIT at 1-2 sessions per week**, keep at least one day between HIIT and leg day, and fill the rest of the deficit with diet plus LISS as needed.

## The two modalities [00:32]
**HIIT** — short, very high-effort bursts (not necessarily running) interspersed with lower-intensity work. Example on a bike: 5-minute warm-up, then 20 seconds all-out, 2 minutes light, repeated for 5-6 intervals.

**LISS** — constant moderate pace throughout, e.g. walking on an inclined treadmill at a steady speed with no bursts of max exertion.

## How the argument flipped
For years LISS was assumed superior because it uses proportionally more fat as fuel during the session — which is true; high-intensity work uses relatively more carbohydrate. But as with fasted cardio, what matters isn't substrate use during the session, it's fat balance over 24 hours.

Opinion then swung to HIIT via the **afterburn / EPOC** (excess post-exercise oxygen consumption) claim: that you keep burning fat for hours afterwards on the couch, while LISS gives you only the session's own burn.

## Why EPOC doesn't carry the argument [02:38]
EPOC is real and well-supported, but its practical relevance is overblown.
- Reviewing a 2006 EPOC study, **Lyle McDonald** highlighted that after 80 minutes of high-intensity cardio the EPOC effect lasted 7 hours — but amounted to only about **80 extra calories**. Compare that with the 700-800 calories burned during the session itself.
- 80 minutes is far more cardio than most people do. At a more realistic ~40 minutes you'd be looking at roughly **40 extra calories**.
- A **2017 systematic review and meta-analysis of 28 studies** concluded EPOC is unlikely to account for any apparent greater fat-loss potential with HIIT.

## The time-efficiency claim [03:39]
A 2017 meta-analysis (lead author garbled in the auto-captions — rendered as "me Gian," treat as uncertain) across 13 studies found **no significant differences between HIIT and moderate-intensity continuous training for any body composition measure**, but HIIT required approximately **40% less training time**.

Nippard's caveats: those subjects were obese and overweight, with no dietary control. Two plausible confounders — HIIT may have blunted appetite so the HIIT group simply ate less, and people who feel like they're working harder may push harder elsewhere in their routine and believe more in the process. Under controlled conditions in healthy, non-obese subjects, he expects the time-efficiency gap to shrink.

Doing the maths: 30 minutes of intervals and 30 minutes of steady-state should burn roughly the same calories, because (as McDonald noted) intervals alternate very high and very low expenditure, so the average comes out similar. Factor in the tiny EPOC contribution and the two modalities produce comparable burns for the same time investment.

## The case against HIIT [06:13]
- **Recovery cost.** As Dr Eric Helms put it in a March 2013 issue of Alan Aragon's Research Review, HIIT is by definition high intensity, which imposes a high level of stress, and that stress has to be recovered from. This matters most when combining cardio with lifting, since you're trying to retain muscle while losing fat.
- **Frequency ceiling.** You can't do HIIT anywhere near as often as LISS.
- **Interference.** It may hurt weight-training strength and performance, and carries higher injury risk.
- **Redundancy.** Weight training already resembles interval training — an all-out 20-30 second bout followed by 2-3 minutes rest, repeated. Train hard enough with weights and you may already be getting most of HIIT's adaptations.

## The case for HIIT
- More time efficient — though not by as much as commonly claimed.
- May blunt appetite better.
- May get you working harder.
- Less boring, which can meaningfully improve adherence.

## The framing correction
Borrowing again from Dr Eric Helms: cardio should not be the primary vehicle for fat loss regardless of whether it's HIIT or LISS. The vast majority of fat loss should come from diet.

## Practical application
- Drive fat loss with **diet first**. Use cardio to top up the deficit.
- **Limit HIIT to 1-2 sessions per week maximum.**
- **Space HIIT away from leg days** — at least one full day of recovery between the two.
- Use **low-impact equipment** for HIIT: bike or elliptical rather than treadmill sprints.
- Nippard's own approach in a fat-loss phase is a combination of HIIT and LISS, with HIIT rarely exceeding twice weekly.
- Otherwise pick whichever modality fits your schedule, you'll adhere to, and interferes least with your lifting.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The 40%-less-time meta-analysis used obese/overweight subjects with no dietary control, so it may not generalise to lean, trained lifters.
- The proposed mechanisms behind that result (appetite blunting, greater self-driven effort) are Nippard's hypotheses, not tested findings.
- Choice ultimately comes down to individual recovery capacity and preference.
- One cited 2017 meta-analysis author name is garbled in the captions and should be verified.
