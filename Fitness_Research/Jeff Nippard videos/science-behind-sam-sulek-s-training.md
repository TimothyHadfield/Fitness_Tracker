# Sam Sulek's Training, Graded Against the Evidence

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Science Behind Sam Sulek’s Training" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ml5uvpfXcLU)) · 2024-02-28 · 12 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Cap a single muscle at roughly **6-8 hard sets per workout**; past that you are into junk volume — split it across two days instead.
- Track weight and reps on at least your heavy compounds and hold most exercises steady for **1-2 months** so progressive overload is actually measurable.
- Take roughly **30% of sets to true failure** (Jeff's own ratio: 3 sets per exercise, 1-2 RIR on the first two, failure on the last), not 98%.
- The stretched portion of the range of motion is the part you cannot afford to cut; control the eccentric, especially on the last rep.
- Effort, consistency and enjoying training outrank every optimisation detail — this is where Sulek scores highest.

## What the audit of 460 sets found
[00:00] Jeff logged every set Sulek posted in 2024 to that point. Volume distribution: back 20%, triceps 25%, biceps 16%, chest 15%, quads 11%. That is one quarter of all training going to the triceps, a muscle that is under 4% of total body musculature, while the quads — nearly 30% of body musculature — got 11%. Concrete extremes: 113 tricep sets vs 9 calf sets and 6 glute sets.

Effort: 96% of all sets to failure, and virtually every non-failure set was forearm work — so 98% of real training was to muscular failure.

Rep distribution: 11% at 0-3 reps, 40% at 4-7, 40% at 8-12, 9% above 13 reps.

## Three weaknesses

**Technique — grade B.** [01:32] Jeff's own published study on optimal hypertrophy technique found two things matter most: (1) a range of motion that puts the muscle in a **deep stretch**, and (2) a **controlled negative**. Sulek gets a B− on ROM — incline presses stopping 3-4 inches off the chest, preacher curls cutting out the bottom quarter where the biceps are most stretched. Two studies on the preacher curl specifically show the bottom half is the most anabolic portion ([Pedrosa 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36828324/), [Sato 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34616309/)); Jeff now stays in the bottom half for the whole set. Eccentric control gets a B+: usually good, but too many free-falling reps, particularly near failure where activation is highest.

**No program structure — grade C+.** [03:04] Sulek improvises workouts in the car and often changes them mid-session. The problem is not variety itself but that random exercise swapping means when you return to a lift you repeat the same weight and reps. Jeff names this as the number one cause of people becoming "lifetime intermediates" after the newbie phase. He does credit swapping an exercise out for pain or genuine boredom — Sulek converted a chest day into a back day on 8 January after his chest tightened, which Jeff calls a smart call.

**Bro split — grade C+.** [05:36] A body-part split gives three-plus upper-body days per leg day while the legs carry far more total musculature, so legs tend to lag unless genetics are excellent. It also breeds junk volume: 13 chest sets plus two drop sets in one January session, when the best studies put the per-workout ceiling at 6-8 sets. Jeff would split that into 6 sets one day and 7 another. Caveat: 90%+ of IFBB pros use this split, so the rules may bend at that level. Dr Mike Israetel told Jeff that even enhanced lifters would be better served by push/pull/legs, upper/lower, or full body.

## Three strengths

**Effort — grade A.** [08:07] Training hard matters more than any of the above; optimising everything on paper is worthless if sets are sandbagged. Jeff still thinks 440 failure sets out of 460 is too much for most people's recovery, and notes most gym-goers err the other way, leaving 10+ reps in reserve. But Sulek appears to recover, enjoys it, and his few injuries can't be cleanly pinned on failure training versus the very heavy loads.

**Rep ranges — grade A.** [09:39] The bulk of work sitting in 4-12 reps is close to ideal for hypertrophy. All rep ranges can produce similar growth, but very high reps are exhausting and very low reps add joint strain. Jeff does slightly more high-rep work himself because studies favour using a variety of rep ranges over a single one.

**Sustainability and enjoyment — grade A++.** [10:40] Sulek is consistent, picks exercises he likes, and clearly loves the gym. Jeff rates this so highly it lifts the overall grade.

**Overall: B.**

## Practical application
- Write the plan down (paper or phone) and log weight and reps. If that's too much, log the heavy compounds — squats, presses, heavy pulls — and wing the isolation work (curls, lateral raises, flies).
- Keep most exercises and workout structure fixed for at least a month or two so you can add weight or a rep each session.
- Ceiling of 6-8 sets per muscle per session; distribute the rest across other days.
- Reach a genuine deep stretch on every exercise; on preacher curls consider living in the bottom half.
- Resist the eccentric deliberately as you approach failure.
- Prefer push/pull/legs, upper/lower or full body over a bro split for even volume distribution.

## Caveats & unknowns
Sulek may be tracking off camera — Jeff never heard it mentioned but can't rule it out. Anabolic enhancement changes the picture: it functions like a second newbie phase where almost any training works, and research shows muscle can be built on drugs without training at all ([Bhasin 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8637535/)), so Sulek's results don't validate his methods. It is also possible that lifters at his level of development tolerate more per-session volume than average. And whether his injuries come from failure training, the loads, or something else is unresolved.
