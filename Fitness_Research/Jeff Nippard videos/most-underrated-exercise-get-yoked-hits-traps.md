# The Barbell 45-Degree Hyperextension: An Underrated Yoke Builder

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Underrated Exercise To Get Yoked: Hits The Traps, Triceps, Lats, Glutes" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J46aPqFl0WE)) · 2019-10-22 · 5 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- The barbell 45-degree hyperextension trains four muscle groups at once — glutes/hamstrings, traps and upper-back erectors, lower-back erectors, and lats plus the triceps long head.
- It has a distinctive inverted-U torque curve that is harder at the midpoint than the good morning, horizontal back extension or reverse hyper.
- Load it in a **moderate 6-10 rep range** as a compound; if you round the back and hold a dumbbell instead, it becomes a glute-focused movement suited to higher reps.
- Treat it as an intermediate movement — master deadlift mechanics first.
- The two big errors are excessive back rounding and hyperextending the spine at the top.

## Why it works
The "yoke" muscles it targets — traps, glutes, lats and triceps long head — are the ones that make you look big in clothes: glutes give lower-body thickness from the side, traps make you look large up top, lats add width from behind, and the long head fills out a t-shirt sleeve.

Per a figure from Bret Contreras's biomechanics book, the 45-degree back extension has a unique torque curve. It is harder at the midpoint of the lift than the good morning, the horizontal back extension or the reverse hyper, and its inverted-U shape stands out even against common hip extension exercises like the deadlift, squat and hip thrust.

Alternatives fall short: glute-ham raises train a similar pattern but don't load the upper body and skew hamstring-dominant; horizontal back extensions are harder to overload with a barbell. You do need access to a 45-degree hyperextension bench.

## Four simultaneous biomechanical actions [01:32]
1. **Hip extension** — glutes and hamstrings drive the movement.
2. **Isometric thoracic extension** — traps and upper-back spinal erectors hold the upper back neutral against the barbell's leverage.
3. **Lumbar extension** — lower-back erectors contribute.
4. **Isometric pull-in** — lats and the triceps long head contract to stop the barbell drifting out in front of you, as they do in a deadlift.

He also rates it for building raw deadlift strength, especially if you tend to miss the lift in the mid-portion, around the knees.

## Setup and execution
- Set the bar on the floor in front of the machine.
- Adjust the pads so their top edge sits at the crease of your hips — roughly where you'd place a barbell for a hip thrust.
- Plant your heels firmly against the foot pads and keep them there throughout.
- Grip the bar at roughly **1 to 1.5× shoulder width** — slightly wider than a deadlift grip. This increases range of motion and involves the upper back more.
- Before pulling, retract the shoulder blades and pack the lats by thinking about tucking the bar in toward you.
- Lift the chest to extend the upper back while simultaneously using the glutes to straighten the hips: drive the hips into the pad as if hip thrusting, and drive the heels down.
- Once the hips are fully extended, reverse and lower the bar under control.

## Common errors
- **Rounding the back too much.** Acceptable only if you're deliberately converting it into a glute-only exercise. As a yoke builder, keep upper and lower back straight. Rounding usually shows up when the load is too heavy or concentration slips.
- **Hyperextending at the top.** The opposite mistake, common in new lifters who think of it purely as a lower-back exercise and take "full range of motion" too literally. Keep the spine neutral throughout — hip extension should be driven by glutes and hamstrings, not by cranking the lumbar spine backwards.

## Practical application
- **Yoke/compound version:** barbell, neutral spine, 6-10 reps.
- **Glute version:** hold a dumbbell, deliberately round the upper back, squeeze the glutes hard at the top, use higher reps. He notes this form change completely transforms the movement.
- Prerequisite: proper deadlift mechanics. Despite its reputation as a beginner exercise, he reserves the barbell-loaded version for lifters who have that down.
- He adds that the neck is arguably the single most important muscle for looking muscular in clothes, and points to his separate neck and trap training videos.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The torque-curve claim comes from a biomechanics text, not a direct training comparison; no hypertrophy study is cited for the exercise itself.
- Requires a 45-degree hyperextension bench, which not every gym has.
