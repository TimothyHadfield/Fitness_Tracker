# Bro Science vs Real Science: Bret Contreras on Mind-Muscle, EMG, Failure and Volume

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Bro Science vs Real Science (New Training Controversies) ft. The Glute Guy" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=92pQFyQnJsw)) · 2018-12-16 · 99 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- **Mind-muscle connection is real science, not bro science.** Contreras and Schoenfeld published a training study where an internal focus grew the **biceps** significantly better; it made no difference for **quads** on leg extensions ([Schoenfeld 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/29533715)).
- **Two roads to hypertrophy: progressive overload and mind-muscle connection. Either alone is insufficient.** Use external focus and load progression on your first one or two lifts of the day; use internal focus and quality of contraction on later, lighter movements.
- **You can drop squats and deadlifts for 8 weeks** and keep your strength if you keep hammering the underlying patterns (hip extension, knee extension, knee flexion) — Contreras' own n=1 experiment.
- **Train to failure selectively**: on movements that don't beat you up, on your last set, and not when frequency is already high. Never three sets to true failure, three times a week, on deadlifts.
- **Volume evidence is contradictory.** Contreras/Schoenfeld/Krieger found 30-45 sets/week beat 6-9; a 6-month study (Barbalho, Steele, Fisher, Gentil — first author's name garbled in captions) found **10 sets/week best, 5 close behind, 15 worse and 20 worst** when sets were taken to true failure.

## The 8-week no-squat, no-deadlift experiment [11:48]
At 42 with achy knees and low back, Contreras cut squats and deadlifts entirely for 8 weeks. Twice weekly (Mon/Thu): **4 sets hip thrusts** (Smith machine or Nautilus Glute Drive — both allow deeper hip flexion than a bench), **4 sets leg extensions**, **4 sets Nordic ham curls** (or lying/seated leg curls when travelling) = 12 hard sets per session.

Results: hip thrust volume PRs; leg extension went from 200 lb × 3×15 to **240 lb (full stack) × 3×15**; Nordics improved. Retesting after: **405 lb ass-to-grass squat** (felt uncoordinated but moved) and **565 lb sumo deadlift**, both close to prior bests. He credits (a) getting out of pain — pain shuts down muscle activation, "like driving with the parking brake on" — and (b) genuine transfer from hard hip- and knee-extension training. An earlier 8-week machines-only experiment found **Smith machine squats transferred well** (free squat +20 lb) while **Smith machine deadlifts and hip thrusts transferred poorly** (both down 40-60 lb).

Takeaway: periodically unload the barbell lifts and build the patterns instead; a step back can be two steps forward.

## Why the big lifts hurt people
Not the mechanics alone — it's **emotional attachment to numbers**. Nobody injures themselves on goblet squats or seated rows because nobody cares about the load there. The failure mode is the planned progression: you show up for 500×5, warm-ups feel heavy, and instead of backing off you round your back to hit the number, get away with it, and repeat until you break. At his Glute Lab gym he trains a couple hundred people a month with **zero injuries in 11 months** by pushing the safest movements hardest: lever squat machine, Hammer Strength deadlift machine, kettlebell deadlifts, goblet squats for higher reps, straddle lifts, hip thrusts, leg press, and lots of abduction work.

## Mind-muscle connection and EMG
- EMG studies consistently show higher amplitude when you focus on the target muscle. Contreras' own T-Nation experiment showed a bodyweight back extension at roughly **6% glute activation** when deliberately avoiding the glutes versus about **32%** when trying to use them.
- The **external-focus literature** (100+ studies: strength, force, jump distance, darts, golf) genuinely favours external focus — **for performance**. Focusing internally recruits extra motor units and is inefficient. Critics conflate performance with hypertrophy.
- Above roughly **85-90% of 1RM** you can't meaningfully change EMG by focusing; internal focus matters most at lighter loads.
- **Andrew Vigotsky** (former Contreras intern) published two letters to the editor and a review cautioning against inferring motor unit recruitment or hypertrophy from EMG amplitude. Contreras defends him as careful rather than contrarian; the point is that a **4% EMG difference between exercises probably means nothing**, and comparisons are only valid at matched range of motion, tempo and relative load.
- Contreras' rebuttal proof-of-concept: an EMG study plus a training study where one group did **biceps curls** and one did **calf raises**. Activation and growth tracked together — curls grew biceps and not calves; calf raises grew calves and not biceps.
- **Tension needs time.** Per the force-velocity relationship, a drop landing spikes EMG for a tenth of a second but builds nothing. This (a point Chris Beardsley emphasises) is why heavy lifts work and why the **later, grinding reps of a high-rep set** are the productive ones — you need high activation *and* slow contraction velocity together. It's also why 30-rep sets to failure can match low-rep sets: over the set every motor unit is recruited and fatigued.
- Practical use of EMG: individualisation. One client got more glute activation from goblet squats and kettlebell RDLs than from much heavier barbell versions, and kept climbing to her hip thrust max; Vigotsky and another client topped out around 50% of 1RM on hip thrusts. Another client hit near-max EMG on **seated hip abduction**, so she was programmed for very frequent, high-rep band and abduction work while the first went heavy and trained infrequently. Both roughly doubled glute size in a year.

## Training to failure
Contreras disagrees with Mike Israetel's near-blanket anti-failure stance and with Eric Helms' stricter limits, while respecting both. His position: **yes, train to failure — but context decides.**
- Three sets to true failure on **deadlifts** three times a week will wreck you. Three sets to failure on **prone rear delt raises** would barely register.
- Rules of thumb: failure on movements that **don't load a deep stretch or huge muscle mass**, on your **last set only**, and not on lifts you're training very frequently.
- Effort and volume are inversely related — high effort with less volume, or high volume with less effort, or a blend. He gave a top-5 Bikini Olympia competitor **10 sets**, but brutal ones with drop sets.

## Contreras' "rule of thirds" for glutes (~36 sets/week)
Why 36 sets is not as insane as it sounds — each third is different [83:57]:
- **By vector:** 1/3 vertically/axially loaded (squats, deadlifts, lunges — most stretch, most damage, most systemic cost), 1/3 horizontal/anteroposterior (hip thrusts — loaded in the contracted position, high tension and activation, little soreness), 1/3 lateral/rotary (abduction, band walks — short muscle lengths, essentially **"penalty-free volume"**).
- **By load:** 1/3 heavy, 1/3 moderate, 1/3 light.
- **By effort:** 1/3 to failure or ~1 rep shy, 1/3 at 2-5 reps in reserve, 1/3 nowhere near failure.

36 sets of lunges to failure would be a completely different proposition.

## The volume studies
- **Contreras/Schoenfeld/Krieger (2018):** 8 weeks, three groups — low (~6-9 sets/body part/week), moderate, high (**30-45 sets**). High volume grew most ([Schoenfeld 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/30153194)). The authors expected an inverted-U and didn't get one; they deliberately did **not** change their recommendations off one study. Caveats: college-age males, 8 weeks, ~3 sessions/week of about an hour, and **any elbow-flexion work (rows, pull-ups, curls) counted toward biceps sets** — not 30-45 sets of curls as many readers assumed.
- **The counter-study:** 6 months, legs trained one day a week (squat, leg press, stiff-leg deadlift) at **5, 10, 15 or 20 sets/week**, all to *actual* failure. Strength and muscle thickness were best in the **10-set group**, with 5 close behind, **15 clearly worse and 20 worst** ([Barbalho et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328502454_Evidence_for_an_Upper_Threshold_for_Resistance_Training_Volume_in_Trained_Women)). Not a plateau — an actual reversal. Nobody has a clean mechanism for it; excess damage from failure training is the leading guess.

## Three-pronged hypertrophy model
Contreras hopes the tension-only camp (Beardsley, Hansel/Menno — names garbled) is right because it would simplify everything, but doesn't think the evidence is there yet. He points to a Journal of Applied Physiology review, *"Stimuli and sensors that initiate skeletal muscle hypertrophy following resistance exercise"* — lead author Wackerhage with Schoenfeld second (remaining co-author names garbled in the captions) — which lays out how little is actually known ([Wackerhage et al.](https://www.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/japplphysiol.00685.2018)). Until then he uses a **"shotgun approach"**: prioritise mechanical tension, but deliberately sprinkle in damage (lunges, long-muscle-length work) and metabolic stress (pump work, high-rep sets). Jeff agrees that even if tension turns out to be the only driver, stretch-biased exercises and pump work would stay in the program.

## Practical application
- First one or two lifts: external focus, progressive overload. Later lifts: internal focus, feel the muscle, don't chase load on lateral raises.
- Rotate away from barbell squats/deadlifts for blocks of ~8 weeks while training the same patterns hard; expect to keep your strength.
- Push hardest on the movements that are safest to push.
- Take failure to the last set of low-damage exercises; keep it away from high-frequency, high-stretch, high-mass lifts.
- Structure high glute volume by vector, load and effort rather than piling on more of the same.
- Sensible working range from the conflicting evidence: roughly **10-20 hard sets/week per muscle**, with brief higher-volume bursts, not 40 sets year-round.
- Experiment: try one set of eight exercises three times a week, or high volume at low RPE, or "compound bro training" (heavy full-body Mon/Wed/Fri plus light high-rep isolation Tue/Thu/Sat).

## Caveats & unknowns
- Several researcher names are garbled by auto-captions (the JAP review's co-authors, the 6-month volume study's first author, the tension-only advocates). Verify before citing.
- The 8-week experiments are n=1 case studies on a 25-year trainee; a novice might detrain far faster.
- Contreras notes Steele and Fisher's lab has repeatedly produced low-volume/high-effort findings — he attributes no bad intent but flags the pattern.
- Nobody has explained the mechanism for 20 sets being *worse* than 10 over 6 months.
- Whether metabolic stress and muscle damage independently drive growth, or merely accompany tension, is unresolved; no clean study design exists to separate them.
