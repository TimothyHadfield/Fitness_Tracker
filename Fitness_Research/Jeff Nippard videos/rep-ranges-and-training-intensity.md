# Rep Ranges and Load: How Heavy Should You Train?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Rep Ranges and Training Intensity | The Fundamentals Series: Chapter 3" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3JOEZb46-dM)) · 2018-08-04 · 14 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- Hypertrophy is similar across a very wide rep range (3 to 35 reps) **when effort and volume are matched**.
- Strength is different: heavier loads and lower reps produce more strength.
- **Put ~75% of your volume in 6-15 reps** — the practical hypertrophy zone. Split the remaining ~25% between sets under 6 reps and sets over 15 reps.
- Effort standard: stay within roughly 2-3 reps of failure on working sets.

## Terminology
[04:04] In the research literature "intensity" means **how heavy the load is**, not how hard it feels. Load and intensity are used interchangeably. Low reps = heavy; high reps = light; moderate reps = moderate load.

## The evidence
[05:08] Two studies from the same research group led by Brad Schoenfeld:

- **2014:** compared a group training with **3 reps** per set against a group training with **10 reps** per set — essentially powerlifting-style vs bodybuilding-style training.
- **2015:** compared **10 reps** against **25-35 reps**.

**Finding in both:** hypertrophy was statistically similar across all conditions — very heavy/low rep, moderate/moderate, and very light/very high rep all produced comparable muscle growth.

**Strength was not equal.** In both studies the lower-rep, heavier groups gained more strength. If strength matters to you, you have to lift heavy.

**Two conditions on the equivalence:**
1. **Effort must be matched.** The studies controlled for this by taking sets to muscular failure. In practice you don't need every set at failure — reserve failure for the last set of the last exercise per body part — but you do need to be within a reasonable threshold, roughly **2-3 reps in reserve**.
2. **Volume must be matched.** Volume is the main driver; the rep range equivalence only holds at equal volume.

Both the "heavy for low reps" and the "light for high reps" camps therefore have a valid case.

## Why 6-15 reps anyway
[08:45] There is no magic rep range, but there is a **practical** one.

**Problems above 15 reps:**
- Heavy fatigue accumulation — very high-rep sets (especially squats) cause large lactate build-up and prolonged soreness, impeding recovery.

**Problems below 6 reps:**
- Higher injury risk. He speaks as a powerlifter who has been injured and notes it's common in that community.
- In the 3-rep study, **dropout was far higher in the 3-rep group** — injuries and people unable to tolerate the heavy loading on their joints — with few or no dropouts in the 10-rep group.
- Accumulating volume becomes impractical. To match volume in that study, the 10-rep group needed only **3 sets** while the 3-rep group needed **7 sets**. Push it to 1RM training and you might need ~10 sets to equal a single 10-rep set — workouts balloon in length and set count.

If you get equal hypertrophy from 3 reps and 10 reps but lower injury risk from 10, the 10 is the rational default.

## Why to keep some work outside the zone
[11:50]
- **High reps (15+):** builds muscular endurance and fatigue resistance, so you can tolerate more volume without tiring as fast. Also, more controversially, metabolic stress (build-up of metabolic byproducts) may itself signal for hypertrophy. Use sparingly.
- **Low reps (<6):** useful from a periodisation standpoint. Occasional heavy training makes you stronger, which lets you push more weight in the 6-15 zone — more tension on the muscle and, in theory, more overall hypertrophy.

## Practical application
- Aim for roughly **three-quarters of your total working volume at 6-15 reps**.
- Allocate about **one quarter** across sets of fewer than 6 reps and sets of more than 15, splitting as suits your goals.
- Keep working sets within **2-3 reps of failure**; take only the final set of the final exercise for a body part to true failure.
- If strength is a goal, deliberately include the heavy low-rep work; if hypertrophy is the sole goal, the heavy work is optional and mainly serves periodisation.
- Recap of the earlier chapters he restates: 10-20 sets per body part per week; each body part trained at least 2× per week; upper/lower is his favourite split.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Equivalence across rep ranges holds only under matched effort and matched volume — sloppy high-rep sets against all-out low-rep sets will not produce the same result.
- Whether metabolic stress genuinely signals hypertrophy is described as controversial.
- He mentions newer research hinting that more than 20 sets per week may suit advanced trainees, but treats that as speculative.
- Heavy low-rep training carries real injury and dropout risk — the effect isn't just theoretical.
