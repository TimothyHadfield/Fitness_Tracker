# Supplement Science with Kamal Patel of Examine.com (Part 2)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "SUPPLEMENT SCIENCE with Examine.com | Interview with Kamal Patel | Part 2" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RlGeczWn-bs)) · 2016-04-25 · 35 min
**Topic:** Supplements

*Positions below are Kamal Patel's (Examine.com) unless attributed to Nippard.*

## Bottom line
- **Patel's top three for natural muscle building: creatine, whey protein, vitamin D.** Nothing exotic beats them.
- **For a pump, use nitrates (beetroot), not arginine or citrulline** — arginine/citrulline have a rate-limiting step that nitrates bypass. **Caffeine is the only reliably predictable stimulant.**
- **There are no effective testosterone boosters.** D-aspartic acid and ZMA don't work. What matters is supporting production — most importantly, getting enough magnesium (and not from a multivitamin, which almost always uses poorly absorbed magnesium oxide).
- **Creatine timing is irrelevant** for muscle building; you saturate over time at 3-5 g/day regardless.
- **For sleep, blue-light-blocking amber glasses beat any sleep supplement for most people.** Melatonin helps you fall asleep more than it improves sleep quality; use sustained-release.
- **Patel's closing line on muscle building: "rather than a magical new supplement, go for a magical new sleep routine."**

## Pre-workout design [01:00]
Asked what he'd put in a homemade pre-workout, Patel named **nitrates** and **caffeine** as the core. He would not include arginine or citrulline — neither is as effective as nitrates for blood flow, because the arginine/citrulline pathway has a rate-limiting step and nitrates don't. Best current nitrate source is **beetroot**; beet *liquid* beats beet powder because you'd need a lot of powder capsules, unless you're happy mixing powder into water as a "beet Gatorade."

Nippard raised the standard argument that citrulline beats arginine (arginine breaks down in the digestive tract and absorbs poorly; citrulline converts to arginine, then to nitric oxide). Patel agreed but said nitrates simply get there more directly. Supplements combining citrulline and arginine are unnecessary.

Beyond that, it depends: **BCAAs** if you train fasted (Patel notes oddly many people he knows do), and possibly **beta-alanine** — though he stresses no supplement has as high a responder rate as protein and creatine.

**Caffeine** is the one stimulant with a predictable response, at least for people not already drinking a lot daily. Most other stimulants either do nothing for a large fraction of people, produce a weak response, or produce too much shakiness.

## Nootropics and huperzine [04:05]
Nootropics were the first supplements Patel ever capped himself, buying bulk powder and running informal self-experiments. His honest finding: **huperzine A worked for him, but so did the placebo he made** — he thinks he is highly placebo-susceptible. His advice: try things, and if they help, great — but don't also try a placebo, because you'll realise it's placebo. With 10-20 major nootropics available, scattershot experimentation will probably turn up one that works for you.

He said nootropics are probably better kept in a separate capsule than mixed into a pre-workout, and better still to cap your own so you can trial combinations.

**Nippard raised a dosing-homogeneity concern:** huperzine is dosed in micrograms while every other pre-workout ingredient is dosed in grams, so if it clusters in one part of a tub you could get a mega-dose. He said he is wary of powdered pre-workouts containing huperzine. Patel agreed capping your own controls for this, and doesn't know a way around it in bulk powder — whey protein is homogeneous, homeopathy is parts-per-trillion, and low-dose nootropics sit awkwardly in the middle. His added point: even setting side effects aside, if the dose varies batch to batch you can never tell whether it's helping you.

## Caffeine tolerance [08:45]
The standard answer is to stop caffeine for a while, and Patel says it's the best and cleanest option — but easier said than done, since people who quit have genuinely bad days, and you have to decide how many bad days you'll trade for the benefit.

Alternatives are thin. **Caffeine + theanine** can both blunt the over-excitatory effects of caffeine and, for a rare minority, accentuate focus — so stacking may let you use a lower caffeine dose. Beyond that, lowering the dose and adding things that synergise is all there is.

On **TeaCrine** (captions garble it as "ton tea" — name uncertain), then a fashionable product: Patel wasn't deeply familiar, but likened it to HMB — when there are only two studies and they show strong effects, you need replication in a larger, more diverse sample before believing it. Possibly worth trying.

## Top three muscle-building supplements [11:18]
**Creatine, whey protein, vitamin D.**

Vitamin D is the surprising one, since trials linking it to muscle gain are few and weak. Patel's reasoning: most of Examine's audience lives above 20 degrees latitude, so if you don't pay attention you won't get enough — especially with darker skin. His own first vitamin D test in 2006 read about **19** (quite low) despite years of taking a multivitamin at over 100% RDA with fat, which suggests his vitamin D metabolism differs from most people's. He thinks a 12-month vitamin D trial would show a stronger effect than short trials, that it may work through mechanisms unlike anabolics, and that the testosterone and immunity links matter.

**Patel's broader methodological point [13:23]:** short trials often fail to report results by intention-to-treat, so dropouts get handled inconsistently. If two of twenty subjects drop out because they got sick, they may have been the ones with low vitamin D — and in a year-long trial, better vitamin D status would have meant better immunity, fewer missed sessions and more gains. Consequence: **the supplements that show up as "muscle-building" are only the ones with obvious direct effects, never the indirect ones.**

## Creatine timing and cycling [14:23]
No meaningful timing effect. Whether you load 10-20 g/day then drop down, take 5 g/day, or even 3 g/day, you saturate the muscle over time; what matters is total lifting over time, not any single session. Nippard cited a study from **Jose Antonio's lab** showing an acute benefit to post-workout timing, which many people then treated as a rule. Patel: the acute effect is useful in competition, but that's not most people; you may saturate slightly faster taking it post-workout, but you'll saturate eventually either way.

On cycling: a creatine researcher Examine interviewed personally stops for a few weeks after about two months — not for a strong reason. Creatine levels drop off slowly after stopping, there's no known sensitisation effect, and you don't stop producing your own. So taking time off doesn't hurt and **saves money if nothing else.**

## Testosterone boosters and magnesium [16:54]
Asked about D-aspartic acid and ZMA, Patel joked "the one everybody should take is horny goat weed" before answering plainly: **there are no good test boosters.** Boosting testosterone is really about maintaining the things that support production — and **the number one item is magnesium**, which many people lack.

Critically: don't assume your multivitamin covers it. Around 90%+ of multivitamins use **magnesium oxide**, which is poorly absorbed; any other form is better. Magnesium is a bulky nutrient, so unless your multivitamin requires several pills you're not getting enough. Food sources: nuts, legumes, and — top source for Americans purely by volume consumed — coffee and tea.

Patel notes that when people describe vague chronic complaints (more social anxiety than they used to have, poor sleep), the answer is often an overlooked mineral. **Correcting a magnesium deficiency improves sleep.** For someone on a good supplement or an excellent diet it's a non-issue, but otherwise it's worth trying.

## Sleep supplements [18:57]
If creatine is at the "helps more than half of people" end of the spectrum, sleep supplements are at the opposite end — it's very hard to predict whether one will work for you, because sleep problems are complicated.

- **Melatonin:** theoretically should help almost everybody, but doesn't. It improves time-to-fall-asleep more often than it improves sleep *quality*. **Use sustained-release**, because with regular melatonin a proportion of people wake after an hour or two and can't get back to sleep.
- **Lavender** (pills or aromatherapy) reduces anxiety and lets intrusive thoughts fade. **Lemon balm** and **glycine** can help too. But all of these work for only a small scattering of people.
- **Blue-light-blocking amber glasses are better than any supplement for most people** — cheaper than a bottle of pills and reusable forever. The only cost is looking silly. Researchers at **John Carroll University in Ohio** recommend wearing them from sunset, which Patel thinks is excessive unless sleep is ruining your life; he suggests starting with **30-60 minutes before bed** and titrating up or down.

**Titration is his general method:** treat non-pill interventions (glasses, aromatherapy) as supplements, vary the dose, and track your response with an app. He's wary of the Quantified Self movement for the healthy "worried well," but for people with an actual problem, tracking is exactly the right time. In one physician-supervised tracking program he worked on, results were astounding. His example: **fish oil helps far more when tracked than when merely taken**, simply because you remember to take it and you notice whether it's working. The advice people never take: if you can't tell a supplement is doing anything and it doesn't have strong enough evidence to justify an unfelt benefit, **stop paying for it** — instead of taking it for 20-24 months on faith.

## Mindfulness [23:05]
Patel: meditation researchers say that if you could put mindfulness meditation in a capsule it would be one of the most powerful drugs available. Bodybuilders are already highly aware of intake gram-by-gram and of movement and posture; the missing third leg is awareness of mental state. Meditators show measurably thicker regions of the brain (mindfulness and compassion meditation research), though he flags the obvious confound — maybe those people had thicker regions to begin with — while thinking the meditation is most likely causal.

The mechanism for body composition is indirect: what limits gains isn't grams of protein, it's **getting sick, stress, poor sleep and injury** — all influenced by mindfulness. Patel, who has had several shoulder surgeries and can no longer lift, describes ruminating over which specific incident caused the damage; teasing those thoughts apart is where the habit pays off.

**Nippard's addition:** he cites Jonathan Haidt (captions say "Jonathan Hey"; he calls him a sociologist — Haidt is a social psychologist) with the argument that a free pill giving better mood, wellbeing, sleep and productivity with no side effects would obviously be taken — and that's essentially what meditation is. Patel calls it **the master habit**: people into health love habits (clean eating, intermittent fasting, the gym), but controlling your thoughts is the ultimate one. For anyone with sleep or anxiety issues, look at mindfulness first — not necessarily formal meditation, just awareness of your thoughts.

## Obscure and emerging supplements [28:43]
**Tendon repair peptides:** Patel pointed to two experimental compounds with three-letter, three-number names discussed on SuppVersity (Adel Moussa's site). They are oral, available online but not on Amazon, have little research and much of it in vitro. He raised them because injuries are hard to treat without injections, and if you're not on growth hormone or steroids there are few options.

**But start simpler:** for a tendon problem, eat the things tendons are made of — collagen. Either food (he suggests Vietnamese soup with tendon) or supplements. Notably, **small doses can work in ways large ones don't: 40 mg of undenatured collagen (UC-II) can help where 10 g of regular collagen doesn't**, via a desensitisation effect similar to allergy treatments — taken away from meals, it passes through the gut somewhat intact and reduces immune attack on your connective tissue. This may apply even without diagnosed rheumatoid arthritis.

**For genuinely stubborn tendon/ligament problems:** platelet-rich plasma is expensive but probably stronger than any supplement. Before PRP, try **prolotherapy** (dextrose injections) — evidence is mixed, likely because efficacy depends on the joint; better for hinge joints like knee and elbow, worse for something like the wrist. See a physical medicine and rehabilitation (PM&R) or sports medicine doctor, and **exhaust these options before surgery**, since even a few hundred dollars is worth it to avoid an operation that could affect you for life.

**Anything new for muscle building?** No. New things come from tiny samples, and Patel won't recommend anything until there are at least two studies. Anything genuinely new and effective is likely to be a pro-hormone, because there are no great non-hormonal ways to boost muscle. He cites **ashwagandha** — "the king of Indian herbs" — which had one study showing massive muscle gain, but in **untrained** individuals, and he is wary because **Indian and Chinese trials report positive results far more often than US trials do** (he notes he is himself Indian).

## Practical application
- Build a pre-workout from **beetroot (liquid preferred) + caffeine**. Skip arginine and citrulline. Add BCAAs only if training fasted; beta-alanine is optional.
- Take **creatine at 3-5 g/day, any time of day.** Loading and timing are optional refinements. Cycling off is harmless and saves money.
- Take **whey protein** and **vitamin D**, especially if you live above 20 degrees latitude or have darker skin. Get your vitamin D level tested rather than assuming a multivitamin covers it.
- **Skip all test boosters.** Supplement **magnesium** in any form except oxide, and don't count what's in your multivitamin.
- For sleep: try **amber blue-blocking glasses for 30-60 minutes before bed** before spending on supplements. If using melatonin, use sustained-release.
- **Track any supplement you take** and stop anything you can't detect a benefit from unless the evidence for an unfelt benefit is strong.
- For tendon issues: collagen first (including low-dose UC-II at ~40 mg), then prolotherapy, then PRP, and surgery last.
- Avoid powdered pre-workouts containing microgram-dosed ingredients like huperzine A (Nippard's homogeneity concern, which Patel accepted).

## Caveats & unknowns
- Recorded in 2016 — the supplement evidence base has moved since, particularly on citrulline, ashwagandha and creatine dosing.
- Several names are garbled by the auto-captions: the fashionable stimulant discussed at [10:17] ("ton tea", most plausibly TeaCrine/theacrine) and the two tendon peptides, which Patel could not name on air. Treat these as unverified.
- Patel repeatedly flags his own placebo susceptibility with nootropics and declines to endorse home-capping in his Examine.com capacity.
- The vitamin D recommendation is explicitly Patel's reasoning from mechanism and trial-design limitations, not from direct muscle-gain evidence, which he concedes is weak.
