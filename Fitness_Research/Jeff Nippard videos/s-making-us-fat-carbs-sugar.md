# What's Really Making Us Fat? Calories, Carbs and Sugar with Stephan Guyenet

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "What's Really Making Us Fat? Carbs? Sugar? (Joe Rogan Response) ft. Stephan Guyenet" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t30tPISf5cw)) · 2019-03-30 · 72 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Energy balance is the main driver of body fatness.** Carbohydrate vs fat ratio makes almost no difference to fat gain or loss when calories are held constant.
- But intake isn't a passive choice — it's **regulated by brain circuits** that set appetite, cravings and food-cue reactivity, largely non-consciously.
- **Sugar isn't the primary cause of obesity.** Sugar intake has fallen ~15-23% in the US since 1999 and ~22% in the UK over half a century while obesity rose.
- Craving and addiction-like eating track the **combination of carbohydrate and fat** (plus salt, glutamate, texture), not sugar alone.
- Practical hierarchy for the average person: **control the food environment → eat simple whole foods → raise protein → exercise, sleep, manage stress →** only then consider restricting carbs or fat.

Guest: Stephan Guyenet, PhD in neuroscience, 12 years researching the neuroscience of body fatness, author of *The Hungry Brain*. (The auto-captions mangle his surname throughout — "Stefan DNA", "G&A", "Guillen a" — the correct spelling is from the video title.)

## The two positions being contrasted [03:36]

**Gary Taubes:** carbohydrates, especially refined carbs and sugar, are the "bad calories" causing obesity; calories themselves are close to irrelevant. Taubes accepts the first law of thermodynamics but argues intake and expenditure are **dependent** — overeat fat and you burn it off; overeat carbs, insulin rises, energy goes into fat tissue and expenditure stalls or drops. In *The Case Against Sugar* he argues explicitly that sugar is overwhelmingly the primary determinant of obesity and chronic disease, and that calorie intake, physical activity and dietary fat are basically irrelevant.

**Guyenet:** calories are the main driver, but body fatness is regulated in the brain, not passively chosen. He and Taubes **agree** that people with obesity are not simply lean people eating more — there is a genuine regulatory change. They disagree on the mechanism: for Guyenet those regulatory pathways act primarily **via calorie intake**.

On consensus: within obesity research from an energy-balance and neuroscience perspective, Guyenet's view is predominant. **David Ludwig** (Harvard) agrees with Taubes almost verbatim; **Robert Lustig** and **Jim Johnson** hold related but distinct positions. Very few researchers accept Taubes's model — the impression of a raging scientific debate comes from popular health writers, not the field.

## The evidence for energy balance [24:41]

**Intake rises with body fatness.** Using accurate methods (respirometry to measure expenditure in weight-stable people, so intake can be inferred), and correcting for height, sex and activity level, calorie intake goes up roughly:
- **+10%** for overweight but not obese,
- **+20%** for mild obesity,
- **+35%** for very pronounced obesity.

This is not because fat itself burns much — heavier people carry **more lean mass**, which is metabolically active, so they sit at a new equilibrium eating more and burning more. Restrict a person eating 30% more by 30% and they lose fat.

**Overfeeding carb vs fat gives the same result.** Overfeed with only carbohydrate or only fat and you see the same fat gain; underfeed and you see similar fat loss. The strongest evidence is a **meta-analysis by Kevin Hall** of ~20 metabolic ward studies, where every morsel is controlled. Holding calories constant, carb vs fat made almost no difference. There was a small but statistically significant difference **favouring carbohydrate** (slightly less fat gain), too small to matter practically.

**So is a calorie a calorie?** "To a close approximation," yes — not identical in every scenario, but for practical purposes the same. Macronutrients still matter in real life, but the **mechanism is calorie intake**: low-carb and low-fat diets both work by reducing spontaneous intake.

**Protein.** Its thermic effect is substantial — roughly **20-25% of protein calories** are lost to it — yet in tightly controlled trials it doesn't move body fatness much. In free-living conditions higher protein works better, mainly through **satiety**, which is still the intake side of the equation. Guyenet: if someone asks how to control intake and body composition, **raising protein is one of the first things he recommends**.

## Why individuals differ [28:17]

Not everyone gains the same fat from the same surplus. Some burn off most of the excess; others store nearly every extra calorie.

- **Genetics.** **Claude Bouchard** overfed roughly a dozen pairs of identical twins. Within a pair, gains were nearly identical in amount and in location; **across** pairs they varied hugely — one pair gaining 10 lb, another 3 lb, one storing in the belly, another subcutaneously all over. In a country like the modern US, about **75% of differences in body fatness are attributable to genetic differences**.
- **NEAT** (non-exercise activity thermogenesis) is one mechanism for burning off a surplus — and it is **non-conscious**; nobody decides to fidget more.
- The only pre-existing measure Guyenet knows of that predicts resistance to fat gain when overfed is **physical fitness level**.

**The thermostat model.** Body fatness runs on a negative feedback loop, like a home thermostat. Lose fat and the **starvation response** kicks in: hunger rises, satiety takes more food, cravings increase, food-cue reactivity increases, metabolic rate falls. Gain fat quickly and there's a defence in the other direction — but it's **weaker and far more variable between individuals**. Almost everyone defends against fat loss; only some defend well against fat gain.

**Free-living data.** The **DIETFITS study from Chris Gardner's group at Stanford** compared low-carb vs low-fat, neither calorie-controlled, both built on whole unrefined foods. At one year: **low-fat lost ~12 lb, low-carb ~13 lb** — nowhere near statistically significant. But the **individual spread was huge and looked identical on both diets**; a few lost enormous amounts, a few gained. Individual variation dwarfs the average difference between interventions. Whether that reflects biology, adherence or life circumstances is unknown, though Guyenet suspects some biology is involved.

## Sugar [47:23]

Guyenet is no fan of refined sugar but calls the current focus a distraction. Its independent harms — it's an empty calorie, and **fructose is metabolised almost exclusively in the liver**, which at high intakes can overload the liver and promote **liver insulin resistance** and metabolic abnormalities — appear only at **higher intakes**. A couple of teaspoons in coffee won't do it.

The bigger issue: sugar **makes us eat more** by increasing the seductiveness of food. Ice cream without sweetness, or fat-free, wouldn't get eaten by the hundreds of calories after a large meal.

**The correlation problem.** NHANES data shows US sugar intake down **15-23% since 1999** while obesity rose; the same trend appears in Australia and the UK (down ~22% over about half a century, covering the entire UK obesity epidemic). Counter-arguments offered by sugar advocates — a threshold effect, intergenerational effects from mothers' or grandmothers' intake, or that only lean people cut sugar — have no supporting evidence Guyenet is aware of. He's careful: this isn't ironclad, and it doesn't show sugar plays no role. What it does refute is sugar being the **primary** cause, since a primary cause would have to correlate.

**Other levers work too.** Physical activity has a large effect on insulin sensitivity: athletes have high sensitivity, make them sedentary for **10 days** and it drops substantially; make sedentary people exercise and it rises. Multi-year RCTs with real diabetes endpoints show exercise **reduces diabetes risk by roughly 45-75%**. **Orlistat**, which cuts dietary fat absorption by about 30%, reduces diabetes risk by roughly **35-45%**; **acarbose**, which reduces carbohydrate absorption, shows something similar. The through-line: **anything that improves energy balance and produces fat loss — even modest loss — substantially protects against diabetes.** Guyenet's summary of diabetes causation: **excess body fatness, physical inactivity and genetics** are the big three, with sugar possibly contributing a little independently.

## Food addiction [58:53]

Whether "addiction" applies to food in the strict clinical sense is contested, but the research community agrees **addiction-like behaviour** around certain foods clearly exists. Surveys of the most craved and most addiction-like foods point not to sugar but to **combinations of carbohydrate and fat**: chocolate (the most commonly craved), ice cream, baked goods — and savoury, sugar-free ones like french fries, pizza, popcorn. Add salt, glutamate (umami) and appealing texture and you get strong dopamine responses. Chocolate also contains **theobromine** (garbled in captions as "di bromine"), a drug that activates the dopamine system. Addiction is, essentially, dopamine spiking hard enough that you over-prioritise the behaviour and it harms your life. Plain sugar alone drives addiction-like behaviour far less often than these combinations.

## Practical application [64:01]

Guyenet's closing recommendations, in order:

1. **Don't blame yourself.** Roughly 75% of body-fatness differences are genetic, largely via how the brain is wired; appetite and cravings are mostly non-conscious circuits doing what they evolved to do in an environment they weren't built for.
2. **Control your food environment**, at home and at work. Dopamine spikes on cues — sight, smell, even just knowing something's in the cupboard. Keep tempting food invisible and out of the house. His own kitchen: fresh fruit that needs peeling (a small effort barrier), salted peanuts still in the shell, no cookies or soda visible. The payoff is that you **stop craving what isn't available**.
3. **Eat a simple whole-food diet** — unrefined, lower calorie density, avoiding refined flours (and flour foods generally), added sugars and added fats. Cook from single ingredients; don't try to make everything maximally delicious. Higher satiety and moderate palatability regulate intake for you.
4. **Increase protein** — reduces intake and supports muscle mass during fat loss.
5. **Regular physical activity, plenty of sleep, managed stress.**
6. **Only then**, if the above hasn't got you to your goal, consider **restricting carbohydrate or fat**.

Guyenet does not dismiss calorie counting — it works for many in the fitness community — but he doesn't think it's the best route for the average person, who wants to eat until full and not be fat. He notes low-carb's real advantage is **simplicity**: one rule, automatically improving energy balance. Nippard's own trick along the same lines: **alternate carbs and fats meal to meal so you rarely combine them**, which does nothing magical hormonally but reduces how much you eat.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Individual variation in response to carb vs fat restriction may be partly real biology, but Hall's meta-analysis didn't examine individual data and the DIETFITS spread can't be cleanly attributed to biology versus adherence.
- Why some people burn off a surplus and others store it is largely unexplained beyond genetics and fitness level.
- Whether food "addiction" meets the clinical definition remains controversial.
- The sugar-down/obesity-up correlation is suggestive, not ironclad, and doesn't rule out sugar as one contributing factor.
