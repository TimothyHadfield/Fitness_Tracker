# Dumbbell Row Technique: Why Controlled "Cheating" Makes Biomechanical Sense

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Do Dumbbell Rows: Build a Thicker Back With Proper 'Cheating'" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=djKXLt7kv7Q)) · 2019-10-15 · 7 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Rows build both width and thickness — shoulder extension (lats) plus scapular retraction (mid traps), supported by research showing high lat *and* trap activation.
- The dumbbell row has a poor resistance curve: no tension at the bottom where you're strongest, maximum tension at the top where you're weakest. You fail because of the top, not because the lats are fatigued.
- A **moderate amount of torso rotation** to generate momentum at the bottom fixes that curve and makes the exercise more effective — an idea Greg Nuckols raised in MASS.
- Torso should sit **0-20 degrees above parallel** to the floor; staying upright allows heavier loads but badly cuts lat range of motion.
- Default rep range: **8-12 per side**.

## The resistance curve problem [00:32]
At the bottom of a row the dumbbell hangs directly beneath the shoulder, so there is virtually no external moment arm and almost no tension on the lats — exactly where you're strongest. At the top, the moment arm is largest and lat tension is highest — exactly where you're weakest. When you fail a row it's because the top portion is so hard; you could probably rep out another 10 or so in the bottom half. The lats aren't necessarily fatigued, they just can't complete the range against that steep profile.

Greg Nuckols's proposed solution in the previous month's MASS issue: use momentum. Without some momentum you can't grind out the last few inches. A little cheating makes the row *more* effective, not less, by generating speed at the bottom that carries you through the top.

## Setup and execution
- Start with your **non-dominant side** on all unilateral work, to prevent imbalances.
- Hand and knee on the bench, other foot planted wide to the side for a stable base.
- Torso close to perpendicular to the line of pull — **0 to 20 degrees above parallel** to the floor.
- Grip the middle of the handle, chest up, head neutral, back straight and not rounding.
- Begin each rep by letting the scapula **protract** at the bottom, so the traps also work a full range.
- Let the dumbbell drift slightly forward at the bottom rather than stopping directly under the shoulder — this stretches the lats more and keeps tension more consistent.
- Drag the dumbbell back in an **arc toward the hips**, not straight up. Lower it down and forward under control.
- Keep the elbow close to the torso rather than flared out to the side.
- If grip or biceps fail before the back, place the pinky toward the back end of the dumbbell, or use straps.

## How much cheating
Three positions viewed from the front:
- **Fully strict** — shoulders stay horizontal, zero torso rotation. He advises against this.
- **Full "bro row"** — heavy torso rotation to heave the weight up, then dropping it on the negative. Also not recommended.
- **The middle ground** — moderate torso rotation that assists the lats at their weakest point, letting other muscles contribute more evenly and preventing premature termination of the set caused purely by the lats' inability to finish the range.

The point of the momentum is to even out the lats' strength curve, not to move more weight for show. Cap it, because momentum is a slippery slope between genuine progressive overload and "progressive cheating."

## Applied to other rows
- **Pendlay row:** he performs it much stricter. It's a more even mix of lats and traps, so it benefits less from moderate cheating.
- **Standing barbell row (more upright posture):** moderate cheating can help if the lats fatigue early and stop you reaching full scapular retraction at the top.
- **Cable row, close neutral grip:** more lat-dominant, so he uses more momentum — leaning forward at the hips and letting the lower-back erectors assist the lats through the end range.
- **Cable row, wide grip:** more scapular-retraction based, more mid trap and rear delt, so he keeps an upright posture and focuses on protracting at the bottom and retracting at the top.
- **Chest-supported T-bar row:** a place where strict form is appropriate.

## Three common errors
1. **Losing control.** Some momentum is fine, but once legs, calves and the planted arm get involved it becomes a different movement and robs tension from the back.
2. **Staying too upright.** Rowing off the dumbbell rack or the back of an incline bench takes the lats through only a partial range. On any row, keep the torso close to perpendicular to the line of pull — don't lean too far back on a cable row, don't sit too upright on a dumbbell row.
3. **Ultra-strict rowing.** Common in new lifters. On dumbbell and cable rows, excessive rigidity seriously reduces overloading potential and can stall progression.

## Practical application
- 8-12 reps per side is his default — the row works as both a heavy compound and a mind-muscle-connection movement, so low and high reps both work, but working each side separately makes moderate reps the time-efficient choice.
- Use a controlled amount of torso rotation to get through the top of the range; ban leg drive and pushing off the planted arm.
- Load reference point: he rarely goes lighter than **80-100 lb dumbbells** on rows. If you've trained for several years and are still on the lightest dumbbells, loosen up and move more weight.

## Caveats & unknowns
- "Moderate" cheating isn't quantified; the boundary between it and a bro row is judgment-based.
- The momentum argument comes from a MASS research review discussion rather than a direct trial comparing strict and cheated rows for hypertrophy.
- His 80-100 lb reference is personal, not a target for everyone.
