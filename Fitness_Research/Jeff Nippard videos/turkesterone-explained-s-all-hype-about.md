# Turkesterone: What the Evidence Actually Shows

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Turkesterone Explained: What's All The Hype About?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_lcSDTnZG0)) · 2022-01-28 · 9 min
**Topic:** Supplements

## Bottom line
- There is **no human research on turkesterone itself**. Every study cited by its promoters is either on animals/insects, or on a *different* ecdysteroid.
- The best-designed human trial (Campbell et al., 2006, 45 trained men, 8 weeks, DEXA) found ecdysteroids performed no better than placebo on fat-free mass, 1RM strength, or testosterone measures.
- The 2019 study everyone quotes is unusable: the supplement was lab-tested and contained **6 mg** of ecdysterone against a labelled **100 mg** — 6% of the claimed dose — and body composition was measured by single-frequency bioelectrical impedance.
- The ISSN's 2018 position review explicitly does **not recommend** ecdysterones for training adaptations or performance.
- Nippard's verdict: not science-based, not worth buying. He'd revisit if better evidence emerges and there are no side effects.

## What turkesterone is
[00:31] Turkesterone is one of many **ecdysteroids** — the plant and insect analogue of hormones like testosterone. Humans make testosterone; plants and insects make turkesterone and related compounds. Because turkesterone and testosterone are structurally somewhat similar, the hypothesis arose that the plant/insect version might also be anabolic in humans.

## The research trail
[01:01] **1970s, Russian team.** Administered turkesterone to mouse liver; the abstract (on a non-secure Russian database) reports stimulated *liver* protein synthesis — which is not the same thing as muscle protein synthesis. The full text turns out to be 145 pages of broken Russian text on rodent liver metabolism, so nothing usable.

[01:33] **1970s–90s.** Scattered papers looked at protein synthesis in various animals and insects, all published in obscure journals with substandard acute designs. Nothing found was compelling enough to prompt human research for decades.

[01:33] **2006, Campbell and colleagues, University of Florida.** 45 weight-trained men randomized to four groups: three took different potentially anabolic supplements (including an ecdysteroid group — not turkesterone specifically, but a structurally similar compound in the same class), one took a placebo. Subjects were blinded, which matters because expectation alone can improve results. Training was a 4 day/week upper-lower split for 8 weeks. Results: **no difference in fat-free mass or 1RM strength** between any group, and **no differences in free testosterone, total testosterone, or testosterone:cortisol ratio**. Body composition was measured by DEXA.

[02:33] **2018, International Society of Sports Nutrition.** A joint review of supplement recommendations concluded firmly that ecdysterones, including turkesterone, are **not recommended** for increasing training adaptations or performance ([Kerksick 2018](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-018-0242-y)).

[03:04] **2019, the study the hype rests on.** 46 men with about one year of training experience, 10-week training program, given either placebo or a Peak Ecdysone supplement "labeled to contain 100 mg of ecdysterone." The ecdysterone groups did show significantly better muscle gains than placebo. EC1 took 2 pills/day; EC2 took 8 pills/day.

## Why the 2019 result doesn't hold up
[04:04] Three problems.

1. **The placebo group lost muscle on average** — odd for beginner lifters on a structured 10-week program.
2. **Measurement.** Body composition was assessed with single-frequency bioelectrical impedance, notoriously unreliable compared to the DEXA used in the 2006 trial.
3. **The supplement was nearly empty.** Lab analysis of the Peak Ecdysone product found **6 mg** of ecdysterone, not the 100 mg on the label — about 6% of the advertised content.

[04:34] Could the pills have been laced with a banned anabolic, as supplement companies sometimes do? The study's supplemental materials show blood and urine samples were collected to test for co-administration of prohibited substances, and **nothing showed up**.

That leaves two plausible explanations: something in the pills that wasn't tested for, or a **false positive**. Nippard consulted **Dr Eric Trexler**, who noted that type 1 errors are not exotic — a p-value of 0.049 is by definition a ~5% chance result, and with small groups of 12–20 people, sampling error means groups don't always balance out despite randomization. Four unusually gifted responders landing in one group is enough. This is why replication across many independent groups matters before a finding is trusted.

[06:35] And note again: ecdysterone is not the same compound as turkesterone.

## Why anecdotes don't fill the gap
[07:05] Turkesterone's case rests on anecdote and marketing. Anecdotes can't control confounders: someone starting a new supplement often simultaneously tightens their diet, trains harder because they feel motivated by the new stack, is a newbie who'd have gained anyway, or placebos themselves into results because they bought the hype. Controlled trials use multiple subjects specifically to strip those variables out.

## Practical application
- Don't buy turkesterone. There is no direct human evidence for it, and the closest-matching evidence is null.
- When evaluating any supplement claim, check three things: was the study in humans, was the tested compound the same one being sold, and was the product's actual content verified by lab analysis.
- Treat single positive studies with small samples and impedance-based body composition measures as weak signals, not proof.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Nippard and Trexler explicitly do not claim the 2019 result *was* a false positive — there is no way to know. The point is the findings don't add up.
- Absence of evidence isn't proof of no effect; Nippard says he'd change his position and take it if convincing evidence emerges, assuming no side effects.
- Nothing in the video addresses safety or side effects — the case against it is efficacy, not harm.
