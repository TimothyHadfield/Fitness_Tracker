# You Are Not Training Hard Enough: How Many Reps Lifters Leave in the Tank

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "You Are Not Training Hard Enough!" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HkUtDVTref8)) · 2018-01-06 · 9 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- In a 2017 *JSCR* study of 160 trained males, the average lifter got **16 reps** with the weight he calls his "10 rep weight" — six reps left in the tank on a typical set.
- **Not one subject out of 160** failed to beat 10 reps. Zero percent.
- Over **40% were training at RPE 4 or lower**; 13% technically trained at a negative RPE, leaving more than 10 reps unused.
- The hypertrophy intensity threshold is probably around **RPE 7**. Leaving more than ~3 reps in the tank on most sets likely costs you growth.
- Roughly half the six-rep gap is bad RPE judgement, half is unwillingness to sit in the discomfort near failure.

## The study design
160 males, all with at least 6 months of training experience and bench press a regular part of their routine. Researchers asked each: *what weight would you typically use for a set of 10?* — not their 10RM, just a normal training day's working weight. They then had subjects perform a set with that weight **to failure** rather than stopping at 10, to see how many reps trainees habitually leave unused.

80% of subjects normally benched first in their workout, so most were tested in roughly their usual fresh state.

## The results [03:34]
Reps achieved to failure with a self-selected "10 rep" load:

- **0%** got fewer than 10 reps. Every single subject beat their usual number.
- **22%** got 10–12 reps — working at roughly RPE 8–10.
- **31%** (the largest group) got 13–15 reps — 3–5 reps in the tank.
- **21%** got 16–18 reps — 6–8 reps in the tank.
- **26%** got 19–20+ reps — practically doubling their usual reps.
- **13%** got more than 20 reps, i.e. more than 10 reps left over, a "negative RPE."
- **Over 40%** overall were training at RPE 4 or lower.

In the video's demonstration [02:32], a subject took 45 lb to failure for 16 reps against a self-nominated 10-rep load — landing exactly on the study average, and Jeff notes he probably still stopped a rep or two shy of true failure.

## Why the numbers matter
Most of these subjects listed hypertrophy as their primary goal. Prior research indicates there is an **intensity threshold you must cross for meaningful hypertrophy**. That threshold is not precisely defined but sits somewhere around **RPE 7**. Leaving more than three reps in the tank on most sets is likely leaving gains on the table; training at RPE 4 or lower is a guaranteed way to leave a lot on the table.

## The RPE-estimation problem [06:40]
A separate study by **Steel and colleagues** flipped the question: given a set load, how many reps do you think you could get? On average subjects **underestimated their max reps by 3–4 reps**, with beginners worst. Note this asks about predicted failure point, not habitual reps.

Combining the two: of the six-rep gap in the main study, roughly three reps come from genuinely mis-reading proximity to failure, and roughly three from laziness or avoidance of the discomfort of a near-failure set.

## Confounders Jeff considers and rejects
- **Freshness:** if subjects normally benched later in a session, testing them fresh would inflate reps. But 80% did bench first normally, so this is minor.
- **Supervision effect:** people perform better when watched by a coach or researcher. Real, but Jeff argues it cannot explain a six-rep disparity on its own — and it likely cancels against the opposite bias, where some subjects **over-report** their typical working weight to impress the researcher.

## Practical application
- Periodically test a "typical" working set to actual failure to recalibrate. You will probably be shocked.
- Keep most hypertrophy work at **RPE 7 or higher** — no more than about 3 reps in reserve.
- Treat RPE as a skill that needs deliberate practice, especially as a beginner. Assume your first estimates are 3–4 reps too conservative.
- If you have never taken a set to true failure, do it occasionally so you know what the ceiling feels like and can anchor your RPE calls against it.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The hypertrophy intensity threshold is not precisely established; RPE ~7 is an approximation.
- Findings are bench press specific in trained-but-not-advanced men; behaviour on other lifts may differ.
- Even the video's demo subject, coached and watched, likely stopped short of genuine failure — reinforcing how hard true failure is to reach.
- The video is built on a review article by **Eric Helms** in MASS.
