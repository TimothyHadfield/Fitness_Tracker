# How Much Do Micronutrients Matter For Fat Loss And Muscle Gain?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How Much Do MICROS Matter For Fat Loss & Muscle Gain?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=moslJYVYI10)) · 2019-03-08 · 5 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- For body composition, the three things that matter are sufficient protein, the right calorie balance, and lifting weights. Everything else is detail.
- Protein target: at least 0.8 g per pound of bodyweight, with 1 g per pound as the easy-to-remember number.
- Micronutrients matter much less than macros for fat loss — but deficiencies are more common than people think and can drag down performance and hormones.
- Getting extremely lean on a junk-food IIFYM approach demonstrably works; whether it's healthy is a separate question.
- Carb-to-fat ratio is individual: some people lose fat better low-carb, some higher-carb, many either way.

## The three pillars
For anyone wanting to add muscle, lose some fat, and improve health, focus on:

1. Sufficient protein — at least 0.8 g/lb bodyweight, 1 g/lb as a simple target.
2. A calorie deficit if the goal is fat loss.
3. Resistance training.

Nippard's point is that overwhelming a relative beginner with minutiae — sodium, calcium, magnesium, organic versus conventional produce — distracts them from the variables actually driving body composition change. Nail the big three first, get leaner and more muscular, and health markers improve as a by-product. Refine the diet later.

## Does food quality matter at very low body fat?
In 2015 Nippard interviewed Cliff Wilson, whom he considers the best natural bodybuilding coach in the world on track record, and asked whether getting truly shredded (below 6% body fat) comes down purely to hitting macros or whether food choices matter. Wilson's answer, which surprised him: it really just comes down to the macros — he's had clients reach 4-5% body fat eating cereal the whole way down.

Nippard largely trusts that judgment, and notes there are by now thousands of examples of lifters reaching 5-6% body fat with good musculature on an if-it-fits-your-macros approach. It has been shown to work in the field.

## Individual variation in macro ratios
Metabolic ward studies show meaningful individual differences: some people do noticeably better on lower-carb, higher-fat (more keto-style) eating, others do far better on higher carbs and lower fat, and for a third group it makes little difference as long as protein and calories are right. This is something to experiment with personally rather than pick dogmatically.

## Where micronutrients actually bite
Micros are much less important for fat loss than macros, but the body is complex and deficiencies do have downstream physiological effects that show up in how you look and perform. Eating a large volume of micronutrient-void junk while hitting macros — especially while training hard — makes deficiency genuinely plausible.

Nutrients that come to mind first:

- **Calcium, magnesium, zinc** — realistic deficiency candidates on a poor diet.
- **Fibre** — affects digestion.
- **Calcium** — affects energy levels.
- **Vitamin D** — particularly if you live in a northern climate with less sun.
- **Zinc and magnesium** — deficiency can downregulate testosterone, which is bad for performance, muscle gain, and probably fat loss.

So if your diet is bad enough to produce these deficiencies, then yes, food choices do matter.

## Practical application
- Beginners: hit 0.8-1 g protein per pound, set calories for your goal, train with weights. Ignore the rest until those are consistent.
- Advanced/contest prep: macros drive the leanness; you can get very lean on flexible dieting, but don't let micro intake collapse.
- Experiment with your own carb/fat split to find what your fat loss responds to, keeping protein and calories fixed.
- Insure against deficiency: eat fruits and vegetables, watch fibre, and pay attention to calcium, magnesium, zinc, and vitamin D (especially in northern latitudes).

## Caveats & unknowns
- Nippard explicitly declines to address whether a junk-food-based approach is healthy, noting that if you're chasing 5-6% body fat, health isn't your primary concern anyway.
- The Cliff Wilson claim is coaching experience, not controlled data.
- The metabolic ward studies on individual carb/fat response are referenced but not named.
- No specific micronutrient doses or supplement protocols are given.
