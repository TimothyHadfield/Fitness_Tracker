# How Much Protein Can You Actually Use In One Meal?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How Much Protein Can You Absorb In One Meal? (20g? 30g? 100g?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJpdPYedWjc)) · 2018-01-30 · 11 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Absorption** is not the limit — humans absorb virtually all ingested protein, even 300 g. The "20-30 g cap" myth confuses absorption with utilisation for muscle protein synthesis (MPS).
- The MPS ceiling is real but soft, and sits closer to **30-40 g** for most trainees — higher for bigger, older people and after full-body training.
- Phillips' review proposes **0.4 g/kg (~0.18 g/lb) per meal** as a reasonable upper limit: ~22 g for a 120 lb person, ~36 g for a 200 lb person.
- **Total daily protein is what matters most.** ~1 g per pound of bodyweight for relatively lean individuals is plenty.
- For optimisation, spread it across **4-5 meals of ~30-40 g each**; but intermittent fasters eating 80-100 g in one sitting are not wasting most of it.

## Absorption vs utilisation
A 2009 paper on digestion and absorption states that virtually all ingested protein is absorbed by healthy humans. "Absorption" here means passage of amino acids through the gut and intestinal wall into circulation — and that capacity is essentially unlimited. The real question is how much protein above ~20-30 g in one sitting actually **supports muscle building**.

## The case for a ~20 g ceiling — the "muscle full" effect
**Stuart Phillips' research group, 2015 review** described a *muscle full effect*: as protein dose increases you eventually hit a plateau beyond which more protein does nothing extra for MPS. That ceiling has commonly been placed around **20 g**.
- The first study to show it compared **20 g vs 40 g of whole egg protein** after a leg workout — no significant difference.
- Another found **30 g of beef protein** as effective as **90 g** for stimulating MPS.
- Further research showed protein beyond 20 g increased **amino acid oxidation** (burned for energy) and **urea production**, implying a per-meal utilisation limit.

## Three factors that push the ceiling up
1. **Body size.** More muscle means more protein to max out MPS. The Phillips review's **0.4 g/kg (~0.18 g/lb)** per-meal figure accounts for this and for individual variability — meaning 22-30 g may be genuinely insufficient for larger lifters.
2. **Amount of muscle trained.** A **2016 study** found **40 g of whey beat 20 g** for MPS. The earlier studies used lower-body-only training; this one used a **full-body routine**. Activating more muscle mass requires more protein to max the response.
3. **Age.** **Anabolic resistance** is a well-established phenomenon — older people need more protein for the same MPS response.

So older, larger trainees doing full-body work could plausibly benefit from **40, 50 g or more** in a single sitting.

## The counter-evidence: intermittent fasting
Most of the above rests on **acute MPS measurements**, not long-term hypertrophy trials, which makes strong conclusions about a hard ceiling speculative. Pushback comes largely from the IF community, where people eat 75-100 g of protein across one or two meals in a 4-8 hour window and still build muscle.
- An IF study (researcher name garbled in captions — flagged as uncertain) found ~**101 g of protein in a single 4-hour eating window** produced **no difference in lean mass preservation** versus a conventional spread-out pattern.
- Another paper found **one meal per day with ~86 g of protein** actually improved body composition versus the same protein split across three meals.
- Limitations: both used **bioelectrical impedance**, which is unreliable, and total daily protein intake was low, which limits real-world applicability.

## Practical application
- **Priority one:** total daily protein. Aim for **~1 g per pound of bodyweight** for relatively lean individuals — that's more than enough.
- **Priority two:** protein quality.
- **Priority three:** if you want to squeeze out every bit of muscle, spread intake fairly evenly across **4-5 meals with ~30-40 g each**. Jeff thinks this is slightly more optimal than one or two huge boluses.
- If you follow intermittent fasting and skew protein into one or two large feedings, don't assume most of it is wasted — evidence and field observation say otherwise.
- If your goal is general shape and size rather than maximal muscle, use whatever meal schedule best lets you hit your daily total.

## Why large doses may not be wasted
- In **mixed meals** with lots of calories from multiple macronutrients, digestion and absorption slow considerably, blunting the "bolus" effect.
- Amino acids not driving MPS may still **limit muscle protein breakdown**, contributing to net muscle balance.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The literature has no clean answer on the exact per-meal utilisation limit. Jeff's read: **~30-40 g** is where the muscle-full effect starts to appear for MPS.
- Acute MPS studies may not predict long-term hypertrophy.
- The intermittent fasting studies have methodological weaknesses (BIA measurement, low total protein).
