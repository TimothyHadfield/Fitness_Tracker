# Neck Training: Warm-Up, Exercises and Loading

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Build A Thicker Neck Fast! (Simple Science-Based Training)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gimeRpdqWQw)) · 2019-04-23 · 8 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- The neck grows fast once trained, precisely because daily life never loads it. Jeff gained about two inches of neck circumference in a year of consistent training.
- Load in the **10–20 rep range, up to 30 reps** — going heavy risks the spine's supporting and soft tissues for no extra benefit if you're training close to failure.
- Always do a **1–2 minute dynamic warm-up** before direct neck work to avoid pulling something or waking up with a "zinger" (a neck so stiff you can't turn your head for days).
- Train forward flexion (sternocleidomastoid), extension (back of the neck), and optionally lateral flexion and rotation.
- Extension gets strong quickly — a single 45 lb plate stops being enough even for 20+ reps, so a head harness becomes near-essential.

## Why train the neck
**Aesthetics.** Side-by-side images differing only in neck width make the difference obvious; celebrities with digitally shrunk necks (his examples: Channing Tatum) look markedly nerdier. Jeff's own neck grew ~2 inches over a year.

**Growth rate.** It responds fast because it receives almost no external loading in daily activity, so any proper stimulus is novel.

**Strength and athletics.** The neck stabilises the head, so strengthening it can improve performance and help prevent concussions and injury. Contact-sport athletes — football, hockey, boxing, MMA — commonly include direct neck work in their strength and conditioning. Jeff and many others also report reduced day-to-day neck pain and headaches.

## Anatomy and movement directions
- **Forward flexion** — mainly the sternocleidomastoid. The primary driver of neck width.
- **Extension** — the back of the neck. Needed for balanced development.
- **Lateral flexion** — also mostly sternocleidomastoid, so not a top priority for width, but helps proportional growth.
- **Rotation / anti-rotation** — matters for certain athletes (MMA fighters, NASCAR drivers). Jeff uses rotation work as pre-activation to warm the whole neck and establish a mind-muscle connection.

## The warm-up (1–2 minutes, do it every time)
1. **8–10 head rotations**, alternating direction so you don't get dizzy.
2. Lying on a bench: **10–12 slow-tempo bodyweight neck curls**. Full range — comfortable stretch at the bottom, chin toward chest at the top. You'll feel a strong contraction and good mind-muscle connection.
3. Turn to the side: **10–12 lateral curls** with head weight only.
4. Same again for the **rear** (extension).
5. A few **5-second stretches** side to side.
6. **Rotational neck curls**: perform a neck curl but aim the chin at the *shoulder* rather than the upper chest, alternating sides rep to rep.
7. **Side-to-side rotations with an isometric hold.** Jeff's favourite — enough on its own to produce a significant neck pump and burn if you have nothing to load with.

## Neck curl (flexion)
**Setup:** hold a plate against the forehead with the shoulders at the back edge of a bench, then curl the neck down and up. Use thick protective headgear or a folded towel between plate and forehead.

**Technique errors to avoid:**
- *Arms doing the work.* Take a looser grip on the plate, or actively press the plate into your forehead, so the arms can't assist.
- *Abs taking over.* Once the neck fatigues, the abs will try to crunch the weight. Curl your legs in under the bench to lock the torso down so the only movement occurs at the neck.

**Loading: moderate-to-light, 10–20 reps, even up to 30.** Heavier loading isn't worth risking the spine's supporting and soft tissues, and training close to failure at higher reps gets the same result with less injury risk.

That said, most people underestimate their neck strength potential. Jeff has done **45 lb for a strict set of 25–30 reps**; Alex from Alpha Destiny is shown doing **90 lb for many reps** (not a recommendation for most people). If you're still stuck on a 5 or 10 lb plate after training a while, raise your standards.

## Neck extension
The simplest setup is a plate on the back of the head while lying chest-down. Jeff no longer uses it, for two reasons: balance is harder to maintain in the chest-down position as the load increases, and the neck is much stronger in extension — a single 45 lb plate stops being enough even for 20+ reps once you're reasonably strong.

**Solution: a head harness.** Jeff uses a NeckFlex (a channel sponsor at the time), which unlike most harnesses can load forward flexion and lateral flexion as well as extension — many harnesses make forward flexion essentially impossible to load. It takes band resistance, includes a door anchor for home training, and includes a chain for loading extension from a cable rack or with a plate. His position: a harness isn't required for neck gains, but it makes training far more convenient and lets you train at home.

**Technique:** full range of motion — full stretch at the bottom, then lift the head only until you reach a **neutral head position** at the top. Do not hyperextend; stop and reverse at neutral.

## Neck bridges
Jeff considers them largely redundant if you're already doing the above, but acknowledges wrestlers and combat athletes benefit from them, and he includes them occasionally himself.

## Practical application
- Warm up 1–2 minutes every session using the seven-step sequence above. Non-negotiable.
- **Neck curls (flexion):** plate on forehead with padding, legs hooked under the bench, loose grip. 10–20 reps (up to 30), close to failure.
- **Neck extension:** head harness strongly preferred over a plate on the back of the head. Same rep range. Stop at neutral, never hyperextend.
- Add lateral flexion for proportional development, and rotation work if your sport demands it.
- Expect visible progress relatively quickly — this is a fast-responding muscle group.
- A complete neck session can be done at home in about 10 minutes with a harness and a door anchor.

## Caveats & unknowns
- No studies are cited in this video; the reasoning is anatomical and practical. Jeff points to his separate neck science video for the underlying research.
- The head harness segment is a sponsored recommendation — he states explicitly that no gear is mandatory.
- The concussion-prevention claim is presented as an accepted rationale in athletic S&C rather than backed by a named study here.
- Heavier neck loading than the 10–30 rep guidance is possible for some (the 90 lb example) but is deliberately not recommended for general trainees.
