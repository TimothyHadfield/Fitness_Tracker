# How Much Fiber to Eat Per Day (and Which Type)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How Much Fiber Should I Eat Per Day?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RRHJ5GSNT6A)) · 2014-08-25 · 6 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- His target: 10% of daily carbohydrate intake as fiber, with an absolute floor of 20 g per day regardless of carb intake.
- The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics recommends 20-35 g per day for adults, and age + 5 grams for children.
- Source matters as much as the number: hitting a fiber goal with supplements and fiber bars misses the micronutrients that whole high-fiber foods carry.
- The satiety benefit of fiber is weaker in the literature than commonly claimed, and appears to depend on viscosity rather than total grams.
- Fiber is not an essential nutrient — you can survive without it — but there are real health benefits to eating plenty.

## Source quality, not just grams
High-fiber diets tend to force high micronutrient intake, because high-fiber foods are usually micronutrient-dense. That link breaks when someone hits a 30-40 g target using Benefiber, Quest bars or Fiber One bars. Fiber has been shown to reduce nutrient absorption — which cuts calories absorbed, but also cuts micronutrients absorbed [01:02]. Loading up on functional fiber supplements gives you the absorption penalty without the nutrient payload.

## The satiety claim is shakier than advertised
The standard claim is that fiber makes you fuller. Nippard says the literature surprised him:

- A 2011 systematic review of 104 randomised controlled studies found only 43% of the studies detected any effect of fiber on appetite, and only by as little as 10%.
- A 2013 review corroborated this: 59% of studies showed no effect of fiber on satiety at all.

He flags the methodological problem honestly — appetite is subjective and hard to measure objectively, and these results do not conclusively show fiber has no satiety effect.

The interesting finding from the 2011 review [03:05]: the studies that *did* show a satiety effect consistently fed subjects more viscous fiber. Viscous fibers reduced appetite more than less viscous fibers. Proposed mechanism: viscous fiber has a high water-holding capacity, holds water in the stomach, and causes stomach distension — a known signal to the brain to reduce hunger.

Neither review's authors are named in the video.

## Viscous fiber sources
- **Passion fruit** — highest proportion of viscous fiber per gram he could find. 100 g gives roughly 10 g total fiber, of which about 5-6 g is viscous.
- **Oats** — he attributes feeling much fuller for longer after switching back to oats from pancakes and toast post-prep.
- **Beans** — black beans and lima beans have somewhat more viscous fiber than other varieties.

## Recommendations
- **Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics:** minimum 20-35 g/day for adults; children, age + 5 g/day.
- **Nippard's rule:** 10% of daily carb intake from fiber. 500 g carbs/day → 50 g fiber/day as a minimum.
- **Floor:** never below 20 g/day, even on 100 g carbs/day.
- **Client habit rule:** at least two servings of green vegetables per day, or one green plus one orange or red vegetable, plus two servings of fruit per day — a structural way to ensure the fiber arrives inside micronutrient-rich food.

## Practical application
Calculate 10% of your carb target, floor it at 20 g, then fill it with whole foods rather than supplements. If satiety during a cut is the reason you're chasing fiber, bias the sources toward viscous types — oats, beans, passion fruit — rather than simply raising total grams.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Satiety research is subjective and methodologically difficult; the null results are not conclusive.
- Fiber is not essential, unlike vitamin C — the case for eating it is health benefits, not deficiency avoidance.
- This is a 2014 video, and the viscous-fiber ranking is based on his own searches, not a formal database review.
