# How Hard Should You Train To Build Muscle? Five Experts Answer

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How HARD Should You Train To Build Muscle?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BjyDaxYpW8o)) · 2020-10-03 · 17 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- Train hard enough to make gains, but not so hard that the **next** session is compromised. That's the whole balancing act.
- For hypertrophy, work sets should generally land within about **2 reps of failure** (roughly RPE 7-9). Below RPE 6 you probably aren't growing effectively.
- Start a mesocycle around **3 reps in reserve** with volume at the low end, then add load/reps weekly so RIR falls by roughly **1 per week**, ending near failure in the final week.
- Recovery check: muscles should feel recovered within about **3 days**. Still fresh after 1-2 days = do more volume. Still very sore at day 4 = too much.
- Leave most sessions **fatigued but not completely exhausted**.

## Terms
RPE (rating of perceived exertion), 1-10, is how hard the set was — RPE 10 = zero reps left, RPE 9 = one more available. RIR (reps in reserve) is the inverse: 0 RIR = RPE 10, 1 RIR = RPE 9.

## Dr Mike Israetel — it depends where you are in the mesocycle
[01:01] Standard advice ("train hard enough to make gains") doesn't go far enough. Better: train hard enough to make gains **but not so hard that your next workout suffers** and can no longer produce gains.

Across a **4-8 week mesocycle**:
- **Start:** around **3 RIR**, volume at the low end of your range — enough that the target muscle is notably fatigued, you get a small pump, and you're at most a little sore. No pump and no fatigue at all is unlikely to be stimulative; a massive pump brings soreness you may not want this early.
- **Each week after:** increase load and/or reps so that RIR **holds or drops by about 1 per week**. So 3 → 3 → 3 RIR is acceptable; 3 → 2 → 1 → 0 is also fine. What you must avoid is RIR *rising* (3 → 4 → 5), which means you're training progressively easier relative to your capacity.
- **End:** when your rep strength flatlines and then dips, you've hit **maximum recoverable volume**. More volume — or even the same volume — now hurts gains. Deload, shed fatigue, restart at 3 RIR and low volume.

[03:35] Why not go to failure in week 1? After a deload and a change of exercises and rep ranges, novelty means your growth sensitivity is already high — you get great results without needing failure, so spending the fatigue is pointless. In the **last week** it's a different story: if the lift won't crash down on you (or you have a good bench spotter), going to true failure is a **really good idea** to make sure you've captured any extra growth living between 1 and 0 RIR, which may matter especially for advanced lifters. Failure training is fine as long as it isn't poisoning the next session, forcing you to cut volume, or giving you nagging injuries — if you can't justify it, don't do it.

## John Meadows (IFBB pro) — it depends how advanced you are
[05:06] Beginners don't need to kill themselves — no failure, no huge volume. Learning proper form and progressively adding weight is enough. Intermediates add a bit of volume and intensity, but the primary goal is still getting stronger; leaving the newbie phase doesn't mean you stop progressing lifts.

Advanced lifters have to challenge the body harder, which is where intensity techniques earn their place: an occasional **cluster set** or **drop set** to finish a workout on a machine. His controversial view: at an advanced stage you can use **any exercises in any order**, as long as they work for you and **a certain amount are taken to failure or beyond** — because growth comes down to mechanical tension. If an exercise gives you mechanical tension, good activation and exhausted muscle fibres, you'll grow; that's what muscle growth *is*. This is especially valuable for naturals, who lack the pharmacological help enhanced lifters use to break plateaus. Form still matters at every level — training harder must not mean excessive technique breakdown.

## Dr Stefi Cohen (25x world record powerlifter) — most people never really push
[08:12] Greatness doesn't come from casual effort. Her observation is that people are stuck in a **"50/50 zone"**: they never train at a genuinely challenging intensity, and they also don't rest when they should — half-passing both halves of the process.

She doesn't take every set to failure and does run relatively lighter accumulation phases, but failure training is **integral** to her strength programming and has been underrated recently. Specificity rules: to raise a 1RM squat you must train at those intensities. Not maxing daily, but challenging yourself in almost every session so the nervous system learns to handle very heavy loads. Structure: **4-6-8 weeks** of accumulation at higher volume / lower intensity to build load tolerance and refine movement, then **4-6-8 weeks** of pushing hard at **90-95-100%** in the 1-5 rep max range. Caveat she states herself: if you train purely for health, ignore all of this. She also stresses blending athlete experience, coaching wisdom and the scientific literature.

## Dr Eric Helms — the intensity threshold
[11:15] One of the few constants in hypertrophy training: **if you aren't hitting RPE 6 or higher most of the time, you probably aren't growing effectively**. Sets must be reasonably close to failure, or heavy enough to force you close immediately (in which case do a lot of sets to compensate for the low reps). Per set, hypertrophy work should be somewhere in the **RPE 5-10** range depending on your goal, and **most of the time RPE 7-9**.

Mechanism: growth requires **mechanotransduction** — mechanoreceptors in the muscle sensing tension. The tension must be high relative to your current adaptation. That can come from pushing a light load near failure, or from ~**80% on the bar** (e.g. 7x3 if you like, though 4x6 is probably preferable).

On the volume-vs-proximity-to-failure debate, his answer is that the truth **must** sit between the extremes: ramping up the stimulus raises recovery cost, which forces you to cut frequency or volume, or leaves lingering central fatigue and muscle damage that prevents you recruiting high-threshold motor units next session. You're balancing an effective stimulus now against an effective stimulus next time. There's probably a **wide continuum of equally effective approaches** — which is why lifters on the high-intensity end and the high-volume end both win titles.

## Greg Nuckols — the summary
[14:19] Depends on the lifter, the exercise and the goal. Two questions: how hard to push each set, and how much volume.

**Per set, for hypertrophy** — generally close to failure, with exercise-specific stopping rules:
- **Squats, deadlifts** (form breakdown is risky): terminate at the point of **technical breakdown**.
- **Rows** (momentum lets you cheat out many extra reps): terminate when you **stop feeling the target muscle** — if the row becomes biceps and lower back rather than lats, cut it.
- Otherwise: stay within about **2 reps of concentric failure**.

**For strength work:** value in some near-failure sets so you get comfortable grinding, *and* value in plenty of lower-stress sets at **3+ RIR** to practise skill and perfect motor patterns. The balance depends on lifter skill, the lift, proximity to a meet, and individual recovery capacity for near-failure sets.

**Volume per session:** depends heavily on individual tolerance, training type and exercise selection — strength sets at 3+ RIR allow far more sets than hypertrophy work like leg press to failure. Rules of thumb: leave the gym **fatigued but not completely exhausted**; muscles should be more or less **recovered within about 3 days**. Totally fresh 1-2 days later means you probably should have done more volume (unless you're deliberately training at very high frequency); still very sore and fatigued **4 days** later means volume was too high.

## Practical application
- Open a mesocycle at **3 RIR** and low-end volume; add load or reps weekly so RIR drops by about 1 per week; save true failure for the final week on safe lifts.
- Keep most hypertrophy work at **RPE 7-9 / within 2 reps of failure**; never below RPE 6.
- Stop squats and deadlifts at technical breakdown; stop rows when you lose the target muscle.
- Strength work: mix near-failure grinding sets with a larger base of 3+ RIR technique sets.
- Deload when rep strength flatlines or dips, then restart the cycle.
- Use soreness/recovery at the 1-3-4 day marks to audit whether your volume is right.

## Caveats & unknowns
The experts disagree on emphasis: Israetel avoids failure early in a block, Meadows and Cohen use failure deliberately, Helms argues the optimum must sit in the middle. Helms explicitly notes the continuum is broad enough that both camps produce champions, so precision here probably matters less than consistency. Fibre-level "delayed growth" between 1 and 0 RIR is described as hypothetical. Recovery-timeline rules of thumb are painted with a broad brush and vary by individual and by training frequency. Cohen's high-intensity prescription is explicitly for people chasing maximum strength or size, not general health.
