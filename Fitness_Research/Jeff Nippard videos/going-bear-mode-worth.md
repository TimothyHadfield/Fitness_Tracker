# Is Bear Mode Worth It? Dirty Bulking vs. Lean Bulking

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Is Going Bear Mode Worth It? (Dirty Bulking Science vs My Experience)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JRltyPPBZMo)) · 2019-01-26 · 9 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Jeff ran bear mode for ~8 months, going from **160 lb lean to 183 lb bulked** (23 lb gained) at 5'5".
- The research Eric Helms cited (Garthe and colleagues — auto-captions render this "Garth") found an extra **~600 kcal/day** in elite athletes training 4x/week produced **no significant extra muscle but over 3x the fat gain**.
- Nutrition is **permissive** to training, not causative: overfeeding cannot force a muscle to grow. Training is the initiating event.
- Real benefits: more strength (more overload), no hunger or cravings, better psychological stability, looking bigger in clothes.
- Real costs: looking smaller with the shirt off, worse work capacity and conditioning, and a longer or harsher cut later — which risks losing more muscle than you gained.

## What bear mode is
A heavy caloric surplus with rapid weight gain, aiming for roughly **15-20% body fat** — which Alex from Alpha Destiny argued to Jeff is the single best way to look extremely muscular day to day without steroids. The look is the "yoked" one: wide neck, mountainous traps, boulder shoulders, upper back popping, a 3D mass effect in a t-shirt.

It is not simply dirty bulking or getting fat. It also involves deliberately training specific muscles — **neck and traps especially** — that create a "naturally enhanced" appearance in the bulked state. The goal is to look as stacked as possible *in clothes*: you may lose the six-pack but should still have a fairly flat stomach.

This inverts the natural bodybuilding priority, where the aim is to look maximal on stage for one or two hours; most natural bodybuilders accept looking much smaller in clothes and without a pump, but look massive on stage because extreme leanness produces striations and shadowing.

## The science: does a bigger surplus build more muscle?
Jeff's case for bear mode: all else equal, a bigger body lifts bigger weights — which is why heavier powerlifting weight classes are stronger. If progressive overload drives hypertrophy, getting stronger over time should mean more muscle over time.

**Eric Helms challenged this** [03:04]:
- Nutrition is **permissive** to weight training. You can't force-feed gains. Nutrition alone will make you gain weight, most of it fat. Training is the initiating event that induces skeletal muscle to grow; that tension stimulus is then *supported* by nutrition — and that support does not require a huge surplus.
- **Garthe and colleagues:** elite athletes on a 4-day/week training program, one group overfed by ~600 kcal/day on average. The overfed group gained **no significantly greater muscle mass** but gained **over three times the fat mass**. Essentially all the extra bulking weight was fat.

Jeff's caveat: this was a single study over 8-12 weeks; more meaningful differences might emerge over longer timeframes — or might not.

**The downstream cost:** even if bear mode yields somewhat more muscle, it definitely yields more fat, meaning the following cut must be **either longer or more aggressive**. According to the available case studies on natural bodybuilding, more aggressive cuts result in **more muscle loss**. So extra muscle built while bulking can be given back on the way down.

For a committed bear mode adherent this may not matter, since the goal was never to look lean or maintain a lean physique — it's to be as big and strong as possible, extra body fat accepted.

## Pros from Jeff's experience
1. **Strength.** All else equal, a bigger body means bigger lifts.
2. **No hunger, cravings or mood swings** [05:06] — the most pronounced benefit for him. Being able to have cheesecake at 1am as part of the plan, eating to fuel gym performance while accepting the fat that comes with it, was psychologically stabilising. Shifting focus away from aesthetics and onto performance is a useful antidote to the skewed body image that chronic leanness-chasing produces, where you perceive yourself as far fatter than you are.
3. **Looked bigger in clothes** — filling out t-shirts and hoodies, without needing a pump.

## Cons from Jeff's experience
1. **Looked smaller with the shirt off**, especially flexing in good lighting. Most people look bigger when leaner even at a lower body weight — see natural bodybuilders who appear far bigger contest-shredded than in the offseason despite weighing less.
2. **Worse work capacity.** He felt sluggish, got winded more easily, particularly on high-volume leg days. Even though he was stronger, reduced work capacity is bad for hypertrophy given the dose-response relationship between training volume and growth. The countermeasure is aerobic conditioning — but cardio is time-consuming and, done too frequently, interferes with muscle growth. He calls this a catch-22.

## Practical application
- If you choose bear mode: be **methodical**, tracking that strength actually scales with the added body weight — otherwise you're just gaining fat.
- Add **2-3 cardio sessions per week** while bear moding so high-volume leg training remains manageable.
- Train the neck and traps deliberately if the goal is the bulked, wide look.
- Remember you do **not** need a large surplus to build muscle; the surplus mostly buys fat.
- Choose based on goal: lean bulk if you want to look lean while building muscle; bear mode if you want to be as big and strong as possible, especially in clothes.
- Jeff says he'd do it again — with the above corrections.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The Garthe study is one 8-12 week trial in elite athletes; longer-term differences are unknown.
- Researcher name is garbled in the auto-captions ("Garth") — most likely Garthe et al., but treat the spelling as uncertain.
- Jeff showed on-screen general recommendations for bear mode vs. lean bulk setups that are not spoken in the transcript and so aren't captured here.
