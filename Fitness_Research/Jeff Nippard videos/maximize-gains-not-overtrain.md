# Overtraining vs Overreaching: How Hard You Can Push Before Progress Stalls

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Maximize Gains and NOT Overtrain | Overtraining Science Explained" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NLqq_dJB0iM)) · 2017-09-30 · 7 min
**Topic:** Recovery

## Bottom line
- True overtraining is real but rare in lifters; most trainees worried about it would gain more by pushing slightly harder.
- Overtraining = a performance drop lasting more than 2-3 weeks. Overreaching = a short-term dip that, caught early, produces supercompensation and better long-term gains.
- Excess **volume** drives overtraining more than intensity, but chronically maxing out is also a fast route to a strength drop.
- Practical volume floor: **40-70 reps per body part, 2-3x per week**, mostly in the **6-12 rep range** for hypertrophy.
- Monitor with a 4-question self-check or morning heart rate; deload by halving volume and dropping load 25-50%.

## Definitions and why the distinction matters
Overtraining is an imbalance between training and recovery producing a decrement in performance that persists beyond **2-3 weeks**. Feeling tired, sore and irritable after a hard block is usually **overreaching** — a shorter, milder version. Caught before that 2-3 week mark and followed by emphasised recovery, overreaching becomes *functional*: the body adapts more strongly (supercompensation), so a short-term performance dip converts into a long-term gain. True overtraining instead gives a long-term drop in performance and lost gains.

Nippard notes PubMed returns ~869 results on overtraining, but most studies are on elite endurance and track athletes with clinical overtraining syndrome — not bodybuilders, and much less general lifters trying to add mass.

## How much training is too much
- **Brad Schoenfeld's 2010 review** concluded overtraining results more from excessive **volume** than from intensity ([Schoenfeld 2010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/20847704)).
- A study by **Fry and colleagues** had trained men perform a **1RM machine squat 6 days/week for 2 weeks**; 1RM strength **dropped 12%** ([Fry 1994](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/7808252)).
- Recent volume-matched data cited: **3x10 vs 7x3** produced the **same hypertrophy**, but the heavy group experienced more fatigue ([Schoenfeld 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24714538)). So unless training specifically for strength, keep loads moderate — mostly **6-12 reps** for maximum muscle.
- Volume ballpark, credited to **Eric Helms**: a low-end target of **40-70 reps per body part, 2-3 sessions per week** ([Helms 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24998610)). Advanced lifters typically need more, so add workload gradually while assessing recovery.

## Monitoring recovery
Two options, subjective and objective.

**Four-question self-survey [02:33]:**
1. How is your sleep?
2. How are your energy levels?
3. Are muscle and joint aches worse than usual?
4. How is your gym strength?

Answered honestly, these can flag an overreached or overtrained state — but honesty and subjectivity are the weak points.

**Heart rate variability (HRV)** — the time interval between heartbeats — is the more objective marker, used in cardiology and now applied in sports physiology. Dedicated devices exist, but a cheap proxy is **average morning heart rate**: establish a baseline over one week, then track deviation. A shift of **more than ~10%** signals you should cut training workload for **1-2 weeks**.

## Deloading and training breaks
Deloads serve two purposes: preventing overuse injury, and resensitising the body to training for better long-term results.

**How to deload [03:34]:**
- Cut training **volume roughly in half**, and drop the **weight by 25-50%**.
- More intuitive alternative: stop a few extra reps shy of failure and **halve the sets on every exercise** for a couple of weeks.

**Full training breaks** are also worth taking occasionally — they give connective tissue a complete rest with no loss of progress. A **2011 study** found a group training continuously for **15 weeks** gained the same muscle as a group that took a **3-week break halfway through** ([Ogasawara 2011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/21771261)).

## Practical application
- Train mostly at **6-12 reps**; reserve heavy low-rep work for genuine strength goals.
- Start around **40-70 reps per body part per week, split over 2-3 sessions**, and only titrate volume up as you advance and recovery allows.
- Track morning heart rate for a week to set a baseline; a **>10% rise** triggers a **1-2 week** load reduction.
- Deload by **halving volume and cutting load 25-50%**, or halving sets and stopping further from failure.
- Consider a planned **~3-week full break** within a long training year — the evidence suggests no cost to gains.
- Address sleep, diet and lifestyle alongside training. Nippard quotes coach **Greg Nuckols**' garden analogy: the body grows only as well as its circumstances allow — fertilise, tend the soil, water, provide sunlight.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Most overtraining research comes from elite endurance/track athletes, not resistance-trained lifters, so direct transfer is limited.
- This guidance targets **advanced trainees**, who must overreach more regularly to progress. **Beginners** can usually progress linearly without deliberate overreaching or deloads.
- Overtraining is not the only cause of a stall: **program monotony** (never varying the plan) can stunt progress, and sometimes the actual fix is pushing harder, not resting more.
