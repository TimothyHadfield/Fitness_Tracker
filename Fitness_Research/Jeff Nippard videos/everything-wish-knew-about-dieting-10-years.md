# Auditing a 2010 Contest Prep Diet: What Ten Years Changed

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Everything I Wish I Knew About Dieting 10 Years Ago (Avoid These Nutrition Mistakes)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OHzpMGMT9YY)) · 2020-03-07 · 11 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Set calories and macros first; a fixed meal plan teaches you nothing and collapses the moment you stop following it.
- Don't drive fat below 15-20% of total calories — low fat intakes are associated with reduced testosterone, bad for health and for muscle retention. On prep he sits around 40-60 g/day.
- Protein of 265 g/day was overkill; sufficient beats excessive, but there's no bonus above that.
- The pre-workout meal is the most important meal of the day and it needs actual carbs — not just fibrous veg.
- Fasted cardio, flax seeds, tilapia, no-carbs-before-bed: all bodybuilding folklore, none of it does anything special.

## The core structural mistake: meal plan vs. macros
[00:30] The old sheet was a rigid meal plan — exact foods at exact times, every day, no exceptions. He didn't know or care what the calories or macros were, believing the specific foods and timings were themselves what got you shredded.

Two fixes. First, calculate calories and macros so you know what you're actually doing and how to adjust when the diet ends. Second, build in flexibility: swapping oatmeal for a slice of toast changes nothing about progress, makes the diet more enjoyable, and reduces the risk of nutrient deficiencies by widening food variety. The failure mode of meal plans is that people adhere only as long as they can tolerate the plan, then have no framework left.

## Macro targets
- **Protein:** 265 g was far more than needed. He'd cut it back — no whey shake at breakfast, no redundant post-workout scoop.
- **Fat:** only 13% of calories, which he calls the diet's most concerning number. Minimum 15-20% of total calories; roughly 40-60 g/day on his own contest prep. He'd add at least another 10-15 g/day to the old plan.
- **Carbs:** slightly low, but he concedes very low carbs may be a necessary evil of getting stage-lean. Not a general recommendation.
- **Calories:** keep them as high as possible while still losing at the target rate of about **1% of bodyweight per week**. The deficit will have to deepen over time as metabolism adapts.

## Fasted cardio
[03:34] He did 30 min of fasted cardio at 7 a.m. believing it was special for fat loss. Research shows that whatever substrate you burn more of during a fasted session, you burn less of over the following 24 hours; the latest systematic review has written this off as a myth. Do cardio whenever it fits your schedule or when your energy is best — first thing on an empty stomach is fine, just not superior.

## Nutrient timing and the anabolic window
[07:07] The old plan had a whey shake immediately post-workout on top of a whole-food pre-workout meal. Redundant: the best evidence on peri-workout nutrition puts the anabolic window at roughly **4-5 hours** long. With a pre-workout meal at 5 p.m. containing chicken, amino acids are still circulating when the 7:30 p.m. whole-food meal arrives. Adding a shake in between is futile — just wait for the post-workout meal.

## Pre-workout nutrition — the biggest violation
[06:07] The pre-workout meal was chicken and broccoli with no usable carb source. Fix: take the 180 g of sweet potato that was sitting in meal 5 and either split it evenly between the pre- and post-workout meals or put all of it pre-workout. Training is what drives the anabolic machinery and preserves muscle in a deficit, so it needs fuel. He'd also drop the broccoli pre-training entirely — fibrous vegetables cause bloating, gas and discomfort during a session, which hurts performance.

## Food myths, one by one
- **Whole flax seeds** — genuinely nutritious (omega-3s, fibre) but with no fat-burning property. Eaten whole, the shell resists digestion; grind them or buy them pre-ground. He'd rotate them out for whole eggs, avocado, nuts or nut butter most days.
- **Tilapia** — was believed to "thin out your skin" or dry you out. Pure folklore. Any fish or protein source works; he'd pick salmon, whose healthy fats also reduce the need for fish oil caps.
- **Carbs before bed** — he avoided them thinking they'd be stored directly as fat. A myth. He now prefers moving some breakfast carbs to the pre-bed meal because he sleeps better with them.
- **Casein pre-bed** — a slow-digesting protein before sleep is a reasonable idea, but he'd swap the powder for cottage cheese: more naturally occurring nutrients, more satiating because you actually chew it.
- **Timing rigidity** — insisting the 10 a.m. meal be eaten at exactly 10:00 rather than 9:57 accomplished nothing.

## Practical application
1. Work out calories and macros before anything else; treat foods as interchangeable within those numbers.
2. Set fat at a floor of 15-20% of calories. Set protein to sufficient, not maximal.
3. Aim to lose ~1% bodyweight per week on the highest calories that still achieves it.
4. Put the day's largest carb serving in the pre-workout meal; keep fibrous vegetables out of it.
5. Skip the immediately-post-workout shake if a whole-food meal is coming within a few hours.
6. Do cardio when it suits your schedule and energy, fasted or not.
7. Add fruit, beans, legumes, nuts, and grains for nutritional completeness — the old diet had no fruit at all, on the mistaken belief sugar would be stored as fat.

## Caveats & unknowns
Very low carbs and a deep deficit may be unavoidable for stage-level leanness, but he explicitly does not recommend that for general audiences. Everything here is framed around a physique-contest prep, not general health dieting. His final verdict on the old diet: **4/10** — credit for plenty of vegetables, adequate (if excessive) protein, and not being criminally low in carbs or calories; penalised for rigidity, too little fat, no pre-workout carbs, unnecessary fasted cardio, no fruit, and thin food variety.
