# The Perfect Push Workout: Seven Movements for Chest, Shoulders and Triceps

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Perfect Push Workout (According To Science)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b6ouj88iBZs)) · 2023-07-03 · 10 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Open with an undulating 8 / 5 / 15 on close-grip incline barbell bench — the set of 8 grooves the pattern and calibrates the heavy set of 5, then the 15 is pump work. Rest 3-4 min between the 8 and the 5.
- An 8-week hypertrophy study found incline bench grew the **upper** pecs significantly more than flat, with no difference at the mid or lower pecs.
- Machine shoulder press 3x10-12: RPE 9 on the first two sets, last set to true failure.
- Internally rotate the shoulder on plate front raises to recruit side delt fibers instead of hammering already-overdeveloped front delts.
- Finish with one all-out AMRAP set (diamond push-ups) — nothing left to interfere with, so give it everything.

## Session structure
5 min general warm-up, then dynamic stretches (arm swings, cable external rotations).

1. Close-grip incline barbell bench press — 8, then 5, then 15
2. Machine shoulder press — 3x10-12
3. Floor reset skull crusher — 3x6-8
4. Bent-over cable pec fly — 3x10-12
5. Machine lateral raise — 3x20 (5 slow eccentric + 15 constant tension)
6. Plate front raise — 2x15-20
7. Diamond push-up — 1 all-out AMRAP

## Close-grip incline bench press [00:00]
The undulating structure exists for a reason: incline bench is not a well-oiled movement for most people, so the moderate set of 8 finds the groove and tells you how much to add for the heavier set of 5. **Rest at least 3-4 minutes between those two sets** so the pecs can actually go up in weight. After the 5, the overloading work is finished, and the final set of 15 is smooth mind-muscle-connection pump work where load doesn't matter. Warm-up sets precede the first working set.

**Incline vs flat evidence [01:00]:** there is now a long-term study measuring pec hypertrophy after 8 weeks of flat vs incline benching. Lower pecs: no significant difference. Mid pecs: no significant difference. Upper pecs: **significantly more growth in the incline group.** So prioritizing incline pressing makes sense for upper pec growth. You could argue incline is simply the better overall chest builder given equal growth elsewhere, but Nippard cautions this was a single study on 47 untrained subjects, so no sweeping generalizations.

**Technique:** grip just outside shoulder width, which shifts further emphasis to the upper pecs. Bring the bar to the highest contact point on your upper chest, press straight up or up and slightly back. The common error is touching too low and pressing out in front of you — keep the bar roughly stacked in a straight line with the shoulder joint, or you limit the load and add shoulder strain. He tucks slightly on the descent and flares on the press; anywhere from 0 to about 30 degrees of tuck is fine and hits most pec fibers similarly.

## Machine shoulder press [03:03]
Chosen over free weights because the delts already got work from the incline press and a machine is less systemically fatiguing, plus you can push closer to failure with no risk of dropping the weight. Machines miss some stabilizers, but that concern largely evaporates if free weights are also in the program.

Set the seat high enough that your arms at least break parallel with the floor — he goes a bit below parallel for a deeper stretch. Thumbless grip (better delt connection for him), constant tension without a full lockout, roughly 1 second up and 1 second down.

**Intensity:** about one rep in reserve (RPE 9) on the first two sets, then the last set all the way to genuine failure.

## Floor reset skull crusher [04:04]
The heavy, strength-focused triceps movement of the week — his other push day uses a higher-rep cable triceps movement instead. Pick a weight heavier than your usual skull crusher load.

Lift the bar off the floor to full or near-full elbow extension with the bar still behind your eyeline, control the negative at least halfway down, then let it drop and come to a complete dead stop on the floor. You can let it roll back slightly and roll it back in for momentum, like a powerlifter initiating a deadlift.

If you don't care about triceps strength, control the whole negative to the floor instead — that's the purer hypertrophy option. Because this is a progressive-overload movement, track weight and reps and aim to increase one or the other weekly. New lifters to this method usually see triceps strength climb fast, which then carries over to conventional skull crushers.

## Bent-over cable pec fly [05:34]
Hinge forward at the hips and fly straight downward. This is **not** a decline cable fly (upright torso, arms converging downward) — here you fly straight out and the torso itself is angled. The decline version emphasizes lower pecs; the bent-over version hits all regions roughly evenly with slightly more mid pec.

Mechanically it is the same as a standing mid-level cable fly, but standing, the cables can pull you backward and the balance struggle limits tension reaching the pecs. Leaning forward over the cables grounds your bodyweight and lets more tension land on the chest. Worth trying if you've ever lost balance on standard cable flyes.

## Machine lateral raise [06:06]
3x20, but split within each set: **first 5 reps with a slow 5-second eccentric, remaining 15 reps constant tension** at a smooth 1 up / 1 down with no pauses. The slow negatives let you find the side delt fibers and lock in the connection, then the remaining reps blast them. Nothing magic about the pattern — it is a tool for people who struggle to feel their delts on lateral raises.

## Plate front raise [07:06]
Extra shoulder volume because delts tolerate a lot of it and are central to an X-frame physique. Normally he dislikes front raises — front delts already get worked by all horizontal and vertical pressing and are usually overdeveloped relative to the side delts.

**The fix:** "steer the wheel" — internally rotate the shoulder as you raise the plate. With an externally rotated arm, the front delt fibers line up with the resistance and the side delt fibers contribute almost nothing. Internally rotating puts the side delt fibers in a much better position to contribute, targeting the middle segments between the front and side heads.

If this causes shoulder pain, swap in a dumbbell Y raise, though he considers internal rotation perfectly normal in healthy shoulders.

## Diamond push-up [08:07]
One all-out set to failure to close the session — nothing left to interfere with. The diamond hand position loads the triceps far harder than a conventional push-up and the narrower base makes the movement harder overall, so expect far fewer reps: he got 21 here against roughly 60-70 conventional push-ups. Control the tempo, don't free-fall, and treat holding form all the way to failure as a skill worth practising.

## Practical application
This is designed as one of two push days per week and deliberately leaves gaps that the other push day covers — so if something feels missing, that is by design. The overload drivers are the incline set of 5 and the floor reset skull crusher; everything else is quality and volume work.

## Caveats & unknowns
The incline-vs-flat study is one trial on 47 untrained subjects, so he supports using incline for upper pec growth but not the broader claim that incline is simply superior. Constant tension is not claimed to be inherently special — he uses it as an attentional focus tool.
