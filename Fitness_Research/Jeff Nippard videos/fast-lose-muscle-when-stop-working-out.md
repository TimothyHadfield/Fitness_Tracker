# How Fast You Lose Muscle When You Stop Training (And How To Avoid It)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How Fast Do You Lose Muscle When You Stop Working Out? (& Ways To Avoid It)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P0vHR2mAw2U)) · 2020-05-17 · 10 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Up to about 2-3 weeks off with normal daily activity causes little to no measurable muscle loss. Past three weeks, most people start to notice shrinkage.
- Two full months of no training can cost roughly half the muscle you built — but only half, and everyday activity alone preserves the rest.
- Reducing training volume to as little as one-ninth of normal maintained muscle mass for 32 weeks (over seven months) in a 2011 study by Bickel and colleagues.
- To minimise loss: eat at or slightly above maintenance, hit 1.6-2.2 g protein per kg (0.7-1 g per lb) and err high if not training, and train each muscle close to failure once or twice weekly even with bodyweight only.
- Muscle memory is real regardless of the mechanism debate — lost muscle comes back far faster than it took to build.

## The timeline of loss
[00:31] Muscle loss is a tug-of-war between muscle protein synthesis and breakdown, gradually won by breakdown. How fast depends first on how little you're doing.

- **Complete bed rest:** a 2016 study from Dirks and colleagues found significant muscle loss in just one week. Caveat: those subjects weren't trained lifters, and unless hospitalised or seriously injured you're not confined to bed for seven days.
- **No lifting but normal daily activity:** a detraining study of 20 subjects with at least a year of training experience had them stop lifting entirely for two weeks while continuing everyday life. There was no significant drop in muscle mass at two weeks ([Hwang 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/28328712)). Just getting up and moving is far better than lying in bed.
- **Around three weeks:** Nippard puts the cutoff here. Fisher and Steele, in their review of evidence-based hypertrophy recommendations, cite up to three weeks as the maximum break you can take without fear of atrophy. Ogasawara's data also shows dips in muscle mass after three weeks of detraining ([Ogasawara 2011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/21771261)), though it's hard to tell from the curve whether the loss was gradual or dropped off sharply after week two.
- **Is it even muscle?** Some experts argue short-break losses are mostly glycogen and water rather than contractile tissue. Nippard thinks they may be right — it would explain why the rebound after short breaks is so fast, since fuel stores refill quickly once training resumes.
- **Two months off:** a 2006 paper from Léger and colleagues put subjects through eight weeks of training (10% increase in muscle size) then eight weeks of abrupt detraining, which cut those gains in half ([Léger 2006](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/16916907)). Glass half full: after two months of nothing but daily activity they still kept half of what they built. These were essentially novices; more experienced lifters may lose less relatively, though the data is thin.

## The three factors that determine how much you lose

**1. How much you're still doing.** The gap between bed rest and walking around is large — everyday movement mechanically activates most muscles to some degree. Above that, high-rep bodyweight work beats walking, and weighted resistance beats bodyweight, but those gaps are much smaller than the bed-rest gap. The key evidence is Bickel and colleagues 2011: cutting training volume to one-ninth of previous levels maintained muscle mass for 32 weeks on average ([Bickel 2011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/21131862)). Low-volume "suboptimal" workouts keep most of your muscle for six or seven months.

**2. Diet, especially calories.** A 2020 paper from Alan Aragon and Brad Schoenfeld noted that a sustained energy deficit compromises muscular potential by inhibiting muscle protein synthesis and anabolic signalling, whereas a caloric surplus provides the ideal environment for growth ([Aragon 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000539)). Since muscle loss is driven by synthesis losing the tug-of-war, the same logic applies: avoid a deficit, eat at maintenance or a slight surplus if avoiding muscle loss is the priority.

**3. Protein.** That same review cites 1.6-2.2 g/kg bodyweight (roughly 0.7-1 g/lb) for drug-free, non-dieting trainees. Err toward the high end or slightly above if you aren't training at all.

## Age
[06:03] Despite the sarcopenia literature, the picture isn't clear-cut. A 2016 review in the Journal of Frailty and Aging found that periods of muscle disuse cause losses of similar magnitude in older and younger people, while stressing the metabolic importance of maintaining muscle mass with age ([Bell 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/26980367)). However, the Bickel paper found the one-ninth volume protocol maintained mass in the younger group only, not the older group ([Bickel 2011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/21131862)) — suggesting people 60 and up may need more volume to hold onto gains.

## Muscle memory
[07:34] The real-world case: Matt H, a client of Nippard's friend Cliff, built his physique over about seven years, then lost it in two months while bedridden and fed only by IV during a Crohn's flare (200 lb down to 165 lb). Rebuilding took just two months of retraining, not another seven years.

The mechanism is contested. A well-known 2010 rodent study showed myonuclei formed during training persist essentially forever, so retraining would immediately restart protein production. A newer human study from Snijders and colleagues found myonuclei increased with training but returned to baseline after a year of detraining ([Snijders 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/30978433)). That has pushed many researchers toward epigenetic changes as the main driver, per a 2018 study from Seaborne and colleagues ([Seaborne 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/29382913)). The mechanism is unsettled; the phenomenon is not.

## Practical application
For anyone under 60 with limited equipment: bodyweight workouts training each muscle close to failure once or twice per week are enough to keep what you've built. Eat at or above maintenance — bodyweight in lb × 16 is a reasonable calorie starting point, then adjust from weight trends — and take roughly 1 g protein per lb of bodyweight.

## Caveats & unknowns
The bed-rest data comes from untrained subjects. The two-month 50% loss figure comes from near-novices; the trained response is unclear. Whether short-term "loss" is contractile tissue or glycogen and water is unresolved. Older lifters may need more than the minimal maintenance volume. The myonuclear vs epigenetic basis of muscle memory is an open debate.
