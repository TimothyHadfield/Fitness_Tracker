# Why "You Can Find a Study to Prove Anything" Doesn't Hold Up

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Addressing \"You Can Find a Study to Prove Anything...\"" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CUoXthyzePE)) · 2014-08-28 · 8 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- The complaint splits into two claims: that studies can be spun to say anything, and that studies simply contradict each other. The first is a reporting problem, not a science problem. The second dissolves once you use the hierarchy of evidence.
- Studies are not on equal footing. An opinion piece sits at the bottom of the pyramid; a systematic review sits at the top.
- When findings conflict, weight the higher-quality, lower-bias evidence rather than treating all studies as interchangeable.
- Science doesn't set out to prove things — it collects evidence and follows it — so the phrase itself is framed in an anti-scientific way.

## Reading one: "you can twist any study to say anything"
Nippard concedes this happens constantly, but locates the fault in science communication rather than in the research. His example [00:44]: a newspaper piece claiming "landmark research" showed sperm and eggs carry genetic memories of pre-conception events, supposedly forcing a rethink of Darwin. The tells were calling a mere review article "landmark" and invoking Darwin at all — a reporter with no grounding in the field overselling a paper.

The key distinction: misreporting a study doesn't make the study's findings less true, and it doesn't make the misreport true. It reveals a communication gap that opens when reporters and bloggers write about science they don't understand.

His fix for a lay reader with no formal scientific training: be wary of provocative headlines and be sceptical of popular media as a source for scientific information. He points to good communicators as an alternative — Neil deGrasse Tyson and Steven Novella generally, and within nutrition and exercise science, Alan Aragon and Brad Schoenfeld, who both do research and translate it for a lay audience.

## Reading two: "different studies give different results"
This is the more common version, and the one he thinks is genuinely answerable. Worked example [03:20]: one study says six meals a day beats three for fat loss; another says meal frequency doesn't matter as long as you're in an energy deficit. Which do you believe?

Answer: consult the hierarchy of evidence. Higher tiers of the pyramid are more reliable, constitute stronger evidence, and carry the lowest risk of bias.

Applying it to the meal-frequency case:
- An online opinion piece claiming grazing is better for fat loss and muscle gain sits at the very bottom of the pyramid. It cited a single study (Bernadot et al. — name as spoken; spelling uncertain from captions) which is likely a randomised controlled study; he could not access the full text to confirm blinding, and gave the authors the benefit of the doubt.
- A systematic review of 25 studies sits at the very top. It concluded there is strong evidence of no association between eating frequency and weight, and limited evidence suggesting no association between body composition and eating frequency.

The review outranks the cherry-picked single study, which he notes didn't even control calorie content between experimental and control groups [06:22].

## How science actually handles conflicting findings
Once evidence weighs heavily enough in favour of a theory, that becomes accepted scientific understanding. When a new study challenges the accepted model, the response isn't to dismiss science wholesale — it's to examine that study closely: what biases were present, how the authors interpreted their results, then move forward from there.

He closes on the wording itself [06:53]: science doesn't aim to prove. Researchers gather evidence and follow it wherever it leads. Everyone carries individual biases, but the scientific method contains a systematic apparatus for reducing bias as far as possible, and studies that don't attempt to do so deserve heavy scrutiny.

## Practical application
- Before accepting a claim, ask where its evidence sits on the pyramid — opinion piece, single RCT, or systematic review.
- Treat a single study contradicting a body of work as something to inspect, not as grounds to discard the field.
- Check whether a cited study controlled the obvious confounder (calories, in the meal-frequency case) before letting it override a review.
- Read the study rather than the headline, or read communicators who do.

## Caveats & unknowns
- He could not access the full text of the meal-frequency RCT and therefore could not verify blinding.
- Neither cited study's authors are clearly named in the auto-captions beyond "Bernadot et al."; treat the spelling as uncertain.
- This is a 2014 video; the specific meal-frequency literature has moved on, though the reasoning about evidence hierarchy has not.
