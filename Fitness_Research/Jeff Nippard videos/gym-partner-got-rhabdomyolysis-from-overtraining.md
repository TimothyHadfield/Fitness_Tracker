# Rhabdomyolysis From Overtraining: A Case Study in Ignoring Recovery

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "My Gym Partner Got Rhabdomyolysis From Overtraining" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LstsevBHzRQ)) · 2019-03-23 · 13 min
**Topic:** Recovery

## Bottom line
- Nippard's training partner Pat developed rhabdomyolysis — extreme muscle breakdown — after a bodybuilding contest prep, and it cost him weeks to a month of training.
- The setup was: extended time at very low body fat, badly suppressed post-contest recovery capacity, and continuing to train at maximum intensity anyway.
- Take deloads. Actually honour your RPE targets — if the set says RPE 9, leave the rep in the tank.
- Slightly smaller acute gains from backing off beat a forced multi-week or multi-month layoff.
- Training is the primary driver of muscle growth; nutrition is permissive to it, not a substitute for the stimulus.

## What happened [02:37]
Pat began getting severe, increasingly frequent muscle cramps and started feeling genuinely sick. He went to the emergency room. Blood work showed his electrolyte levels badly out of balance and, per the doctors, a level of muscle damage comparable to what you'd see in someone from a serious car accident. The diagnosis given was rhabdomyolysis.

Rhabdo results from extreme muscle damage. It shows up after major trauma, in some drug users, and — relevant here — in extremely hard-training athletes.

## Why it happened
Nippard's read on the contributing factors:
- Pat had competed and held a very low body fat percentage for a long period.
- Recovery capacity plummets during and after a bodybuilding contest prep — a well-known consequence of prolonged severe dieting.
- Pat is unusually disciplined and kept training extremely hard through it. The damage compounded and surfaced after the competition.

## Rarity and severity
In roughly 12-13 years in the industry Nippard had heard of exactly one other lifter with it — a powerlifter whose case was far worse: a long hospital stay and close to a year out of the gym. Pat's recovery was weeks to a month, but months later his lower body was still not fully recovered and he was still training it much lighter than normal. His upper body had returned to normal, and he was hitting bench PRs while in a cut.

## Practical application
- **Take your deloads.** Treat them as scheduled, not optional.
- **Follow RPE honestly.** Ego-driven set execution — grinding a rep on a set prescribed as RPE 9 — is where autoregulation stops protecting you.
- **Post-contest and post-diet periods are high risk.** Recovery capacity is suppressed exactly when discipline is highest; that combination is the hazard.
- **More pain does not mean more gain.** Slightly less acute progress from restraint can mean more long-term progress, because you avoid a forced layoff.
- **Listen to warning signs.** Repeated severe cramps plus feeling systemically sick warrants medical attention, not pushing through.
- **Don't overcorrect either.** Nippard credits Pat for staying serious about training afterwards rather than becoming so cautious that the training stopped being productive; he simply knows his limits better now.

## Training vs nutrition hierarchy [00:31]
In a guest lecture for Brad Schoenfeld's exercise science class, Nippard ranked training as the number one variable, above nutrition. His reasoning: training is the spark — the stimulus without which optimal nutrition produces little growth. He cites Eric Helms's framing that nutrition is *permissive* to training: you optimise nutrition to augment the stimulus training creates.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This is a single anecdotal case, not evidence about population-level risk; rhabdo from lifting is rare.
- Nippard notes he isn't sure a formal diagnosis was recorded beyond the doctors saying "rhabdo."
- No specific medical guidance on prevention, monitoring markers (e.g. CK), or return-to-training protocol is offered in the video.
