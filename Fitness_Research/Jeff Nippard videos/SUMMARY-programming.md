# Programming and volume

Everything in the library about how a training week gets built. Part one covers the **training
variables** — how many sets, how often, how hard, how heavy, through what range, with how much rest.
Part two covers the **routines, splits and templates** those variables get assembled into. The videos
span **2015 to 2025** and his position moved a long way on several of these, most of all on volume, on
failure and on the stretched position. Where old and new conflict the newer view is his current one;
the section at the end of part one lays the moves out rather than flattening them.

---

## Part one — the training variables

### The hierarchy

A ladder, and the lower rungs have to be in place before the ones above pay anything
([basics and theory](training-basics-and-theory.md)):

1. **Sustainability**, built on safety and enjoyment
2. **Effort** — sets taken genuinely close to failure
3. **Progressive overload and prioritisation** — more weight or reps over time, tracked, weak point first
4. **The acute variables** — volume, load, frequency, rest, tempo, split

Progressive overload he calls the single biggest driver; tempo he calls explicitly the lowest-priority
variable ([tempo](lifting-tempo-fast-vs-slow-reps.md)). The two failure modes he names most: people
optimise the bottom of the list while neglecting effort and overload, and people **do not train nearly
as hard as they think they do**. By 2023 it had tightened to one mechanism — **tension is the primary
driver**, and everything else is a way of maximising it
([pure muscle growth](train-pure-muscle-growth.md)).

| Variable | Number |
|---|---|
| Weekly volume | **10–20 hard sets per muscle** |
| Per session | **6–8 sets per muscle** (10–12 back, glutes, quads) |
| Frequency | **2× per week per muscle** |
| Effort | **1–3 RIR**; ~2 RIR as the default stopping point |
| Reps | **~75% of volume in 6–15 reps**; load floor ~20–30% of 1RM |
| Rest | **3–5 min compounds, 1–2 min isolation**; never under 1 min |
| Tempo | **1-0-1-0 or 2-0-2-0**; never over 10 s per rep |
| Deload | every **4–8 weeks**: volume −30–50%, RPE down to 6–8 |
| Mesocycle | **3–8 weeks** accumulation, then deload |

### Volume — how many hard sets

**10–20 hard sets per muscle per week** survives from 2016 to 2025 and is Schoenfeld's standing
recommendation too ([Schoenfeld](advanced-techniques-supersets-dropsets-eccentrics.md)). The floor is
the solid part: the 2017 Schoenfeld/Ogborn/Krieger meta-analysis found more than 10 weekly sets roughly
**doubled** hypertrophy against fewer than 10 ([how much volume](much-training-volume-need.md)). The
upper end has never been pinned down.

The landmarks ([Israetel deep dive](training-volume-deep-dive-israetel.md)):

| Landmark | Sets/muscle/week | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **MV** maintenance | ~6–8 | holds what you have; lets you park a muscle while prioritising another |
| **MEV** minimum effective | ~10 (8–12) | least that still grows you; where a mesocycle starts |
| **MAV** maximum adaptive | drifts | the sweet spot, which *moves* from near-MEV to near-MRV across a block |
| **MRV** maximum recoverable | ~20 | the most you can recover from; where you deload |

MRV has an operational definition, not a feeling: bench 225 for 4×10, and if next week you get
8/6/4/4/3, you didn't recover. **Count hard working sets only** — warm-ups don't count, and if they add
meaningful volume your warm-ups are wrong. Sets rather than tonnage is the currency because sets between
**5 and 20 reps produce comparable fatigue**.

**Individual variation is the point, not a footnote.** Israetel's own hamstring MRV is **10–15 sets**
while his side delts take the **mid-to-high 20s**; he's seen people handle 30 and fast-twitch people who
blow up on 6–8 ([roundtable](volume-roundtable.md)). Lower back, erectors and hamstrings tolerate less;
delts and biceps more. Nuckols' mechanism: how easily a muscle loads in the **stretched position** —
RDLs damage hamstrings far more per rep than a curl damages biceps. Expect ~**six months** to learn your
own numbers per body part.

**Allocating by muscle** ([volume and frequency](training-volume-and-frequency.md)): chest, back, quads
and glutes get the full 10–20; biceps, triceps and rear/side delts get plenty indirectly and need only
**4–8 direct isolation sets/week** on top; front delts need none. Schoenfeld says compound work counts
toward assistance muscles but **not 1:1** — 0.8? 0.6? nobody knows. And don't add volume as the first
move: if you're still getting stronger, change nothing, and audit technique, effort, exercise selection
and mind-muscle connection before adding sets.

**The ceiling is unresolved and the notes contradict each other.** In 2018 Krieger said unpublished data
was about to "blow the idea of an upper limit out of the water" — **20–30 sets can still beat 10**.
Schoenfeld's 2019 study found growth still climbing at **30–45 sets/muscle/week**, which Schoenfeld
himself reads as a **~4-week specialisation block for a lagging muscle**, not a year-round dose. Against
that, Fisher and Steele's 24-week trial in women found **5–10 sets** maxed it out, and Barbalho found
**5 sets/week beat 10, 15 and 20** ([volume killing gains](workout-volume-killing-gains.md)). The newest
figure is **Pelland 2024**, 35 studies, dose-response holding from **4 to 43 sets per muscle per week**
([cut sets in half](cut-number-sets-half.md)). His reconciliation: the conflicting studies mostly differ
in **how the sets were spread across the week**.

### Junk volume

[Full note](junk-volume.md), 2022 — the most practically useful correction in the library. Three ways
sets get wasted:

1. **Too many in one session.** Krieger's meta-analysis of nine studies: growth climbs to ~**six sets per
   muscle per session**, then flatlines and may regress. His cap: **6–8 sets per muscle per workout**,
   maybe **10–12 for back, glutes and quads** — flagged as coaching speculation, not a study finding.
2. **Sets that are too easy.** The most common and most damaging: **47% of lifters** taking their normal
   "10-rep" bench weight to failure got **16–20+ reps**.
3. **Ultra-high-rep sets.** Above ~**40–50 reps** you buy fatigue and soreness for no extra growth. Stay
   under ~30 reps and above ~**20% of 1RM**.

The per-session number has been quoted four ways and they don't agree — **~5 sets** in 2019, from rodent
MPS data and explicitly framed as an assumption
([volume killing gains](workout-volume-killing-gains.md)); **5–10** in 2020
([high frequency](high-frequency-full-body-training.md)); **6–8** in 2022; **under ~10** from Schoenfeld.
The direction holds anyway: **20 chest sets on one chest day is at least half junk**, and ten Monday plus
ten Thursday is better work for the same count.

The dissent matters. Helms argues there is likely **no per-session minimum, and probably no meaningful
maximum** — the literature's 10–20 weekly sets across five days is only ~2 sets per muscle per session,
and those protocols match or beat programs with three times that
([full body 5x](training-full-body-5x-per-week-smart.md)). Nippard concedes on camera that he used to
make the per-session argument and now thinks it may be invalid.

### The low-volume experiment

The newest note breaks with all of the above. In 2025 he ran **~6.5 sets per muscle per week for 100
days** while cutting — 1–2 sets per exercise, almost every set to failure — and came out **−5.5 lb fat
against only −1.8 lb lean mass**, gaining strength throughout with new rep PRs in the lowest-calorie
week ([cut sets in half](cut-number-sets-half.md)).

His four criticisms of the volume literature, which he says do not make it wrong: the studies **blast
one or two muscles** while the rest trains normally; they run at **maintenance calories**; they last
**6–12 weeks**; returns diminish. Supporting evidence: **Bickel 2011**, where subjects dropped to
**3 sets/week** and held 100% of size and strength for **32 weeks**; and **Hermann 2025**, where on one
set per exercise the **failure group outgrew the 2-RIR group**. With few sets you can push nearly every
one — the same trade-off he'd drawn from the Dorian Yates question six years earlier: **very high effort
and very high volume don't mix, pick one per block**
([HIT](dorian-yates-hit-training-good-size.md)). His prescription now: **cutting** → 4–10
sets/muscle/week, nearly all to failure; **bulking** → most muscles low, one muscle at **10–30
sets/week**, rotated every few months. He states it's n=1 on an advanced lifter, and that he publicly
reversed his prior position that volume shouldn't change on a cut.

### Frequency

**2× per week per muscle**, which beats 1× at matched volume — the one frequency claim with a real
meta-analysis behind it (Schoenfeld 2016, 10 studies). Whether **3× beats 2× is genuinely unresolved**
([bro splits](bro-splits-vs-higher-frequency.md)).

He is unusually critical of the evidence on his own side. The strongest volume-equated pro-frequency
study, **McLester** (8% vs 1% lean mass), used only **three sets per muscle per week**. In Schoenfeld's
frequency study the difference appeared **only in the biceps**. Wernbom's 2–3× recommendation rests
mostly on studies that weren't volume-matched, and in untrained subjects higher frequency shows no
benefit at all. He states his own bias toward low frequency and says he's correcting for it.

**The honest case for high frequency isn't MPS theory — it's volume spread.** Helms, who trains full
body five days a week, dismisses the MPS rationale himself as "largely theoretical"; the benefit is the
same weekly sets performed fresher, each session under the junk threshold
([full body 5x](training-full-body-5x-per-week-smart.md),
[high frequency](high-frequency-full-body-training.md)). Menno makes the mirror argument: the studies
**understate** frequency, because equating volume removes the free load increase you get from doing every
exercise fresh.

The two contested datasets: the **Norwegian Frequency Project (2012)** — national powerlifting team,
volume equated, **3 vs 6 sessions per body part per week**, the 6-day group gaining roughly twice as much
(**10% vs 5%** in the lifts) but never peer-reviewed, with hypertrophy **not significant (p ≈ .06)**;
and **Colquhoun 2018**, which retried it in early intermediates over 6 weeks instead of 15 and found
nothing. The **novelty confound** is the unresolved objection, conceded by Krieger, Schoenfeld and
Nippard alike: subjects arrive running bro splits, so the high-frequency arm gets a novel stimulus and
the control arm keeps doing what it always did
([Krieger](interview-james-krieger-training-volume-failure-rep.md)).

**Menno holds the most aggressive position in the library** and disagrees with Nippard directly, setting
frequency by training age via the "anabolic window" — positive protein balance lasting **over 72 hours in
beginners** and only hours in the advanced. So beginners are fine at 1×, intermediates want **3+**, and
advanced lifters can go **daily or twice-daily** at 1–2 exercises per muscle, because they aren't
recovering faster so much as **inflicting far less damage**. His warning: when you raise frequency,
**don't scale volume up in proportion** ([Menno](interview-menno-henselmans.md)).

**On bro splits**: they work at matched weekly volume (Hamarsland 2021), and once-weekly frequency still
produces robust growth. The problem isn't the architecture — it's that getting past intermediate means
raising weekly volume, and stacking those sets on one day makes them junk. His hybrid fix: the big volume
bolus, then a **small secondary hit of ~4 sets with one to failure** a couple of days later.

### Effort and proximity to failure

Failure is an **involuntary set endpoint** — despite greatest effort you cannot move the weight — not
"it felt hard" ([proximity to failure](how-hard-to-train-proximity-to-failure.md)). Between absolute and
technical failure he sits in the middle: minor form drift is fine if it buys real reps; once you're
inventing a new exercise it isn't failure.

**Target 1–3 RIR (RPE 7–9), with a good share at ~1 RIR.** Israetel's headline claim, which he says he'd
stake much of his scientific reputation on: **~2 RIR is the best average stopping point**, because
hypertrophy rises roughly *linearly* toward failure while fatigue rises *exponentially*
([Israetel](training-volume-deep-dive-israetel.md)). Load changes the answer: on **heavy sets** you can
leave 4–5 reps and still get a solid stimulus, since most motor units recruit from rep one; on **light
sets over 12–15 reps**, leave no more than 1–2. Nuckols adds exercise-specific rules — stop squats and
deadlifts at **technical breakdown**, stop rows when you **stop feeling the target muscle**
([how hard to train](hard-train-build-muscle.md)).

**The expert range is genuinely wide and he says so** — Zourdos will accept **5–7 reps from failure** in
some contexts, Helms says **0–5 RIR** with the one constant that below **RPE 6** you probably aren't
growing, Schoenfeld sets **1–3 RIR** plus the last set or two to failure. He calls it "the million dollar
question."

**Most lifters are nowhere near the debate.** In the Barbosa-Netto study, 160 trained men took their
self-nominated "10-rep" bench weight to failure and **0% got fewer than 10 reps**; 26% got 19–20+, and
**13% got more than 20**. **Over 40% were training at RPE 4 or lower**, average **16 reps** — six left in
the tank ([not training hard enough](not-training-hard-enough.md)). A separate Steele study found lifters
**underestimate their max reps by 3–4**, so he splits the gap roughly half misjudgement, half
unwillingness to sit in the discomfort.

**Israetel disagrees with that reading** — a real disagreement, not a nuance. Lab rep-max testing with
researchers shouting encouragement is **a meet, not a training session**, and he holds that the more
common beginner failure mode is going *too* close to failure with unstable technique, so beginners should
sit at **4–6 to 2 RIR** and add sets rather than intensity.

**Does failure beat stopping short? The literature genuinely conflicts** and he presents it that way
([pure muscle growth](train-pure-muscle-growth.md)). **Davies 2016** and **Gergich** (15 studies, ~400
participants) find no meaningful advantage; **Lacerda 2020**, one leg to failure and one to RPE 8, found
equal growth with a non-significant trend favouring the **non-failure** leg; **Refalo 2023** finds growth
rising toward failure with a spike from **2 RIR to 0 RIR**; **Robinson 2023** and **Hermann 2025** both
favour failure. Depending on inclusion criteria you can conclude either. His 2023 bridge: **2–3 sets per
exercise, 1–2 RIR on the early sets, last set to failure** — except heavy barbell squats, bench and
deadlift, where you leave a rep or two even then.

The **cost** side is better established than the benefit. Morán-Navarro 2017, volume-matched, in men
averaging eight years training ([when to train to failure](when-train-failure.md)):

| Marker | Not to failure | To failure |
|---|---|---|
| Countermovement jump | Impaired immediately post only | Impaired 24 h |
| Squat performance | Not impaired | Impaired 24 h |
| Bench press performance | Not impaired | Impaired 48 h |
| Creatine kinase | Baseline by 24 h | Baseline by 48 h |

But failure isn't categorically expensive — it depends on the lift. Deadlifts to failure are enormously
fatiguing; lateral raises to failure leave him recovered in a minute
([response to critics](need-train-harder-response-critics-training-style.md)). Hence the standing rule:
**save failure for isolation work and machines, on the last set of the last exercise for a muscle, or
the last week of a block before a deload.**

### RPE and RIR

RPE 10 = 0 RIR = failure; RPE 9 = 1 RIR. The reps-in-reserve version is credited to powerlifting coach
**Mike Tuchscherer** ([RPE guide](best-ways-use-rpe-gains-processed-foods.md)). Uses beyond picking a
load: **autoregulating load** ("3×8 at RPE 8", which beats fixed loading for strength); **correcting a
missed RPE** (came in at RPE 4 against a 6–8 target → add ~**8% load**); **autoregulating reps** (stop
each set at target RPE, whether that's 6 or 14 — how Helms trains); **autoregulating sets** (add sets
until you hit target RPE, **capped at 5–6 per exercise**); and logging your **last set's actual RPE**
week to week as a progression signal.

The critics, answered ([is RPE killing gains](rpe-killing-gains.md)): that beginners misjudge it shows
people **under-push**, not that the tool is broken — fix it with an **anchoring set**, calling "RPE 8"
out loud then pushing to failure to see how far off you were. And RPE is accurate in trained lifters:
average error was **1 rep at RPE 6 and 0.6 reps at RPE 9**. He also notes RPE was built **for elite
powerlifters, not beginners**, and that a survey of WNBF world champions found **none of them train at
maximum effort year-round**.

**Rep speed is the most objective cue available.** Halfway through a set reps have already slowed
~**30%**; the last possible rep moves ~**80% slower**. At 80% 1RM on bench, **50% velocity loss** ≈
**1.6 RIR**. Film a set — no slowdown means not close. His caveats: the equations were built on specific
lifts and don't extrapolate cleanly, and they break down for deliberately slow tempos.

### Rep ranges and load

**Hypertrophy is similar from roughly 3 to 35 reps when effort and volume are matched**
([rep ranges](rep-ranges-and-training-intensity.md)). Schoenfeld's group ran 3 vs 10 reps and 10 vs
25–35 and found comparable growth both times. **Strength was not equal** — the heavier groups won both.
In load terms the equivalence spans about **30% to 85% of 1RM**, with a floor near **20–30% of 1RM**
(Lasevicius 2018) ([heavy weight](heavy-weight-important-size.md)).

So why a practical zone? **Put ~75% of your volume in 6–15 reps.** Above 15 you get fatigue and lactate
for no extra growth. Below 6: higher injury risk — in the 3-rep study **dropout was far higher in the
3-rep group** — and volume gets impractical, since matching the 10-rep group's volume took the 3-rep
group **7 sets versus 3**. Keep some work outside the zone anyway: high reps build the work capacity that
lets you tolerate volume, low reps build the strength that lets you use more load inside it. The edges
drift between notes — **6–15** in 2018, **6–12** in 2019 and 2017, **5–20** from Israetel.

The corollary he returns to: **a bigger muscle is a stronger muscle, but a stronger muscle is not
necessarily a bigger one** ([Krieger](interview-james-krieger-training-volume-failure-rep.md)). In one
unpublished study Krieger reviewed, hypertrophy was similar across high-, low- and mixed-load groups
while **strength gains were wildly different**.

### Range of motion and lengthened partials

This is where he moved furthest, and he moved twice.

**2021 — full ROM wins.** Of the six ROM studies then available, **all four lower-body studies favoured
full range**; upper-body results conflicted. Bloomquist: lighter deep squats produced more growth **at
every measurement site across the whole quadriceps** than heavier partial squats, and trading range for
load is a bad deal — half the range at 315 lb is still **half the work** of full range at 225
([partial ROM](partial-range-of-motion.md)). The one exception he allowed was **constant-tension partials
on free-weight isolation lifts with a slack zone** — skull crushers, dumbbell flyes, lateral raises —
where Goto's 2017 study found the partial group gained **nearly twice the triceps cross-sectional area**
([constant tension](constant-tension-training-legit.md)).

**2023 — the stretch is what matters.** The evidence had turned over
([long-length partials](world-s-smartest-muscle-building-technique.md)): long-length vs short-length
partials, **8 studies, 7 favour long-length**, 1 tie, 0 against; full ROM vs long-length partials,
**5 studies, 4 favour partials**, 1 tie, 0 against — and in that tie the partial group used only **10% of
the full range** and still matched full ROM. Dr Milo Wolf's estimate of the effect size: **5–10% more
growth than full ROM**, worth having but not revolutionary. Two applications: extend the last set past
failure with **3–6 partials**, best on back and hamstrings; or do **every rep of the last set as a
partial**, slightly heavier, failing around rep 10. Three mistakes he names: treating partials as loose
cheat reps, not cutting the range enough (skipping the lockout is ~90% of the range, not a half rep), and
over-extrapolating to condemn every shortened-position exercise.

**2024 — his own study tempered it.** The first ROM study run on highly experienced lifters:
**30 subjects, avg 4.9 years training, 8 weeks, within-subject unilateral** — one side full ROM, the
other lengthened partials only, **every set to failure** under supervision, with Dr Wolf and Dr Pak on
the team ([his own study](everyone-thinks-m-wrong-about-training.md)). Result: **6.6% vs 5.9%, not
statistically significant**; biceps likewise null.

Counterintuitively the null made him use partials **more**. Two readings both hold: partials aren't
better than full ROM, or **you can get full-ROM gains from half the range** provided it's the stretched
half — which means the shortened half contributes little, or full ROM would have won. He is honest that
it tempers his own stretch advocacy, that nine prior studies still favour the lengthened position mostly
in less-trained subjects, and that he is an author on the paper. Where it lands: **full ROM as the
default, reaching a genuine deep stretch**, partials as a substitute where the squeeze is awkward and as
an end-of-set extender on back and hamstrings, and **never shorten the range to add weight**. "Full ROM"
means full within your own mobility — reach the hardest part of the lift and you're probably capturing
the effect, so **just below parallel is likely as good as ass-to-grass** for quads.

### Tempo

**Statistically equivalent hypertrophy from 0.5 to 8 seconds per rep** (Schoenfeld 2015). The one clear
finding against the extremes: reps over **10 seconds are worse**, because you have to strip so much load
that tension falls ([tempo](lifting-tempo-fast-vs-slow-reps.md)). Default to **1-0-1-0 or 2-0-2-0**;
notation is eccentric–pause–concentric–pause, **X** meaning explode. Deliberate prescriptions only for a
specific goal: heavy squats **1-0-X-2**, bench **1-1-X-2**, eccentric overload **3-0-1-0** on a pulldown
(flagged as ahead of the literature), a **3-second eccentric** for technique repair on squat and bench
but explicitly not deadlift, calves **3-1-3-1** for pump work.

The non-negotiable is **controlling the eccentric**. His cue: treat the negative as a **failed positive**,
**1–2 seconds**, with deadlift (lower faster) and squat (load the stretch reflex) as exceptions
([heavy weight](heavy-weight-important-size.md)). By 2023 that had lengthened to a **2–4 second negative
with an explosive concentric**, on the reasoning that the eccentric is very likely the more anabolic
contraction — which he still flags as unsettled — and that most people do the reverse, worst of all on
the final reps that matter most ([pure muscle growth](train-pure-muscle-growth.md)).

### Rest periods

**3–5 minutes on heavy compounds, 1–2 minutes on isolation, almost never under 1 minute.** The textbook
~1 minute for hypertrophy is outdated ([rest](rest-between-sets.md)). Schoenfeld 2016, 8 weeks, 1 min vs
3 min: the 3-minute group got **more hypertrophy in quads and triceps and more strength on both bench and
squat 1RM**. Grgic's 2017 meta-analysis of six studies: **>1 min beats <1 min**. The mechanism is just
that longer rest lets you do more total volume. Don't stopwatch it. Two legitimate exceptions to the
floor — **antagonist supersets** when short on time, and occasional pump work; one illegitimate reason —
**don't shorten rest to burn fat**, the calorie difference is trivial. The notes drift again: 2019 cuts
by **rep range** rather than exercise type ([Q&A](drop-sets-dumb-cardio-kill-gains-stretch.md)), and 2023
compresses to a single floor of **at least 1 minute**.

### Intensity techniques

Schoenfeld's verdict, which Nippard adopts: **nearly every specialised technique lacks strong hypertrophy
evidence**, his own NSCA paper on them was explicitly hypothesis-generating, and none should be written
off outright ([Schoenfeld](advanced-techniques-supersets-dropsets-eccentrics.md)).

**Supersets are a time-saving tool, not a growth tool.** Brentano compared **grouped** supersets (same
muscle back to back) against **separated** ones ([supersets](most-scientific-way-use-supersets.md)).
Grouped supersets kept creatine kinase elevated at **every time point through 5 days**, and the claimed
activation benefit was small, showed up in the **rectus femoris** rather than a prime mover, and on the
chest side showed the **front delts** taking over — the opposite of what you want from a pec deck.
Schoenfeld's own unpublished work found a **negative** effect from agonist–antagonist supersets. So: use
**separated** supersets when short on time, and better still **stagger the rests** — slot the second
exercise mid-rest so total rest on the main lift is unchanged.

**Drop sets buy efficiency, not superiority.** Three studies found no difference at equated volume; one
MRI study found a benefit but was underpowered. Reserve them for **advanced lifters, late in a session,
on small muscles** — leg extensions, preacher curls, lateral raises
([Q&A](drop-sets-dumb-cardio-kill-gains-stretch.md)). Krieger noted in 2016 there was **essentially no
data on drop sets at all**. **Accentuated eccentrics** are the best-supported specialised technique in
Schoenfeld's view, because eccentrics produce different intracellular signalling and a more distal
hypertrophy pattern, so the two may be synergistic — load them two-legs-up/one-leg-down, or with a bar at
**120–130% of 1RM** and a spotter lifting it while you lower. **Constant tension** applies only to lifts
with a circular resistance path where part of the range goes slack; it does **not** transfer to cables
and machines, which already supply near-constant tension.

**Inter-set stretching is a documented reversal.** He had called it bro science; a January 2019 Brazilian
trial changed his mind ([stretching between sets](consider-stretching-between-sets.md)). 29 subjects,
8 weeks, with **30-second static stretches of the trained muscle between every set**: pooled across four
sites the stretching group showed about **50% more growth**, though **only the vastus lateralis reached
significance alone**. Dosing: hold at or **below 30 seconds** (past 60 s reduces force and power) and
stretch to **mild discomfort only**, since stretching to pain has been shown to *decrease* hypertrophy.
The prior evidence was an unpublished calf study and a **1993 study on 26 quail**; mechanism unknown. Note
the tension with his general answer: absent a specific restriction blocking full ROM, general static
stretching isn't worth the time, and FST-7 fascia stretching he calls bro science.

### Progression schemes

Progression means adding **a rep, a set, or some load** to last week, and **adding a set is the fastest
way to add volume**, since one set contributes more total reps than +1 rep or +5 lb
([progression schemes](best-progression-schemes-bodybuilding-bro-split-training.md)). **Intuitive
progression works if and only if you track everything** — constrain it with a **fixed rep range per
exercise** and an **RPE cap** of ~9, last set often to failure. For volatile days, **DAPRE**: take a test
set to failure; targeted 6–8 and got **10**, add ~**10%**; got only **4–5**, drop **5–10%**. Structured
alternatives: **percentage-based**, **linear** (100 lb × 3×8 → 105 × 3×7 → 110 × 3×6, deload, restart at
105 × 3×8), and **double progression** (3×12 up to 3×15, then add load and reset).

The 2023 restatement is simpler: **add reps at fixed weight** in higher rep ranges, **add weight at fixed
reps** in lower ones, and when you can't do either, improve technique — better eccentric control, less
momentum, better range — which he counts as overload because it raises tension
([pure muscle growth](train-pure-muscle-growth.md)). The Fundamentals complement is the **2-for-2 rule**:
two extra reps on your last set for two consecutive weeks means add weight
([basics](training-basics-and-theory.md)). Adding weight while technique degrades is **progressive
cheating, not progressive overload**.

### Periodization and planned variation

**The research supports variation more confidently than any named model.** A review of 15 studies found
13 positive — but Kiely's 2012 critique is that those studies compare "regularly varied training" against
"minimal or no variation," so what they demonstrate is that **variation** matters, not that any model
delivers it optimally ([periodization](periodization-for-bodybuilding.md)).

- **Linear** — intensity up, volume down. Good for peaking and as a progression scheme.
- **Reverse linear** — volume up, intensity down. Generally loses head-to-head (Prestes 2009).
- **Undulating** — waves daily or weekly. Beats linear for strength in most but not all studies.
- **Conjugate** — Fonseca 2014: squat-only grew some quad heads, while **squat plus deadlift, lunge and
  leg press grew all four**.
- **Block** — specialise a body part **4–6 weeks at 3–4×/week**, then drop it to **~2×/week at half to
  two-thirds volume** while something else takes the slot.

**"Muscle confusion" fails as stated.** A within-subject unilateral study ran one leg on an identical
program for 8 weeks while the other had load, rest and set numbers constantly varied: **identical
growth** ([RPE roundup](best-ways-use-rpe-gains-processed-foods.md)). The real caveat: powerlifters after
years of heavy low-rep work grew better once **20+ rep work** was introduced. So **vary block to block
over months, not workout to workout**. His nuance on the other side is that absolutist overload thinking
is also a failure mode — he suspects **monotony and staleness may be the major cause of plateaus in
advanced trainees**, and prescribes a **"free training week"** of unstructured sessions with effort kept
high, distinct from a deload because the goal is freshness, not fatigue
([muscle confusion](using-muscle-confusion-scientifically.md)). Menno dissents on the whole framework:
block periodization **does not outperform a continuous program** in his read, and coaches who change
everything every four weeks keep clients **permanently novice at what they're doing**, so progress always
looks good while the year-over-year photos show nothing ([Menno](interview-menno-henselmans.md)).

### Deloads, overreaching and supercompensation

**A deload is one week where training is modified, not stopped** — active rest beats total rest for most
people ([deloads](deloads.md)).

| Type | What to do | For whom |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | volume **−30–50%** (1–2 fewer sets per movement), RPE from 8–10 down to **6–8** | default for bodybuilders |
| **Taper** | roughly **halve the sets**, cut accessories, keep loads heavy | powerlifters peaking a max |
| **Full week off** | nothing | travel, illness, mental break |

**Schedule them proactively every 4–8 weeks**, more often the more advanced you are. He prefers proactive
on practical rather than evidential grounds: tendons and soft tissue aren't well innervated, so joint
stress accumulates unnoticed and everything feels fine until something tweaks. Nobody loses meaningful
size or strength until **at least 2–3 weeks** off, so a week costs nothing physically. Who doesn't need
one: first-year beginners, and anyone not training hard enough to accumulate fatigue. The 2017 protocol
was more aggressive — **halve volume and cut load 25–50%** — and that note also reports **Ogasawara
2011**, where continuous training for 15 weeks produced the **same muscle** as training with a **3-week
break** in the middle ([overtraining](maximize-gains-not-overtrain.md)).

**Why it works — the fitness–fatigue model.** Every session raises both; when both are high, performance
is masked, and a deload flushes the fatigue so the fitness expresses itself. Israetel's diagnostic is the
sharpest version: **getting stronger week to week inside an accumulation block means you're
undertraining**. Strength should hold roughly flat through accumulation and jump after the deload; run
**3:1 to 5:1** ([roundtable](volume-roundtable.md)). The mesocycle itself: start at **MEV** around
**4 RIR**, add load or reps weekly so RIR falls to 3, then 2, then failure in the final week, then
deload. Length **3–8 weeks**, and **RIR determines it**
([Israetel](training-volume-deep-dive-israetel.md)).

**Supercompensation** — fitness dips during overreaching, recovers, then overshoots, so the gains appear
after you back off ([supercompensation](supercompensation-advanced-training-max-muscle-gain.md)). The
2019 Norwegian BFR study is the first evidence of **delayed hypertrophic supercompensation**: fibre size
**decreased** during training and grew most **10 days after** the final block. He is careful — **13
untrained subjects**, an unusual protocol, and **ultrasound results contradicting the biopsy results
within the same study** — and says explicitly he is **not comfortable broadly recommending overreaching
cycles**. The part he is confident about: **deloading when overworked reliably improves strength
performance**.

### Overtraining and rhabdo

**True overtraining is real but rare in lifters**, and most people worried about it would gain more by
pushing slightly harder ([overtraining](maximize-gains-not-overtrain.md)). The line: **overtraining is a
performance drop lasting more than 2–3 weeks**; a shorter dip is overreaching, which caught early is
functional. **Excess volume drives it more than intensity** (Schoenfeld 2010) — though Fry 1994 had
trained men do a **1RM machine squat 6 days a week for 2 weeks** and 1RM **dropped 12%**. Monitor with a
four-question self-survey (sleep, energy, aches, gym strength) or **average morning heart rate**: set a
one-week baseline, and a shift of more than ~**10%** means cut workload for 1–2 weeks.

The cautionary tale is his training partner Pat, who developed **rhabdomyolysis** after a contest prep —
muscle damage the doctors compared to a serious car accident, costing weeks to a month of training
([rhabdo](gym-partner-got-rhabdomyolysis-from-overtraining.md)). The setup: extended time at very low body
fat, badly suppressed post-prep recovery capacity, and the discipline to keep training at maximum
intensity anyway. The lessons are unglamorous — **take your deloads**, and **honour your RPE targets**.
Grinding a rep on a set prescribed as RPE 9 is where autoregulation stops protecting you.

### Time of day

**Consistency beats timing** ([time of day](best-time-day-train.md)). In a 24-week study of 72
recreationally active subjects there was **no difference at 12 weeks**, but in the **second 12 weeks the
evening group gained significantly more muscle**, with two earlier papers agreeing. His hypothesis is
performance — better hydrated, more fed, more awake — not hormones. The order effect is cleaner: **train
the quality you care about first, while fresh**; cardio first improved endurance, weights first improved
strength and size. If they share a session, cardio goes **after**. Caveats: subjects trained twice a
week, no extreme chronotypes, and the effect is small next to consistency.

### Where his position moved, 2016–2025

- **Volume, up then down.** 2016: **15 sets building to 20**. 2018: **10–20**, with the upper bound
  admitted to be expert opinion on top of the data. 2018–19: Krieger and Schoenfeld push him toward "no
  proven ceiling," **30–45 sets** still climbing. 2022: junk volume puts a hard **per-session** cap on
  it. 2025: he runs **6.5 sets/muscle/week** for 100 days and **publicly reverses** his position that
  volume shouldn't change on a cut.
- **Failure.** 2017–2020: no clear advantage, real fatigue cost, use sparingly. 2023: the meta-analyses
  conflict, so **last set to failure** as a bridge. 2025: on low volume, **nearly every set to failure**.
- **Range of motion.** 2021: **full ROM wins**. 2023: **long-length partials**, worth ~5–10%. 2024: his
  **own study finds no difference**, which he reads as licence to use partials more — while saying openly
  that it tempers the stretch hype he'd been selling.
- **Inter-set stretching.** Called it bro science; **publicly reversed** on one 2019 study.
- **Cable vs dumbbell flyes.** Argued cables were superior for continuous resistance; now does dumbbell
  flyes almost exclusively as long-length partials, the bottom-heavy resistance curve having become a
  feature.
- **Per-session volume minimums.** Used to argue a session needs a minimum stimulus; says on camera after
  the Helms conversation that this may be invalid.
- **Frequency.** Started with a stated bias toward low frequency and a defence of bro splits (2016); moved
  to running **full body 5×/week** himself (2020) — while never claiming 3× beats 2×.
- **Eccentric tempo.** **1–2 seconds** in 2019, **2–4 seconds** by 2023.
- **Rest periods.** Cut by **exercise type** in 2018, by **rep range** in 2019, down to a single
  **≥1 minute** floor by 2023.

### What is genuinely unsettled

- **Where the volume ceiling is.** Pelland 2024 says the response holds to 43 sets; Fisher and Steele say
  10 tops an inverted U; Barbalho says 5 beats 20. Nobody has tested high volume for the **whole body**,
  in a **deficit**, past **12 weeks**.
- **Whether 3×/week beats 2×** — and the best pro-frequency study used three sets per muscle per week.
- **The novelty confound** in every frequency study, conceded by Krieger, Schoenfeld and Nippard alike.
- **How close to failure you need to be.** Credible experts span **0–7 RIR**, and two meta-analyses reach
  opposite conclusions from overlapping evidence.
- **Whether the eccentric really is the more anabolic contraction.** Plausible, not established.
- **Whether metabolic stress signals hypertrophy at all.** He keeps it as a small factor; Menno considers
  every practical prediction it made falsified.
- **Delayed hypertrophic supercompensation.** One study, untrained subjects, biopsy and ultrasound
  disagreeing with each other.
- **How to count indirect volume from compounds.** Not 1:1, and nobody knows what it is.
- **Whether the per-session set ceilings are real at all.** Quoted as 5, 5–10, 6–8 and 10 across four
  years, and Helms argues there's no threshold in either direction.

## Part two — routines, splits and sample sessions

### Choosing a split

Nothing in the library says one split architecture grows more muscle than another. The three
questions that decide it, in order: **does it let you hit your weekly volume, does it allow
recovery, and will you actually run it.** Everything below is downstream of that.

What he has personally run, in order, tells you more than any argument:

| Year | Split | Sessions | Where it's written up |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2018 | Push/pull/legs | 6/week, each muscle 2× | [push day 1](most-effective-push-workout-chest-shoulders-triceps.md), [pull day 1](most-effective-pull-workout-back-biceps-rear.md), [leg day 1](most-effective-leg-workout.md), [push day 2](most-effective-push-workout.md), [pull day 2](most-effective-pull-workout.md), [leg day 2](most-effective-leg-workout-pt-2.md) |
| 2019 | Upper/lower | **6/week** — upper 3×, lower 3× | [leg day](most-effective-leg-day-2019.md) |
| 2019 | Upper/lower | 4/week | [upper day](most-effective-chest-back-workout.md), [posterior-chain lower day](best-posterior-chain-focused-leg-day.md) |
| 2020 | Full body, one muscle prioritised per day | **5/week** | [day 1 legs](most-effective-full-body-workout-muscle-growth.md), [day 2 chest](most-effective-full-body-workout-chest-focused.md), [day 3 back](best-full-body-workout-back-focused.md), [day 4 legs](most-effective-full-body-workout-leg-focused.md), [day 5 shoulders](most-effective-shoulder-focused-full-body-workout.md) |
| 2021 | Powerbuilding PPL | 6/week | [the 6-day PPL routine](push-pull-legs-routine.md) |

| 2023 | PPL, bodybuilding-biased | 6/week | [push](ultimate-push-workout-muscle-growth.md), [pull](ultimate-pull-workout-muscle-growth.md), [legs](ultimate-leg-day-muscle-growth.md), plus the [perfect push](perfect-push-workout.md) / [pull](perfect-pull-workout.md) / [leg](perfect-leg-day.md) second days |
| 2025 | Upper / legs, low exercise count | [upper](upper-body-workout-followed-1-year-transformation.md), [legs](leg-workout-followed-1-year-transformation.md) |

He has changed splits roughly every two years and never claimed any of the changes cost him
progress. The two structural rules that survive every version: **every muscle gets trained at
least twice a week**, and **no muscle gets more than 6–8 hard sets in one session** (see part one).

**Collapsing a split** is the standard fix for a bad week rather than skipping muscles
([travel and adherence](6-ways-always-stay-track-training-diet.md)): **4 gym days → upper/lower;
2 gym days → full body.** Frequency per muscle is preserved either way, and the lost volume is
paid for by training closer to failure.

**On the bro split** ([Sam Sulek audit](science-behind-sam-sulek-s-training.md)): he grades it
**C+**, not F. Two specific charges — a body-part split gives three-plus upper days per leg day
while the legs carry far more musculature, and it breeds junk volume (Sulek did **13 chest sets
plus two drop sets** in one session against a 6–8 set ceiling). The caveat he gives against
himself: **90%+ of IFBB pros use it.** Mike Israetel's line to him was that even enhanced
lifters would be better served by PPL, upper/lower or full body.

**Full body at 5×/week** is the most demanding option and the one he hedges most
([day 1](most-effective-full-body-workout-muscle-growth.md)): written for intermediate to
advanced lifters only, and if you have one or two years of training, run **3 days a week**
instead. It only works because fatigue is managed exercise by exercise — the same selections
would be pointless compromises on a 2–3×/week plan.

### The anatomy of a session

Across twenty-odd sample workouts spanning 2018–2025 the skeleton barely moves:

| Slot | What goes there | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **5–10 min** cardio (treadmill, StairMaster, elliptical) + **2–4 min** dynamic stretching; foam roll tight areas only | — |
| 1 | One heavy, overloadable compound. Low reps, tracked, **never to failure** | RPE 6–9 / 1–3 RIR |
| 2 | Back-off or second compound at **~75%** of the top-set load, higher reps | RPE 7–8 |
| 3–4 | Machine or cable compound — chosen so sets can go closer to failure without joint or stabiliser cost | RPE 8–9 |
| 5–6 | Stretch-position isolation (seated leg curl, overhead extension, dips, cable fly) | RPE 9–10 |
| 7 | Contracted-position or pump isolation, often with an intensity technique on the **last set only** | to failure |
| 8 | Calves / abs / neck, and an AMRAP finisher | all out |

The recurring **ordering logic**, stated in several places:

- **Heaviest first, while you're fresh** — every sample session opens with the movement you're
  trying to add weight to.
- **Weak point first.** [Push day 2](most-effective-push-workout.md) says to swap the overhead
  press and the close-grip bench depending on whether shoulders or chest lag; the
  [chest-focused full body day](most-effective-full-body-workout-chest-focused.md) prioritises a
  muscle purely by putting it first, and the
  [back-focused day](best-full-body-workout-back-focused.md) does the same. You prioritise by
  position, not by adding a day.
- **Never pre-exhaust the weak point** ([weak points](must-train-weak-points-differently.md)).
  Fatiguing a muscle before a compound makes it *less* active in that compound, not more. Light
  **pre-activation** is a different thing and is fine — 2 sets of 15–20 light cable pullovers
  before pull-ups on [pull day 1](most-effective-pull-workout-back-biceps-rear.md).
- **Alternate body parts to buy rest.** On full-body days he deliberately inserts an upper-body
  movement between two leg movements so the quads are fresher
  ([leg-focused day 4](most-effective-full-body-workout-leg-focused.md)), justified by the
  finding that longer rests support more hypertrophy.
- **Calves in the middle**, because at the end he is tempted to skip them
  ([back-focused day](best-full-body-workout-back-focused.md)) — and in
  [2025](leg-workout-followed-1-year-transformation.md) the same trick is offered for anything
  you habitually skip. **Abs on leg day**, because the hip flexors are already warm
  ([2023 leg day](ultimate-leg-day-muscle-growth.md)).
- **Leg curls first**, added in 2025 ([leg workout](leg-workout-followed-1-year-transformation.md)):
  a knee and posterior-chain primer that he says made his squats feel and perform better.

The **effort gradient inside a session** is the most consistent rule in the whole set: heavy
compounds stay **1–3 reps in reserve**, isolation goes to or near failure, intensity techniques
land on the **final set only** ([PPL routine](push-pull-legs-routine.md)). The reason is fatigue
accounting, not softness — [the chest and back day](most-effective-chest-back-workout.md) puts
it plainly: single-joint work carries a much lower systemic fatigue cost, so that's where you
spend your failure budget.

### The top-set / back-off pattern

This is his single most repeated prescription and it shows up in five different guises:

| Note | Top set | Back-off |
|---|---|---|
| [2023 leg day](ultimate-leg-day-muscle-growth.md) | Squat **1×2–4 @ 85–90% 1RM**, RPE 8–9 | Paused squat **2×5 @ ~75%** of the top set |
| [2023 push day](ultimate-push-workout-muscle-growth.md) | Bench **1×3–5** near max | Larson press **2×10 @ ~75%** (295 lb → ~225 lb) |
| [perfect leg day](perfect-leg-day.md) | Deadlift **1×5 @ RPE 8–9** | Stiff-legged deadlift **2×8 @ 50–60%** (405 lb → 225 lb) |
| [minimalist plan](best-minimalist-workout-plan.md) | Heavy **4–6** | Back-off **8–10** |
| [powerbuilding](get-bigger-stronger-same-time-powerbuilding.md) | One heavy **1–3** before your usual volume, once a week per lift | your normal 3×10 |

[The perfect push workout](perfect-push-workout.md) runs a variant — **8, then 5, then 15** on
close-grip incline bench, with **3–4 minutes** rest between the 8 and the 5. The 8 grooves the
pattern and tells you what to load for the 5; the 15 is pump work where load doesn't matter.

The powerlifters back the same structure for different reasons
([big three roundtable](big-three-roundtable.md)): in comp prep a top single is **third-attempt
rehearsal** — "approach the 250 kg squat as if it were 300, so that the 300 feels like 250" — and
in the off-season it is an **over-warm-up**, a single at 80% before working sets at 70%, costing
nothing. Nippard explicitly **rejects** the standard objection that a heavy single blunts the
volume work; his experience is that the volume work feels lighter afterwards. Hancott's
qualifier is the honest one: it only works if the top set is crisp, not a grinder. The
[beginner powerlifting note](programming-for-powerlifting.md) has the conservative version —
a submaximal single at **75–85% 1RM**, **about every other week**, never more than once a week
per lift, mainly on squat, and mainly for confidence.

### Progression schemes

He almost never uses "add 5 lb and hope". Each sample workout carries an explicit scheme, and
the choice is matched to how technical the lift is:

| Scheme | Pattern | Used on |
|---|---|---|
| **Double progression (reps)** | 6 → 7 → 8, then reset heavier in week 4 | Bench ([chest and back](most-effective-chest-back-workout.md)), close-grip bench ([upper body](best-full-upper-body-workout-max-muscle.md)) |
| **Double progression (sets)** | 2×5 → 3×5 → 4×5, then reset heavier | Deadlift, OHP, pull-up — the *technical* lifts, because extra sets buy skill practice ([posterior chain](best-posterior-chain-focused-leg-day.md), [shoulder day](most-effective-shoulder-focused-full-body-workout.md) at 3×6 → 5×6) |
| **Wave loading** | Week 1 **4 reps**, week 2 **5**, week 3 **6**, reset; three cycles = 9 weeks | Squat ([2019 leg day](most-effective-leg-day-2019.md)) |
| **Weekly undulation** | Heavy 4×4 / lighter 3×6, alternating | Squat ([full body day 1](most-effective-full-body-workout-muscle-growth.md)); deadlift 5s on odd weeks, 2s on even ([day 4](most-effective-full-body-workout-leg-focused.md)) |
| **Reps only, load fixed** | Find the heaviest 8 you can do *and still feel*, add a rep weekly | RDL ([leg workout](most-effective-leg-workout.md)) — past a point the low back takes over and the lift stops doing its job |
| **Fixed reps, load only** | Hold reps, add weight | Beginner powerlifting ([programming](programming-for-powerlifting.md)); hip thrust at a fixed **3×15** until execution is mastered ([leg day 2](most-effective-leg-workout-pt-2.md)) |
| **AMRAP** | Match or beat last week's reps in identical form | Pull-ups ([perfect pull](perfect-pull-workout.md)), push-ups ([shoulder day](most-effective-shoulder-focused-full-body-workout.md)) |
| **Time** | Add **5–10 seconds** per week at the same load | 60-second dumbbell holds ([push day 1](most-effective-push-workout-chest-shoulders-triceps.md)) |

Two honest details. On the pull-up AMRAP: **bulking, matching last week's reps is already an
overload** because you weigh more; **cutting, you must add a rep** because the resistance is
falling. And the whole scheme collapses if you swap exercises constantly — random exercise
rotation is what he names as **the number one cause of lifters becoming "lifetime
intermediates"** ([Sulek](science-behind-sam-sulek-s-training.md)). Hold most exercises fixed
for **1–2 months**.

### How the sample workouts changed, 2018 → 2025

This is the clearest arc in the library, and the later material supersedes the earlier.

**2018 — "Science Applied".** Long sessions, **17–19 working sets**, seven or eight exercises,
and the justification for nearly every choice is **EMG activation data**
([push day 1](most-effective-push-workout-chest-shoulders-triceps.md),
[pull day 1](most-effective-pull-workout-back-biceps-rear.md)). Standing dumbbell press over
seated barbell because Saeterbakken's EMG favoured it; Egyptian lateral raises because of a 1995
tendon-excursion paper; grip sequencing on pulldowns wide → medium → reverse. Heavy use of
partner-dependent methods — enhanced eccentrics with a partner pushing the bar down, tri-sets,
pre-exhaustion of *synergists* rather than the target ([pull day 2](most-effective-pull-workout.md)).
Each note flags the caveat itself: EMG is a proxy for stimulus, not a measure of growth.

**2019 — frequency and intensity techniques.** The split goes to **6-day upper/lower**, per-session
volume drops, and the frequency bump is the new stimulus ([2019 leg day](most-effective-leg-day-2019.md)).
Drop sets, accentuated eccentrics and mechanical drop sets are everywhere — and he reports the
research honestly against himself: accentuated eccentrics have **not** shown a clear hypertrophy
advantage, and drop-set evidence is unsettled per Schoenfeld.

**2020 — fatigue management takes over.** On the five-day full-body split, exercise selection is
driven almost entirely by recovery cost rather than activation
([chest-focused day](most-effective-full-body-workout-chest-focused.md)): chest-supported rows
and Smith shrugs to spare the spinal erectors, lying leg curls instead of glute ham raises
because RDLs are coming tomorrow, lat pulldowns instead of weighted pull-ups because squats
already took the day's hard-movement budget. The organising rule is stated outright: **only one
or two highly demanding movements per day**, and **what you damage today dictates what you can
train tomorrow.**

**2021 — powerbuilding, and the stretch idea arrives.** The [6-day PPL routine](push-pull-legs-routine.md)
makes the main barbell lifts **percentage-based and sub-maximal (RPE 5–8)** and pushes the
machines and cables to RPE 8–10. Long-muscle-length training becomes a running theme for the
first time: seated over lying leg curls, dips for the pec stretch, overhead extensions for triceps.

**2023 — fewer exercises, one heavy set, longitudinal evidence.** The Ultimate and Perfect series
drop to **six or seven exercises**. Squat strength is **one hard top set a week**. Citations shift
from EMG to actual hypertrophy trials — Maeo on seated leg curls, Nunes on calf foot position with
**direct measurement over 9 weeks**, Sato on preacher-curl partials, Chaves on incline vs flat.
Inter-set stretching appears (**30 s at 7/10**; 9.4 mm vs 6.2 mm thickness gain in the study he
cites, with the note itself flagging that significance held only for quads and pooled data).
He also, notably, **relaxes on strict form**: controlled body English on rows is now called a
feature, because back exercises are easy at the bottom and hard at the top, so ultra-strict form
can end a set before the back is actually exhausted ([perfect pull](perfect-pull-workout.md)).

**2025 — fewest exercises, everything to failure.** [Upper](upper-body-workout-followed-1-year-transformation.md)
and [legs](leg-workout-followed-1-year-transformation.md) run **six or seven movements with the
last set of every one taken to absolute failure, no spotter, for a full year.** Details get very
granular: leg-extension seat pushed as far back as it goes (**~170% more quad growth** in a 2024
study), lengthened partials past failure on curls and rows, a **30-second loaded calf stretch**
to finish. The measured result after 15 years of natural training was **+2.7 lb lean mass by
DEXA** — which the note itself calls within the noise of the method.

The through-line: **more exercises → fewer; EMG → longitudinal hypertrophy trials; strict form →
controlled cheating on selected lifts; free weights → machines and cables for the hard sets;
and stretch position moving from a footnote to the organising principle.** Where an old note and
a new one disagree, take the new one.

### Minimalist and time-constrained training

The numbers that make the case ([train like a minimalist](train-like-minimalist.md)):

| Weekly sets per muscle | Share of maximum gains |
|---|---|
| **1–4** | **~64%** |
| **5–9** | **~84%** |
| 10+ | 100% |

Strength is cheaper still — a 2017 meta-analysis found **81% of strength gains came from just
1–4 sets per exercise per week**. Health is cheapest of all: **30–60 minutes of lifting per week**
for maximum risk reduction on mortality, cardiovascular disease, cancer and diabetes. And
**maintenance** held for **eight months at one-ninth of baseline volume**. His template for his
brother was **one push, one pull, one leg exercise, once a week** — 20 minutes — and it improved
his strength and resolved his hip pain.

The price is quality: at that volume **most sets go to RPE 9–10**. The four time levers are drop
sets, **non-competing** supersets (bench with rows, not bench with flyes — a 2020 study found
performance dropped when supersetted exercises trained the same muscles), rest tapered
**15 seconds per week from 2 minutes down to 1**, and shorter warm-ups bought through exercise
selection (a barbell squat needs 4–5 warm-up sets, a machine hack squat needs 2). He timed one of
his own push days at **70 minutes, 14 of them actually lifting.**

[The two-day, under-45-minute plan](best-minimalist-workout-plan.md) is the worked version, and
the exercise logic is different from his normal logic: pick movements that **cover two jobs at
once** because you can't afford a second exercise — a dumbbell press or dip that supplies its own
stretch instead of a press plus a fly, a high-incline press for pecs *and* delts, an overhead
triceps extension instead of pressdowns. It scales cleanly: **3 days** = full body day 1 plus day
2 split into upper and lower; **4 days** = both days split into upper/lower pairs; **5 days** =
upper / lower / push / pull / legs.

The caveat he supplies himself: the 64%/84% figures come from a meta-analysis of 15 studies,
**mostly untrained or recreationally trained subjects, only two using properly trained lifters**.
And he isn't arguing for minimalism — he trains 1–2 hours a day himself.

### Home and no-equipment training, and who it actually limits

The programming rules are simple ([home workouts](build-muscle-home-workouts.md)): **3–5 full-body
sessions a week, 1–2 exercises per body part, 3–4 sets each, 1–3 reps in reserve with the last
set of each exercise to genuine failure.** Rep counts are explicitly not prescribed, because with
no external load the only variable that matters is proximity to failure. The named failure mode is
writing "3 sets of 20", hitting 20, and stopping when 40–50 were available. Difficulty is raised
through unilateral variations, constant tension, added range of motion, slower tempo, improvised
load, or no-load contractions.

[The deck-of-cards workout](ultimate-20-min-home-workout-build-muscle.md) is the same idea
gamified — **20 cards ≈ 20 minutes**, card number + 10 = reps, suits set the category, aces are
2-minute rests — justified by a 2019 study where randomised and fixed programs produced **no
difference in muscle thickness or strength but a greater increase in motivation**. The mechanism
that keeps it honest is **intraset feedback**: mid-set, ask whether it's hard enough, and if not
make it harder with tempo (a 5-second negative, 5-second pause, 5-second squeeze) rather than
chasing more reps.

Who this actually costs ([at-home training by level](approach-home-training-make-gains.md)):

- **0–2 years:** little to worry about. Training hard at home should **maintain for at least 3–4
  months** and may still add muscle, especially if the stimulus is novel.
- **3+ years:** genuinely harder, because the volume needed to move the needle is so much higher.
  Three recommendations: buy basic equipment if you can (**~$2,500 CAD** split between him and a
  friend bought a rack, plates and dumbbells), train hard anyway if you can't, and **eat in a
  surplus** — accept some fat gain, it attenuates muscle loss.
- **Everyone:** write off strength temporarily. Strength is specific and needs heavy load; muscle
  memory brings it back fast.

### Training by experience level

| | Beginner (yr 1–2) | Intermediate (yr 1–5) | Advanced (yr 4–5+) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job | Technique, linear progression, learn what failure feels like | Rep progression, then set progression; rotate exercises every **1–2 months** | Specialisation phases, intensity techniques, frequency experiments |
| Progression | **+5 lb/week** at a fixed rep count, **6–12 reps** | **+1 rep/week** in a fixed range, then reset heavier; add a set only as a last resort | Volume up **20–40%** on one or two lagging muscles for 1–2 months |
| Effort | learn failure | **1–3 RIR compounds, 0–2 RIR isolation** | last-set drop sets, myo-reps, eccentrics — sparingly |
| Expected gain | **10–25 lb** male / **6–15 lb** female | **10–20 lb** male / **6–12 lb** female | **1–2 lb/year** male / **0.5–1 lb/year** female |

([every fitness level](make-gains-every-fitness-level.md).) Nutrition barely changes across the
three: **1.6–2.2 g protein/kg**, **+10–20%** to gain, **−10–20%** to lose.

The strength side has its own ladder ([strength standards](strength-standards-by-training-age.md)),
as bodyweight multiples for a male 1RM:

| Level | Squat | Bench | Deadlift |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | **1.25×** | **1.0×** | **1.5×** |
| Intermediate | 1.25–1.75× | 1.0–1.5× | 1.5–2.25× |
| Advanced | 1.75–2.5× | 1.5–2.0× | 2.25–3.0× |
| Elite | 2.5–3.0× | 2.0–2.25× | 3.0–3.5× |
| Freak | **3.0×+** | **2.25×+** | **3.5×+** |

Three conditions attached: technique is assumed constant (full-depth squats, paused bench);
the ratios break down above **250 lb / 113 kg**, so plug your weight in as 250 regardless; and you
don't need to test a max — run a recent hard set under 5 reps through a calculator. The method
for advancing changes at each rung: **linear progression works until roughly the 1–2 year mark**,
then you move to undulating work (one heavy day at 3–5 reps, one lighter day at 6–10 or a
speed/technique day at 2–4), and past advanced you need specialisation phases, weak-point work
and dedicated hypertrophy blocks.

Rippetoe's definitions, which he endorses ([powerlifting programming](programming-for-powerlifting.md)),
are the cleaner test than years: **novice progresses workout to workout, intermediate week to
week, advanced month to month or year to year.**

**Teenagers** ([note](teenagers-train-same-as-adults.md)) get one adjustment and it isn't a
restriction: a 2014 international consensus position stand found **no evidence resistance training
harms linear growth or reduces adult height**, and flagged the reverse risk — failing to do
weight-bearing activity during growth may predispose to long-term bone problems. Injury risk is
the legitimate concern and it's a supervision and ego problem, not an age problem. **Program by
training age and athletic background, not birth date.** The one exception he makes to his usual
hierarchy: for teenagers, technique mastery outranks volume, intensity *and* progressive overload,
at least initially.

### Weak points and specialisation blocks

The prescription ([weak points](must-train-weak-points-differently.md)): **20–25 total weekly sets
including compounds, spread over 3–6 sessions.** Raise frequency rather than piling sets into one
day — twenty glute sets on one day makes far less sense than the same twenty across two to four
workouts. The frequency ceiling scales inversely with muscle size: **small muscles (side delts,
biceps, triceps, abs, calves) tolerate 5–6×/week; large muscles (quads, glutes, chest, lats,
traps) cap at 3–4.** Keep heavy compounds for that area to **2–3×/week** and fill the remaining
frequency with isolation work, because compounds by definition hit multiple muscles and cost more
recovery.

Then: weak-point work early in the session, deliberate mind-muscle focus on every isolation set
(eyes closed, or verbally marking "squeeze" and "stretch"), and drop sets or eccentrics only at
the end, only on isolation, only when conventional training has stalled.

The advanced version is the **specialisation phase** ([every fitness level](make-gains-every-fitness-level.md)):
**1–2 months, volume up 20–40% on one or two muscles** — 12 chest sets becoming 15–17 — and if
recovery suffers, drop something else to maintenance. The strength analogue is the same
([strength standards](strength-standards-by-training-age.md)): Alex Alpha Destiny reached a 405 lb
bench only by dropping his other lifts to maintenance.

**Does a dedicated arm day help?** ([arm days](arm-days-pointless-physique-posing-update.md))
His answer is no, not as a stimulus — it reorganises the same volume. He runs one because judges
across all six of his shows told him his arms needed to come up, and because training biceps
first in a pull session would blunt the back work that follows. The only two legitimate ways to
prioritise a lagging muscle are **train it first** or **give it its own session**; pick the one
whose cost you're willing to pay.

### Powerbuilding and beginner powerlifting

[Powerbuilding](get-bigger-stronger-same-time-powerbuilding.md) resolves the size/strength
tension with an allocation rule rather than a compromise. Rep range **barely matters for
hypertrophy** but **does matter for strength**, so:

- Size-leaning: most working sets **6–12**, roughly **a third in the 1–5 zone**.
- Strength-leaning: majority of volume **1–5**, remainder 6–12+.
- Practise squat, bench and deadlift roughly **2×/week each** (his own: squat 2–3×, bench 2–3×,
  deadlift 1–2×). Earlier-week sessions heavy to **RPE 8–8.5**; later-week sessions technique work
  further from failure, attacking sticking points with deficit pulls, paused pulls, paused squats.
- The big three leave **five muscles underdeveloped — lats, biceps, rear and side delts, calves,
  abs — give each 8–10 sets a week.** He likes a **roaming hypertrophy day** to mop up whatever
  fell short.
- Deloads **every couple of months, weight and volume down 25–50%**, used to improve technique
  rather than to loaf.

The year-scale structure is a four-block macrocycle: powerbuilding → hypertrophy block into
summer with strength work at maintenance → strength block post-summer → a final hypertrophy block
exploiting the new strength to break the earlier plateau. The shortcut for anyone who doesn't
want that is the weekly heavy top set described above.

For a genuine powerlifting beginner ([programming](programming-for-powerlifting.md)) the week is
**lower / upper / lower / rest / upper / lower / rest**, with each lift trained 2–3× and undulated:

| | First half of week | Second half |
|---|---|---|
| Squat | **3×6** | **4×3** |
| Bench | **3×6** | **4×3** |
| Deadlift | **4×3** | **3×6** |

Hold reps fixed and add load; avoid failure; fix technique now, before bad habits get grooved in.
The hierarchy is stated bluntly: **injury, then specificity, then overload** — powerlifting success
is mostly the ability to keep progressing without getting hurt, so slowing progression to stay in
the game is a win.

At the elite end ([big three roundtable](big-three-roundtable.md)) the advice inverts. **All three
world-class lifters test a 1RM almost never** — Hancott maybe once a year — and gauge progress from
bar speed, form quality and whether another set was available. **Beginners are the explicit
exception: test every 6–10 weeks**, because a beginner's response is unpredictable. All three take
**2–3 weeks of no training at all** after their biggest meet, not a deload, and Gibbs settled on
**three competitions and about three months off per year** after a 2014 season of four meets ended
in his worst total of the year. None of them counts macros or keeps a training log any more — and
all three did the meticulous version first.

### Coming back from a layoff

Do not resume at your old volume ([rebuilding after a break](re-build-muscle-after-training-break.md)).
Build a **2–4 week bridge**:

- **Intro phase (1–2 weeks):** compounds at **RPE ≤5** — concretely **50–60% of your old 1RM for
  sets of 4–6**, 5+ reps left in the tank. Isolation to **RPE 7–8**. Volume **7–10 hard sets per
  muscle per week**, **2–3 sets per exercise**, each muscle **twice weekly** (PPL-6 or upper/lower-4).
- **Transition phase (1–2 weeks):** compounds to RPE 6–7, isolation to RPE 8–9, per-exercise sets
  from 2 up to 3.
- **Then** normal training, with simple linear progression usually available for another month or two.

Two selection rules: **soreness is the enemy** — cut stretch-loaded, high-damage movements
(walking lunges, RDLs) out of the comeback plan entirely and prefer cables and machines, which
carry a better stimulus-to-fatigue ratio. And **write the plan down before your first session**;
freestyling makes overdoing it near-certain. Expect strength regain to take **about half the
length of the layoff** — two months off ≈ one month back — a heuristic that starts breaking down
past six months.

### Travel and adherence

[The travel note](6-ways-always-stay-track-training-diet.md): collapse the split rather than
skipping muscles, trade volume for intensity in the sessions you keep, and **autoregulate off
RPE 7–8 rather than chasing prescribed percentages** when a flight and bad sleep have changed what
a given weight means. On diet, loosen by exactly one notch — meal plan → macros → macro ranges →
calories plus a protein minimum → intuitive. The failure mode isn't the bad week, it's using it as
licence to stay off after you get home; most of the scale jump is water.

The same theme runs through [seven things he wishes he'd known](7-things-wish-knew-when-started-lifting.md):
give a program **at least four months** before judging your response to it, and **at least a year**
of consistent hard training before claiming bad genetics.

### What the critique videos reveal about his standards

Three notes are Nippard grading other people's training, and they're the clearest statement of
what he actually thinks matters.

**[Sam Sulek](science-behind-sam-sulek-s-training.md) — overall B.** He logged **460 sets**.
Volume distribution: **triceps 25%** of all training for a muscle under **4% of body musculature**,
quads **11%** for nearly **30%** of musculature; **113 tricep sets against 9 calf sets and 6 glute
sets.** Effort: **96% of sets to failure** (98% of real training). Reps: 11% at 0–3, 40% at 4–7,
40% at 8–12, 9% above 13. The three weaknesses graded C+ to B — range of motion cutting the
stretched portion, no program structure, bro split. The three strengths graded A to A++ — effort,
rep range distribution, and **sustainability and enjoyment**, which he rates so highly it lifts the
overall grade. His own counter-prescription: **~30% of sets to true failure**, which he operates as
3 sets per exercise, 1–2 RIR on the first two and failure on the last.

**[The influencer critique with Israetel](exercise-scientists-critique-fitness-influencer-workouts.md).**
The headline rule is a fatigue rule: **cranking load, intensity and volume all to maximum
simultaneously is what breaks people — turn up two of the three, not all three.** That's his
diagnosis of Ronnie Coleman's injuries against Dorian Yates, who trained just as heavy at lower
volume. Second: **exercise selection is a minor variable**; effort, volume, technique/ROM and
frequency are what drive growth. Third, on the "stop overcomplicating it" criticism, he largely
agrees — adding a technique because you understand the reasoning and slotting it into a sound plan
is good; rewriting your program every two weeks for the influencer of the week is not. Israetel's
sharpest point: there is **not a single long-term study on back training**, so everything said
about back training is inference from biomechanics.

**[Training with a natural pro](train-like-natural-pro.md)** is the opposite lesson — Alberto
Nuñez salvaging a session in an unfamiliar hotel gym on bad sleep. Lead with a **row**, the easiest
movement to set up and progress cleanly when travelling. Use **cluster sets** — one set of 8–12
near failure, rack, **4–5 deep breaths**, repeat for 3–4 mini-sets — so you get one guaranteed
high-quality effort even when pressing falls apart. Choose movements where cheating is structurally
impossible (the dumbbell seal row) so an added rep is real progress. No research is cited in the
whole video; it is the library's best illustration of what "just train" looks like when conditions
are bad.

## How to actually build your week

Work top-down, and stop when the honest answer is "that's enough".

1. **Pick the number of days you will genuinely train**, not the number you'd like to. Then take
   the split that fits it: **2 days → full body; 3 days → full body; 4 days → upper/lower;
   5 days → upper/lower/push/pull/legs or five full-body days; 6 days → PPL or 6-day upper/lower.**
   Every one of these appears in his own history and none of them is the answer.
2. **Fill each muscle to 10–20 hard sets a week, twice a week, never more than 6–8 in one
   session.** That constraint, not the split's name, is what the split exists to satisfy.
3. **Build each session on the template:** warm-up, one heavy overloadable compound at 1–3 RIR, a
   back-off or second compound at ~75%, machine and cable work taken closer to failure, stretch-
   position isolation, then a finisher. Put your weak point first. Never pre-exhaust it.
4. **Attach a written progression scheme to every exercise** — reps, sets, load, AMRAP or time —
   and hold the exercise for **1–2 months** so the scheme has something to measure.
5. **Spend your failure budget on isolation.** Compounds stay short of it; intensity techniques
   land on the last set only.
6. **Deload every 4–8 weeks**, and pick one thing at a time to specialise: a lagging muscle gets
   **20–40% more volume for 1–2 months** while something else drops to maintenance.
7. **When life shrinks the week, shrink the split, not the muscle list** — and pay for the lost
   volume with effort. **1–4 sets a week still returns about 64%** of the gains; nothing returns
   anything.

The rest of it — grip width, foot rotation, tempo, the exact machine — is real but small, and
several of his own notes say so outright. Get the week built, get it written down, get it
progressed, and repeat it long enough to find out whether it worked.
