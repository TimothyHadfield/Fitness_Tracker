# The Most Scientific Way to Train Your Back

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Scientific Way to Train Your BACK | Training Science Explained" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=12xHxUnBEiI)) · 2017-12-30 · 10 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Train the back **2–3x per week**. Be careful above **25 working sets per week** — per Dr Mike Israetel that is where most trainees hit recovery problems.
- Optimal lat pulldown technique: **to the front, medium grip (1.5x shoulder width), pronated**.
- Pull-ups and lat pulldowns produce **equal lat activation** at matched relative load; pull-ups just add more biceps.
- Rows are not just "thickness." They produce **equal or greater lat activity than pulldowns** plus more trap involvement — a row is mandatory for both width and thickness.
- 2–3 light pre-activation sets (one-arm lat pull-ins, cable pullovers) before back work help lifters who can't feel their lats.

## Anatomy
Four muscles for practical purposes:
- **Latissimus dorsi** — drives back *width*. Two main functions: shoulder **adduction** (arm out to the side, brought in) and shoulder **extension** (arm out in front, brought in). Train both.
- **Trapezius** — drives back *thickness*. Upper traps **elevate the scapula** (shrug); mid and lower traps **retract the scapula** (row).
- **Rhomboids** (major and minor) — scapular retraction; hit by the same exercises as mid traps.
- **Erector spinae** — run the length of the spine to the skull, extend the vertebral column.

## Pre-activation [01:01]
Many trainees can't feel their lats because biceps or traps take over. A **2009 study by Snyder and Leech** showed specific coaching cues — palpating the lats, consciously thinking about engaging them — increased lat EMG during the lat pulldown ([Snyder 2009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/19826307)).

Jeff's cues: pull in a straight line that would split the body into front and back halves; laterally crunch into the direction of the pull; palpate the lat and feel it contract. On cable pullovers: lean slightly forward, drive elbows **down not back**, tuck elbows tight to the sides.

Do **2–3 light sets, not to failure**, slow and controlled with mindful contractions, before training proper.

## Vertical pulls
- **Pull-up vs lat pulldown:** a **2013 study by Doma and colleagues** found no difference in lat activation at equal relative loading ([Doma 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24245055)). Biceps were more active in the pull-up — so use pull-ups if you want indirect bicep work, pulldowns if you want to reduce biceps involvement and isolate lats. Otherwise comparable for hypertrophy.
- **Front vs behind the neck:** three studies independently found no difference; one 2002 paper gave a slight edge to the front ([Signorile 2002](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/12423182)). Front also puts the shoulder in a less vulnerable position and allows heavier loads — **pull to the front**.
- **Grip width:** a **2014 paper by Andersen and colleagues** supports a **medium grip at 1.5x shoulder width** over narrow (shoulder width) or wide (2x shoulder width) ([Andersen 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24662157)). Differences were small; the basis was a trend toward higher eccentric-phase activation for lats, traps and infraspinatus, plus heavier usable loads.
- **Grip orientation:** a **2010 Penn State paper** found higher lat activation with a **pronated (overhand)** grip than supinated ([Lusk 2010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/20543740)). A study by Signorile and colleagues found pronated beat neutral ([Signorile 2002](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/12423182)).
- **V-bar / neutral attachment:** Jeff still includes it periodically despite the above, because it lets the lats be trained through **shoulder extension** with a vertical pull.
- Verdict: front, medium, pronated maximises lat recruitment on average — but findings are averages, so if a variation feels distinctly better for your lats, use it.

## Horizontal pulls [04:36]
The width/thickness split is wrong. Rows yield **the same or greater lat activity than lat pulldowns, with more trap involvement**.
- **Chest-supported T-bar row** is a staple for Jeff — more lower back support than a free-standing row.
- A **2009 study** found the **inverted row** beat the bent-over barbell row for lat activation ([Fenwick 2009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/19197209)); a **2005 study** found the **seated cable row** also beat the bent-over barbell row. Taken together, lat activity may be greater when you don't have to stabilise the lower back.
- The bent-over barbell row still earns a place: it thickens the erectors in a way chest-supported rows cannot.
- **Grip on rows:** no activation data exists. Use what's comfortable, or vary periodically. Borrowing from pulldown research, Jeff favours a **slightly wider than shoulder-width double overhand** grip — and notes double underhand carries biceps injury risk in his coaching experience.
- **Dumbbell rows** work both sides evenly (good for asymmetries) but cap your overload at the gym's heaviest dumbbell.

## Face pulls, traps and erectors
- **Rope face pull**, two versions: (1) a **high row** pulled to the eyes with scapular retraction each rep — targets mid traps and rear delts; (2) with **external rotation and scapular elevation** — targets rotator cuff, rear and side delts. Version 2 is great for shoulder and postural health but won't build traps.
- **Shrugs:** a **landmark 1994 study** found that because of upper trap fibre orientation, they can't effectively elevate the scapula when the arm is neutral — so a **slightly wider grip** maximises upper trap recruitment. Wider is awkward and limits load, so: **close grip for heavy power shrugs** using leg drive and momentum, **slightly wider grip for higher-rep strict shrugs**.
- **Deadlifts** train traps and shoulder stabilisers isometrically and build a muscular lower back, but involve so much leg musculature that Jeff classes them as a **lower body exercise**. For a trap/erector focus use **rack pulls or block pulls** — shorter ROM, more load on erectors and traps.
- **Erectors:** any programme with squats and deadlifts recruits them heavily, so isolation isn't required. If you can't or won't do those compounds, add **weighted lower back extensions**.

## Fiber type and rep ranges [07:09]
Lats and traps appear predominantly **type II (fast twitch) dominant** — may respond better to heavier loads. Erectors are more **type I dominant** — may respond better to lighter loads. Jeff still recommends **a variety of rep ranges for every muscle group** for maximum overall development.

## Practical application
- **Frequency: 2–3x per week** for most people.
- **Volume ceiling: be careful above 25 working sets per week** (Dr Mike Israetel).
- To prioritise a lagging back: train it **early in the week, after a rest day, and at least 48 hours away from leg days** for total body recovery.
- Add **3–4 quick sets of wide-grip pull-ups on non-back days** (e.g. at the end of a leg workout) to raise weekly volume.
- Minimum effective template: one vertical pull (front, medium, pronated pulldown or pull-up), one row (chest-supported or cable), face pulls, shrugs, and let squats/deadlifts cover the erectors.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Most exercise comparisons here rest on EMG activation data, which is a proxy for hypertrophy rather than a measure of it.
- Several grip/attachment findings conflict with each other and the effect sizes are described as small.
- No activation data exists for grip variations on rows — that recommendation is inference plus coaching experience.
- Specific volume and set recommendations are described as individual; the video points to a paid program for them.
- One researcher name ("Signorile") is garbled in the captions; it matches Signorile et al. 2002 in the reference list.
