# Peak Week for Bodybuilding: Three Elite Coaches Compare Protocols

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "THE PEAK WEEK ROUNDTABLE feat. Cliff Wilson, Eric Helms, Jason Theobald" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DLjq6Zn86w4)) · 2016-05-11 · 139 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- All three agree peak week is a small fraction of the outcome, but Helms stresses the asymmetry: a botched week can make a stage-ready athlete look six weeks out [08:16].
- Water is the variable everyone leaves alone. Wilson runs it high and flat (2–3 gal/day, men), Helms keeps it normal, Theobald only manipulates enhanced athletes.
- Sodium is the most reliable lever: all three give **1–3 g right before the pump-up**. Wilson alone runs multi-day sodium far higher (8,000–10,000 mg on load day).
- Carbs are the real split: Wilson's **rapid backload** (850–1,600 g in one Friday) vs Helms' **front load** (Tuesday, taper, back up Friday) vs Theobald's case-by-case, including fat loads.
- Nobody supports cutting water while loading carbs — rejected on physiology by Helms and Wilson, on personal experiment by Theobald.
- Helms: "If anyone is talking about hard science behind peak week, they're overstating the evidence" [21:35].

## How much it matters [06:46–09:16]
**Wilson**: small against the whole prep, but importance *rises* with how ready you are and how competitive the show is. **Helms**: the upside is small, the ability to wreck yourself is large. **Theobald**: beginners over-invest emotionally and are always underwhelmed. Helms' meta-point [67:45]: these are **skill sets, not just protocols** — he calls Wilson "an artist with the backload," which doesn't transfer with the spreadsheet. Wilson agrees: 99% of the time he tells enquirers not to use it, and he trialled it off-season on two contest-lean athletes before ever running it at a show [71:24].

## Water
**Wilson** [09:47, 19:34]: high and constant — females **1.25–2 gal/day**, males **2–3 gal/day**, unchanged through show day whatever the loading style. He needs water available to store with Friday's glycogen and wants excretion already running at that level; jumping 1 gal to 3 gal overnight is too abrupt to trust. Sole exception: a bikini competitor who is *too* hard gets a conservative reduction to soften her.

**Helms** [11:18]: the least-changed variable, but he prescribes a number for beginners and stress-prone clients because stress and travel make people drift. Static water also keeps sodium excretion predictable.

**Theobald** [13:23]: naturals steady and high (~1.5 gal); enhanced athletes are "all over the board."

**Spill rescue** (Helms) [21:35]: cut planned carbs, push water up, run multiple pump-ups — he did this himself after spilling at prejudging at the show where he won his pro card.

**Enhanced athletes** (Theobald only) [14:56–27:11]: pull testosterone ~2 weeks out, raise the anti-estrogen (Arimidex), finish on an oral. Taper water if needed (3 → 3 → 2.5 → 2 gal → 3 quarts Friday). Prefers OTC products or a potassium-sparing prescription diuretic (captioned "DIY"/"diai" — likely **Dyazide**, uncertain), and explicitly warns off loop diuretics like Lasix: you can't tell whether a problem is sodium or potassium and "you could kill" someone. Water vs fat diagnosis: roll the skin — granules mean fat remains, skin-on-skin means water.

## Sodium and potassium
**Helms** [37:27–41:32]: the classic sodium/potassium-pump reasoning confuses *extracellular* with *subcutaneous* and *intracellular* with *intramuscular* — not synonyms, and the vascular system is an extracellular space you want filled. Competitors who cut sodium can't get a pump backstage and look muted, "like they're wearing a brown paper bag"; blood pressure, heart rate and dietary sodium all fall through a prep. Protocol: food and potassium normal, water static, then **1–3 g sodium immediately before the pump-up**, shot in water rather than with food so digestion isn't slowed. Half a teaspoon of table salt ≈ just over 1 g sodium. Double load — 2 g before prejudging, 2 g before finals.

**Theobald** [42:02]: **1/8 tsp sea salt per meal** all prep (1/4 tsp for heavy/super-heavyweights) plus condiments. Peak week he keeps the salt and pulls the *condiments* Thursday or Friday for a slight downward shift. Pre-stage: **500 mg–1 g** sea salt mixed into something sweet (highly branched cyclic dextrin).

**Wilson** [47:36–50:37]: from the Saturday a week out, males **4,800–6,000 mg/day**, females **3,000–4,000 mg/day** — high enough that the body adapts to clearing excess before load day. Friday sodium goes to **8,000–10,000 mg**, justified by the sodium-glucose co-transporter and blood volume, since a one-day load makes absorption efficiency critical. Potassium is *depleted* Monday–Thursday to flatten them, then loaded early Friday morning via sweet and red potatoes to roughly **12,000 mg**, tapering across the day. Saturday is even, plus a pre-stage hit.

Helms notes totals converge more than they sound: 2 g × 2 on top of a diet already containing 3–4 g isn't far off [94:37]. All three agree acute single-day sodium is very safe — Helms' default fix for an athlete who looks bad for unclear reasons is *more* sodium. It's **multi-day** loading where Wilson has seen blurring [61:17]. Wilson also reports — flagged by him as controversial and purely observational — using more conservative sodium and lower carb loads with black competitors, attributing it to insulin resistance prevalence [57:43, 78:39].

## Carbohydrates
**Wilson's rapid backload** [74:28–93:37]:
- Two to three high-carb days the Saturday/Sunday a week out, restoring glycogen-storage efficiency before the deplete.
- Monday–Thursday **carbohydrate** deplete, not calorie deplete: carbs to **40–100 g/day**, calories held near normal by shifting into protein and fat. The scale drop is water, not muscle.
- Friday: **850–1,600 g of carbs in one day**. Most male bodybuilders land at **1,000–1,250 g**; female bodybuilders **550–800 g**. He deliberately under-prescribes (estimate 1,200, write 1,050–1,100) because flat beats spilled.
- Foods are practical, not magic: Cheerios plus sweet/red potatoes, after oatmeal proved unmanageable in volume. Fat ~**30–40 g** on load day.
- Show day is moderate: **20–40 g carbs every couple of hours**, fat **15–25 g every few hours** (Reese's cups, olive oil from a tablespoon).
- He rejects the textbook 3-day-deplete/3-day-load supercompensation model — aggressive multi-day loads spill [77:35].
- Key diagnostic: he wants **slight blurring Friday evening**, not sharpness, hypothesising glucose arrives faster than glycogen synthesis absorbs it. Athletes razor sharp Friday night wake up *less* sharp [84:53].
- Only for very lean male bodybuilders and some very lean figure competitors, never bikini. "If there's any pocket of fat you can pinch, don't do it" [59:15]. Expect **6–10 lb of scale gain** by show morning.

**Helms' front load** [79:42–83:50]: load Tuesday, taper Thursday, back up Friday, taper into the show. Everything anchors to the athlete's **refeeds** (1–3×/week through prep at roughly off-season carb levels): load days are **90–120% of the highest refeed**, taper days sit midway between low and high days, show day is roughly **75% of a refeed**. He never front-loads blind, but finalises everything by Friday night because online coaching can't rely on hotel Wi-Fi, time zones or email delays on show morning. He also targets *slightly* spilled Friday night. He credits Brad Loomis ("Brad Lumis") for shifting his front loads toward a more aggressive skip-loading style learned from Skip Hill ("skip Lor," uncertain).

**Theobald** [72:27–90:29]: no single rule. In one day he wrote five different peak weeks — a **fat load** for an athlete who thrives on keto and bloated unpredictably on any carbs; a moderate ~300 g Thursday + 300 g Friday; and one where the athlete simply got a Friday-night cheat meal. He argues for **dietary fat during loading**: he doesn't push carbs as high, and fat helps athletes hold the look rather than burn through it.

**Why load formulas fail** (Helms) [34:21]: muscle glycogen isn't a fixed gas tank — capacity expands and contracts with habitual carb intake, so someone dieted on very low carbs spills on a "body-weight-appropriate" load while someone on high carbs chews straight through it. Theobald used Dan Duchaine's formulas ("Dan Dukan," uncertain) himself and found the same.

**The "dry backload" — unanimous rejection** [30:44–36:25]. Helms: if roughly 3 of 4 grams stored is water, restricting water removes three-quarters of the fullness. Wilson: the body won't obligingly pull water only from subcutaneous space. Theobald's own trial: a full gallon with ~1,200 g carbs looked good; cutting to 3/4 then 1/2 gallon flattened him progressively even on 600 g of carbs.

## Cardio, training, show day
- **Cardio**: all taper. Wilson increases slightly during the deplete, none during the load [98:13]. Helms unchanged unless heavy cardio was needed. Theobald pulls HIIT ~7 days out, walking to Wednesday, then posing.
- **Training — Helms** [100:16]: remove large-eccentric work (RDLs, heavy flyes), nothing below 8–10 reps, RPE down, similar set volume, finished two days out, reordered lower/upper/lower/upper so legs get an extra recovery day. **Theobald** [101:49]: no failure from Monday, legs done 6–7 days out, last session Wednesday, then posing — but he *wants* bodybuilders flexing and pumping legs while carbs come in, unlike coaches who rest them completely. **Wilson** [103:21]: every client gets the *same* template (legs Mon, back Tue, chest Wed, shoulders/arms Thu, full-body circuit Fri) purely so training can be eliminated as a variable mid-week. Helms is sceptical of high-rep 20–30 "sarcoplasmic" depletion work — a large volume increase, and any adaptation probably takes weeks [105:23].
- **Morning flatness** [86:26–89:28]: Helms confirms looking flat on show morning is typical, and athletes peak more reliably in single-evening "live judging" formats. Fix: first meal 3–4 hours before stage time, often 3–4 a.m.
- **Between prejudging and finals**: Helms and Theobald mostly repeat the morning macros (Theobald adds a clean meal out plus 10–12 oz water). **Wilson manipulates aggressively** [96:10] — in a single pro class with judging done he pushes carbs to find the real spill point for next time; with multiple classes he has families photograph the top competitors in *other* classes and adjusts to beat that look, fuller against a fuller rival, flatter and sharper against a leaner one.
- **Pump-up**: Wilson goes aggressive — everything but legs and abs — arguing over-pumping only risks someone who *didn't* eat 1,000+ g of carbs the day before [115:41]. Theobald is conservative: bands and push-ups from 15–20 min out after water and sodium, going hard only on a verified spill. Helms is moderate but saw a large benefit going aggressive after his own spill.
- **Tanning** [106:53–115:11]: Dream Tan is oil-based, never dries, survives sweat and highlights whatever you have — flaws included — so Wilson uses it only on very lean bodybuilders; many organisations (the NPC included) ban it because it wrecks venues and suits, leaving a good spray tan plus light oil as the fallback. Wilson: no oil under bright lights, a little under dim then re-pat; never oil over Dream Tan.
- **Bikini** [61:48–65:53]: Helms runs almost no peak — ~1 g sodium, pump not vascularity. Wilson works from where the athlete is, building sodium up for someone needing size, but for one genetically elite figure competitor he *depletes* sodium and deliberately loses muscle to shrink her. On PMS bloat he keeps an herbal diuretic (Xpel) on hand but rarely uses it — it feels far worse than it looks.

## Points of disagreement
- **Carb strategy.** Helms says openly [70:21] that he gave a client a moderate load instead of the backload they wanted, and that his scepticism is that Wilson works with elite athletes who "would look pretty damn good on any approach." Wilson answers that he developed it in 2010–11 on amateurs, before elite competitors sought him out.
- **Sodium magnitude.** Wilson's 4,800–10,000 mg/day multi-day protocol is far outside what the others run.
- **Potassium.** Wilson depletes then loads it (~12,000 mg Friday); Helms deliberately leaves it alone; Theobald warns loading potassium raises aldosterone [17:32].
- **Legs during peak week.** Theobald wants them flexed and pumped while loading; coaches he references rest them completely; Helms just moves them earlier.
- **An athlete who isn't ready.** All three coaches say pick another show and refuse to crash-diet — Helms: they'll get leaner but "still not lean enough and flat as [expletive]"; Wilson pre-negotiates this before prep begins. **Nippard dissents from his own panel** [128:54]: before turning pro at the 2014 Mayhem he ran a protein-sparing modified fast from Lyle McDonald's rapid fat loss book and dropped ~2–3 lb in the final weeks against a more conditioned field. Helms concedes the result but caveats that Nippard holds muscle unusually well and it would ruin an average-genetics athlete.
- **When to feed up.** Helms and Theobald say ~95% ready. Wilson says 99% for a client coasting along — but agrees with 95% for a high-stress athlete, because the relief of more food raises NEAT and training quality and often drives the last losses [124:52].
- **Scope.** Helms flags that everything he says applies to natural competitors only; Nippard defers to Theobald on enhanced athletes.

## Practical application
- Low-variance default (Helms' whole protocol): hold water constant, hold sodium constant through the week, add 1–3 g sodium 15 minutes before pump-up, load carbs at roughly your biggest refeed.
- Practise any aggressive protocol off-season while contest-lean before using it at a show.
- Target *slightly blurred* Friday night, not razor sharp — Wilson and Helms independently look for the same thing.
- Don't cut water while loading carbs. Minimise every variable you aren't deliberately manipulating.
- Eat 3–4 hours before stage time, even at 3 a.m.
- Psychology: Wilson issues a **day-by-day written forecast** of what the athlete will feel physically and mentally; Theobald uses phone calls and phosphatidylserine; Helms tells clients to stop mirror-checking, and warns coaches that stressed clients show variable weight loss which has tricked him into cutting calories too early [136:12].

## Caveats & unknowns
- There is essentially no research on peak week [21:35]. Wilson labels his own mechanisms as hypotheses — "sometimes I'm just taking an educated guess" [19:04]. The 1:3/1:4 glycogen-to-water ratio underpinning much of the reasoning is flagged by Helms as "a huge question mark."
- Wilson's race-based sodium and carb observations are uncontrolled anecdote from one practice, offered as such.
- Theobald's enhanced protocols involve prescription drugs and are reported, not endorsed; he flags loop diuretics as potentially lethal.
- Garbled names flagged as uncertain: the diuretic ("DIY"/"diai," likely Dyazide), "Dan Dukan" (likely Dan Duchaine), "skip Lor" (likely Skip Hill), "Brad Lumis" (likely Brad Loomis), "wbf" (likely WNBF).
- Recorded 2016; consensus on water and sodium manipulation has moved since.
