# Peak Week for Bodybuilding: Three Elite Coaches Compare Protocols

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "THE PEAK WEEK ROUNDTABLE feat. Cliff Wilson, Eric Helms, Jason Theobald" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DLjq6Zn86w4)) · 2016-05-11 · 139 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- All three coaches agree peak week is a small fraction of the outcome relative to the prep — but Helms stresses the asymmetry: the downside is much larger than the upside. A botched peak week can make a stage-ready athlete look six weeks out [08:16].
- Water is the variable everyone leaves alone. Wilson runs it high and flat (2–3 gal/day for men), Helms keeps it "normal," Theobald keeps naturals steady and only manipulates enhanced athletes.
- Sodium is the most reliable lever. All three load 1–3 g acutely right before the pump-up. Wilson also runs multi-day sodium far higher than the others (up to 8,000–10,000 mg on load day).
- Carbohydrate approach is where they genuinely split: Wilson's "rapid backload" (850–1,600 g in one day, Friday) vs Helms' front load (Tuesday, taper, back up Friday) vs Theobald's case-by-case, including a fat load for a keto athlete.
- Nobody supports cutting water while loading carbs. All three argue against it on physiology and, in Theobald's case, on personal experiment.
- Helms: "If anyone is talking about hard science behind peak week, they're overstating the evidence" [21:35]. Every protocol here is experiential.

## How much peak week actually matters
Wilson: a small percentage against the whole prep, but its importance *rises* with how ready you are and how competitive the show is — if you still need to drop six or seven pounds, peak week can't rescue you; at pro level the gap between first and eighth is tiny [06:46].

Helms takes the more conservative framing deliberately for a public audience: the ability to improve is small, but the ability to wreck yourself is large, and he has seen it repeatedly [08:16].

Theobald: beginners over-invest in it emotionally and are always underwhelmed when they receive their protocol [08:46].

Helms also raises a point that recurs all show — these are **skill sets, not just protocols**. Two coaches running the same spreadsheet get different results because the eye for diagnosis is developed over years. He calls Wilson "an artist with the backload," which does not transfer with the document [67:45]. Wilson agrees: 99% of the time people ask him about the rapid backload, he tells them not to use it, and he trialled it on two contest-lean athletes off-season before ever using it at a show [71:24].

## Water
**Wilson** [09:47]: keep it high and constant all week. Female competitors 1.25–2 gallons/day, males 2–3 gallons/day, unchanged through show day, regardless of whether he's front-loading or back-loading. The rationale for high-and-flat: he needs a large amount of water available to store with glycogen on Friday, and he wants the body's excretion machinery already running at that level so it can dump any excess. Going 1 gallon Mon–Thu then 3 on Friday is too abrupt to trust [19:34]. The one exception he makes is a bikini competitor who is *too* hard — he reduces water conservatively to soften her [10:17].

**Helms** [11:18]: water is the variable that changes least. His practical addition is behavioural — stress and travel make people drift, either forgetting to drink or compulsively refilling. For beginners and high-stress clients he prescribes a number just so it's tracked, while telling them 100 ml either way is irrelevant. He keeps water static partly so that sodium excretion stays predictable, since he does manipulate sodium.

**Theobald** [13:23]: naturals — steady and high (he cites a client who ran 1.5 gal throughout and won classic physique, the overall and open bodybuilding). Enhanced athletes are "all over the board" and depend on how the individual holds water on their compounds.

**Spill rescue** (Helms) [21:35]: cut planned carbs, push water up, run multiple pump-ups. He did exactly this himself — spilled at prejudging at the show where he won his pro card, fixed it by finals, won the class, then faded, possibly from going sodium-flat via sweat and repeated pump-ups.

## Enhanced-athlete water (Theobald only)
Pull testosterone roughly two weeks out, raise the anti-estrogen (he names Arimidex), finish on an oral [14:56]. Taper water if needed — e.g. 3 gal → 3 gal → 2.5 → 2 → 3 quarts Friday. Prefers over-the-counter products or a potassium-sparing prescription diuretic (transcribed as "DIY"/"diai" — most likely **Dyazide**, uncertain) and explicitly warns against loop diuretics like Lasix, because you cannot tell whether a problem is sodium or potassium and "you could kill" someone [27:11].

Distinguishing retained water from remaining fat: have them roll the skin. Granules = fat still there. Skin-on-skin with no granules = water [15:26].

## Sodium and potassium
**Helms** [37:27]: the classic "sodium/potassium pump" reasoning confuses *extracellular* with *subcutaneous* and *intracellular* with *intramuscular* — they are not synonyms, and the vascular system is one of the extracellular spaces you actually want filled. Competitors who cut sodium consistently can't get a pump backstage and look muted, "like they're wearing a brown paper bag." Blood pressure and heart rate drop substantially through a prep, carbs and sodium drift down, and athletes forget what a pump feels like. His protocol: keep food and potassium normal, keep water static, then give **1–3 g of sodium immediately before the pump-up**, shot in water rather than with food so digestion isn't slowed. Half a teaspoon of table salt ≈ just over 1 g sodium [41:02]. He runs a double salt load (2 g before prejudging, 2 g before finals).

**Theobald** [42:02]: 1/8 tsp sea salt added to every meal from the start of prep (1/4 tsp for heavyweights/super-heavyweights), plus condiments (mustard, Frank's, Walden Farms). Peak week he leaves the added salt in and pulls the *condiments* Thursday or Friday for a slight downward shift. Pre-stage: 500 mg–1 g sea salt, mixed into something sweet (highly branched cyclic dextrin) so it's drinkable.

**Wilson** [47:36]: from the Saturday a week out, males **4,800–6,000 mg sodium/day**, females **3,000–4,000 mg/day**. Load day (Friday) sodium goes to **8,000–10,000 mg**, because of the sodium-glucose co-transporter and blood volume — with a one-day load, absorption efficiency and timing matter. Potassium is *depleted* during the Monday–Thursday carb deplete to flatten them, then loaded hard early Friday morning via sweet and red potatoes to roughly **12,000 mg**, tapering across the day. Saturday returns to even levels plus a pre-stage sodium hit.

Helms points out their totals are closer than they sound: his 2 g × 2 on top of a diet already containing 3–4 g is not far off [94:37]. All three agree acute single-day sodium is very safe; it's *multi-day* sodium loading where Wilson has seen blurring [61:17].

Wilson also reports — flagged by him as controversial and purely observational — that he uses more conservative sodium and lower carb loads with black competitors than white competitors, attributing it to insulin resistance prevalence [57:43], [78:39].

## Carbohydrates
**Wilson's rapid backload** [74:28]:
- Two to three high-carb days the Saturday/Sunday a week out, to restore glycogen-storage efficiency before depleting.
- Monday–Thursday **carbohydrate** deplete, not calorie deplete: carbs down to **40–100 g/day**, calories held near normal by shifting into protein and fat. He explicitly rejects near-starvation depletes.
- Friday: **850–1,600 g of carbs in one day**. Most male bodybuilders land at **1,000–1,250 g**; female bodybuilders **550–800 g**. He deliberately under-prescribes (if he estimates 1,200, he writes 1,050–1,100) because flat beats spilled.
- Food choice is practical, not magic: Cheerios and sweet/red potatoes, after oatmeal proved unmanageable in volume. Fat lands around **30–40 g** on load day.
- Show day is deliberately moderate: **20–40 g carbs every couple of hours**, with fat pushed to **15–25 g every few hours** (Reese's cups, olive oil shot from a tablespoon) [93:05].
- He rejects the textbook 3-day deplete / 3-day load supercompensation model — aggressive multi-day loads spill [77:35].
- Key diagnostic: he wants **slight blurring by Friday evening**, not sharpness. His hypothesis is that glucose is arriving faster than glycogen synthesis can absorb it. Athletes who look razor sharp Friday night wake up less sharp Saturday [84:53].
- Restricted to very lean male bodybuilders and some very lean/thin figure competitors. Not bikini. "If there's any pocket of fat you can pinch, don't do it" [59:15].
- Expect 6–10 lb of scale weight gain by show morning [115:41].

**Helms' front load** [79:42]: load Tuesday, taper Thursday, come back up Friday, then taper into the show. Everything is anchored to the athlete's **refeeds**, which he runs 1–3× weekly through prep at roughly off-season carb levels. Load days are **90–120% of the highest refeed**; the taper days between sit midway between low and high days; show day is roughly **75% of a refeed**, adjusted on what he sees Friday night. He never runs a blind front load and insists on babysitting the week, but tries to have every decision made by Friday night because online coaching can't rely on hotel Wi-Fi, time zones or email delays the morning of [80:45]. He also targets *slightly* spilled Friday night. He credits Brad Loomis (transcribed "Brad Lumis") for shifting his front loads toward a more aggressive skip-loading style learned from Skip Hill (name uncertain — transcribed "skip Lor") [79:42].

**Theobald** [72:27]: no single rule. In one day he wrote five different peak weeks — a **fat load** for a female athlete who thrives on keto and bloated unpredictably on any carbs, a moderate ~300 g Thursday + 300 g Friday, and one where the athlete simply got a Friday-night cheat meal because it had worked before. He also argues for **dietary fat during loading**: he doesn't push carbs as high, and fat helps them hold the look rather than burning through it [89:28]. Nippard corroborates from his own experience that fat (peanut butter, chocolate) can improve fullness and vascularity within an hour of stage [90:59].

**Why load formulas fail** (Helms) [34:21]: muscle glycogen isn't a fixed gas tank — storage capacity expands and contracts with habitual carb intake. Someone dieted on very low carbs will spill on a "body-weight-appropriate" load; someone on high carbs may chew straight through it. TDEE, substrate use and what actually gets stored as glycogen are all unknowable per person. Theobald says he used Dan Duchaine's (transcribed "Dan Dukan," name uncertain) formulas himself and found the same.

## The "dry backload" — unanimous rejection
Nippard raises cutting water while loading carbs [30:44]. Helms: if roughly 3 of 4 grams of the stored mass is water, restricting water removes three-quarters of the fullness, assuming glycogen can even be stored. Wilson: the body will not obligingly pull water only from subcutaneous space — it defends the intracellular/extracellular ratio and where the water comes from is a gamble [36:25]. Theobald's own trials: on Wednesday loads with a full gallon and ~1,200 g carbs he looked good; when he cut to 3/4 gallon Thursday and 1/2 gallon Friday he progressively flattened even on 600 g of carbs [33:20].

## Cardio, training, show day
**Cardio** — all three taper. Wilson: slight increase during the deplete, none during the load, nothing that leaves legs sore [98:13]. Helms: unchanged unless the athlete needed a lot of cardio to get in shape. Theobald: pull HIIT ~7 days out, keep walking to Wednesday, then substitute posing.

**Resistance training** — Helms [100:16]: remove anything with a large eccentric component (RDLs, heavy dumbbell flyes), shift rep ranges up (nothing below 8–10), pull RPE down so they're further from failure, keep set volume similar, finish two days out, and reorder to lower/upper/lower/upper so legs get the extra recovery day. Theobald: no failure work from Monday, legs finished 6–7 days out, last session Wednesday, then posing — but he *does* want bodybuilders flexing and pumping the legs while carbs come in, against coaches who rest legs completely [102:19]. Wilson [103:21]: every client gets the **same** peak-week training template (legs Mon, back Tue, chest Wed, shoulders/arms Thu, full-body circuit Fri) specifically to eliminate training as a variable when diagnosing a problem mid-week.

Helms is skeptical of high-rep 20–30 set "sarcoplasmic" depletion work — it's a large volume increase, and the timeline for any glycogen-capacity adaptation is probably weeks, not days [105:23].

**Morning flatness**: Nippard reports always looking flat Saturday morning despite looking great Friday night. Helms confirms it's typical, and notes athletes peak more reliably in single-evening "live judging" formats than in morning-prejudging shows [87:27]. Practical fix from both: first meal 3–4 hours before you expect to be on stage, which often means eating at 3–4 a.m.

**Between prejudging and finals**: Helms and Theobald mostly repeat the morning's macros (Theobald adds a clean meal out with family plus 10–12 oz water). Wilson manipulates aggressively — if it's a single pro class with judging finished, he'll push carbs to find the athlete's actual spill point for next time. If there are more classes, he has families photograph the top competitors in other classes and adjusts to beat that specific look: fuller if the rival is fuller, flatter and sharper if the rival is leaner [96:10].

**Tanning**: Theobald — spray (Liquid Sun Rayz), no Dream Tan in the NPC, sheen from oil but never over-oiled. Wilson — Dream Tan for bodybuilders where allowed, sometimes over a thin spray; it highlights whatever you have, including flaws, so you must be genuinely lean; looks better in person than in photos [108:29]. Helms — Dream Tan is oil-based, never dries, survives sweat, but wrecks venues and complex suits, which is why organizations ban it [110:01]. Nippard uses Jan Tana High Def + Ultra, noting it's water-based and runs. Wilson's oil rule: none under bright lights, a little under dim lights, then re-pat; never oil over Dream Tan (oil on oil runs); a drop of baby oil revives a dry tub of Dream Tan [114:40].

**Pump-up**: Wilson goes aggressive — everything except legs and abs — arguing that over-pumping is only a risk for someone who didn't eat 1,000+ g of carbs the day before [115:41]. Theobald is conservative: bands and push-ups from 15–20 min out, after the water and sodium, with a couple of Reese's, only going hard if a spill is verifiable [117:43]. Helms is moderate but noticed a large benefit going aggressive after his own spill. Nippard uses pump-up selectively to balance proportions — pumping his lagging biceps and back rather than his already-strong chest.

## Points of disagreement
- **Carb loading strategy.** Wilson's one-day 850–1,600 g backload vs Helms' Tuesday front load anchored at 90–120% of a refeed vs Theobald's ad hoc approach including fat loads. Helms says openly [70:21] that he ran a more moderate approach on a client who wanted the backload, and his skepticism is that Wilson works with a genetically elite population who "would look pretty damn good on any approach." Wilson answers that he developed the method in 2010–11 on amateurs before elite athletes came to him for it [70:51].
- **Sodium magnitude.** Wilson's 4,800–10,000 mg/day multi-day protocol is far outside what Helms and Theobald run, though Helms argues the pre-stage totals converge.
- **Potassium.** Wilson actively depletes then loads it (~12,000 mg Friday). Helms deliberately leaves it alone. Theobald warns that loading potassium raises aldosterone [17:32].
- **Legs during peak week.** Theobald wants them flexed and pumped while loading; other coaches he references rest them completely; Helms simply moves them earlier for recovery.
- **What to do with an athlete who isn't ready.** All three coaches say pick another show and refuse to crash-diet — Helms: they'll get leaner but "still not lean enough and flat as [expletive]"; Wilson pre-negotiates this before prep even begins. **Nippard dissents from his own panel**: before turning pro at the 2014 Mayhem he ran a protein-sparing modified fast from Lyle McDonald's rapid fat loss book and dropped ~2–3 lb in the final weeks against a more conditioned field, and believes it was the right call [128:54]. Helms concedes it but caveats that Nippard holds muscle unusually well and it would ruin an average-genetics athlete.
- **When to start feeding up.** Helms and Theobald both say ~95% ready. Wilson says 99% for a client coasting along, but agrees with 95% for a high-stress athlete — because the psychological relief of more food raises NEAT and training quality and often drives the final losses [124:52].
- **Enhanced vs natural.** Helms flags explicitly that all his commentary applies to natural competitors only, and Nippard defers to Theobald on enhanced athletes.

## Practical application
- If you're natural and unsure: hold water constant, hold sodium constant through the week, add 1–3 g sodium 15 minutes before pump-up, and load carbs at roughly your biggest refeed. That is Helms' whole protocol and it is the low-variance option.
- Practise any aggressive protocol off-season while contest-lean before using it at a show. Wilson insists on this.
- Target slightly-blurred Friday night, not razor-sharp. Both Wilson and Helms independently look for the same thing.
- Do not cut water to "dry out" while loading. Three coaches, three separate lines of reasoning, same conclusion.
- Minimise variables you aren't deliberately manipulating — Wilson's fixed training template exists purely so that when something goes wrong mid-week he knows it wasn't the training.
- Psychologically: Theobald uses phone calls and cortisol-directed supplements (phosphatidylserine); Wilson issues a day-by-day written forecast of what the athlete will feel physically and mentally so they can recognise the process as normal; Helms uses video/Skype and tells clients to stop looking in the mirror because their objectivity is broken. Helms adds a coach-side warning: stressed clients show variable weight loss, which has tricked him into cutting calories too early and made the peak harder [136:12].

## Caveats & unknowns
- There is essentially no research on peak week. Every protocol here is experiential, and Helms says so directly [21:35]. Wilson repeatedly labels his own mechanisms as hypotheses — "I think I know what's going on… sometimes I'm just taking an educated guess" [19:04].
- The 1:3 or 1:4 glycogen-to-water ratio underpinning much of the reasoning is itself flagged by Helms as "a huge question mark."
- Wilson's race-based sodium and carb-load observations are uncontrolled anecdote from one coach's practice, offered as such.
- Several names are garbled in the auto-captions and are flagged as uncertain in-text: the diuretic Theobald recommends ("DIY"/"diai," likely Dyazide), "Dan Dukan" (likely Dan Duchaine), "skip Lor" (likely Skip Hill), "Brad Lumis" (likely Brad Loomis), "Dr Joe" (likely Joe Klemczewski), "Zack reps" (client name), "wbf" (likely WNBF).
- Theobald's enhanced-athlete protocols involve prescription drugs and are reported, not endorsed here; he himself flags loop diuretics as potentially lethal.
- Recorded 2016 — the field's consensus on some of this (particularly water and sodium manipulation) has continued to move since.
