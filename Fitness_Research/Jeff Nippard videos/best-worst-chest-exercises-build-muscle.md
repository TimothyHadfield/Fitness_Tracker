# Chest Exercises Ranked: Top 10 Best and 3 Worst

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Best & Worst Chest Exercises To Build Muscle (Ranked!)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NsEbXsTwas8)) · 2022-07-03 · 14 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- **Bench press is #1 (5 stars)** — high tension across the full pec, easy micro-loading, universally available, and uniquely motivating to progress on.
- **Machine chest press and cable press-around tie at 4.5 stars**; dips, incline dumbbell press and banded deficit push-ups all get 4.
- The barbell's shorter range of motion at the bottom is a minor issue, solved by including any one stretch-emphasis movement alongside it.
- Confirmed 2020 finding: **incline benching produces superior upper-chest growth versus flat and decline.**
- Avoid: **standing plate press (1 star), alternating one-arm dumbbell press (1.5), dumbbell pullover (1.5)** — the pullover isn't a chest exercise at all.

## Ranking criteria
Each exercise is scored out of five stars on four things: **personal enjoyment**, **tension-generating potential** (does it actually load the target muscle), **progressive overload potential** (can you reliably add weight, reps or another variable), and **convenience / ease of access** (do most gyms have it, and can you use it without elaborate setup). All ten are exercises he considers good — the rankings are individual, and an exercise's absence from the list doesn't condemn it.

## The top 10

**10. AMRAP push-up — 3 stars.** Bodyweight push-ups to failure. Maximum accessibility; Nippard uses them as an end-of-workout finisher for a metabolic pec burn. The downside: he needs **50+ reps** to reach failure and doesn't feel his chest at all until rep 20-30, which is effectively wasted energy for hypertrophy. **You must go all the way to failure** or the low load leaves the pecs understimulated. Loading them (see #5) fixes the rep count; working up from knee push-ups is a perfectly fine progression.

**9. Dumbbell stretch iso-hold — 3 stars.** Hold dumbbells in the bottom position for ~60 seconds. Use roughly **50% of your 5-rep max** — 100 lb dumbbells for 5 reps means ~50 lb for the hold. A rare exercise where you can directly overload time under tension: **start at 50-60 seconds and add ~5 seconds per week.** Because it is isometric with no dynamic concentric or eccentric, it may be somewhat less effective than full-ROM work — but since nearly everything else is dynamic, it is a useful novel stimulus. There is some (inconsistent) research suggesting a benefit from weighted stretching, especially at the end of a workout so later sets aren't compromised, and **very convincing evidence that training at long muscle lengths is more hypertrophic than at short lengths.** Add these to a program, don't substitute them for dynamic work.

**8. Pec fly machine — 3.5 stars.** The standard pec deck is a flat fly; Nippard prefers a slightly inclined fly machine for more upper-pec emphasis but it's less common. Machines give a **more even tension profile than dumbbell flys**, which have high tension at the bottom and zero at the top. It ranks lower because it's harder to progressively overload — past a certain load it becomes hard to feel the pecs and keep form, so he shifts to adding reps at the same weight or focusing on the mind-muscle connection. Used mainly for extra chest volume without taxing triceps or shoulders. The weight stack makes drop sets easy — **strip ~50% on the last set and get another 10-12 reps.**

**7. Cable crossover ladder — 3.5 stars.** Three sets of crossovers at three cable heights: **set 1 cables low, flying out and up (upper pecs); set 2 cables at chest height, flying straight out (mid pecs); set 3 cables high, flying out and down (lower pecs).** Hits upper, mid and lower chest from one piece of equipment, with the even tension profile cables provide. Not well suited to progressive overload, but an effective pump movement almost everyone has access to, and the per-set variation keeps an otherwise vanilla exercise engaging.

**6. Dip — 4 stars.** From a pure tension standpoint one of the best chest exercises available: a great stretch at the bottom, a strong squeeze at the top, even tension throughout. It drops to sixth only on enjoyment — loading with a belt is fiddly, too little load means grinding out many reps to reach failure, and heavy loading can aggravate the shoulders. A staple in most of his programs and it overloads well.

**5. Deficit push-up plus bands — 4 stars.** Stack a couple of plates under each hand for an extra stretch at the bottom, and loop a band around your back sitting just under the shoulder blades for extra tension at the top. Controlled tempo, usually **12-15 reps** (fewer with a heavier band). Bodyweight deficit push-ups work if that's already hard enough; a partner-loaded plate on the back substitutes for a band. Underrated — **a big stimulus without much fatigue**, at the cost of a slightly annoying setup.

**4. Incline dumbbell press — 4 stars.** [07:08] As of **2020, incline benching has been shown to produce superior upper-chest hypertrophy versus flat and decline** — previously this rested only on biomechanical inference and EMG, and is now confirmed with longitudinal data. Dumbbells add a little extra bottom-end range of motion, possibly a small hypertrophy advantage. It ranks fourth only because hoisting heavy dumbbells into position is annoying and burns real energy before the set starts, especially across multiple heavy sets. **The flat dumbbell press earns the same 4 stars**, just without the upper-chest emphasis.

**3. Cable press-around — 4.5 stars.** Popularised by the **N1 Training crew** and new to Nippard at the time. Essentially a single-arm low cable fly, with two advantages. First, **far more range of motion in the fully contracted position** — normal crossovers stop just before the hands meet, well short of the pec's full contractile capacity. You could cross your hands, but that feels awkward and forces you to alternate which hand goes over, so doing it unilaterally is simply better. Second, it's a rare chance to work each pec individually while staying stable by bracing on the cable machine with the other hand. Usually **10-12 reps**. Great for accumulating pec volume with almost no triceps or delt fatigue.

**2. Machine chest press — 4.5 stars.** Provided it has a smooth resistance profile and full range of motion, a good chest press machine gives Nippard a **better mind-muscle connection with the pecs than almost anything else**, because no attention goes to stabilising. The trade-off is that stabiliser muscles stay less active — not a concern as long as you combine machines with free weights and this isn't your only chest exercise. Machines also lock you into a fixed path, so a machine that doesn't fit your skeleton may not suit you. He rates most Hammer Strength chest presses, and a plate-loaded machine at his own gym especially — nothing else lets him feel his chest that well while progressively overloading consistently and efficiently.

**1. Bench press — 5 stars.** Scores near-maximum on all four criteria: **high tension across the whole pec through the full range of motion; extremely easy to overload, including micro-loading in 5 lb increments or less; and available in essentially every gym.** His favourite exercise for any body part.

*Answering the critics.* The common objection is that the barbell limits range of motion at the bottom. True, but the bench is almost never someone's only chest exercise — include a press-around, deficit push-up or fly variation and the pecs get their end-range stretch benefits there plus the bench's overloading benefits. Nippard also thinks the ROM deficit is exaggerated, particularly measured at the shoulder joint itself. As for the weaker pump: fair on enjoyment, but **the pump correlates with hypertrophy far less than people think, and mechanical tension matters far more.**

He favours flat bench himself (powerlifting background) but rotates in incline bench when emphasising upper chest. The underrated factor: **the bench press is motivating** — people chase bench PRs harder than PRs on anything else, which makes progressive overload more likely to actually happen.

## The three worst

**Standing plate press — 1 star.** It gets gravity wrong. Resistance points straight down but you press horizontally, making it far more of a front-delt exercise than a chest exercise. Doing it lying down is slightly better but still doesn't load the pecs well — **just use dumbbells.**

**Alternating one-arm dumbbell press — 1.5 stars.** It works the chest, but the extra balance demand to stay on the bench limits how heavily you can load the pecs, and each side gets a mini rest between reps. Not detrimental if each side is taken to or near failure, but very unlikely to be optimal.

**Dumbbell pullover — 1.5 stars.** Not actually a chest exercise, despite being used as one. It primarily targets the **lats, triceps and teres muscles** of the back; the only pec involvement is the squeeze at the top.

## Practical application
Build a chest session around a heavy overloading press (bench, or a good machine chest press) plus at least one stretch- or end-range-emphasis movement (press-around, deficit push-up, fly variation). Add incline work specifically for upper chest. Use the pec deck or crossover ladder for extra low-fatigue volume, and iso-holds or AMRAP push-ups as finishers. Drop sets suit the fly machine; time-under-tension progression suits the iso-hold.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The rankings are Nippard's own and weighted partly by personal enjoyment — his #8 could legitimately be your #1.
- Research on weighted stretching is described as inconsistent, though long-muscle-length training itself has convincing support.
- Machine quality and fit vary enormously; a machine that doesn't suit your body may not deserve its high rank for you.
