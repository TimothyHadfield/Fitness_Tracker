# Chest Exercises Ranked S to F

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Best And Worst Chest Exercises (Ranked By Science)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fGm-ef-4PVk)) · 2024-03-11 · 15 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- **Best chest exercise overall: the machine chest press** — deep stretch, high tension throughout, easy overload, safe to take to failure.
- **Worst: the plate press** — an influencer novelty with no upside.
- Only two other movements reach S tier: the **seated cable pec fly** (his favourite isolation lift) and, effectively, the machine press category.
- Ranking criteria: (1) **big stretch under high tension** — fail this and it's automatic F tier; (2) **feels good** — no pain, smooth resistance profile, good pump and mind-muscle connection; (3) **simple progression** — you can add weight or reps.
- Incline and flat bench are interchangeable enough that incline is arguably the better default: a recent study found incline produced **the same mid and lower pec growth as flat, plus more upper pec growth.**

## The full ranking

### S tier
- **Machine chest press** [04:34] — deep pec stretch, high tension across the whole range, locked-in position aids mind-muscle connection and pump, trivially easy to overload, and safer to push to failure than free weights. Crowned best of the best. Quality varies between machines; find a good one.
- **Seated cable pec fly** [10:40] — all the upsides of the cable crossover with the balance component removed, so all the tension from the cables lands on the pecs. His favourite chest isolation movement.

### A tier
- **Barbell bench press** [01:32] — high pec tension provided you bring the bar all the way to the chest, very easy to overload, and he credits most of his own pec development to it. Would have been S tier for him personally; dropped because the barbell stops on the rib cage so the stretch isn't as deep as dumbbells, some people get cranky shoulders (usually from using too much weight), and not everyone gets as much pec activation from it.
- **Incline bench press** [02:02] — same upsides as flat with more upper pec emphasis. A recent study found it matched flat bench for mid and lower pec growth while beating it for upper pec growth. Very close to S tier.
- **Flat dumbbell press** [03:34] — deeper stretch than the barbell since the dumbbells travel past the rib cage, great pump, and fewer shoulder issues because ego is less involved. Knocked from S tier because strong lifters max out the rack (usually 100 lb) and heavy dumbbells get awkward to handle.
- **Incline dumbbell press** — identical to the above with more upper pec emphasis.
- **Dips** [05:04] — possibly the best compound for a deep pec stretch, high tension, and many progression routes (reduce assistance, add reps, slow the negative, then add a weight belt). Docked from S tier because the feel isn't the smoothest and some people's shoulders get cranky. Still worth including.
- **Deficit push-ups** [06:36] — fix the push-up's stretch problem. Overload with a 2-3 second pause in the bottom. If you're going to do push-ups, do them this way.
- **Dumbbell guillotine-style press** [08:38] — the guillotine movement made safe. Flared elbows, shrugged shoulders.
- **Smith machine bench press** [09:09] — studies consistently show the Smith builds muscle across exercises; a great bench alternative with the added confidence of pushing closer to failure.
- **Incline Smith machine press** — same, with more upper pec emphasis.
- **Cable crossover** [10:10] — big stretch, smooth even tension, good pump. Less conducive to progressive overload than pressing since you can't go as heavy, and standing means the cables can pull you backward as you get stronger (fixable by bending forward and flying downward). Not the most stable fly variation.
- **Pec deck machine** [11:10] — seated, stable, big stretch, high tension. Loses to cables only because the fixed movement path may not match your ideal line of pull.
- **Dumbbell fly** [11:41] — he would have said C tier a couple of years ago because there's zero tension at the top where the pec is most contracted. Recent evidence suggesting **the stretch matters more than the contraction** revived it. He does them as lengthened partials, cutting out the top half. Loses a point for feeling less smooth than cable flyes.
- **Cable press-around** [12:13] — the only exercise covered that gets the pec fully contracted, because you press the cable across the midline. He includes it periodically to keep some short-length-biased work in the program while the stretch literature is still developing. Awkward at first, great once learned.

### B tier
- **Decline barbell bench press** [03:03] — biomechanically it should bias mid and lower pecs, and it's clearly an effective chest builder, but more people struggle with upper pec development than lower, and it has slightly less range of motion than flat or incline. His least favourite of the three angles.
- **Decline dumbbell press** — awkward setup, you'll probably need someone to hand you the dumbbells, and the lower pecs rarely need extra emphasis.
- **Banded push-ups** [06:06] — the band adds tension almost entirely to the top half, so tension still isn't maximised in the stretch. Decent as a finisher.

### C tier
- **Bodyweight push-ups** [05:35] — fine anywhere with no equipment, but with gym access there are far better options. Once you're strong you need **30+ reps** to get near failure, you bottom out on the floor before the pecs are fully stretched, and adding weight to your back feels unstable. Decent for beginners or as an end-of-workout finisher.
- **Floor press** [13:15] — excellent for bench lockout strength and smashing the triceps, and he programs it in strength and power blocks, but the limited range never fully stretches the pecs. Just okay for hypertrophy.

### D tier
- **Dumbbell pullover** [01:01] — biomechanics and activation data say the pecs do contribute alongside lats and triceps, but he feels nothing in his pecs, virtually every client says the same, and top bodybuilders don't program it on chest day. A much better lat exercise than a chest exercise.
- **Plyometric push-ups** [06:36] — poor for maximising tension in the negative and the stretch. If you want explosive work, use an explosive tempo on a loaded lift like the bench press instead.

### F tier
- **Hex press** [00:30] — arms stay tucked so the pecs never stretch, and the triceps take over so tension isn't maximised. No evidence it targets the inner chest; if anything it's worse for it, since the inner pec fibres get less stretch.
- **Plate press** [01:01] — a hex press that feels even more awkward with less overload potential. Crowned worst of the worst.
- **Barbell guillotine press** [07:07] — Jeff actually rates the movement: shrugged shoulders, flared elbows, bar to the neck, and it stretches the pecs better than any other barbell press. He notes there's no direct evidence or strong rationale for the shoulder-injury claims (Dr. Mike Israetel agrees). But it goes in F tier purely on hazard: **dropping the bar on your neck would be lights out.** Use the dumbbell version instead.
- **One-arm dumbbell press** [09:09] — no advantage, because each pec already works independently in a normal dumbbell press. Doing one side at a time just makes balance harder and disperses tension away from the pecs.
- **Cross-body standing dumbbell fly** [12:44] — gets the upper pec contracted but the dumbbell provides **zero tension in the stretched position**, and it's essentially a front raise, so the front delts steal the tension. Do dumbbell flyes lying down instead.

## Practical application
- Build the chest around: a **machine chest press**, an **incline press** (barbell, dumbbell or Smith), **dips** or a **flat dumbbell press**, and a **seated cable fly**.
- Bias incline over decline — the upper pecs are the more commonly lagging region, and incline doesn't cost you mid/lower growth.
- Bring the bar to your chest on bench; if your shoulders complain, check whether you're overloading.
- On dumbbell flyes, do them as lengthened partials and skip the top half where there's no tension.
- Keep one short-length movement (cable press-around) in the rotation as insurance against the stretch literature being incomplete.
- Push-ups: do them as deficit push-ups with a 2-3 second pause, or use them only as a finisher.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Several rankings are explicitly subjective — the pec deck vs. cable fly call and the dips "feels good" deduction are his personal preference.
- The decline bench has **no long-term research** comparing pec growth against incline and flat; that ranking is biomechanical reasoning only.
- The stretch-over-contraction premise driving the dumbbell fly and press-around calls is "recent evidence," and he hedges by keeping short-length work in the program until more data exists.
- Shoulder pain is individual: bench press, dips and flared-elbow pressing may drop to D or F tier for anyone with pre-existing shoulder issues. Start light and build tolerance.
- Machine quality varies a lot; the S tier ranking assumes you have a good chest press machine available.
