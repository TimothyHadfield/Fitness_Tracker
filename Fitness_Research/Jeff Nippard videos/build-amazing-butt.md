# Advanced Glute Training: Anatomy, Hip Thrust Variations and Frequency

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Build An Amazing Butt | Advanced Glute Training Science Explained" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ynjshc_LwPE)) · 2017-10-31 · 6 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Don't train only the glute max — the gluteus medius is roughly half its weight and a real contributor to butt shape. Hit it with hip abduction, toes pointed *in*.
- The standard barbell hip thrust with a **neutral pelvis** produced the most upper-glute activity of the three variations tested — beating the "American" (posterior tilt) and banded versions.
- Abducting the hips (~30°) or taking a wider stance lines the fibres up with the line of pull and raises glute activation.
- Because different glute exercises have different recovery costs, **3-4 glute sessions per week** is a realistic sweet spot for most people.
- Change the routine every **1-3 months**; over a year, the thing that matters most is that the weights go up.

## Anatomy and fibre type [00:00]
Gluteus maximus is the largest muscle in the body. It performs hip extension, hip abduction, external rotation and posterior pelvic tilt. Fibre split is about **50/50 fast/slow twitch**.

Gluteus medius is about **half the weight** of the glute max — not a trivial share of the butt. It is slightly more **slow-twitch dominant**, so a somewhat **higher rep range** suits it. Its main job is stabilisation, but you can target it directly through hip abduction.

## Targeting the glute medius [01:01]
A **2014 study by Lee (name uncertain — auto-captions render it "lien") and colleagues** found that **internal rotation (toes pointed in)** activated the glute medius more effectively. Since external rotation biases the glute max, the practical rule is:

- **Toes in** → glute medius
- **Toes out** → glute maximus

Unilateral exercises with a stabilisation demand also load the medius: single-leg hip thrusts, split squats, walking lunges.

## Pre-activation [01:34]
A **2016 study by Fisher and colleagues** found that targeted glute training improved glute recruitment in larger compound movements, attributed to changes in **cortical motor excitability**. Practical read: isolation-style glute activation drills carry over to heavier compounds like the hip thrust.

## Hip thrust variations [02:05]
A **2016 study by Contreras and colleagues** compared three variations:

- **Barbell hip thrust** — neutral pelvis, body positioned further down the bench
- **American hip thrust** — posterior pelvic tilt, body further up the bench
- **Banded hip thrust** — as traditional, but band resistance

Result: the **barbell hip thrust produced the greatest upper-glute activity**, with **no difference between the three for lower glute** activation. Notably, despite the glutes' role in posterior pelvic tilt, the **neutral pelvic position won**.

Individual variation is real: **11 of 13 subjects** peaked with the traditional barbell version, but a few did better on the banded or American variations. Traditional is the safest default; experiment with the others.

Since most trainees are chasing the upper glute "shelf", other data suggests pairing hip thrusts with **hip abduction work such as banded clams**, which selectively targets the upper glutes.

## Abduction and line of pull [03:05]
A **2013 study** found that **30° of hip abduction** increased glute activation during prone hip extension. The mechanism given: the glute max is a **fusiform** muscle, so activation is optimised when the fibres run parallel to the line of pull. Abducting the hips or widening the stance achieves that alignment.

## Frequency and recovery [03:36]
Literature generally favours training a muscle **2x/week over 1x/week**. But per **Brett Contreras**, glute exercises differ in how long they take to recover from:

- **Stretchers** (Romanian deadlifts, squats, lunges) — **3-4 days** recovery
- **Activators** (hip thrusts, cable pull-throughs) — **2-3 days**
- **Pumpers** (banded sumo walks) — **1-2 days**

Build the week around those curves and **3-4 glute days per week** becomes workable.

## Periodization [04:36]
Research points to **periodic variation** as the key factor, so the routine should change — but not so often that you lose the thread on strength progression. His preference: **switch the routine every 1-3 months**. Yearly benchmark: you should be lifting more at the end of the year than at the start. When you suspect overreaching or overtraining, take a **deload of 1-2 weeks at reduced frequency, volume and load**.

## Practical application
- Default hip thrust: barbell, neutral pelvis, feet in a slightly wider/abducted stance, toes out.
- Add banded clams or other hip abduction work with **toes in** for the glute medius and upper-glute shelf.
- Run light glute activation drills before your heavy compound work.
- Spread 3-4 glute sessions across the week: space the stretchers 3-4 days apart, activators 2-3, and slot pumpers into the gaps.
- Use a slightly higher rep range for medius-focused abduction work than for heavy hip extension.
- Re-write the program every 1-3 months; keep adding weight across the year.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Nearly all of the evidence cited is **EMG activation** data, not long-term hypertrophy data — higher activation is a proxy, not proof of more growth.
- The hip thrust findings showed meaningful **individual variation** (2 of 13 subjects responded better to non-traditional variations), so test the variants on yourself.
- The researcher name in the 2014 abduction/internal-rotation study is garbled in the captions and is flagged as uncertain; the 2013 prone hip extension study was not attributed by name.
- The recovery-time categories come from **Brett Contreras' practical framework**, not from a cited trial.
