# Post-Contest Recovery: Why Slow Reverse Dieting Doesn't Add Up

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Reverse Diet Debate Follow-Up: My Take On It" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1zm2YtZnAy8)) · 2016-03-11 · 14 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Creeping back to maintenance in small increments after a cut makes no sense: at your new maintenance you cannot, by definition, gain fat.
- People who "lose weight while reverse dieting" are simply still in a deficit — they haven't reached maintenance yet.
- Jumping straight to maintenance is the *minimum* he'd advise; he'd rather see competitors go straight into a surplus post-show.
- Manage it with a weight cap (roughly 10% over stage weight in the first four months) rather than a slow calorie ramp — how fast you reach the cap matters less than not exceeding it.
- Fast and slow regain land in the same place 3-6 months later; the trade-off is a short ugly period versus a long uncomfortable one, so athlete preference should decide.

## Context
This is his summary after a public debate on reverse dieting. His read of the debate: the coaches involved converged more than they diverged, which he treats as a good sign for the field rather than a disappointment.

## Two takeaways from the debate [01:00]
1. **Eating more in the off-season is broadly smart.** Two reasons: better gym performance (and therefore more muscle), and at least a chance that you'll be able to diet on higher calories next time you cut, particularly early in that cut.
2. **The gradual climb back to maintenance is the part that fails.** If you're apprehensive about fat gain, going straight to your *new* maintenance still doesn't produce fat gain — that's what maintenance means. So the slow ramp buys nothing.

## Why maintenance isn't a good landing spot either [03:02]
He pressed the other side repeatedly on why an athlete should sit in a "buffer zone" at maintenance for a couple of weeks before entering a surplus, and wasn't satisfied by the answers. The best case he could construct for himself is that it's a litmus test: post-prep it's hard to know how deep a deficit someone is actually in, so parking at estimated maintenance avoids overshooting into a surplus too fast. He thinks that's needlessly conservative.

He notes a 3D Muscle Journey video released the same day as the debate — he recalls it as "the recovery diet" — recommending going straight into a **400-1,000 kcal surplus**. He endorses that: if the goal is to regain fat, start sooner rather than later.

**The key intuition [04:37]:** new maintenance feels almost identical to dieting calories. Finish prep at 1,600 kcal and bump to a 2,000 kcal maintenance and, if you've been well below your settling point for a while, you're still food-obsessed, still tempted to binge, and probably still will. Straight to 2,000 beats a slow reverse; 2,400 beats 2,000; going straight back to an old maintenance of 3,000 would be better still.

## The weight-cap approach [05:37]
Instead of prescribing calories, set a ceiling: don't exceed roughly **10% over stage weight within the first four months** post-show. Provided the athlete is genuinely comfortable gaining fat, the rate at which they reach that cap shouldn't matter much.

**The counterargument he acknowledges:** the only way to add 15 lb in one or two weeks is binging, and someone binging is out of control and likely to blow past the cap — a real risk with inexperienced competitors given only a number and no other structure.

**His reply:** tracked macros carry their own failure mode, which he sees constantly. Athletes cheat and binge on the side anyway, while running an internal dialogue of "I've already eaten this many macros, I can't have that." Post-show is exactly when family and friends expect the constraints to lift, and rigid tracking removes the flexibility to just eat the meal. Better, for a while, to lift the tracking constraints and replace them with softer structure.

## The structure he substitutes [07:41]
- Eat **discrete, solid, square meals**. They can be big, but when the meal is done and you feel full, stop — no all-day grazing.
- Eat for physiological rather than emotional reasons, and take that seriously.
- Keep a **protein requirement: 25-40 g per meal**.
- Alternative formulation: track **calories and protein only, plus a fibre target**, in a decent surplus from the outset, with the weight cap on top.
- Once the weight cap is reached, resume tracking more closely.

**Anti-overshoot habits he suggests:** write your goals down and review them each morning; take morning walks (cut cardio, but keep the walk — not as cardio, as a way to get outside and plan the day); and a motivational calendar — he and Robin pinned up photos of people who inspire them and ticked off each day they stuck to discrete meals and stopped when full.

**On binging:** be forgiving of yourself. He thinks it's treated with disproportionate moral weight, and says he doesn't know a single bodybuilder who hasn't binged after a prep. As body weight returns to a comfortable level the drive normalises. If it genuinely becomes uncontrolled — or if fear of adding food is what's blocking a jump to maintenance in the first place — that's a matter for a specialist, not something a coach should accommodate by dieting the athlete more slowly.

## The "match the rate of fat and muscle gain" argument [10:46]
The second objection to fast regain is that fat and muscle gain should proceed at similar rates, so you never look disproportionately fat.

He grants the mechanism. Two weeks into a fast regain, fat is well ahead of muscle, and at (say) 165 lb you'll look *less lean* than you did at that same weight before prep — because prep cost you muscle as well as fat, and the muscle comes back slower than the fat does.

But the cost is time spent feeling terrible. Fast regain gives you a short window of looking fluffy, after which body weight is restored, hunger has normalised and hormones have re-equilibrated to some degree. Slow regain stretches that miserable window out over months. Crucially, **3-6 months later both approaches end up in the same place.**

## Practical application
- Post-cut, go straight to your new maintenance at minimum; for a post-show competitor, straight into a surplus (400-1,000 kcal) is his preference.
- Set a weight cap around 10% over stage weight for the first four months and let rate of gain be governed by athlete comfort, not by a calorie schedule.
- If tracking is producing restrictive internal bargaining, swap it for discrete meals, 25-40 g protein per meal, and the cap; resume tracking at the cap.
- Reduce cardio but keep a daily morning walk as a routine anchor rather than an energy-expenditure tool.
- Coaches should normalise fat gain as a necessary part of progressing show to show, not feed the fear of it.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This is opinion and coaching experience throughout — no studies are cited beyond a reference to a same-day 3D Muscle Journey video. Treat the 3DMJ title ("the recovery diet") as approximate; the captions are unclear.
- Framed almost entirely around post-contest bodybuilders. Someone finishing an ordinary fat-loss phase has far less accumulated deficit and less reason for an aggressive surplus.
- The 10%-in-four-months cap is offered as a general heuristic, not a validated number.
- He explicitly flags that inexperienced competitors may not hold the cap without more structure, and that disordered fear of food or uncontrolled binging is outside a coach's scope.
