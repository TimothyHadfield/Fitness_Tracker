# Five Popular Supplements That Don't Work for Muscle Growth

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Worst Supplements Everyone Takes For Muscle Growth (Science Explained)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=unowqBYbWeg)) · 2022-08-03 · 11 min
**Topic:** Supplements

## Bottom line
- Five popular supplements with weak or absent evidence: **test boosters, turkesterone, BCAAs, fat burners, and beta-alanine** (the last only in the context of typical hypertrophy training).
- Test boosters raise testosterone trivially compared with steroids — ashwagandha's ~96 ng/dL vs a 1,740% rise from injected testosterone.
- Turkesterone has **zero human evidence** and severe quality-control problems: independent lab tests found products containing as little as **0.15% of the label claim**.
- BCAAs are useless without the other essential amino acids. A January 2022 systematic review of 12 studies found **no benefit to performance, strength or muscle mass**.
- Beta-alanine works — but only for efforts lasting **1-4 minutes**, not for typical 6-15 rep sets.
- General rule, per **Dr Eric Helms**: even the best supplements (creatine, caffeine) make a very small difference. Be a **late adopter** — wait a year or two for more studies.

## 1. Testosterone boosters
[00:00] A **2020 study** took the five best-selling test boosters on Amazon and analysed their ingredients for effectiveness.

- **Three of the five didn't disclose ingredient amounts** at all, hiding behind a proprietary blend — an immediate red flag, since a dusting of an ingredient still satisfies the label.
- Of the ingredients present (horny goat weed, boron, ashwagandha, fenugreek and similar), **half had zero human studies showing any positive effect.**
- Three ingredients had one or two positive studies but more research showing no effect: **saw palmetto and boron each had 2 positive studies against 6 showing nothing.**
- **Fenugreek** had 4 positive studies and 3 indeterminate. The Examine database states that although there is limited evidence for an increase in testosterone, more evidence than not denies such an increase.
- **Ashwagandha** looked most promising. Nippard is aware of more positive studies than the paper cited and personally takes **600 mg per day** — but mainly for cortisol, stress and general health, treating any testosterone effect as a bonus.

**Scale matters.** A study of subjects injecting **600 mg of testosterone enanthate** saw levels go from **630 to 2,370 ng/dL — a 1,740% increase.** Ashwagandha, the most promising natural option, raised testosterone by **96 ng/dL.** That could still be worth considering for someone with genuinely low testosterone who wants to avoid steroid side effects, but it is not in the same universe.

**Better ways to support testosterone:** 7-8 hours of sleep nightly, maintaining a healthy body weight, enough total dietary fat, and correcting micronutrient deficiencies — particularly **zinc, magnesium and vitamin D**. As the MASS research review put it, if your testosterone is normal or high, no supplement currently marketed as a test booster is likely to do anything meaningful.

**Why the 4.5-star reviews?** The five products averaged 4.5/5 on Amazon. Positive reviews don't indicate efficacy — placebo plus behaviour change. People who buy a supplement usually also start training harder and eating better, then credit the pills.

## 2. Turkesterone
[03:38] There was **zero human evidence** when Nippard covered it six months earlier, and there was still zero at the time of this video.

The most promising related research on ecdysteroids was a **2019 study by Eisenman and colleagues**, which did find significantly greater muscle gains than placebo from a peak-ecdysone supplement — but when the pills were lab tested they contained **6 mg instead of the labelled 100 mg (6% of claim)**, implying serious quality-control problems.

It got worse. **Nootropics Depot** ran independent lab tests on the top-selling turkesterone products:
- **Greg Doucette's Turk Builder** claimed 5,100 mg per capsule but contained **0.7 mg — 0.15% of the label claim.**
- **Gorilla Mind's** product had **under 1%** of its label claim.
- Some products had **no detectable turkesterone at all.**

Nootropics Depot also sells supplements, so has a possible commercial interest; Nippard, who holds a biochemistry degree, finds their chemistry convincing while acknowledging his own anti-turkesterone bias. He also notes genuine analytical difficulty in distinguishing chemically similar ecdysteroids, so some debate over detection is legitimate.

The combination of a weak science base *and* unreliable manufacturing is the red flag.

## 3. BCAAs
[06:40] Of the 20 amino acids that make a complete protein, **9 are essential** (the body cannot make them). Three of those nine — **leucine, isoleucine and valine** — are branched-chain.

**Leucine triggers muscle protein synthesis** — Nippard's analogy: the key that starts the car. Isoleucine and valine don't trigger MPS but are included because taking leucine alone lowers blood concentrations of the other two, which are needed to build tissue.

But you need **all nine EAAs** to build muscle. Taking BCAAs without the rest is like turning the key with no fuel in the tank. So BCAAs are required for muscle growth yet essentially useless as an isolated supplement.

**The evidence:** a **systematic review from January 2022 analysing 12 studies found no benefit of BCAA supplementation on performance, strength or muscle mass.**

**Narrow exceptions** where EAAs (not BCAAs) might be considered: a vegetarian meal low in protein quality — though a vegan protein shake does the same job; or "anabolic insurance" intra-workout when very lean at the end of a diet, especially training fasted.

Otherwise: just eat enough total protein.

## 4. Fat burners
[08:41] They claim two mechanisms: suppressing appetite (fewer calories in) or a thermogenic effect (more calories out). Both are plausible on paper; in practice little happens.

The most recent meta-analysis found **no indication that fat burners or thermogenic supplements were any more effective than exercise, or than diet and exercise combined.** Caffeine and green tea extract do raise energy expenditure slightly, but so slightly compared with diet and exercise that the practical relevance is questionable.

Caffeine is still worth taking pre-training — **not as a fat burner but because it reliably improves training performance.** The objection is to caffeine repackaged with unproven herbs and sold at a premium as a fat burner.

## 5. Beta-alanine
[10:12] Beta-alanine almost certainly works — just not for typical resistance training. It buffers muscle acidity so the muscle can keep working longer. But **acidity is probably not the limiting factor for sets lasting under ~30 seconds**; lactic acid needs time to accumulate, and a normal 8-12 rep set is over too quickly.

A **2012 meta-analysis** found beta-alanine was only effective for **exercise lasting 1-4 minutes**, with no effect below 60 seconds. Practically, **sets under about 20-30 reps are unlikely to benefit.**

It is genuinely useful for endurance training, very high-rep work, and circuit-style sessions mixing resistance and cardio. But most muscle-building training sits in the **6-15 rep range**, where beta-alanine does nothing.

## Practical application
- Skip test boosters; fix sleep, body weight, dietary fat and zinc/magnesium/vitamin D instead. If you want ashwagandha, 600 mg/day for stress and cortisol is reasonable.
- Skip turkesterone entirely until human data exists.
- Skip BCAAs; hit your total daily protein.
- Skip fat burners; take plain caffeine pre-workout for performance.
- Skip beta-alanine unless you do endurance, very high rep, or circuit work.
- Adopt new supplements late. You lose nothing by waiting a year or two.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Simply tallying positive versus negative studies ignores study quality; Nippard offers it only as a general lay of the land.
- Fenugreek may be worth watching for future research, and ashwagandha may yet prove to raise testosterone modestly.
- The turkesterone lab tests come from a company that sells competing supplements, and detection chemistry for ecdysteroids is genuinely contested.
