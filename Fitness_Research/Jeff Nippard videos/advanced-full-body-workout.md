# Advanced Full Body Workout with Menno Henselmans: BFR, Calf Jumps, Bayesian Curls, Butterfly Laterals

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Advanced Full Body Workout (BFR, Butterfly Laterals, Calf Jumps, Bayesian Curls) w/ Menno Henselmans" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eUYY19p4IRY)) · 2019-07-16 · 24 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Full body, **3 sets per muscle group**, run as a circuit with antagonist supersets wherever the gym allowed — the circuit buys very long rest per exercise (sometimes ~10 minutes) without wasting time.
- Menno's own research on rest periods found longer rests are better for hypertrophy; circuiting is how he gets them while keeping session length reasonable.
- The exercise selection logic is consistent throughout: pick variations that keep high tension in the **stretched** position and across the **full** range of motion, and that allow **eccentric overload**.
- Novel exercises covered: BFR/KAATSU leg extensions, calf jumps, Bayesian (cable) curls, butterfly lateral raises, lat prayers, and stripped static grip holds.

## Session structure [04:53]
Romanian deadlift → leg extension (antagonist superset, with BFR) → calf jumps → machine chest press → lat prayers → butterfly lateral raises → Bayesian curls → grip holds. Three sets per muscle group, circuited.

## Romanian deadlift [05:23]
Menno worked up heavy — around five plates — in sets of roughly 8-12, his own reps landing at 8, 6, 4.

His RDL is idiosyncratic: an "American lockout" RDL. He holds noticeable lumbar arch (he says his lumbar spine is naturally lordotic), pushes the hips through at lockout and allows the knees to bend at the top. It looks like spinal hyperextension but he describes it as flexing and posteriorly tilting the pelvis while letting the hips come forward — effectively a yielding hip thrust that adds glute stimulus at the top.

Nippard's contrasting approach: keep RDLs light, rarely over 315 lb, chasing hamstring stretch and mind-muscle connection because he feels his lower back take over when heavier.

**Menno's response — why heavy feels like "back":**
- Isometric muscle actions generate more afferent feedback and more neuromuscular fatigue than dynamic ones, so the isometrically-loaded erectors dominate the sensation.
- Your attention is on holding the arch under heavy load (back injuries being catastrophic), so it can't be on the hamstrings.
- Objectively, mechanical tension on the fibres is probably *higher* with the heavier load because you produce far more force.
- The reason a stiff-legged/locked-knee version feels like more hamstring is **passive insufficiency**: the hamstrings cross two joints, so with fully locked knees you reach an extreme stretch — but at that length actin-myosin cross-bridges are too far apart to form effectively, which is why you're so much weaker there. That sensation is stretch, not tension.
- His verdict: the light stretch-focused style gives good hamstring stimulation, but the heavier hip-drive style gives better glute stimulation while still hitting hamstrings.

## BFR leg extensions (KAATSU) [09:26]
Done immediately after the RDL as an antagonist superset. BFR lets you get an effective stimulus with very light weight, so it's easy on the knees.

Nippard's report of the experience: he'd assumed BFR was just high reps in disguise. The end of the set feels genuinely different — with ordinary high reps near failure you can pause a second and keep going, whereas with the cuffs on you hit a wall and the muscle simply stops working.

Menno adds a rationale for mixing loads: he cites recent research (described in the captions as by "the Russians," on very advanced subjects — the group is not named clearly enough to attribute) suggesting high-rep and low-rep work trigger **different anabolic signalling pathways**, even if both are ultimately mediated by mechanical tension. That's a possible mechanism for why combining high and low reps in the same workout, or via daily undulating periodisation, appears in some research to increase muscle growth and clearly helps strength.

## Rest periods and circuiting [10:28]
Menno wrote the paper on rest intervals that found longer rests are better for hypertrophy. Circuiting through the gym is his solution: it's time-efficient and yields very long effective rest intervals — sometimes around 10 minutes per exercise — which lets you accumulate more volume at high quality.

## Calf jumps [11:28]
Motivation: every muscle group has a heavy compound "hard-hitter" except calves, where every option is just some variety of calf raise. Calf jumps are his overload solution.

**Execution:** concentric is a cheated calf raise — allow knee bend so the quads help drive the weight up. Then on the eccentric keep the knees essentially locked out and lower slowly. The result is eccentric overload in the stretched position: you get a large calf stretch under a load heavier than you could raise strictly.

Nippard's coaching for beginners: map the movement first — set up, do a normal calf raise, lower slowly for the full stretch, then bend the knees and explode up.

## Machine chest press, high reps [13:00]
Menno was using very high reps because his elbows weren't 100%, so all horizontal pressing was high-rep. He prefers a **convergent** machine (he references Hammer Strength's Iso-Lateral line) because:
- Convergent path adds range of motion at the contracted end, which a barbell can't give you and dumbbells only partly do.
- With a barbell or dumbbells, once your arms reach vertical there's no more horizontal adduction force required — so the pec loses tension in the fully contracted position. A convergent machine keeps resistance pulling in a direction that keeps pec tension right through to full contraction.
- Net effect: greater range of motion *and* stimulus throughout it, whereas on a bench press only part of the range is really effective.

High-rep sets were taken to or very close to failure — Menno notes high-rep work must get close to failure to be effective, because it's roughly the last five reps that maximise motor unit recruitment and engage type II fibres and high-threshold motor units with the greatest growth potential.

## Bayesian curls [15:32]
A cable curl performed leaning away from the stack. The problem with a dumbbell curl is that resistance is only meaningful in the middle of the range: at the bottom (stretch) gravity offers little tension to the biceps, and at the top — especially if you bring the elbow forward Arnold-style so the forearm is vertical — tension disappears again. The Bayesian curl gives high tension in the stretched position, and by leaning forward through the curl you also keep good tension in the contracted position. Same principle as everything else in the session: tension across the entire range stimulates fibres along the whole muscle length. Nippard notes it takes some practice to master and that Menno invented it.

## Butterfly lateral raises [17:05]
A dumbbell lateral raise that doesn't stop at shoulder height. You continue upward by externally rotating the shoulder as you go (which is what avoids impingement in the upper range).

Two rationales:
1. **Greater range of motion.**
2. **Deliberate momentum for eccentric overload.** Dumbbell laterals are hardest in the middle where the lever arm (your arm) is longest, and have no resistance at the bottom. By starting the next rep immediately from the bottom without a pause and throwing the weight up using hip extension, you get tension at the bottom, blast through the sticking point, and get tension at the top — then control the descent. Because you're 20-60% stronger eccentrically, ordinary training chronically under-stimulates the eccentric; this is one of the few ways to overload both phases. Menno notes several studies find eccentric overload produces more growth and strength than regular training, or at minimum a strong trend. The mechanism: actin-myosin cross-bridges are released mechanically as the muscle lengthens, which is more energy-efficient and is why you're stronger eccentrically.

**Trap involvement.** Menno cites a paper by **Johnson et al.** arguing that shrugs as usually performed train the neck rather than the upper traps: the upper trap fibres run close to horizontal, so the shoulder must be abducted (arm elevated) for those fibres to contribute. Butterfly laterals include that phase, so traps get involved. Some people feel traps more than delts — he considers that a mind-muscle-connection issue rather than a real problem, since the high shoulder abduction torque can't be produced by traps alone, and the goal is training the whole shoulder rather than isolating one head.

## Lat prayers [20:11]
A kneeling straight-arm pulldown through full range of motion. Menno's argument: people insist on isolation work for every muscle group but do none for the lats, and when they do a straight-arm pulldown they only perform the bottom half of the range. Combined with deadlifts and rows, which also only load the bottom ~90 degrees of lat range, most people badly under-stimulate the lats.

Supporting reasoning:
- Research indicates high tension in the **stretched position** is important for maximal growth, partly because it loads titin as well as the fibre.
- Stimulating the entire range matters because the length-tension relationship differs across fibres.
- He references research on the quads showing that doing only the bottom portion of leg extensions produced more growth than only the top portion, despite equal tension and equal work volume.
- Part of the explanation is sarcomeres added **in series**, not just in parallel — heavy strength training can literally lengthen a muscle (yoga doesn't do this; heavy training ironically does). A longer muscle then allows more sarcomeres stacked in parallel too. His analogy: to maximise size you want to expand in every direction available.

## Grip work [15:02]
Both had grip limitations. Time-efficient method: after the last set of RDLs (or pull-ups), simply hold the bar to grip failure, strip a plate, hold again, strip another plate, hold again. Stop when a hold exceeds about 30 seconds because the load is then too light. Takes almost no time, may help forearm development and general grip strength. Not a growth priority in itself, but a limiting factor worth removing.

## Practical application
- Full body, 3 sets per muscle group, circuited with antagonist pairings to buy long rests.
- RDL: heavy 8-12 with hip drive at lockout for glutes, or lighter with more stretch for hamstring feel — different emphases, not right vs wrong.
- Substitute a convergent chest press machine for barbell pressing when you want full-range pec tension.
- Add calf jumps for eccentric-overloaded calf work; Bayesian curls for stretch-position biceps tension; butterfly laterals for full-ROM, eccentric-overloaded delts; lat prayers for full-ROM lat isolation.
- Mix rep ranges (high and low) within a session or across a week.
- Grip: descending static holds after your last pulling set, stopping when a hold passes 30 seconds.

## Caveats & unknowns
Much of the reasoning here is mechanistic rather than directly tested — the cross-bridge, titin, sarcomerogenesis and pathway-signalling arguments are explanations for exercise choices, not evidence that these specific variations outgrow standard ones. The high-rep/low-rep signalling study is attributed only vaguely in the captions and is not reliably named. Menno explicitly caveats that his heavy RDL style suits his own structure and that the choice depends on individual leverages. The chest press was run at unusually high reps only because of an elbow issue.
