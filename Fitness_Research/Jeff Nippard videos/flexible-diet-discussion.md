# Flexible Dieting vs Meal Plans: Laurin Conlin's Inclusive/Exclusive Dieting Study

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Flexible Diet Discussion feat. Laurin Conlin and Robin Gallant" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b5bFfcITH3Y)) · 2016-07-29 · 87 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Conlin's master's thesis was, to her knowledge, the **first intervention study** comparing macro tracking against a dietitian-written meal plan. Over 10 weeks of dieting: **no differences on any measure** — weight loss, body composition, RMR, or psychological questionnaire.
- The exception came in the **post-diet period**: the flexible group gained **1.53 kg lean body mass**, the meal plan group lost **0.59 kg**. Significant, but she repeatedly warned against over-reading one small study.
- **26 of 27 participants said they'd choose macros** if the study were repeated — including people who succeeded on the meal plan.
- It's a **continuum, not a binary**. Some people genuinely need a meal plan's structure first, then graduate toward flexibility.
- Conlin's hard line: flexible dieting **does not eliminate cravings** at low body fat and low calories.
- Key numbers: fiber **11–15 g/1000 kcal**, macros within **±5 g**, **3–5 even protein feedings**, no sugar tracking if fiber is in check, salt only in peak week.

## The study design [03:02–08:41]

- Randomised (stratified by fat mass) into **inclusive** (macro tracking) or **exclusive** (RD-written meal plan, with options — not a five-food bro list).
- Intake set from a **3-day food log**, then a **25% caloric reduction**. Protein **2 g/kg**, remainder split **50/50 carbs and fat**. Individualised numbers, identical macro structure across groups.
- **10 weeks dieting**, then a **10-week post-diet period with no intervention** — she wanted to see what people did on their own.
- Testing at baseline and weeks 5, 10, 16, 20: **ultrasound body composition, resting metabolic rate, salivary leptin**, plus the **Three Factor Eating Questionnaire (TFEQ)** at baseline and week 10 — chosen because most eating questionnaires are eating-disorder based and macro trackers can look disordered on those instruments.
- **27 completers.** Age 18–40 (upper limit to avoid menopause as a confounder), average ~25, average body fat **20.5%**, both sexes. Required **a year of resistance training** and roughly **two hours in the gym per day**. Training was uncontrolled, but logs showed **no difference in reported minutes between groups**.
- Bias control: the macro group got carb, fat, protein **and fiber** targets plus a tracking ebook (Sohee Lee's — *uncertain; captions read "SOI Lee's"*); the meal plan group got the plan. **Neither group received education about "good" or "bad" foods** [16:55].

## Results and interpretation [05:36, 21:34–24:10]

Nothing differed in the diet phase. In the post-diet phase the LBM divergence (**+1.53 kg vs −0.59 kg**) was the only significant finding, and she couldn't explain it: reported training didn't differ, and hand-calculated post-diet food logs showed **no difference in protein or calories** between groups.

Three speculative explanations were raised. **Conlin**, via Eric Helms, cited a paper by **Dr Bartholomew, University of Texas** (*uncertain*) on **psychological stress impairing resistance training adaptation** — perhaps the rigid group was more stressed and adapted worse. **Nippard** proposed that a restricted food list produces **micronutrient deficiencies** that downregulate anabolic hormones; macros were matched, so it would have to be micronutrient-driven. **Gallant** proposed a psychological asymmetry: the meal plan group, released into flexibility, feels a "world of possibilities" within the same calories, while the macro group, already used to variety, just wants more calories.

Conlin's caution: averages hide everything; some gained, some lost. "Unless five other people replicate it, who knows if that was just a weird thing with our group. The biggest takeaway is there was no difference."

## Why the prior literature isn't the same question [09:11–11:13]

Her most important clarification: **"flexible dieting" in the research literature does not mean IIFYM.** There it means a **moderate mindset** — you have a party, so you eat a bit less before or after, and you don't binge — versus **rigid restraint**, meaning all-or-nothing thinking ("if it's not on my plan I can't have it"). It's about mindset, not meal plan versus macro app.

So the existing evidence (lower BMI, less disordered eating, better outcomes with "flexible" approaches) doesn't transfer automatically — and those studies are mostly **questionnaire-based rather than intervention-based**. She also flagged a duration effect [09:42]: by week 10 many meal plan participants were sick of the food but adhered because they were in a study — much like a competitor adhering because they have a show.

## The continuum argument [11:43–12:44]

The most useful qualitative finding. Several meal plan participants did very well with no negative aftermath; she interviewed them to find out why. The answer: **they had no structure before, and the plan supplied it.** Portions and food suggestions were the missing piece for people who couldn't read a food label. Meal plans have a real place at one end of a continuum: start with structure, learn, become more flexible as readiness allows. Only one of 27 (a meal plan participant) said he wasn't ready to try macros.

## Restaurants, off-season and prep [30:50–43:38]

**Conlin's restaurant rule:** never trust online restaurant data — "the person who made that is not plating your food." An experienced tracker estimates better by eye. She **always overestimates** (adding ~**10 g carbs**, for instance), orders single-ingredient items — fish, chicken or sirloin plus plain vegetables — and asks staff how things were cooked. She eats out about **once a week during prep**, which she calls high. Nippard's version: add a tablespoon of olive oil for anything visibly cooked in oil — "if you're short 12 grams of fat that day, that's the cost of eating out."

Off-season she is explicitly relaxed: after three straight years of dieting she barely tracked from December to April beyond eyeballing protein. Shared rationale: **save willpower for prep.** She tells off-season clients to take the night out, because denial accumulates.

## Cravings — the sharpest split from popular IIFYM claims [44:08–52:20]

The claim on trial: flexible dieting eliminates cravings because you can fit anything in. **Conlin: "I don't believe that."**

At **low body fat and low calories**, an ice cream sundae (a) won't fit the numbers, (b) if forced to fit, makes the rest of the day miserable, and (c) a substitute "isn't helping." All three rejected protein-treat substitutes — fake ice cream, protein cookies — as unsatisfying and, taken far enough, semi-disordered in themselves.

Nippard's counter-evidence: a questionnaire study by **Westenhoefer** (*captions: "Weston Hofer"*) showing flexible-mindset restraint is more successful at combating cravings, with rigid restraint feeding the binge pathway; and correspondence with **Adrian Meule** (*captions: "Adrien mule"*), whose work found **chocolate cravers told not to eat chocolate for two weeks had their cravings increase**, while non-cravers were unaffected — implying restricting foods you're sensitive to may be a fool's errand. Conlin accepted both **for non-competitors**.

Synthesis: for general-population fat loss, restriction backfires and inclusion helps; four weeks out from a show, a taste can trigger more craving and **sacrifice is unavoidable** — "you need to be strict, but you don't need to be stricter than you have to be." Conlin, following a flexible diet, said flatly: "I'm starving right now."

## Is the debate settled? [56:30–63:07]

All three said no — for the general population, not for the fitness community. Outside the bubble, "five foods to never eat" ads still work, big clean-eating teams still exist, and RDs Conlin knows were surprised macro tracking works. Her framing: **flexible dieting is a broader term for moderation**, not IIFYM specifically. Notably she was *not* anti-clean-eating: **"eating nutritious, nutrient-dense food is not a bad thing."** Both extremes are the problem — "how many Pop-Tarts can I fit" is bad; fitting Pop-Tarts into your diet is fine. She calls herself a "bro IIFYM" person: consistent, repetitive, mostly food she cooks herself, because packaged labels can be wrong.

## Untracked and free meals [63:37–70:20]

**Conlin, off-season:** don't change your weekly intake — adjust the day itself, eating mostly protein earlier so you arrive hungry. The boundary that matters: **"I'm going to not track, but I'm going to be mindful" — not "I'm not tracking so I can binge."** If an untracked meal reliably becomes a binge, that's a conversation, not a macro problem. Her worked example: a week-long cruise run this way — one soft serve daily, protein-anchored meals, unrestricted dinners and drinks, training almost daily — returning **up 0.2 lb**.

**Nippard's client protocol:** dock the day back **750–1000 calories** — a client on 2200 goes to 1200, eaten as mostly protein and vegetables/fruit — leaving ~**1000 free calories** for the meal: **half an appetiser**, a **full entrée**, and **half a large dessert or one personal dessert**. Explicitly for cutting or reverse-dieting clients, **not** prep clients, who get **free feed days rather than free meals**.

**Conlin on untracked meals in prep:** she disagrees, on variance — "I don't know if you ate 500 calories or 5000." Nippard's only exception: a 24-week prep where the athlete is ahead of schedule.

## Timing, protein, sugar, salt, fiber [71:21–83:06]

- **Hierarchy.** Macros first, adherence **within ±5 g**, more relaxed on exceeding protein than falling short. Nippard cited **Joseph Agu**'s (*captions: "Joseph AO"*) protein hierarchy: **total daily protein > protein quality/source > distribution > timing around workouts and bed**.
- **Distribution.** Roughly even protein splits — not 10 g in one meal and 70 g in another — across **3–5 feedings** (3 is hard to hit, especially for heavier men), reasoning from muscle protein synthesis and muscle retention. She acknowledged IF advocates disagree.
- **Timing.** **Carbs around training**, plus a personal and client-tested preference for a **lower-carb, higher-fat breakfast** for satiety and focus — though she has published no data on it. She doesn't recommend fasted training, but for a 5 a.m. trainee whose stomach won't tolerate food: **two scoops of aminos, or half a scoop of protein plus a carb powder**. Timing is real but secondary, and matters more the closer you get to elite competition — the same logic she applied to supplements [76:28], where a 1% edge is worth taking for a competitor and irrelevant otherwise. Nippard added that for clients on 14-hour days, intermittent fasting wins on adherence.
- **Sugar.** Not tracked — if fiber is adequate, sugar takes care of itself. She noted sugar can drive further sugar cravings while dieting; Nippard dismissed the "instantly stored as fat" fear, especially in a deficit. **Gallant's number:** the fructose study she recalled put a ceiling near **90 g fructose/day**; sugar being ~50% fructose makes that **~180 g sugar** — more carbohydrate than most female competitors eat in total. Risk is confined to people on **400–600+ g carbs plus high fat plus high calories**, where dyslipidemia becomes a concern especially if overweight; checkable with a simple blood panel.
- **Fiber.** Track it. She has seen logs with **200+ g carbs and 8 g fiber**. Comfortable minimum band: **11–15 g per 1000 kcal** (Nippard noted the dietetic figure of ~14 g/1000 kcal). She exceeds it in prep for fullness; only very high-calorie eaters risk too much.
- **Salt.** Tracked only in **peak week and the few days before** — set a baseline, then manipulate. Home-cooked salted food is far lower in sodium than packaged food, deli meats or restaurants. Bikini peaking is much less severe than bodybuilder protocols; she cited Cliff Wilson's carb loads of **over 1000 g, often 1000–1300 g**, against her own experimentation at **600–700 g**. Caveat: **you can do more wrong than right if you mistime a peak**, and it only matters if you're lean enough already.

## Where they disagreed

A friendly discussion, but four real divergences:

1. **Whether flexible dieting solves cravings.** **Conlin flatly rejected** the popular claim: at competition leanness the craving has to be endured, and fitting a treat in is counterproductive. **Nippard** pushed the research the other way — Westenhoefer's flexible-restraint data and Meule's chocolate-craver experiment both suggest restriction amplifies cravings. Resolved by **population**: inclusion helps general-population dieters and stops helping stage-lean competitors.
2. **How much structure a good tracker needs.** **Nippard** expected the flexible group to lose *less* fat and argues for a meal plan built **inside** your macros, since logged variety degrades data quality. **Conlin** agreed for advanced competitors but held her subjects weren't macro-hoarding and the null result stands. Both converged on eating mostly the same things anyway — she sets a semi-weekly plan around what's on sale and only fully locks food down in **peak week**, for digestion (asparagus, spinach, tomatoes).
3. **Untracked meals during a cut.** **Nippard** has a docked-calorie protocol for cutting and reverse-dieting clients. **Conlin** won't use untracked meals in prep at all, citing variance. Nippard agreed it isn't for prep clients.
4. **How to read the LBM finding.** Nippard and Gallant each offered mechanisms. **Conlin resisted all of them** — "everything is a stretch at this point" — and kept steering back to the null result as the real conclusion.

## Practical application

- No structure and can't estimate portions? **Start on a meal plan**, then move toward tracking. A continuum, not a test of virtue.
- Protein **2 g/kg**, remainder **50/50 carbs and fat**, on a **25% deficit**, split into **3–5 even protein feedings** with carbs around training.
- Track **fiber** at **11–15 g/1000 kcal**; skip sugar if fiber is adequate; track salt only in the last days of a peak.
- Eating out: ignore online nutrition data, order single-ingredient items, ask how it was cooked, **overestimate** (~10 g carbs or a tablespoon of oil).
- Be deliberately looser off-season — save adherence capacity for prep.

## Caveats & unknowns

- **n = 27**, 10 weeks dieting plus 10 weeks uncontrolled follow-up, in recreationally trained non-competitors at ~20.5% body fat. Conlin was emphatic this doesn't transfer to physique competitors, and a competitor version would be logistically near-impossible at one site.
- The **LBM result is unexplained and unreplicated**. Her own instruction: don't switch to flexible dieting to gain lean mass on the strength of it.
- The **post-diet period had no intervention** and **salivary leptin didn't work**. Training was uncontrolled, though reported minutes matched. She acknowledged this is less stringent than some would like; her lab (Dr Campbell's) is explicitly real-world-outcome oriented.
- The study compared **meal plan vs macros**, not the rigid-vs-flexible *mindset* construct the older literature measures. Different questions; don't pool them.
- **Caption name uncertainty:** "Weston Hofer" is almost certainly **Westenhoefer**; "Adrien mule" almost certainly **Adrian Meule**; "Dr Bartholomew, University of Texas" unverified; "SOI Lee's ebook" likely **Sohee Lee**; "Joseph AO" likely **Joseph Agu**; "Paul Rella" (the on-campus show) and "Mari wal" (Cliff Wilson's client) unresolved. Conlin's advisor is given only as **Dr Campbell**.
