# Is EMG Research Useless? How to Read Muscle "Activation" Studies

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Our Best Work Yet | Is EMG Research Useless? (IMPORTANT Research)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zNWkHC6n8BQ)) · 2018-01-12 · 10 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- A new methodology paper (lead author appears to be Andrew Vigotsky — the auto-captions garble the name badly, so treat the spelling as uncertain) argues EMG data is routinely over-interpreted by the fitness world.
- Correct terminology: EMG amplitude measures **excitation** (a neuromuscular signal), not **activation** (how many motor units are firing). The two are not synonymous, and Jeff commits to saying "excitation" going forward.
- The evidence for a strong link between acute EMG amplitude and long-term hypertrophy is weak. EMG is a proxy, not a hypertrophy measurement.
- Jeff does not throw EMG out. His position: with load, stretch and everything else held equal, more excitation should still mean more activation and probably more growth over time.
- Practical upshot: use EMG to break ties between similar exercises, never to override a long-term training study.

## What the paper actually challenges
The chain of reasoning EMG research implicitly relies on runs: greater excitation → greater activation → greater motor unit recruitment → greater muscle protein synthesis → greater hypertrophy. The paper's authors attack the **last link** in that chain.

Their key citation is a 2014 study whose title was roughly "myofibrillar protein synthesis does not correlate with long-term hypertrophic adaptations." It found that muscle protein synthesis measured in the ~6 hours after a resistance training session did not correlate with hypertrophy across a ~12-week training programme ([Mitchell 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24586775)).

They also cite stretch research showing significant hypertrophy can occur from stretching **in the absence of activation** — a clean demonstration that activation is not a necessary condition for growth.

## Why EMG became popular in the first place
It is cheap and fast. Sticking electrodes on a subject and reading amplitude takes an afternoon; running a 12-week hypertrophy trial with proper measurement takes months and money. That convenience is why the methodology is so abundant in the literature and so heavily used by fitness content creators (Jeff includes himself) to justify exercise choices.

## Jeff's pushback [03:33]
He is not fully persuaded that protein synthesis is disconnected from hypertrophy. His argument: net protein balance is simply muscle protein synthesis minus muscle protein breakdown, so for maximum hypertrophy you want synthesis high and breakdown low — some correlation almost has to exist.

He notes the paper's own authors acknowledge **indirect** evidence linking the two: studies comparing milk protein to soy protein tend to find higher muscle protein synthesis with milk, and separate studies tend to find more hypertrophy with milk. These are different studies rather than one experiment measuring both, so it is suggestive rather than conclusive — but it points in the same direction.

His conclusion is that a decent theoretical basis remains for treating EMG as a stand-in for hypertrophy, provided the confounders are genuinely controlled. He explicitly frames these as rough, same-day thoughts after one read of the paper, not a settled position.

## Practical application
- When you read "exercise X produced 30% more activation than exercise Y," mentally translate it to "more excitation was measured" and downgrade your confidence about growth accordingly.
- Rank evidence in this order: long-term hypertrophy trials with direct muscle measurement > mechanistic/EMG data > anecdote.
- Only use an EMG comparison to choose between two exercises when the other variables really are matched — same relative load, same range of motion and stretch, same effort level. If the loads or ranges differ, the comparison tells you little.
- Do not restructure a whole programme around a single EMG finding. Use it as a tiebreaker for exercise selection where no long-term data exists.
- Stretch-mediated hypertrophy is a separate mechanism from recruitment — an exercise can grow a muscle through loaded stretch without winning an EMG contest.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The researcher's name is badly mangled in the auto-captions; do not cite it without verifying. Same for the 2014 protein synthesis paper, which Jeff recalls only by approximate title and gives no author for.
- Jeff had read the paper the morning of filming and repeatedly flags his take as provisional; he offers to do a full interview with the lead researcher.
- Whether the milk-vs-soy comparison genuinely supports an MPS–hypertrophy link is contestable, since the synthesis and hypertrophy findings come from separate studies.
- The paper does not claim EMG is invalid as a methodology — only that the interpretive leap to long-term hypertrophy is unsupported.
- Roughly half this video is expo/travel vlog footage with no training content.
