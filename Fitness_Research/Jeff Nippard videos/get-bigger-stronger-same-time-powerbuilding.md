# Powerbuilding: How To Get Bigger And Stronger At The Same Time

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Get Bigger & Stronger At The Same Time (Powerbuilding Science Explained)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OPEDjl88P-4)) · 2020-10-26 · 13 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Rep range barely matters for **hypertrophy** — heavy, moderate and light all build muscle if you train hard enough. Rep range *does* matter for **strength**: strength is specific, so you must lift heavy to get strong.
- Combine ranges. Size-leaning split: most working sets **6-12**, roughly a third in the heavy **1-5** zone. Strength-leaning split: majority of volume in **1-5**, remainder 6-12+.
- Practise squat, bench and deadlift about **twice a week each** (his own: squat 2-3x, bench 2-3x, deadlift 1-2x).
- The big three leave **five muscles underdeveloped**: lats, biceps, rear/side delts, calves, abs. Give each **8-10 sets per week**.
- Simplest periodization shortcut: add **one heavy top set of 1-3 reps** before your normal bodybuilding volume, once a week per lift.

## Rep range and hypertrophy
[00:30] The classic personal-training table — heavy for strength, moderate for size, light for endurance — drives the Instagram claim that heavy makes you strong but not big. The evidence doesn't support the size half.

A study by **Schoenfeld and colleagues** split 20 trained men into a moderate-load group (10 reps per set) and a heavy group (3 reps per set), with total volume matched. After **8 weeks both groups gained the same amount of muscle**.

Reviewing the whole literature, **Greg Nuckols** plotted every high-rep vs low-rep hypertrophy dataset: there is **no obvious relationship between rep count and growth**. Heavy, moderate and light all work provided effort is sufficient.

The one floor: a **2018 paper** found growth drops off once you go down to **20% of 1RM** — that's 50-100 reps for most people, so not a practical concern.

## Rep range and strength
[02:31] Here the range does matter. In the same Schoenfeld study the heavy 3-rep group saw **significantly better bench press strength gains** than the moderate group. Strength is a skill: to get better at lifting heavy, lift heavy. Good news for the powerbuilder — heavy "pure strength" work still counts toward size.

## Why not just do heavy low reps then?
[03:01] The heavy sessions in that study took **more than four times as long** to complete and produced higher reported mental and physical fatigue. Mixing ranges is also likely to recruit different growth mechanisms: lower reps signal growth primarily through **mechanical tension**, higher reps primarily through **metabolic stress**, and mid-range reps through a combination. Using all three makes sense.

## Building the week
[04:02] Allocate the heavy slice to the lifts you most want to improve — usually squat, bench, deadlift.

- Practise each of the big three roughly **twice a week**. His own frequency: squat 2-3x, bench 2-3x, deadlift 1-2x (deadlifts cost more recovery).
- Earlier-week sessions: heavy, taken to **RPE 8-8.5** (1-2 reps in reserve).
- Later-week sessions: **technique work further from failure**, honing form. Maxing out every session ingrains bad habits and wrecks recovery.
- Use technique days to attack sticking points: **deficit deadlifts** for a slow pull off the floor; **paused deadlifts** with lighter weight if the back rounds as tightness is lost; **paused squats** for posture and explosiveness out of the hole.

**Accessories:** mostly **6-12 reps**, a few in the **12+** metabolic zone. Place them so they don't sabotage the main lifts — e.g. a heavy Pendlay row the day before heavy deadlifts fatigues the lower back, so use a chest-supported row instead. Accessories let you accumulate volume and satisfy the high-effort urge without the recovery cost of hammering the power lifts.

**What the big three cover:** squats — excellent quads, good glutes; bench — excellent pecs and front delts, good triceps; deadlift — excellent spinal erectors and glutes, good hamstrings. Left behind: **lats, biceps, rear and side delts, calves, abs** — target at least **8-10 sets per week each**. He likes a **roaming hypertrophy day** to mop up whichever body parts fell short; that's where he does most bicep, side delt and ab work.

## Periodization and deloads
[08:07] Beginners (first year or two) don't need periodization — just **linear progression**, adding a little weight each workout. After that plateau hits, options are:

**Block periodization over a year (macrocycle):**
1. Powerbuilding block — size and strength equally.
2. Cutting into summer — pure hypertrophy block, heavy strength work cut back to maintenance only.
3. Post-summer, calories up — pure strength block with just enough accessory work to hold size.
4. Final hypertrophy block, exploiting the new strength to apply more overload and break block-2 plateaus.

**Or the shortcut — top sets.** Add one heavy set of **1-3 reps** before your usual volume once a week per lift (e.g. before your usual 3x10 bench). Progress it over time by adding weight or a rep. It shouldn't go to failure — just reasonably heavy and challenging.

**Deloads:** at least once every couple of months, cut weight and volume by roughly **25-50%**. Not a lazy week — use the lighter loads to improve technique on the big lifts and mind-muscle connection on accessories.

## Practical application
Five steps: (1) mix rep ranges — heavy because strength is specific, lighter to avoid burnout; (2) practise the big lifts ~2x/week with real technique focus; (3) use accessories to cover lats, biceps, rear/side delts, calves and abs at 8-10 sets each per week; (4) linear progression for year one, then block periodization and/or weekly heavy top sets; (5) manage recovery by generally avoiding failure on the big lifts and deloading every couple of months.

## Caveats & unknowns
The rep-range-doesn't-matter-for-size conclusion holds only when sets are taken sufficiently close to failure, and breaks down below roughly 30% 1RM. Individual and study-to-study variation in the hypertrophy data is real; the claim is that no *systematic* rep-range effect appears. The mechanical-tension vs metabolic-stress split is presented as a plausible mechanism rather than settled fact.
