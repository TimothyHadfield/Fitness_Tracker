# Quad Exercises Ranked S–F: Squat Patterns, Machines and the Rectus Femoris Problem

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Best & Worst QUAD Exercises (Ranked Using Science)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kIXcoivzGf8)) · 2024-07-02 · 12 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Best exercise overall: the **hack squat**. The pendulum squat feels better to him but few gyms have one; if there's no hack squat, use the **high-bar back squat**.
- Worst: the **bosu ball squat** — the ankle does the stabilizing and the quads get almost no tension. Squat-plus-press combos and jump squats also go to F tier for hypertrophy.
- S tier: barbell back squat, hack squat, pendulum squat, Smith machine squat, Bulgarian split squat.
- Squat patterns under-train the **rectus femoris**, so include a leg extension ([Zabaleta-Korta 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34743671/)) — ideally with the **seat set back**.
- Free weights and machines build muscle roughly equally, as long as you take sets close to failure ([Haugen 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37582807/)).

## Criteria and anatomy
Three boxes: high quad tension especially in the stretch; feels good (no knee pain, smooth resistance); simple progression by adding weight or a rep weekly.

Four heads: **vastus medialis** (the teardrop, inside), **vastus intermedius** (middle, deep), **vastus lateralis** (outside), and **rectus femoris** (long, flat, on top). A recent anatomy paper suggested a possible fifth head ([Grob 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26732825/)) — no training implications either way.

All four extend the knee. Only the rectus femoris also performs **hip flexion**. On squats it shortens at the hip while lengthening at the knee, so its overall length barely changes — this is why squats hit it less. Studies still show decent rectus femoris growth from squats ([Kojic 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9737272/)), and one non-squat exercise in the program removes the concern entirely.

## The rankings

**S tier**
- **Barbell back squat** — high tension deep in the hole where the quads are most stretched, feels good once your bar path is dialed, and near-unbeatable for progressive overload. Spinal erectors do stabilization work but the quads are still the prime mover. Evidence by observation: every big squatter at a powerlifting meet has massive quads.
- **Hack squat** — everything the barbell squat offers plus faster setup, more locked-in (less stabilization, more direct quad tension), and free choice of foot position without balance concerns. If the machine won't let you reach full depth, put **yoga blocks on your shoulders** to get all the way down.
- **Pendulum squat** — like a hack squat but the weight travels in an arc, which he finds more natural than the hack's linear path. Availability is the only knock.
- **Smith machine squat** — same basic pattern as a free barbell squat minus stabilizer demand, and you can push to or near failure without fear of dumping the bar.
- **Bulgarian split squat** — brutal, huge quad stretch, and unilateral work fixes left-right imbalances. So fatiguing (physically and psychologically) that he programs only **2 sets per workout**. Produces more quad soreness than almost anything else.

**A tier**
- **Front squat** — the front-loaded bar forces an upright torso, increasing the moment arm at the knee and shifting more tension to the quads. Counter-argument that the upper back becomes the limiter he mostly rejects unless you go very heavy. Use a two-finger, cross-arm or strap grip, keep elbows up, squat between the legs, stand with a tall chest. Held at A only because many people find the bar position awkward — bump it to S if it's comfortable for you.
- **Low-bar back squat** — bar 2–3 inches lower on the rear delts. Research shows you can load more weight but tension shifts from quads toward glutes ([Glassbrook 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28195975/)). Still very high quad activity at reasonable depth.
- **45° leg press** — high quad tension, feels good, good overload. Held at A because many machines won't let you go as deep as a squat; aim to get **calves to glutes**.
- **Leg extension** — hips are fixed, so unlike squat patterns the rectus femoris actually stretches and contracts: the first exercise where **all four heads** are highly engaged. A brand new study found significantly more overall quad growth and especially more rectus femoris growth with the **seat set back**, likely from training the rectus femoris at a longer length. A staple on his leg days. The "bad for your knees" claim is thoroughly debunked, though go easy if you already have knee pain.
- **Reverse Nordic** — same movement pattern as a leg extension but more accessible (no machine) and a much deeper quad stretch. Very hard for beginners and awkward to load, so overload is mostly adding reps or depth. Drop it a tier or two if it feels wrong to you.

**B tier**
- **Lunge** — grows both but is more a glute builder than a quad builder; shorter, shallower steps bring the quads in more. He'd rate it A or S for glutes.
- **Goblet squat** — biomechanically close to a front squat and highly quad-activating, but you'll soon squat more than you can comfortably hold, so it's stuck at high reps. Uses: teaching beginners, and as a high-rep finisher.
- **Sissy squat** — like the reverse Nordic, a huge quad stretch; probably the best squat variation for reaching an extremely deep stretch. Hard to load (progress mostly by reps), has a learning curve, and going up on the toes with knees far forward feels awkward to some. A/B, settled at B.

**C tier**
- **Horizontal leg press** — even more limited range of motion than the 45°, so less tension in the stretch, and intermediate lifters max out the stack.
- **Deadlift** — for the quads it's essentially a quarter squat. Sumo adds a bit of quad involvement. Excellent glute and strength builder, poor quad builder.
- **Step-up** — as unpleasant as Bulgarians but less effective: less quad stretch, less stable. Decent glute stimulus, especially if you don't push off the back foot.
- **Pistol squat** — impressive, but stability and overload limits hold it back. Genuinely good if you train at home or bodyweight-only.

**F tier**
- **Squat + press / lunge + curl combos** — your shoulders and arms fail long before your quads do.
- **Bosu ball squat** — the ankle absorbs the stabilization demand and the quads never get loaded. Possible athletic-context uses; useless for size.
- **Jump squat** — burns calories and builds some explosive power, but too little tension and too little overload for hypertrophy.

## Practical application
- Pick one heavy squat pattern as the backbone: hack squat first choice, high-bar back squat otherwise, or pendulum/Smith if available.
- Add a **seat-back leg extension** to cover the rectus femoris (or reverse Nordics if there's no machine).
- Add **Bulgarian split squats at 2 sets per side per workout** for the stretch and for balancing sides.
- Go as deep as the equipment allows — depth is where the quad tension lives. Use yoga blocks on the hack squat if needed.
- Progress by adding a small amount of weight or a rep each week.
- Skip anything that trades quad tension for balance demand (bosu, combos, jumps).

## Caveats & unknowns
- Some short-term studies show similar leg growth from leg extensions and squats, but he doubts leg extensions match squats over the long term because their overload ceiling is lower.
- The rectus femoris concern about squats is real anatomically but studies still show solid growth; one hips-fixed exercise resolves it.
- Front squats and sissy squats are personal-comfort downgrades — if the position suits you, rate them higher.
- Leg extensions don't cause knee problems, but ease off if you already have knee pain.
