# Back Width vs Thickness: Grip Width on Pulldowns and Rows

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Train Back WIDTH vs THICKNESS (Close vs Wide Grip? Rows or Pullups?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PAXkl-AdJFg)) · 2019-07-27 · 8 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- **Vertical pulls (pulldown/pull-up): a medium-to-wide overhand grip around 1.5x shoulder width, pulled to the front, is the sweet spot for lat development / width.**
- **On rows the logic flips: a closer grip emphasises width (lats via shoulder extension); a wider grip emphasises thickness (mid traps and rear delts via transverse abduction and scapular retraction).**
- **Rows build both.** Lehman et al. found the seated row **outperformed the lat pulldown for both lat and trap activation** — everyone should have a row in the program.
- Overhand (pronated) beats underhand (supinated) for lat activation; grip *width* differences are small.
- A **medium-grip barbell/Pendlay row, elbows driven back and out at ~45°**, is the best single blend of width and thickness.

## What "width" actually is [00:31]
Back width appearance comes from two things: **lat development** and **waist circumference**. A smaller waist means a better V-taper and a wider-looking back — and waist size is a diet variable, not a training one. So training-side, width work = lat work.

## The pulldown grip evidence [01:02]
Three EMG studies, each fixing the previous one's flaw:

1. **Signorile et al. 2002** — compared close-grip pulldowns (neutral and supinated) against wide-grip pulldowns to the front and behind the neck. **Wide grips won for mean lat activation, with wide-grip-to-the-front best** ([Signorile 2002](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/12423182)). *Flaw:* the narrow conditions also changed wrist position, so the lower activation may have been the neutral/reverse grip rather than the narrow width.

2. **Lusk et al. 2010** — held wrist position constant while varying width: wide pronated, wide supinated, narrow pronated, narrow supinated. **Width didn't matter; both pronated grips significantly beat both supinated grips** ([Lusk 2010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/20543740)). *Flaw:* absolute rather than relative loading — the overhand grips may have won because you can't move as much weight with them, putting subjects closer to failure.

3. **Andersen et al. 2014** — three overhand widths (narrow, medium, wide) with **relative loading**. **Medium grip came out on top**: higher 6RM strength than wide grip, significantly higher concentric biceps activation, and a **trend** toward higher lat activation ([Andersen 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24662157)).

Nippard's read: a **slight edge to a medium overhand grip at about 1.5x shoulder width, pulled to the front of the neck**. But EMG reports group averages, so he treats it as a first approximation only and turns to biomechanics.

## The biomechanical argument [03:07]
- **Close grip** on a vertical pull emphasises **shoulder extension** — the arm comes down in front of you, in the sagittal plane.
- **Wide grip** emphasises **shoulder adduction** — the arm comes down to the side, in the frontal plane.
- The lats do both. But **rowing exercises (dumbbell rows, cable rows, machine rows) already hit the lats through shoulder extension**, so from a programming standpoint your pulldowns and pull-ups are better spent training the lats through **shoulder adduction** — i.e. a **wider pronated grip most of the time**.

## The width/thickness split is overstated [04:08]
The common belief that wide grips only build width and narrow grips only build thickness is wrong. The Andersen paper's whole-movement activation figure shows **both the lats and the traps are highly active in the pulldown regardless of grip position** ([Andersen 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24662157)). Any grip from **1x to 2x shoulder width**, taken through a full ROM with controlled technique, will improve both width and thickness.

## Rows [04:38]
- **Lehman et al.**: the **seated row outperformed the lat pulldown for both lat and trap activation** — so a row is essential if you want a back that's both wider and thicker.
- **No EMG data exists on grip width for rows**, so this section rests on biomechanics:
  - **Closer grip → width.** A close attachment (e.g. the V-bar on a cable row) lets you drive the **elbows down and in**, emphasising the lats through shoulder extension over the traps.
  - **Wider grip → thickness.** A wide grip more effectively emphasises **transverse shoulder abduction and scapular retraction**, bringing the **mid traps and rear delts** in.
  - **Medium grip (slightly wider than shoulder width) on a bent-over barbell row or Pendlay row**, with the **elbows pulled back and out at about 45°**, gives a blend of lat and trap recruitment — an excellent all-round back movement.

## Practical application
- **Vertical pulls:** overhand grip, ~1.5x shoulder width, pull to the front of the neck. Any width from 1-2x shoulder width still works — pick what's comfortable, lets you feel the lats, and lets you overload.
- **For lat/width on rows:** close or V-bar grip, cue elbows **down and in**.
- **For thickness on rows:** wide grip, cue elbows **back**, squeeze the shoulder blades together.
- **For both at once:** medium-grip barbell or Pendlay row, elbows back and out at ~45°.
- Waist size is a diet job — that half of the V-taper isn't trainable.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Each of the three pulldown studies has a design flaw (confounded wrist position in 2002; absolute loading in 2010); only Andersen 2014 used relative loading, and its lat finding was a **trend**, not significant.
- EMG reports averages, so it can't tell you what maximises *your* lat activation.
- Row grip-width recommendations are **biomechanical reasoning only** — no EMG data has addressed grip width in the row.
- Nippard explicitly warns against black-and-white thinking: comfort, full range of motion, mind-muscle connection and progressive overload matter more than any of these tweaks, which are for emphasising weak areas.
