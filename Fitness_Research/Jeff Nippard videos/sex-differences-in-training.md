# Sex Differences in Training: What Actually Changes for Women vs Men

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "MEN vs WOMEN | Sex Differences in Training | Science Explained (12 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NoUhE8TAGKM)) · 2017-03-15 · 6 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The practical differences are **minor tweaks to the same fundamental program**, not different training philosophies.
- Women can build an appreciable amount of muscle — likely **50-100% of the hypertrophic potential of men**, depending on which literature you weight.
- Women **recover better and fatigue less**, so they can handle **more sets, more reps, more sessions per week, and shorter rest periods**.
- Women are relatively **better at higher reps**, men relatively better at **explosive work** — play to those strengths without going all-or-nothing.
- Most apparent male/female training differences are explained by **muscle mass and body fat**, not sex itself.

## The "bulky" myth, and the opposite myth
[00:30] Lifting is a better route to the "toned" look most women say they want than more cardio is. Building muscle generally produces exactly that look. The monstrous, overtly muscular physique is realistically only attainable with **steroid use** or a highly advanced, extremely rigorous training and nutrition plan specifically dedicated to it.

[01:01] The reverse myth is also false: women do **not** have poor muscle-building potential. Men can average up to **15× more testosterone**, yet natural potential to build muscle is quite similar.

## How similar is muscle-building potential?
[01:32] The answer depends on who you ask.

- **Menno Henselmans** (interviewed for the video) argues men and women have essentially the **exact same relative growth potential**, citing most research.
- A **2001 study** on sex differences in response to strength training found **no difference in muscle gained after 6 months**, and there is other similar research.
- Conflicting evidence exists: one paper found men gained **about twice as much muscle** as women over a six-month strength training intervention.
- **Lyle McDonald** suggests women can expect roughly **half** the muscle gain of men.

Jeff's synthesis: women likely fall somewhere between **50 and 100% of male hypertrophic potential**. The difference in *final result* is largely because women **start smaller**, genetically carry **less muscle and more body fat**.

[02:33] On strength: men are stronger in absolute terms, but **97% of that difference is attributable to muscle mass alone**. Match a man and a woman for muscle mass and they will likely have similar strength. This is the general pattern — differences usually reduce to muscle mass and body fat, not sex per se.

## Difference 1 — Hormones and recovery
[02:33] Women can have about **9× more estrogen** than men, and estrogen benefits not only metabolic health and glucose handling but **muscular repair and protection against catabolism**.

Training implication: women **recover from more work**, so they can do **more sets and reps per workout and more workouts per week**. Since training volume is tightly correlated with size and strength gains, all else equal **women should do more volume than men** to maximise results.

## Difference 2 — Fatigability
[03:04] Why women fatigue less is complex — muscle-level, hormonal and nervous-system factors all contribute, and more research is needed. Two documented contributors:

- **Better blood flow and muscle perfusion**, so fatigue-inducing metabolites clear out faster.
- A **greater proportional area of slow type I fibres**, which are better suited to endurance work.

Net results:
- Women handle **higher reps** better than men.
- Women can do **more reps at a given intensity** (percentage of 1RM) than men.
- Women can likely get away with **shorter rest periods** between sets.

## Difference 3 — Explosiveness and tempo
[04:06] The flip side: women are **less suited to explosive training**. If you play to strengths, men benefit from **more explosive tempos with a forceful concentric**, while women may do better with a **slower tempo**.

Jeff explicitly warns against black-and-white thinking here: men should still include **some slow-tempo and high-rep work**, and women should include **some explosive work**.

## Difference 4 — Upper vs lower body
[04:37] A **1993 paper** reported women at about **50% of male upper-body strength** but **66% of male lower-body strength**. This lower-body relative advantage may be part of why women often enjoy leg training more — they are comparatively better at it.

## Practical application
- Run the same fundamental program structure regardless of sex — the primary hypertrophy driver, **mechanical tension**, does not discriminate.
- For women: bias toward **higher total volume** (more sets/reps per session, more sessions per week), **higher rep ranges**, **shorter rest periods**, and a **more controlled tempo**.
- For men: bias toward **more explosive concentrics** and slightly lower volume/longer rest, while still keeping some slow-tempo and higher-rep work in the mix.
- For women whose upper body lags relative to lower body, expect that gap — it's normal, and extra upper-body work is reasonable.
- Don't program by sex alone; muscle mass and body fat explain most of the differences, so an individual's training status matters more.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The muscle-growth-potential literature genuinely conflicts (no difference at 6 months in one study, ~2× in another). The 50-100% range reflects that uncertainty.
- The mechanisms behind women's lower fatigability are not fully worked out.
- All recommendations here are averages and tendencies; individual variation can override them, and the differences are described throughout as minor variations on a sound baseline program.
