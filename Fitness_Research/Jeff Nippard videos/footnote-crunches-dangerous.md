# Are Crunches Dangerous? The Spinal Flexion Debate

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "FOOTNOTE † : Are Crunches Dangerous?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_ctGsTyQSuw)) · 2017-07-10 · 4 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- The anti-crunch case rests almost entirely on cadaveric pig-spine research that doesn't transfer well to living, training humans.
- Schoenfeld and Contreras (2011, NSCA *Journal of Strength and Conditioning*, "To Crunch or Not to Crunch") reviewed the arguments and rejected them.
- Discs appear to *adapt and strengthen* to progressive spinal flexion, rather than accumulating irreversible damage.
- Practical guardrails: stay at or below **60 total spinal-flexion reps per workout**, leave **at least 48 hours** between crunching sessions, and wait **1-2 hours after waking** before flexion-based ab work.

## The case against crunches, and why it's weak
The core argument is that spinal flexion herniates discs. This comes from in-vitro studies on cadaveric pig spines: a spine is mounted in an apparatus that applies compressive load, then cycled through flexion and extension. In at least five such studies, most discs did herniate — technically true, but with several problems when extrapolated to a human in a gym:

- **No fluid flow.** Cadaveric spines lack the fluid between discs that living human spines have, so their mechanical behaviour differs.
- **Different anatomy.** Pig spines have a smaller flexion/extension range of motion than human spines.
- **Muscles removed.** All musculature was stripped in the cadaveric preparations, which changes spinal biomechanics and gutted real-world applicability.
- **Absurd dose.** Studies applied thousands — sometimes tens of thousands — of flexion cycles in one bout. Far more than even a dedicated ab trainee performs in a session.

## The "limited number of flexion cycles" claim
A popular follow-on idea is that each person has a finite lifetime allowance of spinal flexions, so you should spend them on daily activities (tying shoes, picking things up) rather than "wasting" them on crunches. There is no convincing human evidence for this. And even if such a budget existed, it struggles to explain the many successful athletes who accumulate millions of spinal flexion cycles across full careers without incident.

Schoenfeld and Contreras also highlight research showing **genetics drive most spinal degeneration**, with wear and tear from progressive exercise playing a comparatively minor role — particularly given that rest between sessions relieves disc stress and lets tissue remodel and repair.

## Proposed benefits of dynamic flexion over static core work
The article argues crunching may beat static exercises like planks for several reasons:

1. **Disc nutrition.** Spinal motion increases nutrient delivery to the discs. Age-related decline in disc nutritional status is implicated as the primary cause of disc degeneration, so movement may be protective.
2. **Adaptive strengthening.** Proper doses of spinal flexion strengthen the discs and surrounding tissues, potentially preventing future injury.
3. **Eccentric loading for hypertrophy.** Because eccentric contractions matter for maximising muscle growth, crunches may simply build the rectus abdominis better than their static counterparts.
4. Additional cited benefits: mobility improvements, prevention of low back pain, and sports performance enhancement.

## Practical application
- Cap spinal flexion volume at **≤60 reps per workout**.
- Allow **≥48 hours** between dedicated crunching sessions for recuperation.
- Don't crunch first thing in the morning — wait **at least 1-2 hours after waking**. Discs become more elastic and flexible as the day progresses, reducing prolapse risk.
- Treat crunches as one component of a full-body resistance programme, not an isolated high-volume habit.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This note summarises a single review article's position; the underlying human evidence base on flexion-based ab training and long-term spinal health is thin in both directions.
- The pig-spine studies aren't *wrong* about their own model — the objection is about extrapolation, not fabrication.
- Individual pathology matters. Someone with an existing disc issue is a different case from a healthy trainee, and this material doesn't address that population.
