# Can You Speed Up Your Metabolism? Ranking Every Popular Strategy

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Increase Your Metabolism (Eat More, Lose More)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g9QGQJ1ypp0)) · 2023-06-11 · 14 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Metabolic rates differ enormously between people: at 80 kg, the range in a 6,000-subject study ran from **~1,400 to ~5,700 kcal/day**.
- Only four things clearly work: **building muscle, doing cardio, dieting slowly, and smuggling in extra NEAT**.
- Building **30 lb of muscle** adds roughly **180 kcal/day** — the largest single lever available, and it takes years.
- Cardio is partly self-cancelling: for every 100 calories burned, daily expenditure rises by only about **72 calories** because NEAT drops.
- Green tea, sauna, cold plunges and high meal frequency **probably don't work**. Most of the "boosters" are worth 5–30 calories.

## How different are metabolisms really?
[01:01] "Metabolism" in a fitness context means metabolic rate — total daily energy expenditure. A 2022 study published in *Science* on over 6,000 subjects from diverse backgrounds plotted body weight against total energy expenditure. **At 80 kg (176 lb)**, the slowest person burned about **1,400 kcal/day** and the fastest about **5,700 kcal/day**. That means one person would need to eat under 1,400 kcal to lose weight while another loses on 5,000. This is why speeding metabolism up matters: more calories while dieting means less depletion, better gym performance and better muscle retention.

## It works

**Building muscle.** [06:41] Muscle burns about **6 kcal per pound per day** at rest versus about **2 kcal per pound per day** for fat — three times as much. On Nippard's own body composition, sitting all day, his fat burns ~24 kcal and his muscle ~480 kcal, a 456 kcal difference. Realistically: a newer lifter who adds **30 lb of muscle over five years** goes from burning 2,500 kcal/day to **2,680 kcal/day**, a **180 kcal/day** gain. The biggest booster in the video.

**Cardio.** [09:15] It burns calories, but research shows **energy compensation** — burn more through exercise and your body subconsciously burns less through NEAT (non-exercise activity thermogenesis: fidgeting, shuffling, head-bobbing). Example: normally you burn 500 kcal through NEAT; you add a 30-minute jog burning 500 kcal; your body responds by cutting NEAT to ~350 kcal. There's still a net gain, but on average **100 calories of cardio yields only ~72 extra calories of daily expenditure**. Individual variation in compensation is large. This is why Nippard prefers diet as the primary deficit tool and cardio as secondary.

**Slow dieting.** [11:47] The more aggressively you cut, the more metabolic rate falls. Aim to lose **0.5–1.0% of body weight per week**; the further above that you go, the more likely your metabolism tanks.

**NEAT smuggling.** [12:18] Deliberately adding non-exercise activity: park further from the shop, take stairs over the lift, stand and stretch regularly at your desk.

## It might work

**Water.** [02:33] Each glass of **cold** water burns about **8 calories** — cold beats room temperature because the body spends energy warming it to body temperature. Nippard downgrades it to "might work" because the extra burn could be compensated for (drink more, subconsciously move less), and forced high water intake in a short window risks **hyponatremia / water intoxication**, which is dangerous. **2–3 litres (8–12 glasses) per day is plenty**; most people can just follow thirst. The genuine benefit is fullness at meals.

**Spicy food / capsaicin.** [04:37] A **2017 meta-analysis of nine studies** found capsaicin increased energy expenditure by an average of **69 kcal/day** — but only in studies where subjects' **BMI was over 25**. A bowl of green curry containing ~0.5 g of chilli pepper supplies about **2 mg capsaicin**, which extrapolates most generously to about a **20 kcal boost** against a 700 kcal meal. That's small, though it makes capsaicin a reasonable supplementation candidate. The real mechanism for weight loss is probably satiety: spicy meals get eaten more slowly and with more water.

**Reverse dieting.** [07:12] Gradually raising calories post-diet to "rebuild metabolism." Nippard is skeptical. He has seen people add several hundred calories while holding weight, but his objection is that going slowly is unnecessary — better to **jump straight to your new maintenance**, then increase gradually from there until you reach the top of your dynamic maintenance range and start gaining. He points to an article by Eric Trexler as broadly matching his position.

**Weighted vests.** [10:47] The proposed mechanism is the **gravitostat** — a system of sensors detecting loading on the bones. Convince those sensors you're heavier and expenditure rises while hunger falls. Natural pro bodybuilder **Eric Salazar**, with exercise scientist **James Krieger**, ran a case study wearing a **34 lb vest for 90% of waking hours** through a prep, reaching **5% body fat** with fewer of the usual side effects of getting shredded than in past diets. Nippard suspects it genuinely works but keeps it in "might work" pending published research.

## Probably doesn't work

**Green tea.** [03:35] A **2021 systematic review** found that of four studies on acute metabolic effects, **three found no effect** and one found a **79 kcal/day increase** — but that study had only **10 subjects** and lasted **24 hours**. At least two studies on green tea and long-term fat loss found **no benefit**.

**Sauna.** [05:38] A **1995 Japanese study** found sauna increased oxygen consumption and raised heart rate by **20–25 bpm**. For a 180 lb person that's about **19 calories** for 10 minutes in the sauna versus **14 calories** sitting in a normal room — a **5 calorie difference**, roughly one stick of gum.

**Cold plunges / ice baths.** [06:09] Oxygen consumption does rise and shivering indicates a metabolic response, but sitting neck-deep in an ice bath for 10 minutes burns about **28 calories** versus 14 at room temperature — a **14 calorie boost**.

**Meal frequency.** [08:44] A **2012 study** had subjects spend three days in an isolated respiration chamber eating either **3 meals or 14 meals per day** at identical total calories, and found **no difference in energy expenditure**. A **2015 meta-analysis of 15 studies** found no significant difference in fat mass across 1–2, 3–4, and 5+ meals per day; the graph appears to favour higher frequency, but that is driven by a single study (**Ewell and colleagues**) — remove it and the difference disappears.

## Practical application
- Prioritise the big levers: keep training to build muscle over years, use cardio as a supporting tool, and cut at 0.5–1% body weight per week rather than crashing.
- Add NEAT deliberately throughout the day — stairs, walking, standing, distant parking.
- Drink 2–3 L water daily for fullness and hydration, not as a calorie-burning strategy, and don't force-drink.
- Use spicy food and water for satiety; use capsaicin as a supplement only if you want a marginal edge and are overweight.
- Skip green tea extract, sauna and cold plunges *if metabolism is the reason you're doing them* — they may have other merits, but not this one.
- Eat however many meals per day suits your schedule.
- After a diet, go straight to your new maintenance rather than reverse dieting slowly upward.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Energy compensation varies a lot between individuals — the 72-per-100 figure is an average.
- The weighted-vest evidence is a single case study on one prepping bodybuilder; no published trial yet.
- The capsaicin effect appeared only in subjects with BMI over 25, and the 20 kcal estimate is a generous extrapolation from meta-analytic data to a real meal.
- Nippard flags reverse dieting as needing its own deeper treatment; his position at time of filming is provisional.
- [12:18] Most importantly: **metabolic rate is far less predictive of long-term weight-loss success than people assume.** Fast metabolisms fail and slow ones succeed. The factors more reliably associated with lasting weight loss are being physically active, weighing yourself regularly, weight training, and making lifestyle changes.
