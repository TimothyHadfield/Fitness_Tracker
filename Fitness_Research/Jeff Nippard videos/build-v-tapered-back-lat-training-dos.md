# Lat Pulldown Technique For A V-Tapered Back

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Build A V-Tapered Back: Lat Training Dos and Don'ts" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O94yEoGXtBY)) · 2019-09-17 · 8 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Treat pull-ups as the heavy overload movement (**5-10 reps**) and pulldowns as the mind-muscle-connection movement (**8-12 reps**).
- Default grip: **double overhand, ~1.5x shoulder width, bar to the front of the neck** — best mix of strength plus biceps and lat activation.
- Pull **elbows down and in at only 10-15 degrees** from the top view, not forward at 45 degrees.
- Lift the chest into thoracic extension and **depress the scapulae** before every rep.
- Moderate lean-back is fine; heavy body English mostly isn't.

## Pulldown vs pull-up [00:01]
If forced to choose one, he'd take the pull-up, but both belong in a complete program. The pull-up is the heavy progressive-overload movement in roughly the **5-10 rep** range; the pulldown works better as a feel-and-contract movement — mind-muscle connection has gained empirical support in recent years — while still aiming to overload when possible.

If you can't do a bodyweight pull-up, **assisted pull-ups beat pulldowns** for building pull-up strength. Using pulldowns to build pull-ups is like using push-ups to build a bench press: fine for the pecs, poor for strength carryover.

## Biomechanics [01:01]
A standard overhand pulldown trains several actions at once:
- **Shoulder adduction** (the main action) — lats plus teres major and minor bringing the arms down to the sides.
- **Shoulder extension** — unavoidable since you can't pull the bar dead straight down; handled mainly by the lats and the long head of the triceps.
- **Elbow flexion** — biceps and brachialis.

Load it too heavily and the lower back and momentum take over, which reduces overload on the lats. Hence the **8-12 rep** sweet spot.

## Setup and execution [02:01]
1. **Adjust the knee pads first.** If you have to go onto your toes for your knees to brace, you lose stability and your hips will lift each rep. If you can't keep heels planted with knees secure, stack a couple of 10 or 25 lb plates under your feet.
2. **Grip roughly 1.5x shoulder width, double overhand** ([Andersen 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24662157)). Many people find a **thumbless (suicide) grip** improves lat mind-muscle connection — and unlike on the bench press, there's no safety risk here.
3. Take the bar, sit, brace knees under the pads, heels down.
4. Before rep one, **lift the chest into thoracic extension**. Some prefer a flat upper back to pull straight down; he thinks an extended upper back is a more secure, more powerful position.
5. **Depress the scapulae** — tuck the shoulder blades down — as you pull the elbows in toward your sides at about a **10-15 degree angle viewed from above**. Think elbows down and in, not pulling with the hands and arms.
6. The bar should make **light contact with the top of the sternum / upper chest**.
7. Reverse by actively resisting the negative, feeling the lats stretch on the way up.
8. At full elbow extension, lift the chest and re-depress the scapulae before the next rep.

## Lean-back and cheating [03:33]
Neither perfectly upright nor maximum lean is right. A **moderate lean-back** is acceptable if it makes the lift feel more natural or lets you overload better. Rule of thumb: even leaning back, the **cable should still travel in a roughly straight line viewed from the side**.

As an advanced technique, moderate cheating on the concentric is acceptable **provided you control the eccentric** — a legitimate way to eccentrically overload the lats. What he won't endorse is the extreme version: turning the pulldown into an inverted row and letting the weight fall on the way up.

## Grip variations [04:34]
- **Neutral grip** — emphasises shoulder extension, and many people feel their lats better here. Not his favourite, since rows already supply plenty of extension work, but a solid option.
- **Supinated (underhand)** — same principles, but grip **closer, about 1x shoulder width**, to avoid wrist pain. Reasonable if you want more biceps involvement ([Signorile 2002](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/12423182)).

## Common errors [05:05]
1. **Too much body English.** Outside deliberate eccentric-overload work, stay stable and focus on contracting the lats rather than yanking with arms, lower back and traps.
2. **Not adducting the shoulder enough** on the medium overhand grip — pulling elbows forward toward the front by ~45 degrees turns it into shoulder extension. Elbows should only come forward **10-15 degrees**.
3. **Pulling with the biceps on the underhand version.** The biceps do engage more here biomechanically, but treat it as a back movement: arms are connectors, drive the elbows down as in a pullover.

## Alternatives and program context [06:35]
- **Lat pull-ins** (simple cable setup) work if pulldowns aren't available, but he reserves them for warm-ups or pre-activation.
- For overall back development, include **some form of pull-up and some form of horizontal row** in the routine.

## Practical application
- Pull-ups: 5-10 reps, heavy, progressive. Pulldowns: 8-12 reps, controlled, feel-focused.
- Medium double-overhand grip (~1.5x shoulder width), thumbless if it helps; bar to upper chest.
- Chest up, scapulae depressed, elbows down and in at 10-15 degrees.
- Moderate lean-back permitted; keep the cable path roughly straight.
- Control every eccentric; cheat reps only as a deliberate eccentric-overload tool.
- Include both a vertical pull and a horizontal row for complete back development.

## Caveats & unknowns
Nothing beyond his own hedging: grip choice for emphasising different back regions is covered in his separate width-vs-thickness video, and lean-back preference is partly individual.
