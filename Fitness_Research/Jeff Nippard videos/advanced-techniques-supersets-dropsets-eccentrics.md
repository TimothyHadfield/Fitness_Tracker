# Advanced Techniques and Training Volume with Dr. Brad Schoenfeld

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Advanced Training Techniques: Supersets, Eccentrics, Dropsets, High Volume ft. Dr. Brad Schoenfeld" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ri0v5-osLCQ)) · 2019-04-28 · 79 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- Schoenfeld's standing recommendation is **10-20 hard sets per muscle per week**, periodised (undulating up and down) rather than held at one fixed number.
- His high-volume study found hypertrophy kept increasing up to **30-45 sets/muscle/week**, but he reads that as a **short-term (≈4 week) specialisation block for a lagging muscle**, not a year-round prescription.
- **Drop sets** are a time-efficient way to add volume — equal growth in less time — but there is no clear benefit once volume is equated.
- **Accentuated eccentrics** have a plausible mechanistic case (different intracellular signalling, more distal hypertrophy) and low cost — use them selectively, not on every set.
- **Vary exercises.** Fonseca's study showed rotating squat/leg press/lunge beat squats alone for several quad muscles. Keep free-weight core lifts in permanently; machines can be swapped freely.

## What "evidence-based" actually means
[06:20] Three prongs: the whole body of current evidence (weighted by how strong it is), the practitioner's own expertise, and the needs of the individual. Demanding a PubMed citation for everything is as short-sighted as dismissing science entirely. Generalisability is a continuum: in vitro < rat < untrained human < trained human. Studies report **means, and people are not means** — on the same program one subject can gain 20% muscle and another zero.

## Specialised techniques
**Drop sets** [18:33] — Schoenfeld collaborated on a Japanese study using MRI (gold standard) that found a benefit to drop sets at equated volume, though it was underpowered. Three other studies found no difference at equated volume, so he is sceptical of a true superiority. The real uses: (a) add volume without adding session time — a drop set on your third set instead of a fourth and fifth set; (b) train in less time with no detriment to growth.

**Accentuated eccentrics** [20:33] — the best-supported specialised technique in his view, because eccentrics produce different intracellular signalling than concentrics and show non-uniform effects (more distal hypertrophy vs. the mid/proximal bias of concentric work), so the two may be synergistic. Cost is low: little extra time, not much extra fatigue. Ways to load it [22:03]: two legs up / one leg down on a leg curl or leg press; load a bench press bar to **120-130% of 1RM** and have a spotter lift it while you lower under control. A partner pushing down while you resist also works. He stresses you are stronger eccentrically than concentrically — which is why deadlifts are hard to cheat.

**Supersets** [25:38] — an unpublished study of his showed a *negative* effect from agonist-antagonist supersets; he isn't ready to write them off without replication. The main practical benefit remains time saving.

**Nothing to write off outright.** His earlier NSCA paper on intensity techniques was explicitly hypothesis-generating; the evidence still isn't strong enough for firm conclusions.

## Technique, cheating and lateral raises
[28:13] Technique isn't binary — it's a spectrum. A lateral raise drifting into the sagittal plane becomes a front raise and trains the anterior delt instead of the middle delt. The muscle produces the most force when directly opposing gravity, so an externally rotated (thumbs-up) lateral raise puts the anterior delt in line with the pull. Better technique = better chance of maximising your genetic potential, though he won't put a percentage on it.

Cheat reps: some are fine and may eke out extra gains, but **the vast majority of your training should be in good form**. The progressive-overload trap is that deteriorating form means you add weight while shortening range of motion — not actually overloading. Reserve cheating for advanced lifters on a last set; for novices the injury risk makes the cost-benefit bad.

**Internal rotation on lateral raises** [34:25] — Schoenfeld and Lori Koller reviewed the same issue for the upright row and concluded risk is very limited **as long as you stay at or below about 80-90 degrees of elevation**. Impingement is the humeral head knocking into the acromion process and pinching the supraspinatus; repeated instances can fray the tendon. But a 15 lb dumbbell with 10-15 degrees of internal rotation is a small risk next to a 600 lb deadlift. He wouldn't go heavy on these anyway — **rarely below 6-8 reps, usually above 10**.

## Exercise selection and resistance profiles
[40:33] Cables give more even tension than dumbbells (a dumbbell lateral raise has no tension at the bottom and peak tension at the top). Changing modality alters the strength curve, which he speculates has different stimulating effects that may be additive. It won't decide whether you gain 10 lb of muscle, but it can be a finishing touch for a competitive physique. Machines vs. free weights also changes stabiliser demand.

He favours **varying movements** for novelty. Keep core free-weight patterns (squats, rows) in continuously — take them out for months and your kinematics degrade; a machine can be dropped for six months and picked back up with no deficit.

**Squats aren't mandatory.** From a purely quad-specific standpoint he'd be confident there isn't much difference between squats and leg press over years; squats simply recruit far more additional musculature. Fonseca et al. found rotating squat / leg press / lunge in 2-week blocks produced greater growth in the vastus medialis and rectus femoris than squats alone.

## The volume study
[50:19] Three groups, 8 weeks: **6-9, 18-27, and 30-45 sets per muscle per week**. Hypertrophy increased with volume; the highest group gained most in biceps and quads. Context that defuses the "absurd" number: the highest group trained a little over **3 hours a week**, about an hour per session, three times a week; the lowest group trained roughly 13 minutes per session. The study tested a threshold *for a given muscle*, not what total-body volume should be.

- No threshold was found, but he still expects an inverted-U/hormetic curve to appear eventually.
- Lower-body muscles responded better to high volume than upper body — possibly because no single-joint movements were used.
- Unpublished follow-up: the dose-response was partly driven by **fewer poor responders at higher volumes** (3 sets had fewer non-responders than 1; 5 fewer than 3), not by everyone gaining more.
- Practical takeaway: use high volume as a **specialisation block for a lagging muscle**, anecdotally no longer than about 4 weeks, with deloads built in.

**Counting indirect volume** [56:30] — biceps sets in the study included compound pulling. He insists you should count rows and pulldowns toward biceps volume, but doubts the relationship is 1:1 (0.8? 0.6? unknown). Some studies show equal biceps growth from pulldowns alone vs. curls; an unpublished Brazilian collaboration of his found a benefit for single-joint over multi-joint. He still wouldn't train a pro bodybuilder without direct arm work, and thinks doing 15 sets of biceps plus 15 of triceps *after* 15 sets each of chest and back is overkill. The same logic applies to squats and quads — people wrongly assume that one is 1:1.

Before adding volume, Jeff would first check technique, effort level, exercise variation and mind-muscle connection; Schoenfeld agrees those are usually already handled in advanced lifters, leaving volume and load progression.

## The conflicting study
[67:18] Fisher and Steele's lab ran 24 weeks in women comparing 5, 10, 15 and 20 sets/week and found 5-10 sets sufficient to max out hypertrophy — an inverted U with 10 sets on top. Schoenfeld's possible explanations: (1) his own 8-week study was a short-term replication of the Radaelli/Simão 6-month Navy study, which found *more* than double or triple upper-body growth at high volume; (2) Cody Haun's study (with Mike Roberts) also saw growth rising to around 20 sets and beyond, surviving a correction for intracellular/extracellular water; (3) the Fisher/Steele protocol used a **bro split** (push/pull/legs), and James Fisher's own unpublished analysis suggests **more than ~10 sets per muscle per session** is counterproductive — 30 sets should be split across sessions, not crammed into one. Jeff notes 20 sets of quads in one session is plainly hard to recover from and likely contains "junk sets."

On one-set-to-failure minimalism: in his meta-analysis, **14 of 15 studies favoured higher volume** — the probability of that by chance is nearly zero. It also contradicts everything known about individual variability.

## Practical application
- Default to **10-20 sets per muscle per week**, adjusted individually (some do better below 10, some need more than 20).
- Undulate volume: build toward roughly 20 sets in an overreaching phase, then cut back and deload.
- For a lagging muscle, run a **high-volume specialisation block of about 4 weeks**, then reduce.
- Keep **under ~10 sets per muscle per session**; distribute volume across more sessions rather than piling it into a bro split.
- Train most muscles **~2x/week** (Schoenfeld's own current approach), typically with **RIR ~1** and only occasional failure.
- Use drop sets to buy volume cheaply on a later set; use accentuated eccentrics selectively (one-leg negatives, 120-130% 1RM supramaximal lowering with a spotter).
- Rotate exercises and modalities; keep the core free-weight lifts permanently in the program.
- Keep lateral raises and upright rows to **6-8+ reps, usually 10+**, staying at or under ~90 degrees of arm elevation.
- Count indirect volume from compounds, but not as 1:1 with isolation work — and don't stack 30 sets of direct arm work on top of a full chest and back session.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Nearly every specialised technique lacks strong hypertrophy evidence; Schoenfeld repeatedly refuses cookie-cutter prescriptions and says everything must be individualised.
- The high-volume study was **8 weeks with no midpoint measurement** — gains might have been realised by week 4, or begun regressing after.
- There is **no volume data on the back** at all; extrapolating from arms and legs is a judgment call.
- The agonist-antagonist superset finding is unpublished and unreplicated, as is the single-joint-vs-multi-joint biceps result.
- Impingement risk on internally rotated raises is based on biomechanical modelling, not hard outcome data — individual anatomy varies, and anyone with existing shoulder pain should skip it.
- Overtraining in his study was unlikely because total systemic work was modest; a full bodybuilding routine at those per-muscle volumes would be a very different stress.
