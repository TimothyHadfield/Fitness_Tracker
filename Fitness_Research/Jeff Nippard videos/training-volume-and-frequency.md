# How Many Sets and How Often: Volume, Frequency and Splits

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Training Volume and Frequency | Chapter 2: The Fundamentals Series" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7S0NjKYlJ7I)) · 2018-06-30 · 31 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- **10-20 hard sets per muscle per week.** ~10 sets gets you most of the available growth; ~20 sets roughly maxes it out; ~30 sets flirts with overtraining.
- **Train each body part twice per week.** Two sessions beat one even when weekly volume is matched. Three or more may or may not add anything.
- Count only **tough working sets** — no warm-ups, and each set within 3-4 reps of failure.
- Small muscles (biceps, triceps, rear/side delts) need only **4-8 direct isolation sets/week** on top of compound work. Front delts need none.
- If you're still getting stronger, don't add volume. Add sets only when progress stalls.

## Defining volume
[06:41] Volume is total work: in the literature, roughly sets × reps × load. Since progressive overload already handles reps and weight week to week, the practical variable you manage is **number of sets**.

Track **"tough sets"** — working sets taken to within about 3-4 reps of failure. Warm-ups don't count.

## The dose-response curve
[09:46] Hypertrophy rises steeply with early volume, then flattens into diminishing returns, then eventually reverses (overreaching/overtraining) when you exceed what you can recover from.

- **MEV (minimum effective volume)** — practically, the point where extra sets stop buying much extra growth. For most people, around **10 sets per muscle per week**.
- **MRV (maximum recoverable volume)** — the most you can do and still recover. Around **20 sets per muscle per week**.
- **~30 sets/week** is overtraining territory for most and may be counterproductive.

Recreational lifters can sit near MEV; elite/competitive lifters may want to flirt with MRV for the last increment of growth. He notes a defensible argument that accumulating MEV-level sessions over a whole training career gets you to your genetic ceiling anyway — but experts disagree.

## Allocating sets by muscle
[13:49]
- **Big muscles — chest, back, quads, glutes:** 10 (up to 20) sets/week. Squats count toward both quads and glutes.
- **Hamstrings, abs, calves:** could get away with less, but he gives them the same 10 sets since they're common weak points.
- **Triceps, biceps, rear delts, side delts:** get substantial indirect work from pressing and pulling, but not enough to maximise. Add **4-8 direct isolation sets/week** each — not another full 10.
- **Front delts:** no direct work needed; chest and shoulder pressing covers them. No need for front raises.

**Self-check:** are you getting stronger? If yes, keep going and change nothing. If strength has stalled and you're not visibly adding muscle, that's the signal to add sets. Most people shouldn't need to increase volume much in their first year.

## Frequency
[17:24] The literature supports **2× per week per body part over 1× per week**, even when weekly volume is matched. Whether 3× or more is better remains unsettled.

**Mechanism:** resistance training sensitises the muscle to amino acid uptake and produces a spike in muscle protein synthesis; hitting a muscle twice gives two spikes, which compounds into more hypertrophy over time.

**Practical mechanism:** it's easier to accumulate weekly volume across two sessions. Cramming 10-20 chest sets into one session either makes it very long or degrades effort on the later sets. So in practice volumes usually aren't matched — the 2× approach lets you do more good-quality work.

**Wasted sets:** just as weekly volume has a ceiling, so does a single session. Beyond some point in one workout (he guesses ~20 sets for advanced, maybe 6-10 for a beginner) the later sets contribute much less to hypertrophy.

## Five splits, all fitting 10 sets/week (back as the example)
[19:59]
1. **Upper/Lower — his top pick for beginners and early intermediates.** 4 days/week, 3 rest days. 5 back sets per upper day, varied by plane: e.g. day 1 = 3× wide-grip pull-up + 2× dumbbell row; day 2 = 3× barbell row + 2× lat pulldown. Same logic for lower body.
2. **Full body 2×/week.** Only two gym visits and plenty of recovery, but sessions get long since every muscle needs 5 sets. Similar results to upper/lower; good if you don't want frequent gym trips.
3. **Full body 3×/week** — he prefers this to #2. 3× frequency per muscle, shorter sessions: e.g. back = 4 sets pull-ups, 3 sets barbell row, 3 sets cable row/face pull. Downside: individual sessions may not "feel" like much of a workout. Good for beginners with lower recovery capacity.
4. **Bro split** (chest / back / legs / shoulders / arms+abs, weekends off). Only 1× direct frequency, though overlap (chest day hits delts, back day hits biceps) makes real frequency higher than it looks. Problems: too long between stimuli (6 days between leg sessions — more recovery than anyone needs), and wasted sets from cramming volume into one session. Still workable if it's what you'll actually adhere to and you're getting stronger.
5. **Push/Pull/Legs.** Very effective, 2× frequency per muscle, but 6 days/week — he sees it as intermediate-to-advanced; beginners need more than one rest day.

## Practical application
- Start at ~10 tough sets per big muscle per week, split over two sessions (5 + 5), using different exercises in each to cover both movement planes.
- Add 4-8 weekly isolation sets for biceps, triceps and rear/side delts; skip direct front delt work.
- Keep every counted set within 3-4 reps of failure — volume without effort doesn't count.
- Default split: upper/lower, 4 days a week. Full body 2-3×/week if you want fewer or shorter sessions.
- Only push toward 20 sets/week once progress stalls or if you're training at an elite level.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Individual variation is large — some people tolerate far more volume than 20 sets.
- Whether 3×+ per week frequency beats 2× is not established; benefits may come only via the extra volume it enables.
- Whether MEV-level training over a career equals MRV-level training is debated among experts.
- The exact per-session set ceiling where sets become "wasted" is unknown.
- Volume is often called the main driver of hypertrophy; he personally rates progressive overload as the primary driver, with the two closely linked.
