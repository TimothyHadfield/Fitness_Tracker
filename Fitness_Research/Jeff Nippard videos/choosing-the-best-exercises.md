# Exercise Selection: Which Movements Actually Earn a Place

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "What Are The Best Exercises for Muscle and Strength? | Fundamentals Series Ep. 4" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vyiQw-qiv80)) · 2018-08-25 · 18 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Build the program around **compound (multi-joint) movements** that load large amounts of muscle, then fill gaps with isolation work.
- Machines and free weights both belong in a program. Free weights transfer better and train stabilisers; machines are easier to learn and safer to push.
- Minimum coverage: a squat, a deadlift variation, a horizontal pull, a vertical pull, a horizontal press, a vertical press — plus isolation for rear delts, biceps, triceps, hamstrings, calves and abs.
- Certain muscles are genuinely missed by compounds: the **short head of biceps femoris** (needs leg curls) and the **abs** (need loaded crunches and leg raises) — squats and deadlifts do not cover them.
- If you can't feel a movement in the target muscle, swap it for one you can — but master technique on the big lifts first.

## Compound vs isolation
[01:30] Compounds are multi-joint: a bench press works muscles at the elbow (triceps) and at the shoulder (pecs, delts). Isolation is single-joint: a curl acts only at the elbow. Prioritise compounds for efficiency, general strength and athleticism — but he isn't a minimalist; isolation earns its place too.

## Machines vs free weights
[03:02] Both work; the online tribalism is overdone.

**Free weight advantages:**
- Better strength transfer to other exercises.
- More stabiliser involvement. Example: a dumbbell shoulder press demands more rotator cuff stability than a machine press, and recruits more side delt to keep the arm from drifting forward or back.

**Free weight disadvantage:** harder to learn, which can frustrate beginners.

**Machine advantage:** easier and more approachable — a beginner may be better served by a machine shoulder press than by a standing military press. Both stimulate muscle; use them together.

He recommends having a trainer or an experienced friend watch your technique in person — online coaching and YouTube fall short for form feedback.

## Compound movements by body part
[05:40]

**Legs**
- **Squat** — his #1. Excellent quad activation, also good for glutes. Poor hamstring exercise.
- **Hex bar deadlift** — if forced to pick a single lower-body exercise, this would be it: hits hamstrings (unlike the squat), involves upper body musculature, and hits quads more than a conventional deadlift because you sit lower and the bar is centred in the midplane; also loads the glutes. He doesn't usually recommend it only because it's uncommon and he doesn't do it himself.
- **A deadlift variation** — for hamstrings and glutes. For pure hypertrophy he prefers the **Romanian / straight-leg deadlift** over pulling from the floor: more hamstring involvement, less taxing, more eccentric loading. Technique: push the hips back, keep only a slight knee bend, lower to just below the knees, stand up. For pure strength, use conventional or sumo from the floor.
- **Hip thrust** — best option if the goal is glutes specifically without adding thigh size (squats favour quads over glutes).

**Back**
- One **horizontal pull** first, because it hits the whole back — traps and rhomboids of the mid-back plus the lats. Any row works: barbell, dumbbell, cable.
- One **vertical pull** for more lat emphasis, e.g. lat pulldown.
- As a beginner, don't fuss over grip variations; use full range of motion and a comfortable grip, and cover both pulling planes.

**Chest**
- A **horizontal press** — dumbbell or barbell, it doesn't matter much. Pick one you can do safely, comfortably and consistently, and get progressively stronger at it.
- A **fly** is nearly the same movement pattern with slightly different shoulder rotation, and you can load the press heavier — so the press wins on efficiency. Add a fly (an isolation movement) as optional accessory work if your chest is lagging.

**Shoulders**
- A **vertical press**, ideally with **dumbbells** — activation is higher across the whole delt and notably higher in the side delt, which contributes to a capped, X-frame look. A machine press is acceptable if it's easier for you.

## Isolation movements
[11:16]
- **Rear delts:** worked by horizontal pulls but only as a secondary contributor, never the prime mover — so isolate. His #1 is the **reverse pec deck**; bent-over reverse flyes with cables or dumbbells work too.
- **Biceps:** active in both horizontal and vertical pulls, but as assistance muscles. Add **a curl** — pick one that's comfortable and safe.
- **Triceps:** active in all pressing but not maximally. Use a **pressdown** (arms at sides, bar or rope) and an **overhead extension** — the two heads show slightly different activation patterns, so he includes both on different days, though this is splitting hairs. Any triceps isolation covers the bases.
- **Hamstrings:** one of the four hamstring muscles, the **short head of biceps femoris, does not cross the hip joint**, so deadlift variations don't activate it at all. To maximise the hamstrings you need a knee-flexion movement — **leg curl or glute-ham raise**. It's a small muscle, so this is recommended rather than mandatory.
- **Calves:** **standing calf raise** with a **1-2 second pause at the bottom** of every rep to dissipate elastic energy and momentum. The standing raise covers both gastrocnemius and soleus. For stubborn calves add a **seated calf raise** (more soleus — the larger of the two muscles). Donkey calf raises are a good variety option.
- **Abs:** squats and deadlifts are **not** good ab exercises. Do a **crunch** (upper torso toward the midline) and a **leg raise** (lower torso toward the midline) to cover upper and lower abs, and load the crunch — cable crunch or plate-loaded crunch. For obliques and TVA he likes **planks** (time them and add time week to week) and **anti-rotation work such as Pallof presses**. Compounds do train the core, but a visible six-pack requires direct work.

## Feeling the muscle work
[15:51] If you don't feel a movement in the target muscle — e.g. no lat sensation on wide-grip pull-ups but strong lat activation on a close-grip pulldown — that's a reason to prefer the one you feel. But it isn't the only criterion. On big compounds like squats and deadlifts, master technique first; the biomechanics will take care of activation. Mind-muscle connection matters most on isolation work — rear delts, hamstrings — where other muscles can take over.

## Practical application
A minimum-viable exercise menu: squat + Romanian deadlift (or conventional/sumo if strength-focused) + hip thrust if glutes are a priority; one row + one pulldown; one horizontal press; one dumbbell vertical press; then reverse pec deck, a curl, a pressdown and overhead extension, a leg curl, a paused standing calf raise, and a weighted crunch plus leg raise. Add a fly if the chest lags, a seated calf raise if calves lag.

## Caveats & unknowns
- He hedges on the dumbbell shoulder press side-delt advantage — higher activation "at least in theory".
- Direct hamstring curl work is recommended but the short head is small, so he doesn't call it required.
- Machine vs free weight is presented as complementary, not a ranking; the choice can come down to what a beginner will actually do.
- Grip and exercise micro-variations are deliberately deferred as advanced material.
