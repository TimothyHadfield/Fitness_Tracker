# Good Morning Technique: Glutes, Hamstrings and Squat Carryover

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "HOW TO DO THE GOOD MORNING EXERCISE: Build Your Glutes, Hamstrings And Squat With Perfect Technique" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f23vXjoG2e8)) · 2019-08-06 · 7 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Trains glutes and hamstrings through hip extension, plus a heavy isometric demand on the lumbar and thoracic erectors because the bar sits on the back.
- Load it in the **6-12 rep range**, and even when advanced don't exceed roughly **40-50% of your squat max** — heavier turns it into a "squat morning."
- Set up exactly like a low-bar back squat; the bar on the rear-delt shelf is what gives it squat carryover that an RDL doesn't have.
- Break at the hips first with near-vertical shins; the bar travels forward, ending out in front of the toes at the bottom.
- Depth is limited by hamstring flexibility and hip mobility — stop before the lower back rounds, whether that's parallel or short of it.

## Anatomy and why the exercise is unique [00:03]
Primary work is hip extension by the glutes and hamstrings. Simultaneously the lumbar erectors contract isometrically to hold a neutral spine, and because the bar rests on the upper back the thoracic erectors work isometrically to hold extension. So although it's usually framed as a posterior-chain movement, it hammers the back hard as well.

From the side view the good morning creates a far greater lever arm at the hips and low back than the squat does, which is exactly what makes it such a strong strengthening exercise for those muscles — and exactly why the torque demand means you must start light and let those tissues adapt.

The movement pattern develops hamstrings and glutes much like a Romanian deadlift, but because the bar is loaded on the back rather than held in the hands, it has far more carryover to the raw squat. Nippard calls it one of the most underrated exercises available.

## Loading [01:03]
- **6-12 reps**, moderate weight.
- Going too heavy makes the knees bend excessively, converting the lift into a squat and taking the hamstrings out.
- New to the movement: load far, far lighter than you squat.
- Even advanced: generally stay under **40-50% of squat max**.
- Nail technique before chasing load.

## Setup [01:34]
- Bar in the rack at about armpit height.
- Grip slightly wider than shoulder width. Too narrow causes shoulder pain or tightness; too wide loses upper-back tightness under load.
- **Low-bar position**, resting on the rear delts. It feels like it will slide at first, but with a tight upper back the rear delts form a shelf the bar sinks into and it won't move.
- Hands go *above* the bar with the weight resting fully on the back. A common error (illustrated in the video from Mark Rippetoe's *Starting Strength*) is letting the bar rest in the hands, straining wrists and elbows.
- High-bar on the traps can work but tends to let the bar creep forward toward the neck — less safe.
- Stand up by pushing the hips forward, take three steps back, feet even. Stance at least wider than shoulder width with about **10-15 degrees of toe flare**; use what's comfortable.
- Even pressure across the whole foot — not shifted onto toes or heels.
- Head slightly extended, eyes straight ahead. Elbows pointing slightly back, not directly under the bar (that causes wrist and elbow pain).

## Bracing and execution [03:37]
1. Big breath into the gut, pushing air out against the belt if you wear one, deliberately bracing the lumbar erectors and maintaining the natural lordotic curve.
2. Keep the upper back extended by driving the back up into the bar before descending.
3. Initiate the eccentric by pushing the hips straight back, shins as close to vertical as possible. Unlike a high-bar squat where knees and hips break together, here you break at the hips first and only allow a slight knee bend as the bar comes down and forward.
4. Think of the descent as identical to an RDL — hips back, strong hamstring stretch.
5. Unlike the squat, the bar does *not* stay over midfoot: it travels forward until, at the bottom, it is out in front of the toes.
6. Descend to the point just before your spine would begin to round, then reverse by driving the hips forward while simultaneously driving the back up into the bar.
7. Through the concentric keep the shoulder blades retracted and the spine neutral.

Depth is individual. Some can reach 90 degrees / torso parallel to the floor; others should stop short to avoid lumbar rounding. Nippard cuts his own just shy of parallel, partly because of a lower back issue he was recovering from at the time.

## Common errors [05:09]
- **Too much knee bend** — the most common fault. It converts the lift into a squat pattern and strips out the hamstrings. Some knee bend is fine (don't lock out fully), but the point of the exercise is that the squat is a poor hamstring builder and the good morning, done with relatively straight legs, is a good one.
- **Lack of upper-body tightness** — if the bar slides up or down your back you aren't mashing it into the rear-delt shelf.
- **Not knowing where to stop** — set the safety pins in the rack, start from the bottom on the pins, and let them cap your range of motion while you learn the depth.

## Practical application
Use it as an RDL substitute when you also want squat carryover, or as squat assistance. 6-12 reps, capped near 40-50% of squat max, hips-first descent, depth set by where your back starts to round, pins as a depth guide while learning.

## Caveats & unknowns
No studies are cited in this video; the reasoning is biomechanical. The depth recommendation is explicitly individual and mobility-limited, and Nippard notes he personally restricts his own range because of an existing lower back injury.
