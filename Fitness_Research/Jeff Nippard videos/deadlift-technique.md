# Conventional Deadlift: Full Technique Guide (Setup, Pull, Lockout, Descent)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Build A Bigger Deadlift With Perfect Technique (Conventional Form)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VL5Ab0T07e4)) · 2019-02-19 · 8 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Conventional and sumo are biomechanically similar; the meaningful difference is that sumo is harder on the quads and conventional is harder on the spinal erectors, with hands outside the legs and a more forward-leaning torso.
- Set shins about half an inch behind the bar, bar over the tongue of the shoe, stance slightly narrower than shoulder width, toes straight ahead.
- Pull the slack out of the bar before the plates move — never grip and rip.
- Be explosive off the floor with conventional (unlike sumo, where patience off the floor pays), because lockout is the hard part.
- Load heavy and low-rep, and train it only once or twice a week even when advanced — the recovery cost is high.

## Conventional vs sumo [00:33]
Muscle activation and biomechanics are largely the same. Setup differs in hand placement: sumo hands inside the legs, conventional hands outside. Consequences: conventional is more hip dominant with the torso leaning further forward; sumo puts the hips closer to the bar and the lifter more upright. In both, the knees are far less bent than at the bottom of a squat, so calling sumo a "squat-style deadlift" is wrong — every stance makes the deadlift a hip-dominant movement.

From his coaching experience, shorter and lighter lifters tend to be stronger with sumo, taller and heavier lifters with conventional. The only real way to decide is to learn both over time and give one an honest full training cycle.

## Muscles worked [01:34]
Simultaneous hip extension (glutes and hamstrings) and knee extension (quadriceps). The spinal erectors are taxed more than in sumo, holding a stronger isometric spinal extension. The lats and traps work isometrically to stabilise the shoulder and scapula.

**Loading:** relatively heavy in a low rep zone — roughly 5-10 reps for hypertrophy, 1-5 reps for strength. Overload capacity is extremely high. Cap frequency at once or twice a week; it is among the most physically and mentally draining lifts with a high recovery cost.

## Equipment [02:35]
None of this is required — it just helps performance.
- Flat-soled shoes (Chuck Taylors) or deadlift slippers.
- High socks to avoid shin scrapes.
- A 10 mm lever belt to increase torso rigidity and force transfer.

## Setup [02:35]
- Stand with the shins about half an inch behind the bar, bar roughly over the tongue of the shoe.
- Stance slightly closer than shoulder width. This minimises the distance the bar has to travel and stops the hands from having to sit too wide to clear the shins.
- Unlike sumo, do not flare the toes — feet point straight ahead.
- Get down to the bar by setting the hips back as in a stiff-leg deadlift; once the hips can't go back further, let the knees drift forward until the shins lightly touch the bar without knocking it forward.
- Grip with the arms hanging straight down at shoulder width so the hands land just outside the shins.
- Dig the bar into the base of the hand and drive the pinky into the bar to reduce grip fatigue.
- Pack the lats by thinking about pulling the bar in close to you — this stops the bar drifting out in front during the pull.
- Pull the lower back into a neutral position, creating a stretch in the hamstrings, and hold that flat lower back for the whole lift. Some thoracic (upper back) rounding is more acceptable and less dangerous, but if you start with a slightly rounded thoracic spine, don't let it round further as you pull.
- With the hips still high and anteriorly tilted, take a deep breath into the gut.
- Check: you should be able to draw a straight line connecting the scapula, the middle of the bar and the middle of the foot.

## Grip options [03:35]
- **Alternate (over-under):** lets you pull more weight, but can create imbalances if you never switch which hand supinates. This is what Nippard personally uses.
- **Double overhand:** probably the safest, but limits true strength potential because grip gives out first.
- **Hook grip:** wedge the thumb between the fingers and the bar; effective but with a painful adjustment period.
- **Straps:** allow double overhand on heavy work, but do at least one heavy set per session without them so raw grip strength doesn't suffer.

No grip is perfect.

## Execution [05:06]
**Initiate.** Pull the slack out of the bar so it bends slightly under your grip just before the plates leave the floor. Do not grip and rip or yank the bar — you'll slam into the weight of the plates before they move, wasting energy and losing position.

**Pull.** In powerlifting circles it's common knowledge that the sumo pull is slower off the floor while conventional is fast off the floor but harder at lockout. So coaches tell sumo pullers to be patient off the floor, while conventional pullers should be explosive to exploit the "rubber band" effect and carry that momentum to lockout. Lead with the chest up, focus mainly on driving the hips forward while holding isometric contractions: in the lumbar spine to prevent lower back rounding, at the shoulder blades to prevent upper back rounding, and at the shoulder to keep the bar tight against the legs. The bar moves in a perfectly straight line over the middle of the foot, dragging up the shins.

**Lockout.** This is the hardest part of the conventional pull because the bar travels further off the ground. To break the sticking point, force the hips forward and get the chest up — you often need to "unroll" the upper back to lock the shoulders out. You're locked out when knees and hips are straight and the chest is lifted with the shoulders slightly back. Do not overdo it: no excessive backward lean, no lumbar hyperextension, no squeezing the shoulder blades together, no shrugging the bar.

**Descent.** Either let the bar fall fairly freely with your hands guiding it, or resist slightly for about a one-second negative, which helps the plates land in the right spot as the bar tracks down the legs. Set the hips back first with straight knees — exactly like a stiff-leg deadlift — and only once the bar clears the knees let the knees bend and drop the bar straight down against the shins. Do not push the bar out in front of you to clear the knees; that destroys your position. He calls hips-back-first absolutely crucial for conventional in order to hold a straight bar path over mid-foot.

**Between reps.** Let the plates come to a full and complete reset on the floor — no bouncing. Then restart the cycle: breathe in, pull the slack out, drive the hips forward, lock out, set the hips back, follow the bar back down in a straight line.

## Practical application
Shins half an inch behind the bar, feet straight and slightly inside shoulder width, hands just outside the shins with the lats packed and the lower back neutral. Brace, pull the slack out, then pull explosively with the bar dragging up the shins over mid-foot. Finish with the hips, not a lean-back. Reset each rep from a dead stop. Program it heavy, once or twice a week, 1-5 reps for strength or 5-10 for size.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The sumo-for-shorter-lifters / conventional-for-taller-lifters pattern is his coaching observation of an average, not a rule — he explicitly says the only way to know is to train both.
- Equipment (belt, flat shoes, straps, high socks) is performance aid, not a requirement.
- Note: at [02:05] the transcript's stated rep ranges appear garbled (both are described as size); the clear intent from the rest of the video is heavier/lower reps for strength and 5-10 for hypertrophy.
