# Why Clean Eating Fails: Flexible Dieting For Fat Loss

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Why You Shouldn't Eat Clean: How To Lose Fat More Effectively" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ytN366VCGls)) · 2020-08-16 · 10 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Fat loss comes down to a **net caloric deficit over time**, plus adequate protein. There are no magic fat-burning or fat-storing foods.
- **Flexible dieting beats rigid clean eating** for long-term results — better adherence, less binge eating, better one-year weight loss and better three-year maintenance.
- "Clean" has no coherent definition, and the inconsistencies (protein powder is clean, white potatoes aren't) show it's arbitrary.
- Frame it as **including nutrient-dense foods rather than excluding "bad" ones**. No food, at any amount or frequency, has a measurable negative effect on its own — junk food is only a problem when it dominates the diet.
- Rigid, dichotomous food rules are linked to **orthorexia, disordered eating and binge eating**.

## How popular it still is
[00:00] A survey of over a thousand people aged 14-24, covered in the **MASS Research Review**, found **88% viewed clean eating positively** (only 12% negative), and **71% saw it as entirely healthy with no unhealthy or harmful features**.

## Problem 1 — nobody can define it
[01:01] A typical bodybuilder's clean list runs to six or seven foods: chicken, egg whites, brown rice, sweet potato, oatmeal, protein powder. Even that short list contradicts itself. "Unprocessed, whole, natural" is supposedly the criterion, yet **protein powder** — highly processed, refined and synthetic — always makes the cut, and oatmeal is extensively processed too.

Brown rice over white rice at least has a rationale: milling removes bran and germ, stripping some B vitamins and fibre. But as the MASS write-up points out, there's no defensible reason **sweet potatoes** are clean and **white potatoes** aren't — neither is milled or processed. The distinction seems to come from white simply resembling white rice and white bread.

In fact white potatoes may be one of the best fat-loss foods available: one study found **potatoes were by far the most satiating** of the common carbohydrate sources tested.

Definitions also depend entirely on the diet camp: keto says all carbs are dirty, paleo bans grains and dairy but allows meat and veg. As **Dr Helms** puts it in the MASS review, if you tried to eat clean by every group's definition you'd have **an empty plate**. Without a clear definition you can't build guidelines on sound nutritional principles.

## Problem 2 — needlessly inconvenient
[02:33] True commitment to clean eating means skipping special occasions and meals out because the menu is off-limits. That's a real cost for no benefit, because you'll lose fat just as well eating flexibly — including some processed junk food — as long as you're in the **same net caloric deficit** and eating **enough protein**.

## Problem 3 — worse for long-term fat loss
[03:33] A **2012 study** gave two groups the same diet except one group was forbidden bread. After **16 weeks both groups lost the same amount of fat**, but the no-bread group had **far more dropouts** — they couldn't adhere. The more foods you forbid, the harder consistent adherence becomes.

A study comparing **flexible control vs rigid control** reinforces this. *Flexible control* means flexible meal scheduling, a flexible weight-loss timeline, and a **non-dichotomous view of food** (no good/bad, clean/dirty). *Rigid control* means fixed timelines, dieting as fast as possible, and foods sorted into allowed and forbidden. Flexible control was associated with **lower BMI, less binge eating and better weight loss over a one-year program**. The same researchers' **three-year follow-up** found flexible control also produced **better maintenance of the weight loss**.

The anecdotal case agrees: the strictest clean eaters are pre-contest bodybuilders, who almost without exception show **cyclical, unsustainable** fat loss — lean for a few weeks before a show, higher body fat the rest of the year.

## Problem 4 — disordered eating risk
[05:05] If some foods are good, others must be bad — and that black-and-white thinking is where it goes wrong psychologically. Research links it to **orthorexia** (obsession with eating only "clean" or "healthy" foods) and to more severe eating disorders.

- **Stewart and colleagues, 2002:** rigid dieting strategies — but *not* flexible ones — were associated with eating disorder symptoms.
- **A 2020 paper** in over 1,300 subjects found **inflexible eating beliefs and high rigid restraint** were more likely to be associated with **recurrent binge eating**.

Flexible dieting / IIFYM isn't automatic immunity — squeezing a Pop-Tart into your carbs doesn't inoculate you. But simply accepting that **there is no such thing as a bad food** is a good starting point. As the MASS review states: no food, independent of amount or frequency consumed, has a measurable negative effect on you. Junk foods that are high-calorie, highly processed, highly palatable and low in micronutrients are **only problematic if they dominate your diet**.

## Problem 5 — it may not even be healthier
[06:35] Some foods do promote health (fruits, vegetables) and some cause problems in excess (ice cream, chips) — but **the dose makes the poison**. Even water kills at a high enough dose. What matters is how much junk you eat and what the rest of your diet looks like.

Eliminating enough foods creates **nutrient deficiencies**. Studies from **Kleiner and colleagues** found male bodybuilders getting only **46% of the RDA for vitamin D**, and women hitting just **52% for calcium**, while also being deficient in **zinc, copper and chromium**.

## Practical application
[07:37] Following Helms' framing: **focus on including nutrient-dense foods rather than excluding bad ones**. Make a real effort to eat more fruits, vegetables, grains and healthy fats — but don't feel obliged to eliminate junk food or any specific food, whether your goal is health, fat loss or both.

For fat loss, the mechanism is a **caloric deficit over time**, with **adequate protein** as the secondary lever. Every diet that has ever produced fat loss shares that one feature. Fat loss gets mystified because mystery is marketable.

Options for hitting the deficit:
- **Tracking** calories, protein, and optionally macros (he periodically uses MyFitnessPal to check he's in the right ballpark). Tracking itself doesn't cause fat loss and there are no magic macros — it's just a way of confirming your intake matches your goal, while letting you fit in foods a clean-eating plan would forbid.
- **Auto-regulation** works just as well or better for some people, without the bother. Three points of focus: (1) prioritise nutrient-dense, minimally processed whole foods **without eliminating anything**; (2) be **consistent** — similar meals at similar times — while staying relaxed enough that missing a meal or eating something different occasionally doesn't matter; (3) track progress with a **body scale and progress photos**.

## Caveats & unknowns
He opens by conceding nutrition is individual — if clean eating works for you and you can stick to it happily, that's fine, and not every problem listed will apply to everyone. Flexible dieting is explicitly not a guarantee against disordered eating. The auto-regulated approach is only sketched here; he says it needs a full video.
