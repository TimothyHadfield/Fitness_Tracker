# Meal Frequency, Breakfast, Protein and Green Tea: A Response to Dr. Mark Hyman

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "5 Simple Steps THAT WON'T Burn Fat Without Eating Less - A Response to Dr. Mark Hyman" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P_p4b8KfWHY)) · 2015-01-23 · 7 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- "Counting chemicals instead of calories" is incoherent — everything in the physical universe is chemicals, and anything is toxic at a high enough dose.
- **Meal frequency does not affect weight status or body composition.** A 2009 systematic review across 60 studies found no association.
- **Eating late at night and skipping breakfast are not fat-loss problems.** Energy balance over time is what determines weight change.
- **High protein does raise thermogenesis and blunt appetite** — but it's a modest effect, and protein without a calorie deficit still means weight gain. Appetite suppression is the bigger practical lever.
- **Green tea catechins produce only a slight and unreliable increase in fat oxidation**; the antioxidant hype is overblown.

## Two meta-lessons Nippard draws
1. **Medical doctors are not automatically authorities on nutrition and fitness.** MD credentials don't transfer to this domain.
2. **Follow the money trail.** Be especially sceptical of anyone claiming secret or special knowledge they'll sell you.

## Tip 1: "Eat more frequently for a faster, hotter metabolism"
False. A **2009 systematic review referencing 60 studies** concluded there is strong evidence of **no association between eating frequency and weight status**, and the limited available evidence suggests **no association between body composition and eating frequency**.

Hyman also dismisses calories-in-calories-out out of hand. Nippard quotes the **2004 landmark paper "Is a calorie a calorie?"**: a calorie is a calorie from a purely thermodynamic standpoint, because the human body — like any living organism — cannot create or destroy energy, only convert it between forms.

On late-night eating: a myth. A study on **78 police officers** found **greater weight loss** after a six-month diet where **most carbohydrates were eaten in the evening** rather than at breakfast. Nippard treats even this as noise — over time, energy balance is the major determinant.

On breakfast: a **2014 paper in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition** concluded that a recommendation to eat or skip breakfast for weight loss had **no discernible effect on weight loss in free-living adults attempting to lose weight**.

**The one frequency caveat he grants:** eating **3 to 5 high-protein meals per day** does appear beneficial for lean mass accrual and building muscle compared with a lower-frequency diet — he points to the research of **Dr. Stuart Phillips** on this.

## Tip 2: "Protein increases fat burning and controls appetite"
Directionally correct but massively exaggerated. A high-protein diet **does** increase thermogenesis and does mean burning more calories per day than a low-protein diet. But it's not a miracle cure: **raise protein without lowering overall calories and you will gain weight.** The more important mechanism is **appetite suppression** — people demonstrably eat less when they raise protein. Protein is also good for building muscle, which Hyman never mentions.

Hyman's biggest error in this tip is a bad extrapolation: he cites a study where raising protein to **36% of daily calories** caused an extra **71 kcal/day** burned, then converts that to **7.4 lb of weight loss over a year**. Nippard's objection: where does the extrapolation stop? By that arithmetic you'd lose 21 lb over three years and 210 lb over thirty. Linear extrapolation of a small daily energy difference has no real-world application.

Hyman's associated claims that low-fat diets don't work, and that low-carb diets work but are unhealthy and cause haemorrhoids, are dismissed: **any diet produces weight loss if it puts you in a calorie deficit.**

## Tip 3: "Green tea turns up your fat-burning thermostat"
Hyman cites a study where supplementing **catechins** (green tea compounds) without changing caloric intake increased thermogenesis. Nippard again calls this over-weighting a small thermogenic effect, and cites **examine.com**'s conclusion: there appears to be a **slight but unreliable increase in fat oxidation** associated with green tea catechin consumption.

On the antioxidant claims — that catechins prevent free-radical damage to metabolism (presumably meaning oxidative damage to mitochondrial function) — Nippard makes three points:
- The body produces **its own antioxidants** to defend against reactive oxygen species.
- **Oxidative stress isn't inherently bad.** For example, **heat shock proteins**, which activate protective cellular functions, are **inhibited by antioxidants**.
- He quotes neurologist **Dr. Steven Novella** (transcript garbles the surname as "Nolla"): antioxidants may one day matter for treating certain diseases or routine health maintenance, but there is **insufficient evidence to make confident predictions or specific recommendations** — and regarding the marketing hype for antioxidant products, the clear recommendation is **healthy skepticism**.

## The "chemicals" framing
The video was triggered by Hyman's post that "too many people are counting calories and not enough are counting chemicals." Nippard's reply: literally everything physical is made of chemicals — he shows the full chemical constituent list of all-natural blueberries — and **anything is toxic at a sufficient dose**. Chemicals are not bad in themselves. (See the companion note on the appeal to nature.)

## Practical application
- Set total calories first; meal timing and frequency are downstream details.
- Eat whenever suits you — late-night carbs and skipping breakfast are both fine for fat loss.
- Do use **3-5 high-protein meals per day** if muscle gain is a goal (per Stuart Phillips' work).
- Raise protein for appetite control and muscle, not as a metabolic trick — and only alongside a deficit if fat loss is the goal.
- Treat green tea and antioxidant supplements as marginal at best; don't budget them into a fat-loss plan.
- When someone converts a small daily calorie difference into a dramatic annual weight figure, distrust the arithmetic.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Several studies are cited by year and journal only, without author names.
- The 78-police-officer carb-timing study is presented as a counter-example rather than as a strong recommendation — Nippard explicitly calls it noise relative to energy balance.
- The antioxidant position is that evidence is currently insufficient, not that antioxidants are useless.
