# Lengthened Partials vs Full Range of Motion in Trained Lifters: Jeff Nippard's Own Study

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Everyone Thinks I'm Wrong About Training" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qxl5mL7gzlg)) · 2024-09-24 · 13 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- In the first range-of-motion study ever run on highly experienced lifters (avg 4.9 years training, 30 subjects, 8 weeks), lengthened partials and full ROM produced **statistically identical growth** — 5.9% vs 6.6% for one measure, no significant difference for biceps either.
- Half reps done in the stretched half work just as well as full reps. That means the squeeze/shortened half contributes very little to hypertrophy.
- Practical read: use whichever you prefer. If full ROM on an exercise is awkward or painful, lengthened partials get you the same result for half the range.
- Lengthened partials must be done with the same technique discipline as full reps: controlled eccentric, brief pause in the stretch, explosive concentric. They are not cheat reps.
- This tempers, but does not overturn, the stretch-focus case — 9 of 10 prior studies still favoured the lengthened position, mostly in less-trained subjects.

## Why the study was run
[00:30] Jeff had been publicly arguing for at least a year that the stretched part of a lift matters more than the contracted part, and it's one of three criteria (high tension + high stretch) he uses to rank exercises. Critics argued the supporting studies were nearly all on untrained lifters or beginners, so the finding might not generalise.

The specific skeptic argument: lengthened partials work only via **sarcomerogenesis** (sarcomere addition — muscle growing longer rather than thicker), which appears to happen mainly in beginners and stops being possible after a few months of training. If true, lengthened partials would be a beginner tool only.

## Prior evidence going in
[02:01]
- **5 studies comparing shortened partials to lengthened partials:** lengthened partials caused significantly more growth **every single time (5/5)**.
- **5 studies comparing lengthened partials to full range of motion:** lengthened partials won **4 out of 5 times**.

## Study design
- 30 subjects, average 4.9 years lifting experience — deliberately not beginners.
- **Within-subject unilateral design:** each subject trained one side of their body with full ROM and the other side with lengthened partials only. Every subject is their own control, which removes the risk in a two-group design that one genetic outlier skews a group average.
- 8 weeks. Four sets per exercise, mixing a moderate rep range with a slightly higher one.
- **Every set taken to muscular failure**, with a research team supervising each set. Subjects reported training harder in the study than they did outside it.
- Growth measured by ultrasound.
- Run in New York, with Dr Wolf (a leading range-of-motion researcher) and Dr Pak on the research team.

Lengthened-partial instruction given to subjects: identical technique to full reps — control the negative, brief pause in the full stretch, explosive positive — just with the range cut in half. The common error is treating partials as loose, jerky, bouncy cheat reps.

## Results
[08:09] Full ROM 6.6% vs lengthened partials 5.9% — **not statistically significant**. Biceps visually favoured lengthened partials on the graph but also showed no significant difference. Accounting for random variation, genetics and measurement error, neither condition beat the other.

## What Jeff concluded
[09:12] Two valid readings: either lengthened partials aren't better than full ROM (so keep doing full ROM if you like it), or **you can get full-ROM gains from half the range of motion**, as long as it's the stretched half.

Counterintuitively, the null result made him use lengthened partials *more*. His reasoning: on some exercises the full squeeze is harder or more annoying to execute, so if cutting it out costs nothing, why do it? Subjects eliminated the squeeze entirely on every exercise and still matched full ROM — which means the shortened half can't contribute much to growth, or full ROM would have won.

He also says the study genuinely tempers the stretch hype. He still believes the stretch is the most important part of the range for most muscles, but focusing on it exclusively doesn't supercharge gains in experienced lifters.

[10:45] Dr Wolf's own reaction: a single null study should shift your prior only slightly. He still bets on lengthened partials for both beginners and advanced lifters, just by a smaller margin than before, and thinks the answer likely depends on which exercise you're doing.

Notably, Dr Wolf had been training with **lengthened partials exclusively for a year** — not a single full-ROM rep — before the results came in.

## Practical application
- Do lengthened partials with real technique: controlled eccentric, pause in the stretch, explosive concentric, range cut roughly in half at the stretched end.
- If you like full ROM, keep it — it's clearly effective.
- Substitute lengthened partials on exercises where the full squeeze is uncomfortable, awkward, or where the top of the movement is easy anyway.
- If you skip the stretch entirely, you're probably leaving growth on the table.
- Calves and hamstrings are the muscles where the earlier studies found the biggest lengthened-partial advantages — reasonable places to bias toward partials.

## Caveats & unknowns
This is one 8-week study; a single null result shouldn't overturn nine prior studies favouring the stretched position. Effects may be exercise-specific and muscle-specific. The subject pool was experienced lifters, so the beginner literature still stands on its own. Jeff is an author on the study, which is a conflict of interest worth noting, though he had the results withheld from him until all statistics were finalised.
