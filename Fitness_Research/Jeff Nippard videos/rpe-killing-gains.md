# Is RPE Killing Your Gains? Five Criticisms Answered

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Is RPE Actually Killing Your Gains? (Response to Athlean-X & Critics)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AXEONcNhL1E)) · 2020-12-03 · 12 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- RPE is just a way of *counting* effort, exactly like counting sets and reps. Tracking it doesn't make you train less hard — in practice it makes people train harder, because it exposes how far short of failure they were stopping.
- The growth-stimulating intensity threshold sits around **RPE 7-8** depending on the exercise and your experience. Below that you're likely leaving growth on the table.
- Beginners *can* use RPE; they just need to calibrate it. Use the "RPE test set" (below).
- Training all the way to failure has **not** been shown to beat stopping short for hypertrophy — the literature is mixed, and failure carries a fatigue cost.
- Well-trained lifters gauge RPE accurately: in a recent study, subjects calling RPE 9 on squat/bench/deadlift were off by only **0.6 of a rep** on average.

## What RPE means
A 1-10 rating of how hard a set was. RPE 8 = you believe two more reps were available. RPE 10 = you could not have completed another rep with good form. It answers the question sets-and-reps can't: *how hard should this set be?* "As hard as possible" or "harder than last time" isn't a usable instruction.

## Criticism 1: fine for intermediates, useless for beginners
[01:31] Nippard concedes RPE is uncontroversially useful for intermediate/advanced lifters — it's near-universal in drug-tested powerlifting. The beginner objection is that novices misjudge it. There's real evidence for that worry: a **2017 study** asked subjects to take their usual 10-rep weight to true failure, and **47% managed 16 or more reps** ([Barbosa-Netto 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29112055/)).

His counter: that finding shows most gym-goers under-push, not that the tool is broken. Nobody wakes up one day as an intermediate with accurate RPE — you learn it by reflecting on sets. Mike Israetel's point, quoted here: even if beginners misjudge RPE it barely matters, because the intensity threshold for a beginner is so low. Novices progress best on excellent technique and small incremental strength gains, *not* on training close to failure. You earn the right to train near failure once technique is solid.

**The RPE test set:** on an exercise you can fail safely (e.g. incline dumbbell press), when you think two reps are left, say out loud "I'm at RPE 8" — then push to failure. Two more reps = your rating was accurate. Three or four more = you're overestimating and need to push harder.

## Criticism 2: people "hide behind science" to be lazy
[04:31] Agreed in principle that some lifters fuss over details instead of pushing — but the fix is to remind them of the ~RPE 7-8 threshold, not to blame science. The literature as a whole says most people aren't going hard *enough*.

## Criticism 3: studies are done on untrained subjects / aren't hardcore
[06:02] Study protocols are usually harder than people assume. Example: **Bjørnsen and colleagues (Norway)** had national-level powerlifters do blood-flow-restricted front squats — 4 sets at 30% 1RM with the first and last sets to complete failure, legs occluded. Researcher **Chris Barakat** notes that most studies simply take every set to failure to standardise intensity, and subjects routinely report never having trained that hard, especially on lower body, partly because six people are training competitively in the lab at once.

On subject training status: many studies do recruit trained lifters, and most people overestimate how advanced they are. Nothing fundamental changes physiologically between beginner and intermediate — the rate of progress just slows. Studying beginners is actually useful because effects are detectable in 8 weeks; in near-genetic-ceiling pro naturals, no study could detect a difference in a reasonable timeframe. Science is a tool alongside coaching experience, not a replacement for it.

## Criticism 4: RPE isn't accurate anyway
[09:35] A recent study (lead author's name is garbled in the captions, so not stated here) had 10 well-trained lifters call out when they hit RPE 6 (4 reps left) and RPE 9 on squat, bench and deadlift. Average error: **1 rep at RPE 6, 0.6 reps at RPE 9**. Since bodybuilding rarely uses RPE 6, the RPE 9 result is the relevant one — experienced lifters know when they're one rep out. Accuracy also improves with practice ([Steele 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29204323/)).

## Criticism 5: it's too much information
[10:36] The criticism he sympathises with most. But RPE is intuitive — you're just asking how many more reps were available, and assuming beginners can't handle a 1-10 scale is patronising. Judge the individual's training IQ and appetite for detail. For a low-interest client, skip the jargon and just ask: "On a scale of one to ten, how hard was that? Could you have pushed harder? Did your technique slip?" After technique, applying appropriate effort is the most important thing to learn in the gym.

## Practical application
- Target **RPE 7-8+** on most hypertrophy work; that's the threshold worth defending.
- Run an RPE test set periodically on a safely-failable exercise to recalibrate.
- Don't take everything to failure — it isn't clearly superior and it costs you on later sessions.
- With beginners or low-interest clients, use the plain-language version of the scale rather than the terminology.

## Caveats & unknowns
Beginner accuracy genuinely is lower, and Nippard doesn't dispute the 2017 data showing widespread under-exertion — he disputes the conclusion drawn from it. The RPE-6 accuracy result (1 rep off) is worse than the RPE-9 result, so far-from-failure ratings are less reliable. Failure training isn't dismissed; it has a place, just not as the default.
