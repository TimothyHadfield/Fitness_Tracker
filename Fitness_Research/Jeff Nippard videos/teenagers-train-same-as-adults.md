# Should Teenagers Train The Same As Adults?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Should Teenagers Train The Same As Adults? (Science Based)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H_GrjIZkxFI)) · 2019-03-06 · 4 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Lifting does not stunt growth. A 2014 international consensus position stand found no scientific evidence that resistance training harms linear growth in childhood or adolescence or reduces adult height.
- The opposite risk is real: failing to do moderate-to-vigorous weight-bearing activity during growth may predispose someone to long-term bone health problems.
- Injury risk is the more legitimate concern, and it is a supervision and ego problem, not an age problem.
- For teenagers, technique mastery outranks volume, intensity, and progressive overload — at least initially.
- Program by training age and athletic background, not calendar age.

## The two real concerns
The standard worries about teenage lifting are (1) stunted height or growth potential and (2) injury risk. Nippard thinks most people get the first one backwards and underrate the second's actual cause.

## Stunted growth: the evidence
A 2014 international consensus position stand, published under a large number of organisational and regulatory bodies, concluded:

- Failure to participate in moderate-to-vigorous weight-bearing physical activity during these stages of growth may predispose individuals to long-term bone health implications — i.e. *avoiding* external resistance during developing years may itself be harmful.
- No scientific evidence indicates that resistance training has an adverse effect on linear growth during childhood or adolescence, or reduces eventual adult height.

Nippard considers the growth-plate myth blown out of proportion and has a separate full-length video tracing its origin.

## Injury risk
He treats this as the more legitimate of the two. Teenagers are typically newer to weight training and face a strong temptation to load more weight than they can handle. The mitigations are supervision-based:

- Have a spotter available, ideally a more experienced adult.
- Make sure the lifter isn't biting off more than they can chew load-wise.

The same position stand's injury section concluded that weight-bearing exercise may be critical for reducing injury later in life — build strength early and you're off to a good start.

## Program by training age, not birth date
Age alone is the wrong input. A 14- or 15-year-old who has played sports since age five and has strong general athletic ability is likely better suited to an advanced program than a 30-year-old who has never played a sport. Previous training experience and athletic ability should weigh into programming decisions at least as much as age.

## The one real exception for teenagers
Nippard normally emphasises training volume, intensity, and progressive overload as the drivers of muscular gains. For teenagers he makes an exception: set those aside initially and focus on mastering technique. Chasing PRs is tempting at any age and especially so as a teenager, but form comes first — more important than volume, more important than intensity and effort. That is his number one piece of advice.

## Practical application
- Teenagers can train essentially like adults; no special growth-related restrictions are warranted.
- Train under supervision, with a spotter, especially early on.
- Prioritise technique before adding volume, intensity, or load progression.
- Assess a young lifter's athletic and training background to decide how advanced their program should be.
- Resist the pull toward frequent PR attempts.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The position stand is described but the authors are not named in the video.
- No specific set, rep, frequency, or load prescriptions are given for youth training.
- The advice to deprioritise volume and intensity is framed as an initial phase, with no stated timeline for when a teenager should transition to normal progressive overload programming.
