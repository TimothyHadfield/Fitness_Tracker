# The Deck Of Cards Home Workout: Randomised 20-Minute No-Equipment Training

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Ultimate 20 Min At-Home Workout To Build Muscle (No Equipment + Free Program!)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WLrkT4F7tEc)) · 2020-04-19 · 9 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Deal 20 cards from a shuffled deck: 20 cards ≈ 20 minutes, 30 cards ≈ 30 minutes. Each card is one set. Card number + 10 = reps; face cards = 20 reps; aces = 2 minutes rest.
- Suits set the exercise category: spades = push, clubs = pull, hearts = legs, diamonds = isolation (biceps, triceps, abs, calves).
- Hitting the rep target is not the point. Use **intraset feedback** — mid-set, ask whether it's hard enough, and if not make it harder so you finish within 3-5 reps of failure.
- Make sets harder with tempo, not more reps: 2-3 second (up to 5 second) negatives, 1-2 second (up to 5 second) pauses in the stretch, and a hard squeeze at the top.
- Don't rest between most sets. Rest only when consecutive cards hit the same body part, or when you draw an ace. Run these 3-5 times a week.

## Why randomise
[00:00] A 2019 study split subjects into a randomised program (varying reps and exercises) and a fixed program (same reps, same exercises) for eight weeks. Muscle thickness and strength were no different between groups, but the randomised group showed a greater increase in motivation to train. When motivation is the binding constraint, variety buys consistency.

## The rules
- Shuffle, deal 20 cards face down. More volume needed? Deal more cards, at roughly one minute per card.
- Reps = card number + 10. A 7 means 17 reps, a 2 means 12 reps. Face cards count as tens, so 20 reps.
- Aces are rest cards: two minutes.
- Suits: spades push, clubs pull, hearts legs, diamonds isolation.
- Otherwise run continuously with no inter-set rest.

## Warm-up
Five minutes of light cardio to raise core temperature and break out of at-home relaxation mode — jogging on the spot, skipping rope, mini-hoop basketball, jumping jacks. Then full-body dynamic stretching: 5-10 lying thoracic spine rotations, step-throughs, leg swings, arm circles. He stresses not skipping this when training at home.

## The biggest mistake: chasing rep counts
[02:33] Most home trainees pick a number — 10, 20 reps — hit it and stop, never getting close enough to failure to recruit the highest-threshold fibres. You don't need to reach failure, but leaving more than about 3-5 reps in the tank wastes the set's muscle-building potential. Rather than inflating rep counts into the 50s, make each rep harder.

## Worked example
- **7 of hearts → 17 reps walking lunge** (per leg, so 34 total). Harder options: wear a loaded backpack, use dumbbells if available, minimise back-leg assistance by treating each rep as a single-leg squat driving the front heel into the floor, and take longer strides for more hip range of motion and glute involvement.
- **Queen of spades → 20 reps push-up or incline push-up.** Three intensity levers: slow the negative to at least 2-3 seconds, pause 1-2 seconds in the stretched position, actively squeeze the pecs at the top of every rep. If you approach the rep target with 10+ reps still in you, exaggerate hard on the last few — 5 second negative, 5 second pause at the bottom, 5 second pec squeeze at the top. This intraset tempo adjustment gets you near failure without doing 50-60 reps and delivers overload through tempo and mind-muscle connection instead of load.
- **2 of clubs → 12 reps pull.** Doorway pull-up bar (full ROM pull-ups), a tree branch, or banded pulldowns. If form fails before the target, use a band for assistance or just stop at failure and move on.
- **10 of diamonds → 20 reps isolation, e.g. biceps curls.** Gallon jug curls are a legitimate way to overload the biceps — Nippard was sore for two or three days after filming them. Bands or dumbbells substitute fine. If the last reps feel easy: 5 second negatives, maximum squeeze at the top, constant tension by not resting at the bottom. On low cards, add 2 second pauses at the midpoint on both the concentric and eccentric.
- **Ace → 2 minutes rest.**

## Rest periods
[06:05] Best available research says resting longer than one minute between sets for the same body part beats resting under a minute. On this format, though, consecutive cards usually hit different body parts, so legs recover during push sets. Resting between every set at home invites distraction and lost momentum. So: rest only if you genuinely need it between sets hitting the same body part (two cards of the same suit back to back), or when an ace comes up. The continuous pace adds a conditioning component and builds strength endurance for higher-volume gym training later.

## Progressive overload
[07:37] Methodical overload — going from 4 to 6 reps on a squat — is impractical with limited equipment and low motivation. You could add a magazine to the backpack, a cup of water to the jug or one push-up per week, and that would work, but most people won't sustain it. This format is a deliberate temporary substitute: randomness for enjoyment and motivation, balanced by structure from good technique, mind-muscle connection and appropriate exertion via intraset feedback.

## Practical application
Warm up 5 minutes cardio plus dynamic stretching. Deal 20 cards, run them straight through, adjusting tempo mid-set so each set finishes 3-5 reps from failure. Use it 3-5 times a week while gym access is limited, or as a boredom-breaker within a normal routine.

## Caveats & unknowns
The randomisation study found no strength or hypertrophy advantage — only a motivation advantage. Nippard explicitly frames the card workout as a temporary solution for restricted-equipment periods, not a replacement for progressive-overload programming.
