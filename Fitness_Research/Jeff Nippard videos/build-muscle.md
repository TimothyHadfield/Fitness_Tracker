# How Muscle Growth Works, From Basics to Molecular Signalling (5 Levels)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Build Muscle (Explained In 5 Levels)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lu_BObG6dj8)) · 2021-07-22 · 21 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Lift weights, eat protein, and progressively overload. **1.6–2.2 g protein/kg/day** (~0.7–1 g/lb), or **1 g per cm of height** if you're very high in body fat.
- Most sets should stop within **2–3 reps of failure**; effort is the most fundamental variable.
- **10–20 hard sets per body part per week** for most people and most muscles; the volume response is an inverted U, not a straight line.
- Rep range barely matters for hypertrophy from **3 to 30 reps** as long as sets are close to failure. Below ~20% of 1RM growth drops off. Practical default: majority of sets in **6–12 (up to 15)**, with smaller chunks lower for strength and higher for variety.
- **Mechanical tension is the primary driver of hypertrophy.** Muscle damage and metabolic stress are almost certainly not.

## Level 1 — Lift weights and eat protein
[00:31] Weight training signals an adaptive response: the muscle responds to the demand by getting bigger, the way fingertips callus from guitar strings. But signalling isn't enough — you need building blocks, which are amino acids from dietary protein (fish, chicken, meat, dairy, beans, lentils, protein powder). Training tells the muscle to grow; protein supplies what it grows from.

## Level 2 — Progressive overload and protein targets
[01:31] Lifting the same weight for the same reps two years from now won't keep muscle growing. Progressive overload is the gradual increase of stress on the muscle — practically, increasing some training parameter over time.

Example on biceps: 3×10 in week 1, 3×11 in week 2, 3×12 in week 3, then back to 10 reps with more weight in week 4. When weight and reps stall, other overload routes exist: add a set at the same weight and reps, control the eccentric better, improve the mind-muscle connection, or swap in a new exercise and restart the process.

**Protein:** 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day ([Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)). A 165 lb / 75 kg lifter targets ~120–165 g/day. Higher may help in some advanced situations. If you're very high in body fat, grams-per-bodyweight breaks down (a 300 lb person doesn't need 300 g), so use **1 g per cm of height** instead — a 5'9" / 175 cm person targets ~175 g. Spreading protein over **3–5 meals** may be slightly better, but is far less important than hitting the daily total.

Note: training is a much more powerful contributor to muscle growth than diet, by a wide margin.

## Level 3 — The acute training variables
[04:35] **Effort** is the most fundamental. A minority of experts insist every set go to muscular failure (inability to complete another rep despite maximal effort), but mainstream consensus is that failure isn't required — you just need to be close. **Don't leave more than 2–3 reps in the tank on most sets.** Leaving 5+ reps makes sense for recovery, especially when training primarily for strength.

**Volume** was historically sets × reps × load; since around 2015 practitioners have treated it simply as the number of hard sets. More volume is *not* a linear driver of growth — the relationship is an inverted U where extra volume stops helping and eventually becomes counterproductive. The sweet spot differs per person, and many lifters would do better cutting volume and raising effort and execution quality. Working range: **10–20 sets per muscle per week**. Example on a 4-day upper/lower: 6 hard chest sets Monday + 6 Thursday = 12/week.

**Intensity** means load, not effort. As long as sets are taken sufficiently close to failure, hypertrophy is similar across **3 to 30 reps**. Below **20% of 1RM** growth is significantly less — but subjects averaged 67 reps per set at that load, which is impractical anyway ([Lasevicius 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29564973/)). So the 6–12 zone is a myth in the sense that you can grow well outside it. It still has practical value: under 6 reps risks unnecessary joint strain and makes volume harder to accumulate; consistently above 12 reps risks burnout since high-rep sets are harder to recover from. Suggested split: **majority of sets in 6–12 or 15**, smaller portions of lower reps for strength progression and higher reps for stimulative variety.

**Exercise selection** is as much art as science. Multi-joint compounds (squats, presses, rows) give more hypertrophic bang per set by loading large muscle mass and driving efficient total-body strength. Isolation work still matters, particularly for smaller muscles — biceps, rear delts, side delts, abs — that get overshadowed if you train compounds exclusively. There are no mandatory exercises; after mastering basics, experiment to find what suits your mechanics.

**Frequency** likely plays a relatively minor role. The latest studies show similar results between once weekly and 3+ times weekly per muscle ([Schoenfeld 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/)). Nippard still recommends hitting a muscle **at least twice a week**, and prefers upper/lower and full-body splits over bro splits because they usually allow more weekly volume and higher-quality volume per session. Rest periods, tempo and advanced intensity techniques are smaller pieces still.

## Level 4 — What actually drives growth
[10:09] Dr **Brad Schoenfeld**'s 2010 paper (500+ citations) proposed a three-factor model: mechanical tension, muscle damage, metabolic stress ([Schoenfeld 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20847704/)).

**Mechanical tension** is force trying to stretch a muscle fiber — passively when stretched, actively when actin is pulled by myosin heads during contraction. Tension remains **undisputed** as the primary driver of hypertrophy.

**Muscle damage** — microtears and cellular disruption, visible under a microscope as disrupted normal muscle architecture. It may partly explain DOMS, though soreness has multiple causes and isn't fully understood. The claim that damage causes growth is most likely false. Common-sense check: a marathon causes enormous muscle damage and does nothing good for muscle growth. A review from **Wackerhage and colleagues** argued damage in that context would if anything *decrease* growth ([Wackerhage 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30335577/)). **Damas and colleagues** argued damage doesn't correlate with hypertrophy long-term ([Damas 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29282529/)): early in a program damage is huge (hence the soreness of a new routine) with an associated spike in muscle protein synthesis, but the great majority of that synthesis goes to repairing damaged tissue rather than building new tissue. Only once damage subsides does MPS get directed toward hypertrophy. The devil's-advocate case — eccentric training causes more damage *and* more growth — confuses correlation with causation; the likelier explanation is that eccentrics let you load the muscle more heavily, producing more tension. Honestly, muscle damage is poorly understood; even the methods of measuring it are contested, so any damage-based theory of growth is speculative at best.

**Metabolic stress** — accumulation of metabolites like lactate and hydrogen ions plus the muscle hypoxia after training, associated with the pump. Most likely not a driver, because the relationship breaks down repeatedly:
- Shorter rest periods cause more metabolic stress but significantly **less** growth than longer rests.
- Partial ROM causes more metabolic stress but often **less** growth than full ROM.
- Training to failure causes more metabolic stress but **at best equal** growth to stopping a few reps shy.
- Blood flow restriction causes enormous metabolic stress but doesn't enhance hypertrophy on its own, and only helps alongside training when the training is highly suboptimal ([Nyakayiru 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30694972/)). An author of a popular BFR study told Nippard the benefit still traces back to tension — BFR effectively "cheats" you 20 extra reps' worth of the same effect, just arriving sooner.

Practically: apply progressive tension increases to the muscle. Lift with consistent good technique, use the acute variables plus progressive overload to push intramuscular tension up over time, and attend to mind-muscle connection (on appropriate exercises) and eccentric control, both shown to increase intramuscular tension.

## Level 5 — The signalling cascade
[15:46] Dr Tromlin's framing: growth signalling is like dominoes with many branching side-chains, not one linear path — many interconnected pathways with many downstream effects.

1. **Stimulus.** A load heavy enough to create active mechanical tension in the muscle.
2. **Mechanosensors** detect the tension. Which molecules do the sensing isn't settled. Top candidates: **costameres** (protein collections in the fiber membrane that hold fibers together and transfer force during contraction, [Ervasti 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12556452/)); **titin** (the largest protein discovered in humans; runs parallel to the fiber, so plausibly senses stretch — but probably only at long muscle lengths); and **filamin**, which binds actin and is therefore a good tension-sensing candidate. Per the authors of the 2018 review on this: conclusively identifying major hypertrophy stimuli and their sensors is one of the big remaining questions in exercise physiology ([Wackerhage 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30335577/)).
3. **mTOR**, a master regulator of cellular growth generally (also implicated in many cancers for the same reason), receives the signal.
4. mTOR signals the **nucleus**, whose DNA machinery produces **mRNA** — the blueprint for new muscle protein.
5. The blueprint goes to a **ribosome**, which strings amino acids together in **translation** — this step is what "muscle protein synthesis" refers to. Products include contractile proteins plus more ribosomal and mTOR proteins, keeping future anabolic potential high.
6. If synthesis exceeds breakdown, protein balance is positive, new contractile proteins are incorporated into fibers, and you get **myofibrillar hypertrophy**.

**The leucine branch.** Amino acids from dietary protein enter the cell, and **leucine also activates mTOR**. Roughly **3 g of leucine** is needed to stimulate mTOR, found in about **20 g of high-quality protein** (some research shows greater anabolic responses at higher doses). Leucine's stimulative window is much shorter than training's, so maximum mTOR stimulation requires both — leucine complements the training stimulus. The other eight essential amino acids (nine including leucine) go to the ribosome as raw material.

**The testosterone branch.** Manipulating testosterone within the natural range plays a relatively minor role. With injected high-dose testosterone, the hormone crosses the cell membrane and either binds an androgen receptor directly or converts to DHT which binds the receptor; that complex enters the nucleus and drives more blueprint production, raising MPS further.

**Open questions he'd raise with an expert:** sarcoplasmic hypertrophy — growth of non-contractile content (glycogen, organelles, non-contractile proteins) now supported by some solid new evidence, with the speculative possibility that higher-rep, higher-volume training biases toward it; and myonuclear addition — the idea that MPS is limited by the number of nuclei in the fiber, and that satellite cells donating nuclei could raise the ceiling. Both are speculative; we can't even assume nuclei are in fact limiting.

## Practical application
- Protein 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day (or 1 g/cm height if obese), across 3–5 meals, ~20 g quality protein (≈3 g leucine) per feeding.
- Train each muscle at least 2×/week, preferring upper/lower or full-body over bro splits.
- 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week; if you're at the high end and not growing, cut volume and raise execution quality.
- Stop most sets 0–3 reps from failure; never leave more than 2–3 in the tank on hypertrophy work.
- Most sets in 6–12 (to 15) reps, with some lower-rep strength work and some higher-rep sets.
- Progress by adding reps then weight; when stalled, add a set, tighten eccentric control, or rotate the exercise.
- Chase tension, not soreness or pump. Longer rests and full ROM beat short rests and partials.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The mechanosensor step is explicitly unresolved in the literature.
- Muscle damage is poorly understood and even its measurement is contested.
- Sarcoplasmic hypertrophy and myonuclear addition are areas of active debate; the rep-range/sarcoplasmic link is Nippard's own speculation.
- Frequency findings are mixed; the recommendation of 2×/week is a preference layered on top of ambiguous evidence.
- The volume sweet spot varies individually and must be found experimentally.
- A footnote in the video's description qualifies the rest-period/metabolic-stress point.
