# James Krieger on Training Volume, Failure and Rep Ranges

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Interview with James Krieger on Training Volume, Failure & Rep Ranges" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HMmw57is3jk)) · 2016-02-17 · 64 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- **Krieger:** volume shows a dose-response relationship with hypertrophy, and in the (then-unpublished) Krieger & Schoenfeld update the response was still climbing into **double-digit weekly sets per muscle** — double-digit weekly sets produced roughly **30% more hypertrophy** than single-digit.
- **Krieger:** one set to failure definitely builds muscle — that isn't in dispute. The question is cost-benefit: more sets give more growth but not proportionally more, and you have to want to spend the gym time.
- **Krieger:** on a volume-equated basis failure has, at most, a *small* edge. But equating volume misses the point — the reason to stop short of failure is precisely to accumulate more sets.
- **Krieger** defends his 2010 meta-analysis against Fisher's "beware the meta-analysis" critique on statistical grounds: significance thresholds are arbitrary, effect sizes favoured multiple sets in **every one of the eight studies**, and he used a deliberately conservative random-effects model.
- **Krieger:** higher frequency (2–3x/week) beats 1x/week on a volume-equated basis; he was personally running **5–6 sessions per muscle per week, stopping 3–5 reps short of failure**.
- **Both agree:** a bigger muscle is a stronger muscle, but a stronger muscle is not necessarily a bigger one — powerlifting is a poor route to a physique.

## Why weight-training studies keep finding "no difference" [03:34]
Krieger's framing of the whole field. Resistance-training studies typically run ~10 subjects per group, hypertrophy varies enormously between individuals, and the changes being measured are small. Small effect + large variance + tiny n = you will fail to detect real differences.

His worked example: a study comparing roughly **six sets to one set** of biceps curls plus a leg exercise found no statistically significant difference, but the six-set group's mean gains were substantially larger. A power analysis showed you would have needed **close to 100 subjects per group** to reach significance. Resistance-training studies never have that.

That is the motivation for meta-analysis: pool studies to detect a trend no individual study can see.

## The 2010 single-set vs multiple-set meta-analysis [05:37]
- Only **eight studies** met inclusion criteria — a small pool, and Krieger says so.
- Analysed as **sets per exercise**. Roughly **three sets per exercise produced ~40% greater effect sizes than one set**.
- Four to six sets per exercise was larger still but not significantly different from three — only a couple of studies used that range.
- Effect sizes: a standardised statistic that lets you combine different hypertrophy measures (fibre area, muscle circumference, limb lean mass) onto one scale. Downside: it doesn't translate cleanly back into percent gains.
- Dose-response: like drug dosing — increasing dose increases response up to a plateau, and beyond some point can turn negative.

## The updated Krieger & Schoenfeld analysis [09:43]
In review at the time of recording, so Krieger was guarded, but the hints he gave:
- Analysed as **weekly sets per muscle group** rather than sets per exercise — he considers this the better unit, since multiple exercises per muscle confound the per-exercise count.
- The dose-response climbed **all the way into double-digit weekly sets**.
- **Double-digit weekly sets ≈ 30% more hypertrophy than single-digit weekly sets** [23:56].
- No plateau could be identified, because almost no studies use double-digit weekly sets. His summary: *"more is better"* — where it stops, nobody knows.
- Explicit call for studies at **20–30 sets per week**; Schoenfeld had a proposal out to run exactly that on trained subjects.
- The only dose-response volume/hypertrophy study on **trained** subjects at that point was **Ostrowski (1998 or 1999 — Krieger unsure of the year)**.
- He also added an analysis giving percent-gain figures for interpretability.

Nippard pushes back [12:13]: the meta-analysis rests on eight studies, **seven of which found no significant effect** — isn't calling volume "the driver" jumping the gun? Krieger's counter is the core statistical argument of the episode [21:54]: the effect size favoured the multiple-set group in **every single study**. If there were truly no difference, you would expect random scatter around zero — some favouring single sets, some multiple, some neither. You don't see that. You see a consistent direction the individual studies lacked the power to certify.

## The Fisher critique, and statistical significance [24:56]
Fisher (2012), "Beware the meta-analysis", in *Journal of Exercise Physiology Online*. Nippard puts two objections to Krieger; Krieger had never publicly responded before.

**Objection 1 — heterogeneity.** Trained, untrained, elderly, coronary-artery-disease subjects all pooled together.
Krieger's answer [35:18]: a meta-analysis does not pool individuals. It pools the **within-study effect** — the difference between groups inside each controlled trial — and asks whether that difference consistently favours one condition. Further, he used a **random-effects model**, which explicitly builds in uncertainty for unmeasured between-study variance. It is a very conservative model that makes significance *harder* to reach. He found an effect anyway.

**Objection 2 — the underlying studies mostly weren't significant.**
Krieger's answer [26:32]: Fisher and colleagues over-invest in statistical significance, which is an arbitrary agreed threshold. A p of .04 is "significant" and .06 or .07 is not, despite a 6–7% type-one error rate still being low. He would rather see **effect sizes and confidence intervals** reported.

The demonstration case is **Ostrowski** [28:37]: hypertrophy in the highest-volume group was **almost double** that of the lower-volume groups, and it was still not statistically significant — because there were only ~10 people per group. With n that small, **type-two errors** (declaring no difference when one exists) are routine.

**Ostrowski's design, as Krieger describes it** [30:11]: three exercises per muscle group, three groups —
- 1 set/exercise = **3 weekly sets per muscle**
- 2 sets/exercise = **6 weekly sets per muscle**
- 4 sets/exercise = **12 weekly sets per muscle**

The 12-set group's quad hypertrophy was clearly better than the other two. Triceps were trained **twice weekly** in that study while legs were trained once, so the triceps arms were effectively **12 and 24 weekly sets** — and there was little difference between 12 and 24, though both beat 6. Krieger reads this as double-digit weekly sets being where the benefit lives.

**On fixing the field** [32:43]: you essentially can't. Resistance-training studies use college students over a semester or quarter, hence the near-universal 12–15 week durations. Krieger's own overtraining study on runners (published in *Journal of Applied Physiology*) paid subjects, still had dropouts, had to be run twice, and ended with **six people per group**. Until someone funds 50–100 subjects per group, meta-analysis, protein-synthesis data and extrapolation are what we have.

## Training to failure [12:44]
Krieger's position, in order:
1. **Little research exists** comparing failure to non-failure for hypertrophy, and what exists is limited. One study he recalls compared a failure to a non-failure group on **lean body mass** (not direct hypertrophy) and found no difference.
2. **Volume-equated, there is probably a small edge to failure** — but the framing is wrong, because the entire reason to stop short of failure is to do more sets.
3. **One set to failure absolutely produces hypertrophy** [20:23]. Demonstrated repeatedly. But protein-synthesis data makes it implausible that one set equals multiple sets for *maximising* growth.
4. For the average person who just wants some muscle, **single sets will work great**. For maximising, volume wins.
5. Diminishing returns exist, and past some point extra volume becomes counterproductive — Krieger locates that harm more in **joints and soft tissue** than in the muscle itself [24:56].

**Nippard's case for failure**, which Krieger partly accepts: (a) the **size principle** — full motor-unit recruitment only at or near failure, leaving units unstimulated otherwise; (b) practically, lifters told to leave "a couple of reps in the tank" leave far more than that, so failure functions as a way of **standardising effort**.

Krieger agrees on (b), especially for beginners, who have no calibration for proximity to failure [38:24]. For experienced trainees it's different — and he cites a study in the same journal Fisher published in, on **competitive, advanced powerlifters** who trained "quite a ways" short of failure at high frequency and made excellent gains. His conclusion: advanced lifters can train short of failure, **provided they compensate with more volume per session or more frequency**.

## Load, rep ranges and volume load [15:17]
- **Stu Phillips' lab**: three sets at **30% 1RM to failure**, versus three sets at 30% work-matched to a 90% group, versus three sets at **90% to failure**. The work-matched non-failure 30% group did *not* match the 90% group's hypertrophy. The 30%-to-failure group had the most protein synthesis — and also by far the greatest **volume load**.
- Krieger's reading: any rep range works **provided sets go to failure**, but with light loads (roughly **<60% 1RM**) you need a substantially higher **volume load** — not necessarily more sets — to match.
- Schoenfeld's trained-subject study: **3 sets of 30–40 reps to failure vs 3 sets of ~8–12** produced essentially equivalent hypertrophy.
- The floor: **3 sets of 3 reps to failure is not enough volume load** to drive much growth. Krieger reports Schoenfeld hinting at a study then in review comparing **3 sets of 3–4 reps to failure vs 3 sets of 8–12**, in which the 8–12 group appeared to get superior hypertrophy.
- Krieger endorses (with Schoenfeld) **mixing rep ranges** to target different fibre types, while noting the evidence for it — especially in trained subjects — is thin.

## Drop sets as a substitute for reps left in the tank [40:59]
Nippard's proposal: instead of leaving a rep in the tank to preserve volume, take sets to failure and recover the lost volume with a **drop set**. Krieger: theoretically plausible, but **there is essentially no data on drop sets** — he flags it as an open research area he wants to raise with Schoenfeld. He had personally used drop sets in the three weeks before his physique contest, when he ramped frequency to 4–6 days per body part and needed to make up for reduced per-session volume; he also liked that the lighter loads were easier on the joints.

Downside both acknowledge: going to failure early compromises the subsequent exercises for that muscle, so total volume may end up lower.

**Nippard's practical compromise** [43:34]: leave a rep in the tank, then take the **last set of each exercise** to failure — or at minimum the last set of the last exercise for that body part. Krieger says that's how he trained for years: pick a load on four sets of eight such that the **fourth set hits failure at the eighth rep**.

## Frequency [44:35]
Krieger: the research as it stands favours **spreading volume across the week**.
- **Schoenfeld's study**: bro split (each muscle 1x/week) vs the **same weekly sets** split across **3 days per muscle**. Higher frequency won on hypertrophy.
- A study Krieger attributes to **"McCluster"** — almost certainly **McLester** *(auto-caption garble; flagging rather than guessing)*: **3 sets to failure once weekly vs 1 set to failure three days weekly**, volume low overall, with the higher-frequency group superior.
- **Krieger & Schoenfeld's own frequency meta-analysis**: **2–3 days per week beats 1 day per week**, volume-equated.
- Above 3x/week the literature nearly vanishes — Krieger can name **three studies**, all showing benefit, all with caveats.
- Protein-synthesis time course gives a theoretical rationale for higher frequency.

**The novelty-effect objection** [47:38]. Nippard's criticism, which Schoenfeld himself has acknowledged: all subjects came in running bro splits, so the group switched to higher frequency got a *novel* stimulus while the control group kept doing what they'd always done. Nippard wants the mirror-image study — recruit high-frequency trainees, then split them. **Krieger agrees this is a genuine limitation** and that you can't currently separate frequency from novelty.

**Krieger's own program** [39:58]: each body part **5–6 days per week**, stopping **3–5 reps short of failure**. His overload variables are volume and frequency, not proximity to failure. He was ~6 weeks in and explicitly calling it an experiment, not a recommendation. Menno Henselmans' client results were part of what prompted it — Nippard asks if he's being coached by Menno; he isn't.

**Nippard's objection to high frequency** [49:12] is subjective and he says so: on upper/lower and push/pull/legs he gets one or two exercises per muscle per session and never feels he's pushed hard enough. Krieger concedes the personality fit is real — high frequency **requires holding back**, because you're back on the same muscle tomorrow. He cites **Martin Berkhan**, a low-frequency low-volume advocate who announced a high-frequency experiment and never posted a follow-up, as someone whose "go balls-out every session" style simply doesn't survive high frequency.

## Strength vs size: does powerlifting build a physique? [52:16]
Nippard's premise for taking up powerlifting was that getting stronger would let him move more volume in bodybuilding rep ranges and therefore get bigger. He isn't convinced it worked.

Krieger on **relative volume (sets × reps × %1RM) vs absolute volume (sets × reps × load)**: no good answer exists; both matter, and both are limited in isolation — a high-rep light set produces enormous absolute volume for less hypertrophy per unit.

The more important point [54:50]: people badly underestimate **neural adaptation**. Krieger describes a paper he had just peer-reviewed (accepted with minor revisions by him, rejected by the other two reviewers, so likely unpublished): untrained subjects, high-load vs low-load vs mixed, **all sets to failure**, equal set counts. **Hypertrophy was similar across all groups. Strength gains were not** — the low-load group gained little strength while the high-load group gained enormously.

Conclusion both men endorse: **a bigger muscle is a stronger muscle, but a stronger muscle is not necessarily a bigger one.** "My poundages are going up so I must be getting bigger" does not follow, especially under powerlifting-style training. Nippard adds the observation that the strongest lifters in his weight class often carry noticeably less muscle than he does.

Krieger on the deadlift [58:28]: he cut it from his own training, on the grounds that if the sole goal is size there are better choices — and notes Menno has said something similar. He is **not convinced full powerlifting cycles benefit a physique**; some lower-rep work, sure, but competing and training as a powerlifter is a different goal with size as a byproduct.

Nippard's own audit of what powerlifting did to his physique [59:30]:
- **Added** size/density to lower back, glutes and hamstrings, which he attributes to deadlifting.
- **Neglected**: biceps get virtually nothing, triceps not bad, side and rear delts very little — producing the rolled-forward powerlifter posture unless upper back and rear delts are trained directly.
- **Psychological upside**: an objective number to chase, which relieves the monotony of consecutive bodybuilding off-seasons.

## Points of disagreement
- **Nippard vs Krieger on the strength of the volume evidence.** Nippard argues that "take sets to or near failure" is better grounded at present than "volume is the driver", given seven of eight studies were null. Krieger flatly says he disagrees, and answers with protein-synthesis data plus the consistent direction of effect sizes.
- **Nippard vs Krieger on failure.** Nippard presses the size-principle case for failure and the practical case that lifters misjudge their proximity to it; Krieger holds that failure's volume-equated edge is small and that failure's real cost is foregone volume. They converge on Nippard's compromise (last set to failure).
- **Nippard vs Fisher, via Krieger.** Krieger's rebuttal on both heterogeneity and significance is the most substantive disagreement in the episode, and it is with an absent third party.
- **Nippard vs Krieger on high-frequency training** — but framed as personal fit rather than physiology. Nippard doesn't feel he trains hard enough per session; Krieger says that's exactly the adjustment high frequency demands.
- **Both against the fitness-industry default** that powerlifting is a good hypertrophy strategy.

## Practical application
- Aim for **double-digit weekly sets per muscle** if maximising size is the goal. Accept diminishing returns and the roughly doubled time cost for ~30% more growth.
- If you just want to be reasonably muscular, **lower volumes work fine** — Krieger says single sets will work great for most people.
- **Beginners: train to failure**, because they cannot yet gauge how far from it they are.
- **Advanced lifters can train short of failure**, but must pay for it with more volume per session or more frequency.
- A workable middle: leave a rep in the tank on early sets and take the **last set of each exercise (or at least of the last exercise per body part) to failure**.
- Hit each muscle **at least twice weekly**; higher works but requires deliberately holding back.
- With light loads (<60% 1RM), expect to need **more total volume load**, not just more sets.
- Don't take rising gym numbers as proof of hypertrophy. If you powerlift, **add direct work for biceps, side delts and rear delts** or your physique will lag where the big three don't reach.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The headline dose-response numbers come from a paper **in review at recording**, described only in hints; Krieger repeatedly declines to give detail.
- **Where volume plateaus is unknown.** No studies existed at 20–30 weekly sets.
- Only **one** dose-response volume study on trained subjects existed (Ostrowski), and it was null by conventional statistics.
- Failure vs non-failure for hypertrophy is **barely researched**, and the studies that exist have design limitations.
- **No data on drop sets** at all.
- **Frequency above 3x/week** rests on three studies, all caveated. The novelty confound is unresolved and both men agree it matters.
- Krieger's 5–6x/week experiment was **six weeks old and self-experimental** — he explicitly refuses to draw conclusions from it.
- Individual variance in response to volume is repeatedly flagged as large and genetic.
- Name flags: **"McCluster"** is almost certainly *McLester*; the Ostrowski publication year is given as "1999 or 1998, I don't remember"; the powerlifter near-failure study and the Phillips-lab study are cited without full references.
