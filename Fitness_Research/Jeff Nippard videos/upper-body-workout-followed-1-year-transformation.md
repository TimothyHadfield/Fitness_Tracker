# The Upper Body Workout Behind Nippard's One-Year Transformation

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Upper Body Workout I Followed For My 1 Year Transformation" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=928aRhhPP8I)) · 2025-05-27 · 13 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Seven movements: incline barbell bench, seated cable fly, weighted pull-ups, high cable lateral raises, deficit Pendlay row, cable overhead triceps extension, Bahnjian-style cable curl.
- Last set of every exercise taken to absolute failure, no spotter, for a full year.
- Result after 15 years of natural training: +2.7 lb lean mass by DEXA, plus measurable added biceps peak by ultrasound.
- He promoted weighted pull-ups from A tier to S tier off the back of this experiment — he attributes most of his back growth to them.
- A 45° incline barbell press replaces needing both a flat press and a vertical press.

## 1. Incline barbell bench press
His most effective pec builder of the year. Hits chest and front delts with bonus triceps, and even side delts, which stabilise the bar against drifting forward or back.

**Why barbell over dumbbell:** overload potential. Over a full year dumbbells become bulky and awkward once you're strong, while you can always add a little to the bar. Why free weights over the Smith machine: more stabiliser involvement, especially side delts. He pushes back on the trend of only using the most stable machine version of every lift — free compound lifts still have real value even for pure hypertrophy.

**Evidence:** a study from Chavez and colleagues found the incline press grew the mid and lower pecs just as well as flat bench, with the added bonus of more upper pec growth. That makes a 45° incline time-efficient — you don't really need both a flat press and a vertical press if you meet in the middle.

**Cue this year:** slightly narrower grip to increase range of motion, picking up more triceps without losing chest tension.

**Failure protocol:** last set to absolute failure with no spotter — press until you can't, then dump the bar forward onto the safety bars.

**Common mistake:** bouncing the bar off the chest. You can lift more that way, but progressive overload only counts if technique is consistent. Control the descent, slight pause on the chest, press up and slightly back.

## 2. Seated cable fly
Slows things down and puts tension purely on the pecs, with a bigger stretch than the heavy press.

**Setup:** bench set well out in front of the stack. Grab both handles, hold them tight to the chest, walk out, then ease into the seat rather than getting pulled backward. Press the handles forward to the start position.

**Execution:** controlled negative, as deep as the shoulders comfortably allow, then squeeze forward focusing on bringing the *elbows* together, not just the hands — that keeps tension on the chest and stops triceps and front delts taking over. Sweep the weight out and back in a wide arc, covering as much space as possible on the negative.

**Why over a pec deck:** he can get deeper than most pec decks allow. Why seated over standing: more stability, so no tension is lost to ankle or low-back stabilisation. The goal is pure pectoral isolation.

**Common mistake:** letting the elbows drop, which puts the pecs at a mechanical disadvantage and worsens the line of pull. Keep elbows up and think about hugging a big tree.

## 3. Weighted pull-ups
The biggest driver of his back gains. Lats handle shoulder extension, mid traps and rhomboids fire on scapular retraction, low back stabilises, biceps and brachialis handle elbow flexion, and core is involved since you're not seated as on a pulldown.

**Why over lat pulldown:** they're harder and he didn't want to avoid hard exercises; and with pulldowns, once you're strong it becomes nearly impossible to avoid swinging. On pull-ups swing is more limited, especially with weight strapped on and a pause at the bottom of each rep. He is explicit that pull-ups aren't inherently better than pulldowns — each has strengths.

**Cues:** think chest to the bar rather than chin over the bar, which keeps you in control. Pull with the back, not the arms — arms are connectors — by driving the elbows down and in, which activates lats and stops biceps taking over. He uses liquid chalk and straps so grip doesn't cap back gains, and a grip between fully pronated wide and neutral, purely for shoulder comfort.

**Common mistake:** worrying too much about feeling the lats. Pull-ups aren't a "feely" exercise. Pull up, lower under control, add weight over time, and the lats grow whether or not you feel them.

**Progression achieved:** day one, 30 lb for six reps. End of year, 60 lb for six reps. Straight progressive overload, last set to failure.

**Regressions:** band- or machine-assisted pull-ups with assistance gradually reduced; eccentric-only pull-ups, starting at the top off a box and lowering slowly.

## 4. High cable lateral raises — 2-3 × 8-10
Just two or three sets, pushed hard. Done right these almost exclusively target the side delts, arguably the key fibres for the wide, tapered X-frame.

**Why cables:** consistent tension across the range, unlike dumbbells which have no tension at the bottom and peak only at the top. Setting the cable high makes the movement hardest when the delt is most stretched, since peak tension with a cable always occurs at a 90° angle between arm and cable — which happens early in the range with a high pulley.

**Evidence:** a study published in December (2024) compared cable to dumbbell lateral raises. Each subject trained one arm with cables and the other with dumbbells, twice a week, five sets per session, all sets to failure. After eight weeks both grew the side delts about the same. Nippard's caveat: subjects stopped the cable range of motion directly at their side, missing the cross-body sweep that he considers a main cable advantage. His conclusion is that cables vs. dumbbells probably isn't a true game changer — what matters is picking a variation where you feel the side delts and pushing it hard with good form. He uses cables on upper body day and dumbbells on push day. He also concedes the importance of the stretch was somewhat exaggerated on social media for a while.

**Common mistake:** shrugging the weight up, letting upper traps take over. Sweep out, not up. Drill: stand against a wall and press the back of your forearm sideways into it, building pressure gradually — that's the exact intention to use.

## 5. Deficit Pendlay row
Recruits essentially the whole back: mid traps from scapular retraction, upper and lower traps from scapular stabilisation, lats from shoulder extension, and spinal erectors firing isometrically the whole set to hold the horizontal position. Standing on a bumper plate increases range and gives the lats a deeper bottom stretch than a floor-level row.

**Hypertrophy tweak:** keep the explosive concentric but slow the negative rather than the usual explosive-up-drop-down style, maintaining eccentric tension. On the last set he pushes past failure with lengthened partials — when he can't get the full top squeeze, he grinds out another three or four reps in the stretched position where the lats are strongest. He notes people are tired of lengthened partials but argues this is a spot where the strength curve justifies them.

**Common mistake:** not getting fully horizontal. A more upright torso isn't necessarily wrong, but at parallel gravity pulls directly against the scapular retractors, forcing mid traps and rhomboids to work much harder. A higher torso angle only matches it by adding weight — pound for pound the Pendlay row is more challenging.

Use straps, or at minimum chalk; grip should not be the limiter.

## 6. Cable overhead triceps extension
What current research shows is likely the single most effective triceps movement, and not just for the long head. A study comparing overhead extensions to pushdowns found 50% more long head growth, 40% more lateral and medial head growth, and 40% more total triceps growth with overhead extensions. (Researchers not named.) If he only had time for one triceps exercise, this is it.

**Common mistake:** it happens in setup. Most people set the cable far too low and burn energy twisting into position. Instead set the cable higher, squat underneath it, use your legs to lift the weight into place, then step forward once or twice.

## 7. Bahnjian cable curl
Several new biceps studies dropped recently, including one comparing the Bahnjian cable curl (name as spoken in captions — spelling uncertain) to the classic preacher curl. Result: non-significant trends favouring the Bahnjian curl, nothing statistically significant. That could be because the study was underpowered — 15 subjects, 10 weeks — or it could mean both grow biceps equally.

He still runs Bahnjian curls on upper body day because on the totality of evidence he suspects a slight edge, mainly from the combination of long muscle length and high tension in the lengthened position. On his pull day he trains biceps again with preacher curls and hammer curls. All to failure.

## Practical application
Run one heavy overloadable movement per pattern (incline barbell press, weighted pull-up, Pendlay row) and progress the load; use cables for the isolation work where the tension curve matters (fly, lateral raise, overhead extension). Take only the last set of each to failure. Straps and chalk on all back work. Track a single number per lift over a year — his pull-ups went 30 lb × 6 to 60 lb × 6.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The cable vs. dumbbell lateral raise study found no difference; his preference for cables rests on a range-of-motion argument the study didn't test.
- The Bahnjian vs. preacher curl study was small (15 subjects, 10 weeks) and found no significant difference; the exercise name is garbled in the captions.
- Only the Chavez incline press study has named authors; the triceps and lateral raise studies are unnamed here.
- Results are a single-subject year-long anecdote (n=1) around otherwise evidence-based choices; +2.7 lb lean mass by DEXA is within the noise range of the method.
