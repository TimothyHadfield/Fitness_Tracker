# Top Shoulder Exercises for 3D Delts (with Dr Mike Israetel)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Top 3 Shoulder Exercises For 3D Delts" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=21lYP86dHW4)) · 2023-11-30 · 11 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- **Side delts are the priority.** Mike argues 70-90% of all delt volume should go to side delts; front delts are already hammered by chest pressing and rear delts by proper back training.
- Front delts: **machine shoulder press, 6-10 reps** (Jeff) or **EZ-bar front raise, 10-20 reps** with an explosive concentric and a 4-5 second negative (Mike).
- Side delts: **cross-body cable lateral raise** (Jeff) or the **"super ROM" lateral** that continues past the usual endpoint (Mike), both 10-20 reps with a controlled negative.
- Rear delts: **reverse pec deck** (Jeff) or **super ROM reverse cable fly** (Mike) — reach and sweep as far as possible, elbows locked, load minimised.
- Universal cue from Mike: control the last negative "as if you're going to do another rep" — that final eccentric may be the most growth-promoting portion of a set.

## Volume distribution across the three heads
[00:00] The common mistake is over-volumising front delts, which already get heavy stimulation from all chest pressing. A German study from the 1990s found bodybuilders had **five times the front delt size** of non-lifters but only **three times the side delt size** — evidence the front head is habitually overemphasised relative to the side.

Mike goes further: rear delt training is also overrated, because properly trained backs develop rear delts well. Visually, when someone reads as having big shoulders it is almost always the side delts doing the work; rear delts fill in the rest. Hence his 70-90% side delt allocation.

## Front delts

**Jeff — machine shoulder press.** [01:00] Seated position increases stability so nearly all tension goes to the shoulders, and most people can push a machine closer to failure without dropping the weight or breaking form. He favours a Panatta machine for its large range of motion and deep stretch at the bottom, and because it is unilateral — each shoulder works independently, useful for correcting left/right imbalances. **6-10 reps.** Any decent press machine works; a seated dumbbell shoulder press gives a similar unilateral effect.

**Mike — EZ-bar front raise.** [02:31] Two reasons: it gives the front delts a huge range of motion, and it is a rare front-delt isolation movement. Most people default to a compound press, which is fine, but if triceps volume is high, pecs are sore, or the shoulder joints are irritated from pressing, overhead pressing may not be available.

His ideal version is on a free-motion machine so the hand can travel behind the plane of the body for a bigger stretch; the EZ bar is the accessible substitute. Cues: **keep the chest up** — caving the chest reduces the front-delt stretch. Treat it like a curl where the elbows never bend: raise all the way up high, then milk the eccentric, hardest through the middle, chest up at the bottom. **10-20 reps, explosive positive, 4-5 second controlled negative**, holding control especially at the midpoint where tension peaks. He identifies the most effective portion as roughly **45° to 135°** of arm elevation — cutting that out loses most of the benefit. On going past the top: no reason to suspect danger if it is pain-free.

## Side delts

**Mike — "super ROM" lateral.** [04:34] A lateral raise that simply keeps going past the normal endpoint. On hand position: keep the **pinkies up** as much as tolerable rather than thumbs up. The reason is fibre alignment — thumbs-up puts the resistance path in line with the front delt fibres, while the pinky-up position aligns it with the side delt fibres. If the pinky-up position hurts, thumbs-up is acceptable. **10-20 reps, strong eccentric control on every rep.**

**Jeff — cross-body cable lateral raise.** [05:34] Standard laterals going straight out to the side are fine, but the cross-body version adds a real stretch: holding the arm at the side gives little stretch, whereas pulling it across the body loads the side delt in a lengthened position. He also notes anatomy research showing the deltoid has closer to **seven compartments** rather than three distinct heads, each with fibres fanning in slightly different directions — so hitting the delts from varied angles helps ensure all fibres get worked. Execution: position the torso so the stretch is felt on the side delt, then sweep the weight out and back, like drawing and flicking a sword.

## Rear delts

**Mike — super ROM reverse cable fly.** [06:34] Cues: cross the cables and **go past the hand connection** at the stretch. To avoid asymmetry, alternate which hand is on top every set. Keep the **elbows locked out** so the work stays in the rear delt — you will use much less load, but minimising load while maximising muscle involvement is the goal here. Then pull **all the way back**, further than feels natural, into a hard peak contraction, hold and feel the burn accumulate, then slowly arc back out into the stretch. The extra range past the usual stopping point costs few reps because that portion is easy, but sharply increases metabolite accumulation and the connection to the muscle.

**Jeff — reverse pec deck.** [08:05] Mike's cue transforms it: **reach forward far** at the start, which pre-stretches the rear delts. You are not pulling — you are arcing, paint-brushing your hands across as wide a distance as possible. Punch forward, then sweep out. Jeff never lets the arms pull inward; every rep sweeps out and paints as much of the floor as possible. This will substantially reduce the load used but hits the target muscle far better.

**The last-negative cue.** [09:08] Mike: on the final rep, act "as if you're going to do another one" — keep controlling, pause at the end, then release. That last eccentric may be one of the most muscle-growth-promoting portions of any rep in any set, and most lifters throw it away by dumping the weight.

## Practical application
Bias delt volume heavily toward side delts. One front-delt movement (machine press at 6-10 reps, or EZ-bar front raise at 10-20 with a 4-5s negative), the bulk of your sets on side delts (cross-body cable raises and/or super-ROM laterals, 10-20 reps, pinkies up), and one rear-delt movement done with light load, locked elbows, a long reach forward and a full sweep back. Control every negative, and treat the last one as if another rep is coming.

## Caveats & unknowns
The 70-90% side delt figure is Mike's opinion, not a study finding. The German bodybuilder study is observational and from the 1990s. Mike acknowledges "some nuances" to the 45-135° claim on front raises. Extending range past the normal endpoint is presented as safe only if pain-free — joint pain is the signal to stop. Jeff and Mike differ in style: Mike pauses hard in the stretch position and uses much lighter loads, Jeff is more of a constant-tension lifter.
