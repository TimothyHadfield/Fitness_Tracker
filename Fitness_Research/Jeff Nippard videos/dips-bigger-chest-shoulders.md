# Dip Technique For A Bigger Chest And Shoulders

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Do Dips For A Bigger Chest and Shoulders (Fix Mistakes!)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yN6Q1UI_xkE)) · 2019-03-05 · 7 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Load the dip and train it in the 6-12 rep range as a mass and strength builder, not just as a bodyweight high-rep finisher.
- Dips hit the entire pec, not just the lower chest — Contreras' EMG work showed weighted dips producing higher peak upper-chest activation than incline bench with 225 lb.
- The two big errors are anterior scapular tilting and staying too upright; both cause shoulder pain.
- Set up with scapulae retracted and depressed, lean the torso slightly forward, lower for ~2 seconds to about 90 degrees at the elbow.
- Because dips are less technical than the bench or overhead press, sets can be pushed closer to failure than on those lifts.

## Muscles worked [00:01]
- **Pecs (upper and lower)** via shoulder adduction — bringing the arm across the body toward the midline, as in a bench press.
- **Anterior deltoids and the clavicular (upper) head of the pec** via shoulder flexion — bringing the arm forward, as in a front raise.
- **Triceps** via elbow extension.

## What the EMG data says [00:32]
In 2010 Bret Contreras ran a solo EMG experiment and found the weighted dip activated the *lower* pecs most. But the weighted dip still produced higher peak EMG activation for the *upper* chest than incline bench press with 225 pounds. So the lower chest being most active does not make the dip a lower-chest-only exercise — it trains the whole pec significantly, making it a strong overall mass and strength builder.

## Loading and rep ranges [01:02]
- Like the pull-up, the dip is often treated as bodyweight-only and used as a high-rep finisher. That undersells it.
- It's a multi-joint movement performed in a strong position, so it's smarter in most cases to load it in a **moderate-to-heavy 6-12 rep zone**.
- As a high-rep finisher, bodyweight sets of **30+ reps** are fine.
- Reaching failure at 6-12 reps generally means adding external load: a dip/loading belt, or a partner placing a dumbbell between your legs.
- **Progression rule:** if you're at or near failure in that range with bodyweight only, master form first. Once you hit **12 reps with bodyweight**, start adding weight gradually.
- Assisted dip machines are fine and will usually be needed by beginners and heavier individuals to maintain proper form.

## Proximity to failure [01:32]
Despite being a heavy compound, the dip is much less technical than the bench press or overhead press, so it's more acceptable to push sets close to failure — letting the muscles accumulate fatigue under heavy load in a way the standard presses may not provide. Caveat: if you're new to the dip, start at relatively low exertion levels and work up to high-effort sets only as technique is mastered, since small form deviations under heavy load cause pain.

## Execution [02:33]
1. Use a dip rack or parallel bars; grip **just outside shoulder width**.
2. Prop up with elbows and shoulders fully extended, weight on the handles, legs curled behind you so your feet clear the floor each rep.
3. Before descending: **retract and slightly depress the shoulder blades** for a stable shoulder joint.
4. **Lean the torso slightly forward** to shift emphasis onto the chest — but not so far it becomes a floating push-up. Fully upright is also wrong (shoulder pain, less pec involvement).
5. Lower under control over about **2 seconds**. A smooth controlled negative matters because it stops you overrunning your shoulder's natural range.
6. On the way down keep **knees pinched together and hips extended** to prevent tipping — knees coming forward creates momentum and shifts your centre of balance (a form of cheating).
7. Keep the scapulae retracted and the **thoracic spine extended** — don't let the upper back round.
8. Descend until the **upper arm and forearm make roughly a 90-degree angle**, or until you feel a strong stretch in the pec and triceps. For some that's slightly below 90. Most people with healthy shoulders should aim to get the upper arm parallel to the floor.
9. **No pause at the bottom** — reverse by driving the hands down into the handles while holding torso position.
10. At the top, confirm shoulder blades still retracted and upper back still extended.

## The most common errors [04:33]
**1. Anterior scapular tilting.** Nippard calls this the most common dip error and the reason dips have become controversial as a shoulder-wrecker. People know to retract and depress the scapulae on bench press but don't carry the same cue to the dip, despite the two being biomechanically similar. Fix: pull the shoulder blades together and tuck them down before dipping, think about keeping the upper back straight, don't go too deep, and reverse the movement before you lose that stable shoulder position.

**2. Staying too upright.** People assume a more upright torso hits the triceps more. In reality, pecs, delts and triceps are all highly active when the dip is done with a natural forward inclination. Excessive uprightness adds stress to the shoulder joint while removing pec contribution.

On grip and posture manipulation generally: Nippard isn't a fan of tweaking grip width or torso angle to target different muscles. Find a comfortable groove that suits your own anthropometry and get better at it.

## Variations and substitutes [06:05]
- **Bench dips** — not recommended. Torso is out in front of the shoulders, making anterior scapular tilting far more likely early in the range of motion.
- **Assisted dip machine / weighted dip machine** — usable, but he doesn't think machines truly replace the strength and mass-building potential of a properly performed free-weight dip.
- **Weighted push-ups** — a decent replacement, but you may not get the same degree of pec stretch, and loading past a certain strength level is awkward.

## Practical application
- Program dips as a loaded 6-12 rep compound, adding weight once bodyweight 12s are comfortable.
- Set up: grip just outside shoulder width, scapulae retracted and depressed, slight forward torso lean, knees pinched and hips extended.
- 2-second controlled negative to upper arms parallel to the floor, no bottom pause, drive back up without losing scapular position.
- Push these closer to failure than you would bench or overhead press — but only once technique is solid.
- Optional finisher use: bodyweight for 30+ reps.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The EMG data is a single solo experiment by one researcher (Contreras, 2010); EMG activation is not a direct proxy for hypertrophy.
- Optimal depth is individual — "slightly below 90 degrees" for some, and anyone with shoulder issues should not force upper-arm-parallel depth.
