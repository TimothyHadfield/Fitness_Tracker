# Detox Diets: Busted, And Occasionally Dangerous

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Do Detox Diets Work For Fat Loss & Health? (What The Science Says)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wYPM2eCHePc)) · 2018-09-10 · 9 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Verdict: **busted**. No robust evidence that any popular detox diet removes toxins or manages weight, and documented cases of real harm.
- The liver, kidneys, skin, lymphatic system and GI tract already form a self-cleansing detoxification system that needs no dietary assistance.
- Detox protocols never name the toxin they remove — which is exactly what keeps them untestable.
- Forced overhydration is the concrete danger: hyponatremia, seizures, brain damage.
- Any weight-loss diet with adequate protein and micronutrients beats the best detox diet available.

## The premise and the source material
[00:31] The claim is that the body accumulates toxins over time which make you fat or unhealthy, so a periodic detox flush is needed. Nippard builds the video around a Science-Based Medicine article by Dr. Steven Novella, a clinical neurologist at Yale University School of Medicine, plus a linked piece by pharmacist Scott Gavura (transcribed as "Scott Guevara" in the captions — spelling uncertain), then checks the peer-reviewed literature.

## The hydration danger
[01:01] Novella cites a 2016 British Medical Journal case report: a 47-year-old woman presented with seizures from euvolemic hyponatremia secondary to polydipsia. Her detox plan had her drinking enormous volumes of water — Novella notes some plans go as high as 60 litres. Drinking beyond need overwhelms the kidneys and drops blood sodium; the result can be delirium, seizures, and brain damage if the sodium is corrected too rapidly ([Biswas 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17551067/)).

Healthy adults can use thirst as an adequate hydration guide. Drinking past thirst does nothing extra for health or fat loss.

## Three strikes against detox diets
[02:02]

**1. The toxins are never named.** Depending who you ask you'll hear food additives, salt, meat, fluoride, prescription drugs — always nonspecific. If a plan claimed to remove a specific toxin, that would be testable. Vagueness is what keeps detox claims outside the reach of science while sounding scientific.

**2. "Chemicals" is not a category of harm, and the body already handles the real ones.** Air, food and drugs are all chemicals. For genuinely harmful substances, humans have evolved a sophisticated removal system: skin, kidneys, lymphatic system, GI tract, liver. The mental model of the liver and kidneys as filters holding toxins until cleaned out is physiologically wrong — the liver converts toxic substances into other substances which are then excreted in bile or urine. These systems are self-cleansing.

**3. The direct evidence is negative.** A paper in the *Journal of Human Nutrition and Dietetics* reviewed popular commercial detox diets, including Dr. Oz's weekend cleanse and the lemon detox diet, and concluded there is no compelling evidence supporting detox diets for weight management or toxin elimination, and that given the costs and health risks health professionals should discourage them ([Klien & Kiat](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269769478_Detox_diets_for_toxin_elimination_and_weight_management_A_critical_review_of_the_evidence)).

## What actually would qualify as a toxin worth removing
[04:04] Not everything is harmless. Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) are highly lipophilic, accumulate in adipose tissue, and can take years to break down. Certain heavy metals — arsenic, mercury, lead — are toxic at low doses. But both categories are heavily regulated in developed countries: the EU, USA and Australia have been steadily banning POPs since the 1970s. Most people have detectable mercury, but generally not at levels high enough to cause adverse effects, so the average person is unlikely to benefit much from metal detoxification.

[05:05] The interesting part: POPs and heavy metals like lead and mercury are the one class of compounds that *isn't* readily cleared by the liver and kidneys, so they're the only plausible candidates for a genuine dietary detox. The same paper notes that while no commercial detox diet is supported, certain nutritional components may possess detoxification properties. Examples from their table:
- **Citrus pectin**, found in citrus peel — a natural chelating agent, possibly useful for eliminating lead.
- **Olestra**, the fat substitute (banned in Canada and the EU, sold in fat-free chips in the US) — may absorb PCBs and eliminate them in the feces. PCB production was banned in the 1970s, and olestra carries its own health concerns, so don't get hopeful.

Most of this work is animal research. It's a direction for future study, not a protocol.

## Weight loss
[07:10] The authors' framing: it's plausible that energy-restricted detox diets produce short-term weight loss, but unclear whether they help maintain a healthy weight long term. Given the vast range of alternative diets that supply adequate protein and micronutrients while facilitating weight loss, it's questionable whether detox diets have any utility at all.

## Practical application
- Avoid detox diets entirely. There is no version currently worth doing.
- Hydrate to thirst. Never follow a plan that prescribes water volumes far beyond thirst.
- For fat loss, use a sustainable diet you can actually adhere to: variety of nutrient-rich foods, a moderate calorie deficit sustained over time, adequate protein.
- If a product claims to detox you, ask which toxin, by which mechanism. There won't be an answer.

## Caveats & unknowns
The nutritional-component research (citrus pectin, olestra) is mostly animal data with large gaps before anything resembling an evidence-based toxin elimination plan could exist. Populations with genuine high-level heavy metal or POP exposure are a different clinical question from the general public and aren't addressed here.
