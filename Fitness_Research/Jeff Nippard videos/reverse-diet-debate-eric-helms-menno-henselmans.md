# The Reverse Dieting Debate: Helms & Henselmans vs. Norton & Fitschen

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "THE REVERSE DIET DEBATE: Eric Helms + Menno Henselmans vs. Layne Norton + Peter Fitschen" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=swrul81qco8)) · 2016-03-07 · 91 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Nippard's definition `[01:03]`: slow, methodical calorie reintroduction after a cut, to raise metabolic rate and limit fat regain. Both teams attacked the definition rather than the practice.
- **Against (Helms, Henselmans):** post-show the goal is fast recovery, and fat regain is the mechanism of recovery, not its cost. Helms wants ~5% over stage weight within 2-4 weeks.
- **For (Norton, Fitschen):** Norton's real protocol is a large immediate calorie jump then slow increases — close to the other side's. His fear is rapid regain triggering binge-restrict cycling.
- Strongest new evidence came from the against side: Henselmans' then-in-review reanalysis found **no metabolic damage** once RMR is corrected for body composition — across Minnesota, Biosphere, anorexia patients and both bodybuilder prep papers.
- Unresolved: whether fast fat regain permanently raises a body-fat set point, and whether offseason calorie-building lets you diet on more food next prep.
- Agreed: the TDEE drop is mostly NEAT, psychology sets the rate, and nobody should spend most of their career dieting.

## The two positions

### Against — Eric Helms and Menno Henselmans

**Helms `[05:42]`.** Recover as fast as possible; the fastest route back to normal metabolic rate and TDEE is putting fat back on, and striated glutes are only useful on stage. What he objects to is holding an athlete in a deficit for three to seven weeks or months post-show — that prolongs the diet, delays the hormonal state needed to train hard, and pushes people toward disordered eating.

His evidence is his own coaching data: 3D Muscle Journey ran a conservative reverse-diet approach until roughly two years prior and dropped it at about a **10% success rate**. He treats the accepted line that "the reverse diet is harder than the diet itself" as a red flag, not a fact — and says even the athletes who succeeded on it did not later diet on higher food or start leaner.

Physiology `[12:22]`: most of the TDEE downregulation is **NEAT**, not RMR. RMR does fall at very low body fat, but at best accounts for **20-30%** of the total drop — so manipulating food to "correct metabolic adaptation" targets the wrong variable. He concedes part of the NEAT effect is skeletal muscle efficiency, which can't be modified.

Health `[09:46]`: menstrual cycle loss is common (more common than not in some female bodybuilding categories), with bone density consequences; and the **Minnesota semi-starvation study** showed anorexia-like symptoms are biologically induced by dieting. His alternative, the "recovery diet": gain to the low end of the settling point, then increase slowly.

**Henselmans `[24:38]`.** With research assistant **Anastasia Senko** he had a paper in review reanalysing metabolic damage — negative adaptations persisting after weight regain. Method: measured vs. **predicted** resting energy expenditure using formulas correcting for body fat percentage and composition, accuracy comparable to organ-based measurement. The correction matters because organ mass, not muscle mass, dominates lean mass's contribution to BMR.

Findings `[28:48]`, all in one direction:
- **Minnesota starvation experiment** (average ~**5.5% body fat**, untrained but active): once original body weight was regained, the measured-to-predicted ratio was unchanged. No metabolic damage.
- Replicated on the **Biosphere** data and several chronic under/overfeeding studies.
- **Anorexia nervosa patients**: no damage after composition was restored; a trend in the *opposite* direction, BMR higher than before.
- The **two existing bodybuilder contest-prep papers**: no damage once corrected for composition.

This contradicts **Dulloo** *(captions: "Alfred Dulu"/"Doo" — uncertain)*, whose fat-mass argument is the origin of the fat-stores-memory model.

Mechanism `[31:20]`: BMR tracks body composition (fat mass contributes marginally). Thermic effect of food is acute and proportional — at 10% TEF, 100 calories eaten burns 10 — and can't be chronically manipulated. Adaptive thermogenesis is measurable during under- and overfeeding **in as little as a day**, generated 1-3 days before instruments detect the change; thyroid hormone and sympathetic nervous activity take **at most 8-14 days** to recover; insulin secretion, heart rate, creatinine clearance and fluid balance recover almost instantly.

So `[34:57]`: two weeks at maintenance clears the adaptations, after which expenditure is near-computable from composition plus TEF. The one genuine error reverse dieting fixes is returning to your *old* maintenance — an 80 kg male who dieted from 3,000 to 2,000 calories no longer has a 3,000-calorie maintenance. His practice: a conservative surplus that functions as maintenance for one to two weeks, then increase — which, he notes, resembles Norton's.

### For — Layne Norton and Peter Fitschen

**Norton `[14:26]`.** His ideal reverse is "quickly jump calories up pretty high, get somebody feeling really good," then add slowly. He agrees competitors have no reason to stay stage-lean year round, citing a client pushed to nearly **450 g carbs/day** while very lean who still reported low testosterone — Norton insisted on more aggressive gain for health.

His case for the slow phase:
- A female client `[19:31]`: **121 lb** on ~**101 g carbs, 101 g protein, 41 g fat** *(carb figure garbled in captions)*. Built to **130.5 lb at ~165 g carbs, 57 g fat**, then pushed to **315 g carbs, 81 g fat** with **no further weight gain**. Later dieted to **108 lb on 130 g carbs, 45 g fat**. He raises the underreporting objection himself.
- A paper (name not recalled) where strict restriction for **5-8 weeks** produced metabolic and gene-expression adaptations lasting **up to a year**.
- A rat study `[22:35]`: animals dieted down ~**one-seventh of body weight**, refed ad libitum, overshot original body fat, added ~**50% more fat cells**, and kept gaining until individual fat cells returned to pre-diet *size*. Inference: set point may be sensed as fat cell size and caloric flux, not a body fat percentage — so rapid regain could raise it permanently.
- **Brownell**'s weight-cycling animal experiment *(captions: "Brown Lell" — uncertain)*: matched total calories, steady overfeeding vs. cycling; cyclers gained more fat on identical intake.

`[46:11]` He regrets popularising "metabolic damage," crediting the term to **Scott Abel** *(captions: "Scott Ael" — uncertain)*, and did not coin "reverse dieting" either — he credits "Berto," presumably Alberto Nuñez. `[48:16]` His broader frame is obesity: nobody should live permanently at 1,200-1,500 calories.

**Fitschen `[36:59]`.** Splits by context: stage-lean athletes should **not** be reversed slowly — it only extends time to feeling normal; a general-pop dieter who isn't suffering can go slower. After the initial food bump and cardio cut, his rate depends on where they feel normal, target weight gain, offseason length, headroom before the next cut, and psychological tolerance. `[41:32]` His takeaway, endorsed by all four: you make progress when you're *not* dieting — he and **Alberto Nuñez** each graphed their careers by months dieting vs. not.

## Where they actually disagreed
- **Does fast regain damage the set point?** `[70:15]` Norton's one surviving concern: fat regains fast, lean mass slowly, and he cites Dulloo *(uncertain)* that the hyperphagic response doesn't normalise until lean mass does. He wants the two paced to arrive together. Helms and Nippard `[72:49]` push back — if the endpoint is identical six months later, a fluffier interim is irrelevant to anyone not psychologically triggered by it.
- **Does offseason calorie-building carry into the next prep?** `[80:33]` Helms says it works for some and not for him: in his 20s he gained slowly on ~4,000 calories, yet in 2009 he was doing 5 HIIT and 7 lifting sessions weekly averaging ~**1,800 calories** on low days for half a pound of loss, after stalling three months at 190 lb. Norton's IFBB bikini pro `[77:57]` is the mirror image: coming from under 1,000 calories and 4+ hours daily activity, maintaining on 130 g carbs / 45 g fat, she **lost 10 lb** while being built to ~350 g carbs and 80 g fat — then lost nothing going below 100 g carbs. Helms' counter `[79:00]`: those are inherited disasters returned to normal, not a default goal.
- **Intuitive vs. structured eating post-show.** Nippard proposes free eating until the settling point. Helms `[58:31]` refuses: hunger signals are non-functional after a show and behaviour lags physiology. His 2007 reverse-diet plan was a list of restaurants — **178 lb on stage to 226 lb two months later, +48 lb** — and he couldn't pull back; by 2011 he could eat intuitively, 9 lb over stage weight four months out. Henselmans `[62:35]` is blunter: the obesity epidemic is what listening to your emotions produces.

## Where they converged
`[42:33]` Fitschen says the sides barely differ, and he's right. All four agree adaptive metabolisms cut both ways `[53:21]` — Henselmans' own range is ~2,000 calories or below for photoshoot condition and over 4,000 bulking, little composition difference across that swing; that tracking should loosen across an offseason (Fitschen: wide macro ranges, then calorie ranges with protein and fibre minimums); and `[81:33]` that adaptive thermogenesis is mostly NEAT, nervous-system regulated rather than thyroid-driven. `[87:11]` Helms' closing reframe: diet breaks, nonlinear weekly calorie distribution and refeeds matter far more than an offseason two years ago. Norton is "pretty underwhelmed" by refeeds metabolically — the bump vanishes as soon as calories drop — and uses them for psychology.

## Practical application
- Jump calories substantially straight away post-show, and set the first 1-2 weeks at maintenance-or-slight-surplus calculated on your **new**, lighter body, not pre-diet maintenance.
- Helms' target: ~**5% over stage weight within 2-4 weeks** (a 180 lb competitor up ~10 lb within a month, "at least, and more"), then a measured surplus.
- Give structure, not freedom. Helms' sequence: show night eat anything; Sunday loose structure (three meals, no grazing, restaurants fine); Monday numbers — higher and more flexible than prep numbers.
- Norton's slow phase: **10 g carbs and 2 g fat per week** is below food-label error individually but substantial over 20 weeks. He counts **±5 g on each macro as 100% compliance** ("macro clouds").
- Norton's normality check `[75:54]`: if breakfast takes over 30 minutes to prepare, you're not recovered.
- Replacing lost NEAT via step targets is defensible but risky — Helms warns it becomes another 24/7 neurosis on top of macros and burns athletes out across 4-8 month preps.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Henselmans' metabolic damage paper was **in review**, not published, at recording.
- Norton's set-point argument rests on **one rat study** plus one animal weight-cycling experiment; he acknowledges this and can't see how a human version would run.
- His client cases are self-reported intake; he calls the "lost 10 lb going up to 350 g carbs" case one of the biggest outliers he has seen.
- Little data on refeeds or diet breaks as competitors actually use them — existing work is mostly intermittent calorie restriction (severe deficit two days, maintenance five).
- **Uncertain names from auto-captions:** "Alfred Dulu"/"Doo" (almost certainly Abdul Dulloo); "Brown Lell" (likely Brownell); "Scott Ael" (likely Scott Abel); "Dr. Corey Probes," quoted for "self-control is fatiguing"; "Dr. Megan Clabunde," an eating disorder specialist Norton interviewed.
