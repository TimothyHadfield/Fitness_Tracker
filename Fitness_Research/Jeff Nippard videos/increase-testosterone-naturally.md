# How to Increase Testosterone Naturally

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How to Increase Testosterone Naturally | Science Explained" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yoP6NCGb3oM)) · 2017-06-29 · 12 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Acute post-workout testosterone spikes from heavy compounds are **too small and too brief to drive muscle growth**. Heavy compound training works because of progressive tension overload and large muscle mass involvement — the hormone bump is a side effect.
- Raising testosterone from a **hypogonadal level (~300 ng/dL) to normal (~600 ng/dL) clearly helps** body composition. Moving from mid-normal to high-normal probably does little.
- The biggest natural levers are **body composition and calorie intake**: sit around **10-15% body fat**, avoid aggressive deficits and chronic overfeeding.
- Keep **fat at 20-40% of calories**, avoid zinc deficiency, supplement **up to 3,000 IU/day vitamin D** if sun exposure is low, sleep properly, limit alcohol and de-stress.
- Testosterone-boosting supplements are essentially not worth the money.

## Acute training-induced testosterone is a red herring [00:00]
Foundational work from the 1990s and early 2000s led by **Dr. Bill Kraemer** showed that higher training intensity (heavier loads) and more muscle mass involved (compound movements) produce larger testosterone increases. That led to a generation of programming built around chasing the hormone spike.

But more recent data from **West and colleagues at McMaster University** concluded that post-exercise increases in these hormones **cannot be used as proxy markers for hypertrophic potential in human skeletal muscle**. In their data testosterone did rise with a high-intensity, large-muscle-mass protocol, yet there was **no difference in muscle protein synthesis between groups**.

Crucially, these rises are **acute** — testosterone spikes and returns to baseline within about an hour. That's nowhere near the chronic hormonal shift needed to affect muscle growth.

## How much of an increase would actually matter? [01:33]
A 2017 paper gives a harmonised normal range for healthy non-obese American and European men aged 19-39 of **264-916 ng/dL**.

**The landmark 1996 *New England Journal of Medicine* study** split 40 men into four groups over 10 weeks:
1. Trained naturally
2. **600 mg testosterone/week + weight training**
3. **600 mg testosterone/week, no training**
4. Placebo, no training

The training programme was crude — 4 sets of 6 on squat and bench, 3x/week. Results: the testosterone-plus-training group gained most, placebo least. The striking finding is that **600 mg/week with no resistance training produced more muscle mass than training naturally did**. A better-optimised programme might have narrowed that gap.

But note the scale: baseline was around **500 ng/dL**, and after 10 weeks of injections subjects were around **3,000 ng/dL** — orders of magnitude beyond any acute training-induced change.

**Bhasin and colleagues** showed that even without weight training, raising testosterone from **306 to 570 ng/dL over 20 weeks** produced roughly **3 kg (about 6.5 lb) of fat-free mass** by hydrostatic weighing and DEXA. So correcting a genuinely low level almost certainly helps. Whether going from mid-normal to high-normal does anything is unclear.

## Training volume and overtraining [04:39]
Overtraining is defined in the literature as any increase in training volume and/or intensity resulting in long-term performance detriments. Overdo it long enough and testosterone drops.

**Volume is worse for testosterone than intensity.** A study by **Fry et al.** found that performing **10 one-rep-max squats every day for two weeks did not change testosterone concentrations**. By contrast, multiple studies find large volume increases reduce it.

**Concurrent training:** the most recent work by **Jones et al.** found that combining strength and endurance in the same session, **3 days/week for 6 weeks, did not significantly decrease testosterone** versus strength training alone. Earlier research did report a decrease. The likely reconciliation is total volume — do too much of both and physiological stress pushes the endocrine balance toward catabolism (testosterone down, cortisol up).

## Diet [05:40]

**Soy is not a problem.** The most comprehensive meta-analysis on the topic (32 studies) concluded that neither soy foods nor isoflavone supplements alter bioavailable testosterone in men. Examine.com's practical framing: **1-2 servings of soy food daily and under 25 g of soy protein from non-concentrate sources** doesn't affect testosterone; effects appear only when super-loaded. Soy protein *concentrate* is fine even in high amounts because its isoflavone content — the compounds that bind estrogen receptors — is negligible.

**Body composition and calories are the main drivers.**
- A 12-month case study of a natural bodybuilder through contest prep: as intake fell from **4,750 to 2,100 calories/day** and body fat from **14.8% to 4.5%**, testosterone fell from **991 to 227 ng/dL — a 77% drop**.
- A 2008 study on competitive wrestlers found **just 2-3 weeks** of caloric deficit at **500-1,000 calories/day** caused a **62% decrease** in testosterone.
- Conversely, overweight individuals tend to have lower testosterone, and chronic overfeeding also reduces it.
- Estimated sweet spot: **10-15% body fat** for most men.

**Macronutrients.** Higher-fat diets generally outperform low-fat, though total energy intake is likely the real driver. Keeping fat at **20-40% of calories** should be sufficient. There's a slight trend toward saturated fat raising testosterone more than other fats. Magnitudes are small: one study showed a low-fat diet reduced testosterone by **12%** in older men; another showed a higher-fat diet raised it by **13%** in young men.

**Micronutrients** matter mostly through deficiency:
- **Zinc deficiency lowers testosterone** — eat meat, shellfish, beans and certain nuts.
- **Vitamin D** regulates testosterone. Depending on sun exposure, supplementing **up to 3,000 IU/day** can increase it.

**Supplements to skip or discount:**
- **D-aspartic acid** — weak evidence; appears to work for only 6-12 days before levels return to normal, with no body composition changes.
- **Ashwagandha** — one study showing a **15% increase** in young healthy men; otherwise no evidence.
- **Tribulus** — no evidence. **Horny goat weed** — no human evidence. **Fenugreek** — conflicting evidence.

## Sleep, stress and sexual activity [09:14]
The sleep correlation is weaker than expected but real:
- A **2011** study: cutting sleep from **8 to 5 hours for 5 nights reduced testosterone by 10.4%** in young men.
- A **2012** study: cutting from **10 to 4 hours for 5 nights** produced only a non-significant trend.
- **33 hours of straight sleep deprivation** produced a **27% average drop** in 24 young men.

Sexual and social factors:
- A study in the *Journal of Evolution and Human Behavior*: a **5-minute conversation with an attractive woman raised testosterone by 30%** in men.
- Sexual arousal from pornographic film increases testosterone secretion; one study found watching a **4-minute erotic clip before training significantly improved 3RM squat strength**.
- A **2015** paper: single men and men in new relationships (under 12 months) had significantly higher testosterone than men in long-term relationships. Earlier research found polyamorous men and women had higher testosterone than single people, with singly-partnered individuals lowest of all.
- **These are correlations only** — higher-testosterone people may simply be more likely to seek multiple relationships.
- The generalisable advice: **have regular sex**. Testosterone rises after evening coitus in both men and women, while on nights without intercourse it declines through the night.
- **Ejaculation itself is not associated with testosterone changes** — the ejaculatory process is regulated by serotonin and nitric oxide, not testosterone.

Also: de-stress (meditation, relaxation) and avoid excessive alcohol, which reduces testosterone.

## Practical application
- Don't program around hormone spikes. Program around progressive overload.
- Hold body fat around **10-15%**; avoid both prolonged aggressive deficits and chronic surpluses.
- Fat intake **20-40% of calories**, including some saturated fat.
- Get zinc from food; supplement **vitamin D up to 3,000 IU/day** if sun-deprived.
- Keep cardio **low intensity, low-to-moderate frequency, shorter duration** when muscle and strength are the goal, so total training stress stays manageable.
- Sleep adequately, limit alcohol, manage stress, maintain regular sexual activity.
- Don't buy testosterone boosters.

## Caveats & unknowns
- It is unclear whether moving within the normal range (e.g. 600 to 800 ng/dL) produces any measurable body composition benefit.
- Relationship-status and testosterone findings are correlational and the causal direction is unresolved.
- Individually, the natural levers here are small. The argument is that stacking several of them may add up to something worthwhile — not that any one is transformative.
- The 1996 NEJM injection study used a supraphysiological dose and a poorly optimised training programme; it establishes that exogenous testosterone works, not that natural manipulation does.
