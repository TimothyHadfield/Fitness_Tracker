# Lateral Raise Technique for Capped Side Delts

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Build Capped Shoulders: Optimal Training Explained (Side Delts)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v_ZkxWzYnMc)) · 2019-01-29 · 8 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- The lateral raise may be the single most important isolation exercise for physique, because pressing movements barely hit the side delt.
- Train it light and high-rep: **10-20 reps, sometimes up to 30**. Most people can't maintain form past **15-30 lb dumbbells**.
- Progress by improving technique, mind-muscle connection and pump — not by adding weight indefinitely.
- Raise in the **scapular plane, 15-30 degrees forward**, and stop when the elbow reaches shoulder height.
- Going too heavy is by far the most common error; turning it into a front raise is the second.

## Why isolate the side delt
Capped lateral delts create the X-frame — wider shoulders and the illusion of a smaller waist. And the heavy compounds don't do the job: a **2013 study** found the free-weight lateral raise and cable lateral raise both produced very high side delt activation, while the shoulder press and bench press produced comparably minimal activity. So the side delt needs isolation work.

**Muscles involved:** primarily **shoulder abduction** (raising the arm out to the side), handled by the lateral deltoid. Unless the raise is perfectly out to the side there's some shoulder flexion as the dumbbells drift forward, involving the **front delts**. The scapular upward rotation component is handled by the **upper traps** — which cueing can partly take out of the movement.

## Variation choice [01:09]
Jeff's personal favourite is the **Egyptian cable lateral raise**: pass the cable between your legs and lean *into* the direction of the raise. The rationale for leaning comes from data from McMahon et al. (auto-captions render this "mcmahon atoll") showing the middle deltoid matters more near the **end range** of motion while the supraspinatus is more involved near the **beginning**. Passing the cable between the legs also gives a comfortable position where the cable can't hit your body.

Cables also provide more consistent tension than dumbbells, since a dumbbell's circular resistance path means peak tension at the top and minimal tension at the bottom. The hypertrophy implications of that aren't clear, so Jeff's advice is to pick a variation you like, can feel in your delts, and can get better at over time. Bands, machines and kettlebells all work; experimentation is fine, but there's a limit to the extra benefit from making the movement increasingly convoluted.

## Load and rep range [02:41]
Single-joint isolation with a long lever arm means **lighter weight, higher reps: 10-20, or even up to 30**. There's a point — usually around **15-30 lb dumbbells** — where further load increases guarantee form breakdown. Past that, progressive overload should come from **better technique, better mind-muscle connection, or a better pump** rather than more weight.

For perspective: Kai Greene at 300 lb body weight does high-rep working sets with 20 lb dumbbells.

## Technique [03:12]
**Reduce momentum.** Performing the movement seated helps, though the bench can get in the way; alternatively keep the glutes flexed throughout to prevent hip movement.

**Grip:** index finger up against the inside head of the dumbbell, pinky closer to the middle of the handle (unless it feels awkward). This forces the side delts to work harder by increasing the internal rotation moment at the top. Many find a thumbless grip more comfortable; full grip or false grip are both fine since the weight is light.

**Clicking shoulder?** Try flexing/pulling the lats in between reps — in Jeff's experience this often reduces it.

**The rep:**
- Retract the shoulder blades.
- Initiate by leaning **out and up with the elbow** rather than lifting the dumbbell straight out to the side.
- Raise in the **scapular plane — about 15-30 degrees forward** when viewed from above.
- Think about flexing only the lateral delt to move the weight, keeping forearms and upper arms soft.

**Cues Jeff finds useful:**
- Think about pulling the distal (outer) aspect of the delt toward the medial (inner) aspect throughout the positive.
- Think **"sweep the weight out"** rather than "lift the weight up" — sweeping out recruits the side delts over the traps, which get more involved when you shrug the weight up.
- Past the first half of the concentric, **slightly internally rotate** — pinky slightly higher than thumb — which may shift emphasis off the front delt onto the side delt. **Caveat:** research suggests this cue may increase impingement risk. If you use it, keep the shoulder blades retracted, lift in the scapular plane, and end the ROM once the elbow reaches shoulder height.

**Greg Nuckols' suggestion** (MASS Research Review panel, given to Jeff just before recording): because lateral delt fibres run in many directions, it may make most sense to do **some reps internally rotated, some neutral, and some externally rotated** so all fibres are stimulated meaningfully.

**The negative** is a controlled reversal. As an advanced technique you can keep constant tension by starting the next rep without the dumbbells coming to a full stop — but while mastering form, release tension at the bottom and start each rep from a quick pause, which prevents swinging and momentum as fatigue sets in.

## Common errors [05:44]
**1. Going too heavy.** Even at a high level, most people can't hold form beyond roughly 30 lb dumbbells. Load more and you progressively recruit other muscles that are already better trained and better targeted elsewhere. Keep posture consistent — fatigue at the end of a set should show up as an inability to raise the arms as high with everything else held identical, not as body English. (Jeff isn't against controlled cheating, but treats it as an advanced intensity technique.)

**2. Turning the lateral raise into a front raise.** The scapular plane is only 15-30 degrees forward of perpendicular. Lifting excessively out in front defeats the purpose, emphasising the front delt, which is already hammered by any bench or overhead pressing.

## Alternatives
Apart from cable, band and machine variations, Jeff doesn't think there's much of a replacement. A properly performed **upright row** hits the lateral delts well but, being a compound, involves the traps much more heavily.

## Practical application
- Include a dedicated lateral raise; pressing won't cover the side delt.
- 10-20 reps (up to 30), with dumbbells in the 15-30 lb range for most people.
- Once form limits load, progress via technique, mind-muscle connection and pump instead of weight.
- Rep pattern: retract scapulae → lead with the elbow out and up → stay in the scapular plane 15-30 degrees forward → stop at shoulder height → controlled negative → brief pause at the bottom.
- Consider splitting reps across internal, neutral and external rotation within a session.
- Sit down or keep glutes flexed to kill momentum.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The McMahon citation's name is garbled in the auto-captions; treat the spelling as uncertain.
- Whether the dumbbell's inconsistent resistance curve matters for hypertrophy is unclear.
- The pinky-up internal rotation cue carries a possible impingement risk per research Jeff references but does not name.
- Greg Nuckols' multi-rotation suggestion is offered as an idea worth trying, not an evidenced protocol.
