# Bench Press Tips From Coaching a 405 lb Bench

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How I Coached My Client To A 405 Lb Bench (My Best Bench Press Tips)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rlF0uYMfCyA)) · 2020-02-20 · 13 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Treat every warm-up rep exactly like a working rep — same setup, same technique, same cues.
- Gear (heeled shoes, belt, wrist wraps, chalk) each adds a small amount; none is worth 30 lb alone, but stacked they matter.
- Use a moderate elbow tuck: tuck on the negative, touch at the bottom of the sternum, flare as you press.
- Consistent bar path and honest RPE are what actually let progression continue week to week.
- Bench tends to be one of the first lifts to suffer in a deficit and one that responds well to a surplus.

Note: this is a training vlog with a fair amount of chatter; the genuinely instructional content is modest and is captured below.

## Tip 1 — Warm up like you mean it
Make every warm-up rep look like it has heavy working weight on the bar. Same setup, same technique, same mind-muscle and strength cues you'd use on your top set [04:33].

## Tip 2 — Use training gear (from the ground up)
- **Heeled shoe:** lets him plant the heels — a requirement for competitive powerlifting — and feel more stable on the floor, contributing to better leg drive.
- **Belt:** the most common question he gets about his bench. Increases intra-abdominal pressure so that when he performs the Valsalva manoeuvre and holds his breath before descending, he creates a more rigid compartment through the torso, reducing energy loss through the system and making a more rigid column head to foot. He also finds the belt gives him something to push air against, and is stronger with it than without. Effect size varies between people — worth experimenting with, same logic as squat and deadlift.
- **Wrist wraps:** keep the wrist neutral. Many people let the bar settle back into the upper hand rather than sitting directly over the wrist joint. That creates a moment arm between the joint and the load, and the forearm has to work to stop the bar tipping back. Bar directly over the joint removes that moment arm; wraps make holding that position easier.
- **Chalk:** potentially the biggest of the four, especially if you take a wider grip. On bars that aren't gritty, the hands tend to slip outward as the set goes on — unsafe and inefficient. Chalk gives a stable grip.

He frames all of these as a shotgun approach to cues: throw a lot of things at a lifter, keep what works for you, discard what doesn't. (He also notes he's sponsored by the gear brand shown, but says the brand doesn't matter.)

## Tip 3 — Find the appropriate elbow tuck
Three positions compared [09:40]:
- **Wide grip with flared elbows, touching the upper chest** (near-guillotine) — how most bodybuilders bench. Not optimal for strength; fine for hypertrophy, though some people get cranky shoulders.
- **Extreme tuck** — bar brought way down toward the stomach with elbows out in front of the bar. Common among powerlifters but, in his view, only optimal if you're wearing a bench shirt, which nobody in a regular gym is.
- **Moderate tuck (his recommendation)** — let the elbows tuck slightly on the negative and bring the bar to the highest contact point on your chest, usually the bottom of the sternum: not down on the stomach, not up on the upper chest. Then flare as you press — think tuck down on the negative, flare out as you press up, rather than staying tucked with elbows ahead of the bar.

## What the client (Pat) credits
- **Consistent setup and bar path**, so that week-to-week progress is real rather than the bar being cheated up.
- **Hitting the correct RPE.** If you train too hard and can't recover, the following weeks stall. Leave the ego at the door to preserve room for progression.
- **Being in a calorie surplus.** Nippard adds that in his coaching experience bench is one of the first lifts to take a hit when cutting, and Pat's bench exploded during a surplus despite some added fat.

Session structure shown: three sets of three at roughly 85% of 1RM, autoregulated by RPE and pyramided up rather than fixed. With a ~415 lb max the prescribed weight was ~350 lb, but they ramped further since Pat was restarting a program and the weight felt light. He worked up toward 365 lb.

## On stalling and restarting a block
Nippard's own 10-week program was split into three blocks: block one focused on adapting to increased frequency with technique emphasis, moderate volume and low-to-moderate intensity; block two increased effort and intensity mainly through added top sets at higher RPE, plus a slight volume increase; block three opens with a deload and finishes with an AMRAP test week, typically as many reps as possible at around 90% of 1RM.

He got sick at the start of the deload week and the light weights felt heavier than in block two, so the deload didn't function as one. He cancelled the max test as the responsible move, planning to rerun block one or truncate it and rerun the peaking-style block two before deloading and testing.

He cites a theory from fellow YouTuber Radu Antonio: people stop progressing because of a repeating cycle of micro-injuries and illnesses — you get hurt, progress stalls, you recover, you catch a cold, it stalls again — and that without those interruptions progress would be more steadily incremental. Not all of it is preventable.

## Practical application
Set up your warm-ups identically to your top set. Try a belt, wrist wraps, heeled shoes and chalk one at a time and keep what helps. Touch at the bottom of the sternum with a moderate tuck, flare as you press. Film or otherwise verify your bar path is repeatable. When sick or unrecovered, cancel the test rather than push through — and expect bench to be your first lift to suffer in a deficit.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Gear effects are individual; he repeatedly says none of these will add 30 lb on its own.
- The moderate-tuck recommendation is for raw strength — he considers the flared bodybuilding style fine for hypertrophy.
- Radu Antonio's micro-injury account is described as a theory, not evidence.
- Pat's surplus, program and technique changes were concurrent, so the 405 lb result can't be attributed to any single factor.
