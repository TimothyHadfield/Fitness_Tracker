# Overrated or Underrated: 21 Controversial Fitness Topics with Layne Norton

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "OVERRATED: Explaining Controversial Fitness Topics" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0ucinlkWQ0)) · 2023-04-20 · 13 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

Nippard and Dr. Layne Norton (PhD in nutritional sciences, pro natural bodybuilder and powerlifter) rank items across training, nutrition, supplements and general fitness topics.

## Bottom line
- Training to failure is **overrated** — stopping within a couple of reps of failure gets nearly all the benefit.
- Creatine monohydrate is the one supplement worth the money; turkesterone, BCAAs, EAAs and testosterone boosters are not.
- Ice baths are objectively bad for muscle growth — they blunt muscle protein synthesis and hypertrophy long term.
- Fasted cardio, the anabolic window, "eating clean", detoxes and spot reduction are all overrated or outright meaningless.
- Full range of motion is underrated in general, but the specific thing that matters is loading the stretched position and the hardest part of the lift.

## Training

**Training to failure — overrated.** Bodybuilders overstate its importance. You only need to be reasonably close, usually within a couple of reps. Useful occasionally so you know what failure feels like, but the benefits are essentially the same stopping a few reps short.

**Sumo deadlifts — underrated.** Muscle activation differences between sumo and conventional are very small; the choice is individual. Norton uses sumo because his long legs make conventional hunch him over and load his lower back with fatigue on top of heavy squatting.

**Full range of motion — split.** Norton says **underrated**: many people train partials because the deep, uncomfortable positions feel alarming, and his research has shown that full ROM, or at least training in the stretched position, appears critical for maximizing hypertrophy. Nippard says **overrated within the savvy fitness crowd**, who put it on a pedestal — what actually matters is reaching the stretched portion and working through the hardest part of the lift. Both agree that simply doing half reps is not optimal.

**Powerlifting for bodybuilding — underrated.** Low reps still build muscle, and getting stronger means more weight in higher rep ranges later, which is progressive overload. Varying rep ranges is a good idea in both directions — bodybuilders should do some low-rep work, powerlifters some higher-rep work. Nippard adds a psychological benefit: natural bodybuilders can go months or years without visible physique progress, so an objective strength outcome is a useful motivational anchor.

**Lifting straps — underrated.** Unless you compete in powerlifting or strongman where grip is part of the sport, grip failure limits rows and pull-ups and can force suboptimal technique. Nippard gets a better mind-muscle connection with his back using straps, and trains forearms separately.

## Nutrition

**The post-workout anabolic window — overrated.** Getting quality protein and carbohydrate after training is probably a good idea, but nothing supports the 30-second urgency. Total daily protein intake matters far more. If you ate 1-2 hours before a 1-2 hour session, eating again 1-2 hours after is perfectly fine.

**Liver / organ meats — overrated.** Nutritious, with plenty of vitamins and minerals and high-quality protein — but so do fruits and vegetables, and you can get the same nutrients from a protein shake plus a multivitamin.

**Detoxing — overrated.** Norton's standard challenge: which specific compounds, by what mechanism, and why isn't the liver and kidneys already doing it? Nobody has an answer. It will not help you lose weight either — most detox products are laxatives, and the weight lost is from not eating and defecating.

**Kiwi fruit — split.** Norton calls it overrated as a policy: no single food is necessary. Nippard says underrated relative to more popular fruits like apples, oranges and bananas — it has more vitamin C per calorie than almost any fruit, and a few studies show improved sleep from eating kiwis. Both agree no food should be on a pedestal.

**"Eating clean" — overrated.** There is no objective definition (a vegan and a carnivore will give opposite answers). Even seemingly obvious cases fail: when calories are equated, high-sugar and low-sugar diets show no difference in body composition. Norton notes the concept drives disordered relationships with food.

## Supplements

**Turkesterone — overrated.** Popular almost entirely because of social media hype, largely because the name sounds like testosterone; with a duller name it would be nowhere. Serious quality control problems mean you don't know what you're taking. Mechanistically it does nothing: no increase in mTOR, no increase in muscle protein synthesis, no receptor for it in the muscle cell, no apparent effect on muscle protein breakdown.

**BCAAs — overrated.** Norton changed his mind around 2017-18 and removed BCAAs from his supplement line: if you get enough high-quality protein, adding BCAAs does nothing. Narrow exceptions where they might help: vegans struggling to hit protein, improving protein quality; and some evidence for reduced delayed-onset soreness, though whether that is independent of total protein intake is unknown.

**EAAs — better than BCAAs but still overrated for the cost.** You need all nine essential amino acids to build muscle tissue, so almost by definition they beat BCAAs. Possible niche: training fasted, or training in the morning when food sits badly in your stomach.

**Creatine monohydrate — underrated (Norton) / properly rated (Nippard).** Norton: underrated because it's boring and unsexy, yet it's the most effective safe supplement on the market with over three decades of research. Nippard: people know it works, but the boost is genuinely small — nobody blows up from creatine. Both agree the basic, cheapest, most mainstream form is the most effective one, so don't waste money on other forms.

**Testosterone boosters — overrated.** Two reasons: most don't work at all, and the ones that do produce very modest increases within the physiological range. Small changes in testosterone within normal range do not produce muscle gain — anabolic steroids raise testosterone multiple-fold above natural levels, which is what it takes.

**Ashwagandha — underrated.** Not many people discuss it, and there is compelling research showing it works through multiple mechanisms to improve recovery, strength, a small amount of testosterone ("who cares"), and possibly muscle mass. It also appears to have a notable anti-anxiety and stress-reducing effect for a herb, working through multiple pathways as an adaptogen.

## Hot fitness topics

**Ice baths — overrated, and actively harmful for hypertrophy.** They do reduce delayed-onset soreness and may improve recovery slightly, but they **attenuate muscle protein synthesis and hypertrophy over the long term** — objectively bad for muscle growth. Not a good idea if maximum muscle is the goal.

**Sauna — overrated for physique, possibly useful for health.** Some evidence suggests sauna reduces cardiovascular disease risk. For muscle and body composition it does nothing. On heat shock proteins: activating them can increase muscle protein synthesis mechanistically, but randomized controlled trials on sauna show **no increase in lean body mass** — the human outcome data is what counts, not the mechanism. Not harmful; the hype just outruns the evidence.

**Fasted cardio — overrated.** It does increase fat oxidation during the session, but that is not the same as losing body fat. Several RCTs equating calories and work show no difference between fasted and fed. Research also shows that oxidizing more fat during the session means oxidizing less across the rest of the day, so the 24-hour net is zero. If it wakes you up and makes the workout better, do it — it just isn't special.

**Spot reduction — overrated.** No compelling independent evidence exists; the studies showing an effect tend to be supplement-company sponsored. Your stubborn area is simply the last place fat will come off. Crunches will not target belly fat; you keep losing fat overall.

**"Toning" — overrated / meaningless.** A muscle can get bigger or smaller. It does not get more toned. The appearance people call toned is enough muscular development to see the muscle plus low enough body fat to see it. For women worried about getting too big: once you reach the muscle mass you want, stop training harder. You get the look by losing fat and/or building muscle — no wraps or creams required.

## Practical application
Take creatine monohydrate (cheapest form), consider ashwagandha, skip everything else on the supplement list. Train close to failure rather than to it. Use straps freely on back work. Load the stretched position. Don't schedule your protein around a 30-minute window; hit your daily total. Skip the ice bath if you're training for size — and if you like the sauna, use it for cardiovascular health, not gains.

## Caveats & unknowns
Both admit bias on sumo deadlifts (both pull sumo). BCAA effects on soreness may not be independent of total protein intake. The full-ROM disagreement is really about framing — they agree on the underlying mechanics. Norton's "kiwi is overrated" is a blanket policy on single foods, not a specific criticism.
