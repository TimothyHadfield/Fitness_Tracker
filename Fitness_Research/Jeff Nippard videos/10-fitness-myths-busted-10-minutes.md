# 10 Fitness Myths Busted (Myth-Busting Season 1 Recap, Part 1)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "10 Fitness Myths Busted In 10 Minutes" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YspLU2ttOlc)) · 2018-10-08 · 10 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- High protein does not harm bones or kidneys in healthy people; the osteoporosis scare rests on 1970s-80s methodology.
- The anabolic window is roughly 4-6 hours around training, not 30 minutes — total daily protein beats timing.
- Sugar makes overeating easy but isn't independently causative for obesity or type 2 diabetes; US sugar intake fell after 1999 while obesity kept climbing.
- Per-meal protein: 0.4-0.55 g/kg bodyweight across four meals (32-44 g/meal for an 80 kg lifter).
- "Starvation mode" doesn't exist as a fat-loss stall — look at under-reporting, water retention, or suppressed spontaneous activity instead.

## 1. High protein wrecks your bones and kidneys
**Verdict: busted.**
[00:31] The osteoporosis claim traces to a handful of outdated studies from the 1970s and 80s using obsolete methodology. A 2017 systematic review from the National Osteoporosis Foundation, using newer and better methods, concluded dietary protein's effect on the skeleton appears favourable to a small extent and is not detrimental. On kidneys, a 2007 World Health Organization report concluded that while high protein intake clearly contributes to deterioration in patients who *already have* renal disease, restricting protein in healthy subjects has no foundation.

## 2. The post-workout anabolic window
**Verdict: busted as commonly stated.**
[01:04] Two problems. First, assuming a normal pre-workout meal containing protein, its amino acids are still available for delivery to muscle in the post-workout period — the pre-workout meal doubles as the immediate post-workout meal. Second, resistance training elevates muscle protein synthesis for at least 48 hours, with the initial spike in sensitivity lasting about 2.5-3 hours. Schoenfeld and others therefore propose a roughly 4-6 hour window around training, pre- and post-workout meals included.

Exceptions where you should get protein in fast: training fasted, or training twice in the same day. Otherwise total daily protein matters far more than timing.

## 3. Sugar makes you fat and causes diabetes
**Verdict: busted, with a caveat.**
[02:06] Sugar makes it much easier to over-consume total calories, but doesn't appear to play a causative role in obesity or type 2 diabetes. NHANES data 1980-2013 shows sugar intake has actually decreased since 1999 while obesity rates continued to rise. Rather than villainising a single nutrient, look at diet and lifestyle as a whole, with particular caution around calorie-dense, highly palatable, highly processed foods.

Numbers: the WHO recommends sugar occupy no more than 10% of total calories, but that figure is based on preventing dental caries. Nippard prefers Alan Aragon's ceiling of 50 g fructose per day, which — if fructose is roughly half of total sugar — works out to about 100 g total sugar per day for otherwise healthy active people.

## 4. Weight training stunts growth in kids
**Verdict: busted, cleanly.**
[03:06] The myth originates in 1960s observational data finding Japanese child labourers tended to be short. Correlation isn't causation, and essentially every regulatory body now holds the position that no scientific evidence indicates resistance training adversely affects growth during childhood or adolescence.

## 5. You can only absorb 20-30 g of protein per meal
**Verdict: busted on "absorb", real question is "use".**
[03:37] The gut has a virtually unlimited capacity to absorb amino acids into the blood — eat 500 g of protein in one meal and essentially all of it is absorbed. The useful question is how much can be used for muscle building. A study published a month after his original video answered it: consume **0.4 to 0.55 g of protein per kg bodyweight per meal, spread across four meals per day**. For an 80 kg (175 lb) person that's **32-44 g per meal, four meals a day**.

Counterpoint: intermittent fasting research suggests you can still maximise muscle building eating all your protein at once, and IF adherents eating 80-100+ g in a single meal don't appear to struggle to build muscle. Setting IF aside, his best guess for the per-meal maximum is somewhere between 20 and 40 g for most people. Total daily protein still takes priority over distribution or timing.

## 6. Fasted cardio burns more fat
**Verdict: busted.**
[05:08] Burning more fat during a session doesn't mean losing more fat over 24 hours. Paoli and colleagues (captions render this "peléan" — name uncertain) showed that burning more fat during a cardio session means burning less over the following 24 hours. A 2017 systematic review concluded fasted versus fed exercise doesn't increase weight loss or fat mass loss. There's no specific harm either — no negative impact on muscle mass. Do cardio when it fits your schedule and energy; net 24-hour energy balance decides fat loss.

## 7. Carbs before bed make you fat
**Verdict: busted.**
[06:12] The reasoning is that metabolism slows during sleep. It doesn't reliably: sleep metabolic rate is lower than resting metabolic rate in obese subjects but *higher* than resting metabolic rate in healthy non-obese subjects. On top of that, dietary carbohydrate isn't readily converted to body fat — de novo lipogenesis is uncommon in humans. Eat on the schedule that suits your preferences, appetite and life, while staying aware that late-night snacking driven by sleep deprivation or TV can add calories.

## 8. Foam rolling
**Verdict: mixed, no strong case either way.**
[06:42] Research is extremely mixed. Some studies show reduced muscle soreness, others none — particularly when compared against regular dynamic stretching. Same conflict for range of motion: many studies show improvements, but the effect may be negated when foam rolling is added to a dynamic stretching routine. A good warm-up including dynamic stretching appears sufficient on its own for injury prevention and recovery. Since there's no detriment, Nippard still foam rolls **2-3 minutes before training**, especially on leg days or when unusually tight.

## 9. Energy drinks are harmful
**Verdict: partly true, dose-dependent.**
[07:42] The two ingredients of concern are sugar and caffeine. Sugar in fluid form makes it easy to over-consume nutrient-devoid empty calories, so choose diet versions — you'd need **32 cans of a diet drink to reach the acceptable daily intake for acesulfame K**, and **6 cans for the ADI of sucralose**, and those ADIs are themselves conservative lifetime-consumption estimates. Caffeine at moderate doses is genuinely beneficial for energy, focus and performance, but can cause cardiovascular problems in excess; **Health Canada recommends no more than 400 mg caffeine per day**. Actual safety depends on age, activity level, heart condition, and pregnancy status. His position: moderate consumption is fine for non-pregnant, otherwise healthy active adults.

## 10. Starvation mode
**Verdict: busted.**
[08:43] Metabolism does slow as you lose weight, but there's no point at which fat loss stops or reverses on very low calories — visibly true in genuinely starving people, who are emaciated, not weight-stable. If you're not losing on low calories, three likelier explanations:
1. **You're eating more than you think.** People are notoriously bad at tracking and reporting intake, and overweight people tend to under-report most.
2. **Water retention is masking fat loss.** Stress, poor sleep, high fibre intake and similar can hold water mass while fat mass falls.
3. **Suppressed spontaneous physical activity.** Very low calories subconsciously cut fidgeting, walking around and general movement, lowering total daily energy expenditure.

## Practical application
- Set a total daily protein target first; distribute it over ~4 meals at 0.4-0.55 g/kg each if convenient, but don't stress timing.
- Treat any meal with protein within a few hours either side of training as covering the window.
- Cap total sugar around 100 g/day (≈50 g fructose) as a working ballpark if otherwise healthy and active.
- Cap caffeine at 400 mg/day; prefer diet energy drinks if you drink them at all.
- Foam roll 2-3 min pre-training if you like it; don't expect it to do more than a dynamic warm-up.
- Diet stalled? Audit your food logging, check for water retention, and check whether you've gone sedentary — before blaming your metabolism.

## Caveats & unknowns
This is a rapid recap; Nippard explicitly notes he skipped detail and that several of these "myths" contain real truth in some circumstances — they're rarely binary. The captions garble at least one researcher name (myth 6). Per-meal protein figures come from a single then-new paper.
