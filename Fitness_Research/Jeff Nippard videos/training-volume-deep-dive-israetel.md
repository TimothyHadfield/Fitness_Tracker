# Training Volume Landmarks: MV, MEV, MAV and MRV (ft. Dr. Mike Israetel)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "TRAINING VOLUME & HYPERTROPHY: How Much Do You Need? ft. Dr. Mike Israetel" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2kwl5LiuCs4)) · 2018-06-17 · 69 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Productive training lives between **MEV (minimum effective volume)** and **MRV (maximum recoverable volume)**. Start a mesocycle at MEV and climb toward MRV, then deload.
- Ballpark numbers for an average intermediate, per muscle per week: **maintenance ~6-8 hard sets**, **MEV ~8-12 (call it 10)**, **MRV ~20**. These are starting points, not laws — expect to spend ~6 months learning your own.
- Count **hard working sets**, not tonnage. Warm-ups don't count. Keep reps between **5 and 30**, and mostly **5-20**.
- **~2 reps in reserve is the best average stopping point** — hypertrophy rises roughly linearly toward failure but fatigue rises exponentially.
- Save true failure for the **last week of a mesocycle before a deload**, and preferentially on low-fatigue exercises (leg extensions, pushdowns, curls) rather than squats.

## Why volume landmarks exist
[07:16] Israetel and James Hoffman originally coined **MRV** while working with collegiate teams — not to advocate more volume, but the opposite: to tell coaches there is a ceiling of work an athlete can recover from, beyond which training is unproductive, counterproductive, and eventually dangerous. Sport practice, weight training and technique work all fill the same cup.

[08:47] The full framework, four landmarks:
- **MV (maintenance volume)** — the least work needed to hold what you have. Roughly **half the average of MEV-to-MRV**: for many people **6-8 hard sets per muscle per week** maintains size and strength. Notably lower than MEV, which is what makes prioritisation possible.
- **MEV (minimum effective volume)** — the least that still produces gains. For the average intermediate, about **10 sets per muscle per week (range 8-12)**.
- **MAV (maximum adaptive volume)** — theoretically the volume that yields the most gains at a given moment. Practically hard to pin down because it **moves**: at the start of a program MAV sits near MEV, and by the end of a mesocycle it drifts up to near MRV. This is exactly why you can't just "start at MAV."
- **MRV (maximum recoverable volume)** — the most you can recover from, defined operationally: if you bench 225 for 4 hard sets of 10 and next week you get 8/6/4/4/3, you didn't recover. Around **20 sets per muscle per week** is where most people's MRV sits on average, per Israetel's coaching data and now Schoenfeld and other formal research.

[10:51] His pointed argument against "minimum effective dose" thinking: MEV means by definition minimum effect. Why aim there? Optimal volume is somewhere between MEV and MRV.

## Individual variation is the whole point
[21:33] Israetel's own hamstring MRV is **10-15 sets/week** (leg curls plus hip hinges combined); his side delt MRV is in the **mid-to-high 20s**. A one-size program prescribing 15-20 sets for everything would burn his hamstrings off the bone and leave his side delts undertrained. That's the flaw in fixed-volume programs — 5/3/1 is structurally excellent but identical volume for everyone: right for a few, too much for a very few, not enough for most. Good programs should say "start around here, see how you respond, adjust."

[21:02] Volume landmarks also shift cycle to cycle: quad MRV might be 22 sets one mesocycle, 20 the next, 24 the next. Learn your ranges over ~6 months per body part, then adjust weekly based on performance.

## How to count volume
[14:26] Volume technically is force × distance, but comparing tonnage across people is useless and range of motion is roughly constant within a person (one reason Israetel and Eric Helms push consistent technique — you can't compare squats done to different depths).

[16:28] The reason to use **number of hard sets** (Israetel credits Greg Nuckols with the framing) is that it tracks **fatigue** best. Very heavy low-rep sets are disproportionately fatiguing per rep; very high-rep sets are not fatiguing per rep at all. Sets between about **5 and 20 reps** produce broadly similar fatigue — 10 sets of 6 and 10 sets of 15 land in the same neighbourhood — which makes sets a workable common currency.

**Warm-ups don't count.** [18:00] They're easy enough that they can actually *reduce* fatigue — if you're fried on Monday, doing only warm-up sets on Wednesday can drop fatigue further by Friday than skipping entirely (blood flow, possibly neurological and hormonal effects). Also, if your warm-ups add meaningful volume, your warm-ups are wrong (working up to 405 in sets of 10 is insane).

**Drop sets and supersets:** [19:30] Safe default is to count a superset as two sets. In practice, a leg press superset into squats might genuinely be two sets of fatigue, while cable curls supersetted into 3-5 upright rows to finish the biceps might proxy as **one set**. Roughly 1.5 sets is a decent guess but it's individual — experiment.

## Rep ranges
[24:09] Anything **below 5 reps** is disproportionately fatiguing and underwhelming for hypertrophy — great for strength, poor stimulus-to-fatigue. His illustration: 20 total reps as 95% 1RM squat singles will destroy you; four sets of 10 gives 40 reps of decent work at a fraction of the fatigue and more hypertrophy.

[25:09] At the other end, **sets of 50-70 reps** waste most of their reps: the first 50-60 are just accumulating fatigue and lactate to get fast-twitch motor units firing once the slow-twitch fibres fade. Maybe the last 5-10 reps are challenging. Huge volume for no reason — inefficient unless you're injured.

So the hypertrophy window is roughly **5-30 reps, and mostly 5-20**. Israetel says he doesn't question a program in that band; he starts asking why when he sees anything below 5 or above 30 (even above 20).

## Effort: how close to failure
[27:10] The right effort metric is **relative intensity** — for bodybuilding work, **reps in reserve**; at very low reps where RIR is 1 or less, RPE (8.5, 9.5) works better. Below roughly **5 RIR** there are still hypertrophic effects but they're much smaller. Productive range: **~4-5 RIR through to failure**. Combined with rep range: **hard sets of 5-20 reps leaving no more than 4-5 reps in reserve.**

[29:42] On the Fischer and Steele position (failure matters more than volume): Israetel grants they have **one valid point** — the closer to failure, the greater the hypertrophic response *per unit of volume*. A set of 12 to failure grows more than a set of 12 with reps left. But fatigue accumulation from approaching failure is **exponential**, while hypertrophy gain is roughly **linear**.

[30:42] Hence his headline claim, which he says he'd stake much of his scientific reputation on: **around 2 reps in reserve is the best average place to end a set for hypertrophy** — the best trade-off of stimulus to fatigue. Stopping at 4 RIR gives low fatigue but modest benefit; going to failure gives linearly more growth at exponentially more fatigue.

[32:13] The failure-training camp answers "how do I grow today?" but never "how does today set up next week?" Israetel used to train all sets to failure and crashed into MRV every third week — unable to match his sets and reps, which is exactly the operational definition of exceeding MRV.

[33:14] He notes every good review of the literature finds **volume more important than relative intensity**, and that in the real world almost no bodybuilder — even heavily assisted ones — takes every set to concentric failure; they keep reps in the tank to do more sets.

### When failure does belong
[37:53] Not "where can we get away with it" but "when is it the best use of effort." The answer falls out of progressive overload: start a mesocycle at ~**4 RIR**, add weight while holding reps, and RIR falls automatically — 3, then 2, then to failure or near it in the **final weeks of the accumulation phase**, right before a deload. Two reasons that works: you need something special to overload the last week, and the deload is coming to erase the fatigue.

[39:27] Exercise selection matters here: don't take squats to failure unless you have a death wish; bench only with a trustworthy spotter; **leg extensions, triceps pushdowns and biceps curls — fail away**. Any week other than the last, failure training is just robbing next week.

[39:57] Asked about the common rule "leave 2-3 in the tank on the first sets, take the final set of the final exercise to failure" — Israetel calls it **safe but not best**, because you still have that muscle's next session to worry about.

## Beginners should train further from failure, not closer
[42:31] Israetel pushes back hard on the idea that beginners undertrain effort. He argues the statistically more common beginner problem is going *too* close to failure, treating training as catharsis and assuming a one-to-one relationship between effort and gains.

His reasons beginners should stay away from failure and from MRV:
1. **Technique degrades** near failure before technique is stable, and badly-learned motor patterns are very hard to un-learn.
2. **Injury risk** — poor-technique reps near failure raise both acute and wear-and-tear injury risk, in exactly the population that pays most for getting hurt.
3. **They don't need it.** Novice gains come easily. Any missed stimulus can be made up by **adding sets**, which also buys more technical practice at submaximal loads and builds better lifters later.

Prescription for beginners: run from MEV up to about **two-thirds of the way to MRV**, then recycle; keep effort between about **4-6 RIR and 2 RIR**, and focus on getting strong for reps.

[46:03] Nippard raises the ~400-subject study where people were tested to failure with their usual "10-rep" weight; a large share got far more reps, averaging roughly **6-8 reps left in the tank**. Israetel's rebuttal: lab rep-max testing involves researchers shouting encouragement, with the subject cast as hero — that is a **meet, not a training session**. Failure in training means failure under normal circumstances, and those subjects were likely sore for a week and a half from one set. He also argues beginners see mostly **neural adaptations** in the first weeks-to-months anyway, and that the field routinely overdoses beginners (giving 3×10 to people who'd progress on much less).

[49:05] He concedes a real exception: some trainees — he notes this is statistically more common in women — genuinely are timid with weight and effort, and with those clients pushing the pace is right. But he considers that the minority.

[51:08] His preferred way to walk someone toward failure gently: **say nothing about failure at all. Just add ~5 kg to the bar every week and keep the rep target the same.** Sooner or later they miss a rep — and that was failure.

## Structuring the mesocycle
[59:13] A **mesocycle** is a sequence of similarly structured microcycles (a microcycle is usually a week). It has two parts:
1. **Accumulation phase** — start at MEV and apply overload week over week (more weight, more reps, more sets, or a combination) until you reach the most you can recover from. RIR falls as you go.
2. **Deload** — once fatigue is maxed and overload can't be applied, take a week to shed fatigue, then restart the next mesocycle at MEV.

The mountain analogy: base camp, summit, descend, rest, climb the next mountain. Mesocycles are discrete units defined by the application of overload and the accumulation of fatigue — not by an arbitrary calendar length.

**Length:** typically **3-8 weeks** of accumulation. **RIR determines the length** — you can't start below ~5 RIR (too easy) and you can't sustainably train to failure every week, so the RIR range sets the runway. Beginners and intermediates accumulate fatigue slower and improve fast enough that they can sometimes milk **8+ weeks**; advanced lifters can't. Israetel's colleague Jared Feather, a natural pro with ten years of training, was finding a **4:1** (four accumulation weeks, one deload) tough and looking at moving to **3:1**. A "12-week mesocycle" usually means either unexplained easy periods or training far from failure with no real overload.

**Scale:** microcycle ≈ 1 week; mesocycle ≈ 1-2 months; macrocycle ≈ 6 months to a year.

## Using maintenance volume to prioritise
[61:17] Within a mesocycle, take your priority muscles from MEV up to MRV, and park everything else at **maintenance volume** so it doesn't shrink. Run 2-4 mesocycles on the same priorities, then rotate — body parts build **adaptive resistance** and need a break from being emphasised.

[66:21] This becomes more necessary as you advance. For a beginner, the sum of every muscle's individual MRV still fits under total-body systemic recovery capacity, so you can emphasise everything. For an advanced lifter that sum exceeds whole-body recovery — prioritising everything at once might be double your systemic MRV. So progressively more body parts sit at MEV, then at maintenance, while one or two get pushed. Extreme illustration: Branch Warren took a year off leg training (below maintenance) to bring up his upper body; a saner version is ~**6 sets per leg muscle** for a year while crushing upper body.

## Practical application
- Track **hard working sets per muscle per week**. Ignore warm-ups. Count supersets as 2 sets until experience tells you otherwise.
- Work in **5-20 reps**, at **2-4 RIR** for most sets, with **~2 RIR as your default stopping point**.
- Start each mesocycle at **~10 sets/muscle/week** and add sets and/or weight weekly. Deload when you can no longer match prior performance — around **20 sets/week** for most people.
- Take sets to failure only in the **final week before a deload**, and only on **low-risk isolation work**.
- Keep de-prioritised muscles at **6-8 sets/week** so they hold while you push others.
- Spend six months finding **your own** MEV and MRV per body part; expect them to differ wildly (his hamstrings 10-15 vs. side delts mid-to-high 20s).
- Beginners: stay at **4-6 to 2 RIR**, run MEV to two-thirds of MRV, add sets rather than intensity, and let progressive loading walk you toward failure naturally.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Every number here is an **average for an average intermediate**; Israetel repeatedly stresses individual variation and that landmarks drift between cycles.
- MAV is admitted to be a **nebulous, theoretically-driven concept** — practically hard to identify, so don't chase it.
- Nippard and Israetel **disagree** on failure and effort for novices: Nippard thinks many general-public lifters need permission to push harder; Israetel thinks the more common failure mode is beginners training too close to failure with unstable technique.
- Superset and drop-set accounting is left to individual experimentation — no clean rule.
- Consistency of measurement matters more than the absolute number. The cooked-vs-raw food-weighing analogy: pick a method, stay consistent, and use direction of change rather than hitting a magic number.
