# The Science of Calf Training: Why They're Stubborn and How to Load Them

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Science of Calf Training Fully Explained (8 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xLikAVQTKqc)) · 2016-11-25 · 9 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Calves look "stubborn" for four plausible reasons: they're already trained from daily walking/standing (so less newbie effect), skeletal structure and insertion height cap their look, the soleus is **70-96% slow twitch**, and androgen receptor density may be lower in the calves.
- **Tempo is the whole game.** Use a **2-3 second negative, a pause at the bottom, and a 1 second positive with a squeeze at the top** — bouncing hands the work to elastic tissue instead of muscle.
- Heavy standing calf raise at **6-8 reps** close to failure for the gastrocnemius; seated calf raise at **15-20 reps** for the soleus.
- **Feet point straight ahead.** Turning them in or out reduces activation — turning them out can roughly halve it.
- Split the work across **two leg sessions per week** rather than doing it all in one.

## Why calves seem stubborn [00:32]
No direct research on the question, but four inferences:

1. **Pre-trained from daily life.** The calves are worked any time you walk, run or stand still, so they may already be closer to their genetic ceiling when you start training them — meaning less of the "newbie effect". Not true stubbornness, just a higher starting point.
2. **Skeleton and insertion.** Someone with small ankles and high-inserting calves will probably always have smaller-looking calves than someone with thick ankles and low insertions. That doesn't stop them growing — it just means the endpoint is relative to the start, which is true of every muscle.
3. **Fibre composition.** The **soleus**, which lies underneath the gastrocnemius but is actually *larger* in cross-sectional area, is **70-96% type I (slow twitch)** by various estimates. Slow twitch fibres are repeatedly shown to be less hypertrophic in response to high-intensity training. This applies to the soleus only, not the gastroc.
4. **Androgen receptor density** may be lower in the calves — some research supports this — which could explain acute resistance to growth even though they will grow given enough time.

## The workout

**Pre-activation: 5-10 minutes on the StairMaster.** Nippard does cardio before every session, but rates it especially important before calves — warm joints and blood flow, but mainly because the calf mind-muscle connection is hard to establish. A few hundred reps of bodyweight plantar flexion works as pre-activation.

**1. Single-leg standing calf raise — the heavy movement.** [03:03]
- **6-8 reps**, taken **quite close to failure**.
- Done single-legged to correct a right/left imbalance; bilateral lets the stronger side take over, unilateral forces each leg to fire equally.
- Purpose: fully activate both fast and slow twitch fibres of gastroc and soleus.
- **Tempo:** 2-3 second negative → slight pause at the bottom → 1 second positive → slight squeeze at the top. The pause matters because bouncing out of the bottom lets **elastic tissue** do the brunt of the work; pausing lets stored elastic energy dissipate so the muscle has to overcome the load.
- Loaded with a hand-held plate here only because the gym lacked a standing calf raise machine — either works about the same. Free-weight advantage: you can lean slightly forward or back to find the posture where you feel the calves best, rather than being locked into a machine's strict up-and-down path.
- He often does these **barefoot** because the top of a shoe can restrict plantar flexion range of motion.

**2. Horizontal leg press calf jump.** [04:34]
His own invention, borrowing the calf jump he first heard of from **Menno Henselmans**. Bend the knees and hips slightly and mimic jumping up against the seat. Two purposes:
- **Improves the resistance curve.** Accelerating explosively out of the bottom offsets the fact that the calves' strength curve declines or levels off through the concentric — momentum from the bottom carries into the top range where the calves are weaker.
- **More natural movement pattern** than the strict vertical path of a calf raise machine.
- He uses the leg press rather than a standing machine because it loads the spine less — injury-preventative, or in his case rehabilitative.

**3. Donkey calf raise.** [05:35]
A 2000 book by **Boeckh-Behrens and Buskies** (*name reconstructed from garbled captions — uncertain*) compared relative EMG activity across a variety of calf movements and found the **donkey calf raise came out on top**. That might warrant doing it first and with the greatest intensity. But it's hard to load heavily without someone to sit on your back or a dedicated machine, so he does it with a plate on the lower back for **higher reps**, again with controlled tempo and a pause at the bottom.

**Foot position:** point the feet **straight ahead**. Pointing them in and pointing them out have both been shown to decrease activation — pointing out, according to one source, cuts activation **roughly in half**.

**4. Seated calf raise — soleus isolation.** [06:36]
With the knee bent, the gastrocnemius is much less active. The gastroc crosses both the ankle and the knee; a bent knee shortens it at the knee so it can't shorten (contract) as much at the ankle — **active insufficiency**. The soleus takes the brunt.

Since the soleus is predominantly slow twitch, he prefers **15-20 reps** with the same slow controlled tempo. The evidence is genuinely mixed: a 2007 paper (name garbled — *uncertain*) and a paper by Mitchell et al. both found slow twitch fibres more responsive to higher reps, but a later **2012** paper found them *less* responsive to high reps. His theoretical case for high reps anyway: by the **size principle**, slow twitch motor units are recruited first and stay active longest in a higher-rep set, so they accumulate more fatigue and possibly a larger hypertrophic stimulus.

## Practical application
- 5-10 min StairMaster before calves, every time.
- Single-leg standing calf raise: 6-8 reps near failure, 2-3s eccentric, pause at bottom, 1s concentric, squeeze at top. Barefoot if shoes limit ROM. Go unilateral if you have a side-to-side imbalance.
- Leg press calf jump: explosive out of the bottom.
- Donkey calf raise: higher reps with a plate on the lower back, feet straight ahead.
- Seated calf raise: 15-20 reps, same controlled tempo.
- **Split this across two leg days per week** — he thinks most people would benefit from breaking the whole workout up, and legs should be hit roughly twice weekly anyway.

## Caveats & unknowns
- There is **no direct research** on whether calves are genuinely more genetically stubborn; everything in that section is inference.
- Whether type I fibres actually respond better to light weight and high reps is **unresolved** — he cites papers on both sides and says the jury is out; his high-rep soleus preference is theoretical (size principle), not demonstrated.
- The donkey calf raise ranking comes from a 2000 EMG book whose authors' names were garbled in the captions; verify before citing.
- Splitting calves into two sessions is qualified as individual and dependent on your whole routine.
