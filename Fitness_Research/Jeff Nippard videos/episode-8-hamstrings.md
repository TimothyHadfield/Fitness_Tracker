# Hamstring Anatomy, Exercise Selection and Injury Prevention with Andrew Vigotsky

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Episode 8 - Hamstrings Feat. Andrew Vigotsky" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ilbnkYedYs8)) · 2015-02-15 · 56 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

Podcast (Ice Cream for PRs) with Nippard, co-host Robin, and guest **Andrew Vigotsky**, then research consultant for The Strength Guys, finishing a BSc in kinesiology at Arizona State University where he did biomechanics research and worked with **Bret Contreras**. Positions below are attributed where the two differ.

## Bottom line
- **Vigotsky's central recommendation: don't dichotomise. Train the hamstrings with both hip extension and knee flexion movements**, across a variety of rep ranges and loads.
- **Squats are a poor hamstring exercise** — hamstring EMG in squats sits around **20% of maximum voluntary contraction**, because the hamstring shortens at the knee while lengthening at the hip and its overall length barely changes. Deadlifts are only modestly better.
- **Vigotsky's single best pick if forced to choose one hip-extension movement: the 45-degree hyperextension**, because torque on the hip extensors stays highest through the whole range (you're never perpendicular to the ground).
- **Hamstring fibre type is roughly an even split of type I and type II** — so no basis for training them exclusively heavy and low-rep.
- **Ankle position on leg curls doesn't matter** once load is equated. You're stronger dorsiflexed because the gastrocnemius contributes; do whatever's comfortable.
- For injury prevention, **eccentric, long-length hip extension work** (good mornings, RDLs/stiff-leg deadlifts) and the **Nordic ham curl** are the tools.

## Anatomy [02:57]
The hamstrings are four muscles in two groups:
- **Medial:** semimembranosus and semitendinosus. Originate on the **ischial tuberosity** (the lower "wing" of the pelvis), insert on the medial side of the **tibia**.
- **Lateral:** biceps femoris **long head**, also originating on the ischial tuberosity, inserting on the **fibula**; and biceps femoris **short head**, which originates on the **femur itself** and inserts on the fibular head.

Three of the four are **biarticular** — crossing both the hip and the knee — so they produce both **knee flexion** (leg curl) and **hip extension** (hip thrust, RDL). The **short head of biceps femoris crosses only the knee** and therefore cannot contribute to hip extension.

**The argument this is used for, and Vigotsky's answer [07:06].** People argue leg curls are superior because all four hamstring muscles cross the knee while only three cross the hip. Vigotsky: "I don't think it's that dichotomous." The short head is not a large muscle relative to the rest of the hamstring group, so hip-extension-only training would still grow the hamstrings, just probably not to the same extent. **Best to work both hip extension and knee flexion.**

## Leg curls vs stiff-leg deadlifts: the EMG evidence [08:09]

Vigotsky cites a **regional-differences study by Brad Schoenfeld and colleagues** (published roughly late 2014): **lying leg curls produced higher activation in the distal portion** of the hamstrings (both medial and lateral, and notably more for the lateral), while **stiff-leg deadlifts produced similar proximal activation** to leg curls. So: equal near the hip, more near the knee for the leg curl. Vigotsky is careful that what this means for hypertrophy is not yet known.

**Why that doesn't settle it in favour of leg curls.** Vigotsky invokes Schoenfeld's three hypertrophy mechanisms — **mechanical tension, metabolic stress, muscle damage**. Because a lying leg curl works the hamstrings at **shorter muscle lengths**, there's less strain and stretch and therefore **less muscle damage**. The stretch-based mechanism is what hip extension movements supply. Hence: **include both** and you get the best of both worlds.

Nippard's clarification for listeners: the hamstrings are stretched at the hip when you're bent over, and stretched at the knee when the knee is extended. So an exercise that puts hips flexed and knee extended simultaneously produces the fullest stretch and, per Vigotsky, more muscle damage.

## Seated vs lying leg curls, and active insufficiency [10:44]

**Active insufficiency:** a muscle can't contract maximally at one joint when it's already shortened at another.

- **Lying leg curl:** hips extended, so the hamstring is already shortened at the hip. When it then tries to shorten at the knee, it can't do so as forcefully — active insufficiency.
- **Seated leg curl:** hips flexed, so the hamstring is lengthened at the hip and there's no active insufficiency at the knee. It can contract more forcefully.

Nippard proposes this as a case for seated leg curls being superior. **Vigotsky is careful here:** active insufficiency is fundamentally about **torque** — how much force the muscle can produce at a given point — and he says plainly he doesn't know how that translates to hypertrophy. But since **mechanical tension** is one of the mechanisms, less tension in the muscle **may** signal less growth. So: "there might be something there," not a settled claim. He also thinks the seated leg curl is more comparable to a good morning or stiff-leg deadlift in terms of the muscle-damage/stretch mechanism.

Asked whether to combine seated and lying leg curls for these reasons, Vigotsky: **"I think that might be a good idea. It certainly can't hurt."**

Nippard adds a related observation: on a flat lying leg curl bench, people instinctively prop themselves up on their elbows to take the hips out of full extension and stretch the hamstrings — because that genuinely puts the hamstring in a better position to curl the weight.

## Ankle position on leg curls [14:21]

Nippard's experience: **dorsiflexed** (toes toward shin) he's stronger; **plantarflexed** (toes pointed) he gets a better mind-muscle connection.

**Vigotsky's own informal EMG experiment.** He picked a load he could do for about 12 reps, ran a set dorsiflexed (his normal style) and a set plantarflexed. Dorsiflexed, 12 reps were easy; plantarflexed was noticeably harder. Plantarflexed showed **slightly higher hamstring activation — but not much**, and he concluded that **if he had equated relative intensity (a true 12RM for each), activation would have been similar regardless of ankle position.** The higher activation plantarflexed came from working harder against a load that was relatively heavier for him, not from a better hamstring position.

**Corroborating study:** a **2000 paper Vigotsky refers to as "Kuro et al."** (the auto-captions garble the name — treat it as uncertain) found the same thing: subjects developed **more torque with ankles dorsiflexed, but EMG was the same as with less torque plantarflexed**.

**Mechanism [19:02]:** the **gastrocnemius is also biarticular** — it plantarflexes the ankle and flexes the knee. Plantarflexed, the gastroc is already shortened at the ankle and hits active insufficiency, so it can't contribute much to knee flexion. Dorsiflexed, it comes out of active insufficiency and contributes more, which is why you're stronger. Nippard's summary, which Vigotsky endorses: **the hamstrings aren't better at moving the load dorsiflexed — you've just recruited another muscle into the job.**

**Practical take-home:** do whatever is most comfortable. Nippard now dorsiflexes all the time because he can move more weight and it isn't worth the awkwardness of forcing plantarflexion.

## Leg curl variations

**Gliding leg curl [21:35].** Vigotsky's personal favourite because **it works knee flexion and hip extension at the same time** — efficient, and it feels great. Setup: hang from a bar in a Smith machine or squat rack with feet up on a box so your body forms an **L** (torso vertical, legs parallel to the ground); then **extend the hips and flex the knees simultaneously** until your torso and upper legs are parallel with the ground and your lower legs are perpendicular. Loading options: a plate on your waist/lap, or **switch to unilateral** (his usual choice).

**Nordic ham curl / glute-ham raise [23:36].** Ankles pinned, fall forward, curl yourself back up against bodyweight. Vigotsky hasn't done Nordics but has done a lot of glute-ham raises, which are similar with a slightly different axis of rotation making them easier. Both are very hard; regressions are to push off a pole or **use a band**, which gives most assistance at the hardest (bottom) position, flattening the eccentric tension curve.

**Glute-ham raise vs lying leg curl [25:39].** Nippard proposes the GHR should be superior because the hips have to be held extended **isometrically** while the knee flexes. Vigotsky's counterpoints:
- The **torque curve differs sharply**. GHR is hardest at the bottom with the body straight out, and gets much easier toward the top; Vigotsky calls the GHR torque curve steeper than the leg curl's from experience.
- **The GHR doesn't have constant tension.** With a lying leg curl the hamstrings are always producing force; on the GHR there's a period (kneeling at the top) where they aren't. Nippard's analogy: like a lateral raise at the bottom, there's no tension left on the target muscle.
- He credits **Bret Contreras's article "Gutting the Glute-Ham Raise"** (T Nation) for the torque analysis, noting how much work the hamstrings must do to raise the body.

**Vigotsky's verdict:** he's confident the Nordic/GHR is better **for injury prevention** because it's eccentric-accentuated, but for **building hamstrings he won't say one is better than the other.** "I don't like dichotomising exercises."

**A resolved confusion [28:13].** Nippard expected the GHR's isometric hip extension to produce more proximal EMG than a passive lying leg curl. Vigotsky's rebuttal: if that logic held, the **stiff-leg deadlift** — pure hip extension — should show more proximal activation than a lying leg curl, and the Schoenfeld regional-differences data shows **equal proximal activation**. Nippard accepts the correction.

## Hip extension movements [29:45]

Vigotsky's ranking rests on **Bret Contreras's article "Are All Hip Extensions Created Equal?"**, which compared torque curves across the horizontal back extension, good morning and 45-degree hyperextension. Finding: the **45-degree hyperextension keeps hip-extension torque highest throughout the entire motion, because you're never perpendicular to the ground** — and when you are perpendicular (e.g. lockout of a deadlift), you lose the tension entirely.

**"If I pick one, it'd be the 45-degree hyper."** But he stresses matching stance to your training: **if you're a sumo deadlifter, go wider with the feet turned out**, which emphasises the **lateral hamstrings — which have a greater hip extension moment arm** and are therefore better hip extensors than the medial two.

Both agree the **45-degree hyperextension ranks above the conventional deadlift** as a hamstring builder. Nippard also notes he thinks the movement is misnamed — it's hip extension, not back extension.

## Why squats (and deadlifts) don't build hamstrings [32:19]

**Squats.** The hamstrings act as **dynamic stabilisers**. On the descent the knee and hip both flex, so the hamstring **shortens at the knee and lengthens at the hip — net length barely changes**; standing back up reverses it, again with no net length change. Contrast the quadriceps, which lengthen on the way down and shorten on the way up — a far greater stimulus. Vigotsky: there's a little stretch in the hamstrings in the deadlift and hardly any in the squat, "at least according to my calculations," adding that the one study on this he believes has its calculations wrong.

**EMG corroboration [35:23]:** all the squat EMG research on hamstrings comes out around **20% of maximum voluntary contraction** — Nippard's assessment, agreed to by Vigotsky, is "not good at all." Nippard points to a researcher named **Ebben** (first name garbled in the captions as "urban"; likely William Ebben — treat as uncertain) who wrote a paper specifically on why the squat is not a good exercise for developing the hamstrings.

**Conventional deadlift [33:51].** Better than the squat: at the start position the hips are much higher and the knees more extended than at the bottom of a squat, so you **begin with a longer hamstring**. Great hip flexion with relatively little knee flexion means a stretched hamstring, putting you in an advantageous position to use the hamstrings for hip extension — especially once the bar passes the knees, at which point the knees are fully extended and the rest is pure hip extension. Still, Vigotsky's blunt summary: **"the deadlift itself isn't a huge hamstring exercise, neither is the squat."**

## How to read EMG research [36:27]

Vigotsky's explainer: **EMG (electromyography)** measures the electrical potential (voltage) that spans a muscle fibre to tell it to contract. An **electrode** — effectively a very sensitive voltmeter — is placed on the skin over the muscle and reports voltage to a unit, sampling something like **2,000 values per second**, producing a graph of electrical potential across a movement. Critically, **an electrode only picks up what is directly underneath it** — one on the deltoid tells you nothing about the triceps.

**What EMG can be used for:** how hard a muscle works to accomplish a task; whether a muscle is on or off and when it switches; and how much harder one muscle works than another — the last only if you **normalise** properly.

**Vigotsky's own published study** (the month before recording): the effect of **load on good morning EMG activity and kinematics**. He placed electrodes on the **medial and lateral hamstrings and the lumbar and thoracic erector spinae** and compared **50%, 60%, 70%, 80% and 90% of 1RM**.

**The big methodological failure: not equating load [40:04].** Vigotsky cites a squat-depth study (captions garble the name as "Quatre Rossano" — very likely **Caterisano et al.**; treat as uncertain) that used the **same absolute load for partial and deep squats**. If you use, say, 80% of your deep-squat 1RM for a partial squat, the partial is trivially easy and the muscles don't have to work hard — so of course the deep squat showed huge gluteus maximus numbers. **The subjects simply weren't challenged in the partial condition.** Nippard notes this is precisely the study cited by advocates of ass-to-grass squatting to claim the glutes and hams "turn on X% more" below parallel. Vigotsky's view: many studies have failed to use **relative loading**, though the research is improving as this limitation gets recognised.

## Fibre type [41:37]

The bro-claim: hamstrings are predominantly type II / fast-twitch, so train them in lower rep ranges.

**Vigotsky:** from what he's seen the hamstrings have a **fairly even blend of type I and type II fibres**. He points to **Dan Ogborn** having written on training for fibre-type composition and to **Chris Beardsley**'s article covering fibre-type composition of essentially every muscle. Because the split is even, **training for one specific fibre type isn't beneficial** — instead, as **Brad Schoenfeld** consistently argues, **train across a variety of rep ranges and loads**.

Nippard says he looked this up and confirmed the even split, and speculates (explicitly flagged as speculation, "don't cite me on this") that the myth may originate from observations in sprinters showing greater type II composition in the hamstrings, which doesn't hold across populations.

Asked whether fibre-type-specific training is ever worthwhile in concept, Vigotsky: not unless a muscle is very strongly dominant in one fibre type, and he can't name such a muscle off the top of his head.

## Hip thrusts [45:42]
There's a lot of hamstring EMG activity in the hip thrust and Vigotsky says unpublished data is "looking good" — but he **wouldn't use it as a hamstring developer, because the hamstrings don't go through a large range of motion in it.** He uses it for glutes.

## Programming a hamstring session [44:11]

Nippard's proposed template: **hip extension movement first, finish with a couple of leg curl variations in higher rep ranges.** Vigotsky agrees it's far better than squats plus some deadlifts — but adds an important caveat: **you can and should switch it around.** The hip extension movement doesn't have to be the heavy low-rep one and the knee flexion doesn't have to be light and high-rep. You could do **glute-ham raises as your low-rep knee flexion work** and **45-degree hyperextensions for sets of 20** as your hip extension work. His firm requirements: **work at both high and low intensities across different rep ranges, and definitely include both knee-joint and hip-joint movements.**

## Injury prevention [46:44]

**Vigotsky's good morning paper** related the good morning to **hamstring strains in sprinting**. One study examined **maximum hamstring length during a sprint**; strains occur when the hamstrings are stretched most, i.e. at peak strain. Vigotsky found that **at every load tested, the good morning stretched every one of the hamstring muscles to a greater degree than sprinting does**. That may make the good morning a good exercise for preventing sprinting hamstring strains, and presumably in other sports — and likely extends to similar **eccentric hip-extension movements like the stiff-leg deadlift and RDL**. The **Nordic ham curl** has separately been shown effective at preventing hamstring strains.

**Mechanism [49:18].** Vigotsky is careful not to overclaim — he can't yet say the good morning specifically prevents injury. Extrapolating from other research: **a lot of eccentric long-length work increases the number of sarcomeres in series**, so the muscle gets longer, and there's a **shift in the strength curve according to the exercises you do**. Because the good morning demands high force from the hamstrings at **maximal hip flexion** — presumably where the strain occurs — the muscle may be stronger at that point and better able to sustain the strain during sprinting. In Nippard's phrasing, which Vigotsky accepts: it **changes the length-tension relationship** so the muscle can contract more forcefully in a lengthened position.

**ACL [48:18].** There is research on using the hamstrings to prevent ACL injuries. The commonly purported mechanism: the tibia tries to translate anteriorly; if the hamstrings aren't strong enough to pull it back, the ACL takes the load and eventually fails. Quad/hamstring imbalance is implicated.

## Practical application
- Build hamstring training around **both a hip extension movement and a knee flexion movement**, every time. Don't pick a side.
- Best hip extension pick: **45-degree hyperextension** (constant torque); match stance width and foot flare to your deadlift stance, going wide and turned out if you pull sumo to bias the lateral hamstrings.
- Consider running **both seated and lying leg curls** — plausible (not proven) benefit from different lengths and active insufficiency profiles.
- **Don't rely on squats for hamstrings** (~20% MVC). Deadlifts help somewhat more but aren't a hamstring exercise either.
- **Ankle position on leg curls is a non-issue.** Use whatever is comfortable — dorsiflexed lets you use more weight because the gastroc contributes.
- **Train across a variety of rep ranges and loads**, not exclusively heavy — hamstring fibre type is roughly even. Rep ranges are interchangeable between the hip and knee exercises: heavy GHRs plus 20-rep hypers is as valid as the reverse.
- Add **eccentric long-length work — Nordic ham curls, good mornings, RDLs/stiff-leg deadlifts** — for hamstring strain prevention, especially if you sprint.
- Use hip thrusts for glutes, not hamstrings.
- Reading EMG studies: check whether **relative load was equated across conditions**. If not, the result may be an artefact of one condition simply being harder.

## Caveats & unknowns
- **Vigotsky repeatedly declines to convert EMG or torque findings into hypertrophy conclusions.** Higher activation, more torque and less active insufficiency are all suggestive, not established, for growth.
- Several researcher names are garbled by the auto-captions and are flagged as uncertain: the 2000 ankle-position study ("Kuro et al."), the squat-depth study ("Quatre Rossano", plausibly Caterisano), and the first name of Ebben.
- Vigotsky's ankle-position EMG test was an informal self-experiment on one subject with load not properly equated — he says so explicitly.
- The good morning's injury-prevention benefit is an **extrapolation** from stretch magnitude and from eccentric-training research, not a demonstrated outcome.
- The hip thrust hamstring data was unpublished at the time of recording.
- Vigotsky disputes the calculations in the one study on hamstring length change during the squat.
