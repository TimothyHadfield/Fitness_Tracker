# Weight Gain Is Not Fat Gain: Using Reductio ad Absurdum on the Scale

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Weight Gain Does Not EQUAL Fat Gain!" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wxpcnq85-T4)) · 2014-08-30 · 7 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- A single weigh-in is a data point, not a result. What matters is the trend across many of them.
- The arithmetic makes the panic reaction self-refuting: three pounds of *fat* in a week would require an excess of about **10,800 calories** (3,600 per pound × 3), and would require that none of it went to glycogen or was burned.
- If you didn't cheat on your diet, a jump on the scale is almost certainly water or gut content, and it reverses once the cause does.
- Stressing over each weigh-in is not neutral — it can be actively counterproductive to the goal.
- A prolonged stall is a genuinely different problem from a random fluctuation, and should be treated as one.

## Why weigh in at all [01:19]
If what ultimately matters is how you look on stage or how you feel, why measure body weight? Some successful bodybuilders skip it entirely and go by the mirror, first stepping on a scale at the competition weigh-in. Nippard is sympathetic to this when the scale is doing psychological damage — if you look leaner in the mirror, then step on the scale and it ruins your day, you're weighting the wrong instrument.

His own reason for collecting weigh-ins from clients is narrow: **generate data points and track a general trend.** The number's value is in the aggregate, not individually.

## Reading the trend, not the points [02:21]
On a plotted weight-loss curve there are visible peaks and valleys, but the trend runs downward. Agonising over the transient fluctuations is failing to see the forest for the trees.

**Sex differences in the pattern** [02:51]: the peaks-and-valleys shape is more typical of male dieters. Female dieters more often show a **staircase** pattern — weight stalls, then drops suddenly. Because of this, monthly comparisons may be more informative than week-to-week readings for some women.

He also notes it is possible, if uncommon but not abnormal, to start a diet at the beginning of a plateau and see no weight loss for several weeks or even a month or two. That is not a reason to abandon the diet — energy balance eventually asserts itself.

## The reductio [03:51]
Reductio ad absurdum: you establish a statement by showing its denial produces a contradiction. Applied to the scale:

Suppose a dieter is up three pounds this week and believes it's three pounds of fat. For that to be literally true they would have had to consume 3,600 calories per pound × 3 = **over 10,000 calories above their maintenance intake**, in one week, *on top of* what they were already eating. It further assumes every one of those calories was partitioned to fat — none to glycogen, none oxidised for fuel. The conclusion is absurd, so the premise is false. If you didn't cheat, the rational inference is water or something similar.

This is the core transferable move: convert a scary scale number into the calorie surplus it would require, and see whether that surplus is physically plausible.

## Non-fat reasons the scale goes up [04:56]
- Water retention from a range of sources
- Menstrual cycle phase
- Large spikes in sodium intake
- Stress or lack of sleep
- Water retention from new drugs or medication
- Food still in the gut from a previous day

Retained water generally leaves once the factor causing it returns to normal.

## Fluctuation vs. stall [06:28]
Nippard closes by drawing a line he wants kept clear. Everything above concerns **random fluctuations around a trend**. Weight loss also genuinely stalls on a cut, and that is a different animal — a prolonged stall is a real problem to be diagnosed and addressed between a coach and client, not something to be waved off as noise.

## Practical application
- Treat individual weigh-ins as inputs to a trend line; judge progress on the line.
- Weigh under consistent conditions and read the direction over weeks, not days.
- For female dieters, consider monthly comparisons rather than weekly, given the staircase pattern.
- When the scale jumps, run the calorie arithmetic before reacting: is that surplus even possible given what you ate?
- If the mirror and the scale conflict and the scale wrecks your day, the scale may be the wrong primary tool for you.
- Distinguish a two-week wobble from a genuine multi-week stall, and escalate only the latter.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The male peaks-and-valleys vs. female staircase distinction is presented from coaching observation, with no study cited.
- 3,600 calories per pound of fat is a standard working approximation, not an exact physiological constant.
- No guidance is offered on how long a stall must persist before it counts as a stall rather than noise — it is deferred to the individual coach.
