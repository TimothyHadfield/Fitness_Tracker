# When Should You Trust The Results Of A Study?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "When Should You Trust The Results Of A Study?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y4tOfE7hv9k)) · 2019-03-09 · 3 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- Evidence sits in a hierarchy: anecdote and expert opinion at the bottom, meta-analyses and systematic reviews at the top.
- A single study — even a randomised controlled trial — is much weaker evidence than most people assume.
- Three quick trust signals: a large effect size, a small p-value (below 0.01), and a result explainable by a known mechanism.
- The publishing system rewards positive findings, so false positives are common in the literature.

## The hierarchy of evidence
From weakest to strongest:

1. Anecdote, opinion pieces, blog posts, expert opinion.
2. Case studies on a single individual — weak because that person may be atypical, so the result may not generalise.
3. (Intermediate designs, up through single randomised controlled trials.)
4. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews — the strongest form, because they pool results across multiple studies.

Meta-analyses are still not infallible; they have their own pitfalls and are not undeniable. But a meta-analysis carries more weight than any one RCT.

## Three things to look for in a single study
Nippard draws on an article series in the MASS Research Review, the first of which was titled "When to trust the results of a study." Its argument: findings of individual studies are far less trustworthy than people believe, because there are many routes to a false positive and the publication system disproportionately rewards positive results.

The simplest markers of a trustworthy result:

- **Large effect size** — literally that: in an exercise science context, a large amount of actual muscle growth, not a marginal difference.
- **Small p-value** — below 0.01 suggests the result is unlikely to be a fluke and more likely a real, meaningful effect.
- **A plausible known mechanism** — the finding should be explainable by physiology we already understand.

## The mechanism test, illustrated
His example: a study where one group chews Juicy Fruit gum and another chews spearmint gum, and the Juicy Fruit group sees five times the gains. There is nothing in that flavour you would expect to drive muscle growth, no supporting studies, and no mechanism. That combination is a signal to read the finding sceptically regardless of how the statistics look.

## Practical application
- Prefer meta-analyses and systematic reviews over single studies when forming a position.
- Before acting on a headline finding, check: is the effect big, is p < 0.01, and does a known mechanism explain it?
- Discount case studies and anecdote accordingly — the individual may simply be unrepresentative.
- Treat surprising results with no mechanism and no supporting literature as probable false positives until replicated.
- MASS Research Review is his recommended resource for condensing and correctly interpreting the literature.

## Caveats & unknowns
- These three criteria are explicitly a rough ballpark; Nippard notes there is much more to critical appraisal than this.
- No specific researchers or trials are cited beyond the MASS article series itself.
