# Overhead Barbell Press: Technique for Shoulder Development

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Build Bigger Shoulders With Perfect Training Technique (The Overhead Press)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_RlRDWO2jfg)) · 2019-01-08 · 7 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Load the barbell overhead press in the **5-10 rep range**; dumbbell shoulder presses suit **8-15 reps**.
- The OHP is front-delt dominant. A 2013 comparison of four shoulder press variations found **dumbbell presses beat barbell presses for side-delt activation, and standing beat seated in both cases** — so add a standing dumbbell press and lateral raises if side delts are the priority.
- Press in a straight vertical line: tilt the head back to clear the bar, then push the head forward once the bar passes.
- Elbows point out at roughly **45 degrees**, not fully flared (shoulder pain) and not straight in front (weak).
- Flex the glutes. Loose glutes push the hips back behind the bar and turn the press into a forward, low-back-straining bar path.

## Muscles involved
Shoulder flexion (raising the arm overhead) driven by the **anterior deltoid**, with a lesser contribution from the **clavicular (upper) head of the pecs**. Elbow extension recruits **all three triceps heads**. Viewed from behind, **scapular upward rotation** is handled by the **upper traps**.

## Why it's worth doing — and its one weakness [00:32]
Two strengths: it is a basic multi-joint barbell lift, so it allows a lot of progressive overload (though strength usually comes slower here than on other lifts — be patient); and it is a strong general strength builder through a huge range of motion, nearly **180 degrees of shoulder flexion**.

The weakness is that it is heavily front-delt dominant. The **2013 study on four shoulder press variations** found dumbbells better than barbells for side delts, and standing better than seated for side delts in both cases. Caveat on dumbbell presses: as the load increases, the bulky dumbbell heads can hit the shoulders on the way down and cut the range of motion short. The practical answer is to combine and periodically vary press variations while keeping the OHP as the bread-and-butter vertical press.

## Setup [01:35]
- Set the bar in a squat rack at squat height, roughly **armpit height**.
- Grip **slightly wider than shoulder width**, hands just outside the shoulders. Get the elbows underneath the bar, bend knees and hips slightly, stand up to unrack.
- Take **two to three steps back**, feet about shoulder width with a slight **15-degree foot flare**.
- Point the elbows slightly outside the shoulders.
- **Grip:** a thumbless ("suicide") grip is usable once you're experienced — not for beginners — provided the wrists are stacked, i.e. viewed from the side the bar sits above the wrist joint, not behind it near the fingers.
- Externally rotate the shoulders so the elbows sit at about **45 degrees**.
- Bar rests on the upper chest/collarbone. **Flex the glutes** to keep the spine neutral. Take a deep breath into the gut, pressing air out against a belt if worn, to raise intra-abdominal pressure and torso rigidity.

## Execution [03:05]
- Press while holding the breath. Drive the bar **straight up** — not up and forward, not up and back. From the side the bar path should be essentially a straight vertical line.
- Tilt the head back slightly to clear the chin, then push the head forward to neutral once the bar clears the face.
- Keep knees, hips and shoulder blades motionless through the concentric. **Do not shrug** to initiate the press off the chest — it makes the lift easier short-term but weakens bottom-end concentric pressing ability and caps true max strength long term.
- Lock out fully, bar centred over the shoulder joint. At lockout there should be a straight line through **barbell → shoulders → hips → mid-foot**, keeping weight balanced over the middle of the foot rather than shifted onto toes or heels.
- **Eccentric:** drop the elbows down and out at **45 degrees**, not straight down in front of the delts. Let the bar come to a **full dead stop** on the chest, take another full breath, then press the next rep.

## Common errors [05:07]
1. **Partial range of motion** — the most common. Not locking out at the top, not touching the chest at the bottom. Consistent ROM is what makes progressive overload real rather than "progressive decreases in range of motion."
2. **Touch-and-go reps** — not inherently injurious, but they create inconsistent, bouncy reps that lean on momentum and the stretch reflex at the bottom, blunting the brute strength needed to move the bar from a dead stop.
3. **Not flexing the glutes** — leaves the hips behind the bar, cuts stability, pushes the bar path forward, strains the lower back and makes form inconsistent.

## Alternatives
Seated and standing dumbbell shoulder presses both work as substitutes or additions; unilateral loading helps prevent imbalances and asymmetries. **Seated allows heavier loads; standing raises stabilisation demands, meaning more core and side-delt involvement.** With dumbbells you can press straight up with the bells out to the sides, since there's no face to clear. Rep range for dumbbell variations: **8-15**.

## Practical application
- Barbell OHP as the primary vertical press, **5-10 reps**, dead-stop reps, full lockout.
- Add a **standing dumbbell press (8-15 reps)** plus lateral raises when side-delt width is the goal.
- Rotate/vary press variations periodically rather than relying on one.
- Cue checklist per rep: glutes tight, breath held, elbows 45 degrees, bar straight up, head through at the top, dead stop at the bottom.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Expect slower strength progress on the OHP than on other barbell lifts.
- The thumbless grip is only suggested for experienced lifters with stacked wrists.
- The 2013 press-variation study is cited by year only; no author is named in the transcript.
- The side-delt advantage of dumbbells may be partly offset by range-of-motion loss at heavier loads.
