# Jeff Nippard — the whole library in one file

Everything from the 50 notes in this folder, distilled to what you'd actually act on.
Organised by decision, not by video. Each section links to the note with the full detail.

A note on dates: these videos span 2016–2025 and **his position has moved**, most of all on
volume and on stretch-position training. Where the old and new conflict, the newer view is
his current one. The [contradictions section](#where-he-changed-his-mind-and-where-hes-unsure)
at the end lays out the live disagreements rather than papering over them.

---

## The hierarchy

His recurring frame — the variables at the top of the list decide your results, and the ones
at the bottom are rounding errors. Fix them in order.

1. **Sustainability** — a program you'll actually run for years, kept safe and enjoyable enough to stick to
2. **Effort** — sets taken genuinely close to failure
3. **Progressive overload** — more weight or more reps over time, tracked
4. **Volume** — hard sets per muscle per week
5. **Frequency** — how those sets are spread
6. **Exercise selection** — what movements fill the slots
7. **Rest periods**
8. **Tempo** — explicitly the lowest-priority variable

The two failure modes he names most often: people obsess over items 5–8 while neglecting
2 and 3, and people **do not train nearly as hard as they think they do**.

## Programming

### Volume — how many sets

**10–20 hard sets per muscle per week** is the consensus figure across every video that touches it,
and it's Schoenfeld's standing recommendation too. The landmark structure ([Israetel](training-volume-deep-dive-israetel.md)):

| Landmark | Sets/muscle/week | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **MV** — maintenance volume | ~6–8 | holds what you have; lets you park a muscle while prioritising another |
| **MEV** — minimum effective volume | ~10 (8–12) | least that still grows you; where a mesocycle starts |
| **MAV** — maximum adaptive volume | drifts | the sweet spot, which *moves* from near-MEV to near-MRV across a block |
| **MRV** — maximum recoverable volume | ~20 | the most you can recover from; where you deload |

Count **hard working sets only** — warm-ups don't count, and every counted set must be within
about 3 reps of failure. Sets are the currency (rather than tonnage) because sets between roughly
5 and 20 reps produce comparable fatigue, which makes them a workable common unit.

**Individual variation is the whole point, not a footnote.** Israetel's own hamstring MRV is
10–15 sets while his side delts take the mid-to-high 20s. Expect to spend about six months
learning your own numbers per body part.

**Don't add volume as the first move.** If you're still getting stronger, change nothing. Before
adding sets, check technique, effort, exercise selection and mind-muscle connection.

### Junk volume — the three ways sets get wasted

[Full note](junk-volume.md). This is the most practically useful correction in the library.

1. **Too many sets in one session.** Krieger's meta-analysis of nine studies: growth climbs to
   roughly **six sets per muscle per session**, then flatlines and may regress. Nippard's practical
   cap: **6–8 sets per muscle per workout**, maybe 10–12 for back, glutes and quads.
2. **Sets that are too easy.** The most common and most damaging. In the study he cites, when
   lifters used their normal "10-rep" bench weight and went to failure, **47% got 16–20+ reps** —
   leaving 6–10 or more in the tank. Training that way, you never pass intermediate.
3. **Ultra-high-rep sets.** Above ~40–50 reps you're accumulating fatigue and soreness for no
   extra growth. Stay under ~30 reps and above ~20% of 1RM.

The direct consequence: **20 chest sets on one chest day is at least half junk.** Ten on Monday
and ten on Thursday is better work for the same set count.

### Frequency — how to spread them

**2× per week per muscle.** This beats 1× even at matched weekly volume. Whether **3× beats 2×
is genuinely unresolved** — and he's careful about this, noting the strongest pro-frequency study
(McLester) used only *three sets per muscle per week*, which is nothing like real programming.

The honest case for higher frequency isn't muscle protein synthesis theory — it's **volume
spread**: the same weekly sets done across more sessions, each set performed fresher and better,
and each session staying under the per-session junk threshold. ([high-frequency training](high-frequency-full-body-training.md))

**On bro splits** ([note](bro-splits-vs-higher-frequency.md)): they work when weekly volume is
matched, and once-a-week frequency still produces robust growth. The problem isn't the split's
architecture, it's that progressing past intermediate means raising weekly volume, and stacking
those extra sets onto one day makes them junk. What actually matters in a split, in order:
does it let you hit your weekly volume, does it allow recovery, and will you stick to it.

### Effort — how close to failure

[Full note](how-hard-to-train-proximity-to-failure.md). Failure is an **involuntary set endpoint** —
you physically cannot move the weight — not "it felt hard."

**Target 1–3 RIR for most hypertrophy work**, with a good share at ~1 RIR. Israetel's headline
claim, which he says he'd stake his reputation on: **~2 RIR is the best average stopping point**,
because hypertrophy rises roughly *linearly* toward failure while fatigue rises *exponentially*.

Load changes the answer. On **heavy sets** you can leave 4–5 reps and still get a solid stimulus
(most motor units are recruited from rep one). On **light/high-rep sets above 12–15 reps**, leave
no more than 1–2.

Experts genuinely disagree on the threshold — Zourdos will go as far as 5–7 RIR in some contexts,
Helms says 0–5 RIR, Schoenfeld says 1–3 plus some sets to failure.

**Where failure belongs:** the last set of an exercise, or the last week of a block before a
deload — and on machines and isolation work where failing is cheap and safe. Never on heavy
squats or deadlifts.

**Two ways to calibrate your RIR estimates**, since they're usually wrong:
- **Anchoring sets** — go genuinely all-out on something safe, calling out when you think you
  have two reps left, then keep going and see how far off you were.
- **Film a set.** Rep speed is the most objective cue available. If the concentric doesn't slow
  down at all near the end, you're not close. (One dataset: bench at 80% 1RM with reps slowed
  50% ≈ 1.6 RIR.) This breaks down if you deliberately use a slow or mind-muscle-focused tempo.

### Reps and load

Hypertrophy is **similar anywhere from 3 to 35 reps when effort and volume are matched**
(two Schoenfeld studies). **Strength is not** — heavier and lower reps wins there, every time.

So why 6–15? Practical reasons, not magic:
- **Below 6:** higher injury risk and much higher dropout (in the 3-rep study, from injuries and
  joint intolerance), plus it takes 7 sets to match the volume of 3 sets of 10.
- **Above 15:** heavy fatigue and lactate accumulation, prolonged soreness, impaired recovery.

**Put ~75% of your volume in 6–15 reps**, split the rest between heavier and lighter work.
Israetel's version: 5–20 reps, and he starts asking questions outside 5–30.

### Rest

**3–5 minutes on heavy compounds, 1–2 minutes on isolation.** The old textbook "1 minute for
hypertrophy" is dead: Schoenfeld 2016 found 3-minute rest beat 1-minute rest for quad and triceps
growth *and* for bench and squat 1RM. Grgic's 2017 meta-analysis: >1 min beats <1 min.

Rest serves two purposes only — enough recovery to hold performance across sets, and time
management. Don't stopwatch it. **Never shorten rest to burn fat**; the calorie difference is trivial.

### Tempo

**The lowest-priority variable.** Anything from **0.5 to 8 seconds per rep is statistically
equivalent** for growth. Only reps over ~10 seconds are clearly worse, because you have to strip
so much load that tension drops.

Default to **1-0-1-0 or 2-0-2-0**. Use deliberate tempo only for a specific job: explosive
concentrics on heavy squats/bench (1-0-X-2), a 3-second eccentric to fix a recurring technique
fault, or an 8-second total rep on calves for pump work.

### Range of motion

**Full ROM wins.** Of six studies, all four lower-body ones favoured full range. Trading range for
heavier weight is almost always a bad deal — halving the range while adding 40% load still halves
the work. Deep squats beat heavier partial squats at every measurement site in the quad.

**The one real exception:** constant-tension partials on **free-weight isolation lifts with a slack
zone** — skull crushers, dumbbell flyes, dumbbell lateral raises. A 2017 skull crusher study found
partials grew roughly twice as much. This does **not** transfer to cables and machines, which
already supply near-constant tension.

"Full ROM" means full within *your* mobility. As long as you reach the hardest part of the lift
you're probably getting the effect — so **just below parallel on squats is likely as good as
ass-to-grass.** ([note](partial-range-of-motion.md))

### Progression, periodization, deloads

**Progressive overload is the thing.** Add weight at the same reps, or add reps, or add weekly
sets. Trommelen's sharpening: adding weight while *dropping* reps isn't progression, it's trading.
Whether your lifts are climbing is the single best indicator that everything else is working.

**Periodization** ([note](periodization-for-bodybuilding.md)): the evidence supports *variation*
more strongly than any specific model — a 2012 Kiely critique that remains unresolved. Don't run
one model. Undulate reps within the week, progress linearly on main lifts within a block, rotate
exercises, and use block-style specialisation for weak points.

**Mesocycle structure** ([Israetel](training-volume-deep-dive-israetel.md)): start at MEV,
overload weekly until you can't recover, deload, restart. Accumulation runs **3–8 weeks** — the
RIR range sets the length, since you can't start below ~5 RIR or train to failure every week.

**Deloads** ([note](deloads.md)): schedule them **proactively every 4–8 weeks**; the more advanced
you are, the more often. The standard version cuts **volume 30–50%** (one or two fewer sets per
movement) and drops RPE from 8–10 to **6–8**. Frame it as a technique week. He prefers proactive
over reactive because joint and tendon stress accumulates *unnoticed* — connective tissue isn't
well innervated, so things feel fine right up until they don't. **True beginners and people who
aren't training hard enough to accumulate fatigue don't need one.**

**Specialisation** for a lagging muscle: take it to high volume for about **4 weeks** while parking
everything else at maintenance (~6–8 sets), then rotate. This becomes necessary as you advance,
because the sum of every muscle's individual MRV eventually exceeds your whole-body recovery.

## Exercise selection

### The criteria he ranks by

Every 2024–25 tier list uses the same three tests, in order:
1. **High tension in the stretched position** — fail this and it's automatic F tier
2. **Feels good** — no joint pain, smooth resistance profile, real mind-muscle connection
3. **Simple progression** — you can add weight or reps

That first criterion is the big shift in his recent thinking. The recurring flaw he penalises:
**dumbbells give zero tension at the bottom where the muscle is most stretched, and peak tension
at the top where it's shortest.** Cables, machines and leaning variations fix this — which is why
so many machine and cable movements now outrank their free-weight equivalents in his lists.

### Best picks per muscle

| Muscle | Top choice | Also build around |
|---|---|---|
| **Chest** | Machine chest press | Incline press, dips, seated cable fly |
| **Back** | Chest-supported row | Lat pulldown (wide or neutral), cable row, Meadows row |
| **Front delts** | Machine shoulder press | Dumbbell overhead press |
| **Side delts** | Cable lateral raise (cable at hand height) | Cable Y raise; lean-in dumbbell raise if dumbbells only |
| **Rear delts** | Reverse cable crossover | Sideways reverse pec deck, low rope face pull |
| **Biceps** | Face-away (Bayesian) cable curl | 45° preacher curl, hammer curl (brachialis/peak), EZ-bar curl for heavy work |
| **Triceps** | Overhead cable extension | EZ-bar skull crusher, bar pressdown, cable kickback |
| **Quads** | Hack squat | High-bar back squat, seat-back leg extension, Bulgarian split squat |
| **Glutes** | Walking lunge | Machine abduction (upper), hip thrust machine (mid), RDL (lower) |
| **Hamstrings** | RDL + leg curl (both needed) | Seated curl beats lying; Nordics |
| **Calves** | Standing raise + seated raise | Pause at the bottom of every rep |
| **Abs** | Weighted crunch + ab wheel | Modified plank, Pallof press |
| **Neck** | Plate-loaded flexion + extension | 10–15 reps, 2–3×/week |
| **Traps** | Shrug with arms ~30° abducted | Wide-grip upright row, rope face pull |

**The single most useful finding in the exercise-selection videos:** a 2023 study found the
**overhead cable triceps extension produced ~40% more triceps growth than pressdowns.** The long
head is the only triceps head crossing the shoulder, and on pressing it shortens at the elbow
while lengthening at the shoulder — so its length barely changes and presses under-train it.
Overhead work is the non-negotiable triceps movement.

### What compounds genuinely miss

This is the case for isolation work, and it's anatomical rather than aesthetic:

- **Biceps femoris short head** — crosses only the knee, so no amount of deadlifting touches it. Needs leg curls.
- **Rectus femoris** — the only quad head crossing the hip; on squats it shortens at the hip while lengthening at the knee, so its length barely changes. Needs leg extensions, ideally **seat set back**.
- **Abs** — squats and deadlifts are not ab exercises. EMG shows low abdominal activity in the squat, and it makes sense: the abs do spinal flexion and anti-extension, neither of which happens.
- **Neck** — the strongest single finding here. In Conley's 12-week trial, a rigorous 4×/week program with squats, RDLs, deadlifts, rows and shrugs produced **zero neck growth**. Adding nine weekly sets of neck extensions produced **+13% cross-sectional area**.
- **Traps** — deadlift trap work is isometric, and eccentric actions appear necessary to maximise growth.

The same logic explains why **squats are a poor hamstring exercise** (hips and knees extend
together, so hamstring length barely changes) and why the **deadlift is only C-tier as a back
builder** (barely stretches the lats, tension is isometric, no pump) despite being an outstanding
strength and posterior-chain lift.

### Movements he'd drop

Renegade rows and all row+curl / row+press combinations (the small muscle fails first and the back
gets nothing), plate press and hex press, front raises of any kind (redundant if you press at all),
bosu squats and squat+press combos, waiter curls, donkey kicks and fire hydrants, spider and drag
curls, bench dips.

## Technique

Full notes: [squat](squat-technique.md) · [bench](bench-press-technique.md) · [deadlift](deadlift-technique.md) · [RDL](romanian-deadlift-technique.md) · [warm-up](warm-up-and-mobility.md)

**Squat.** High bar for most people. Weight over **mid-foot** the whole way — never cue "weight on
your heels," it throws balance off and makes the hips shoot up. Break at hips and knees together,
knees forward and slightly out tracking over the toes, ~1-second descent, **no pause in the hole**,
explode up. Depth: at least parallel. Knee valgus is usually poor hip external rotation — fix with
toe flare, actively driving the knees out, or a band around the knees. Mild butt wink is common
and not a concern unless severe.

**Bench.** Powerlifting setup is also the best hypertrophy setup: scapulae **retracted and
depressed throughout**, arch built, four points of contact, feet back with heels planted. Grip no
wider than ~1.5× shoulder width (a 2007 study; reduces injury risk without costing strength).
Elbows at **~45°** from the torso, bar down and slightly forward to just below nipple level, then
press **up and back toward the face** with heel drive. Sticking point sits in the first 20–40% of
the lift. He argues the ROM difference between a big and small arch is exaggerated — the humerus
position relative to the torso ends up similar.

**Deadlift.** Shins about half an inch behind the bar, bar over the tongue of the shoe, stance
slightly inside shoulder width, toes straight. **Pull the slack out before the plates move** —
never grip and rip. Be **explosive off the floor** (unlike sumo, where patience pays), because
lockout is the hard part. On the way down, **hips back first with straight knees**, and only bend
the knees once the bar clears them. Train it 1–2×/week even when advanced — the recovery cost is high.

**RDL.** He says people botch this more than any other lift. Hips **straight back**, not down.
Shins vertical — picture a wall in front of your knees. **Stop when your hips can't travel further
back without the lower back rounding**, usually just below the knee to mid-shin. More range is not
better here: going to the floor adds lumbar rounding, not hip range.

**Warm-up.** Actually raising body temperature is the part that matters — in the Fradkin review the
two studies showing no injury benefit were the ones weighted toward stretching rather than warming
up. Three phases: **5–10 min cardio to a light sweat**, then dynamic stretching (3–4 min lower
body, less upper), then a **progressive pyramid up to your working weight on the main lift only**.
Use dynamic, not static, stretching. Foam rolling is optional — helps soreness and ROM, doesn't
hurt strength, skip it if time is tight.

## Nutrition

### Calories by goal

| Goal | Calories | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Bulk** | +5–10% over maintenance | 1–2% BW/month (beginner), 0.5–1% (intermediate+) |
| **Cut** | deficit sized to the rate | lose 0.5–1% BW/week (up to 1.5% only at high body fat) |
| **Recomp** | +5–25% (muscle-first) or −10–20% (fat-loss-first) | — |

Rough maintenance estimate: **bodyweight in lb × 14–18**.

**Bulking** ([note](how-to-bulk.md)): rate of gain is what determines fat gain. Dirty bulks run
50–100% over maintenance and just fill fat stores. Set fat at **20–30% of calories** — too little
lowers testosterone, too much adds fat, and dietary fat is stored as body fat more readily than
carbohydrate is. Carbs fill the rest. **Train harder on a bulk, not less** — the calories only
become muscle if the stimulus is there.

**Cutting** ([note](how-to-get-lean.md)): Helms' five keys in priority order — energy deficit,
progressive resistance training, appropriate deficit size, sufficient protein, appropriate cardio
volume. The lifting is what makes the lost weight fat rather than muscle.

**Recomposition** ([note](body-recomposition.md)) is possible for **nearly everyone**, not a rare
edge case: new lifters, overweight lifters, detrained lifters, steroid users, and — his added
category — anyone who's been in the gym for years without really training hard. The only group it's
impractical for is advanced lifters near their genetic ceiling with already-optimised training.
A 2013 Garthe study found overfeeding by 600 kcal/day produced **no extra muscle but over three
times the fat**.

### Protein

| Phase | Target |
|---|---|
| Bulk | **1.6–2.2 g/kg** (0.7–1.0 g/lb) |
| Cut | **1.8–2.7 g/kg** (0.8–1.2 g/lb) |
| Recomp | bulk numbers or slightly higher; or 1.2–1.6 g/lb of **lean** mass |
| High body fat shortcut | **1 g per cm of height** (Helms) |

The Morton meta-analysis puts the optimum at **1.6 g/kg**, with the confidence interval reaching
**2.1–2.2** to cover individual variation. **1.2 g/kg is definitively insufficient.** Push higher
if you're older, obese, or doing lots of full-body work. **Women respond the same as men** — their
starting point is smaller, not their response.

**Per meal** ([Trommelen](protein-dose-per-day-and-per-meal.md)): the old 20–25 g ceiling is wrong.
About **20 g gives near-maximal MPS for 4–5 hours**; **40 g adds roughly 10–20% more** — real but
small. The response is a dial, not a switch: even 5 g does something, it's roughly linear to 20 g,
then incremental returns fall off sharply.

Trommelen's two protocols:
- **Minimalist:** 20 g at breakfast, 20 g at lunch, 20 g at dinner, **40 g before sleep**
- **Maximalist:** at least 40 g per meal, at least 4 meals per day

**Pre-sleep is the underrated lever.** In his study **30 g did not significantly raise overnight
MPS; 40 g did** — the night is 7–8+ hours, far longer than a 20 g dose covers. In a sample of 300+
national-to-Olympic athletes, total protein averaged ~1.5 g/kg but **over 80% came from breakfast,
lunch and dinner**, leaving a ~12-hour overnight gap. Adding a pre-sleep feeding fixes the total
*and* the distribution. It does not make you fat and does not hurt sleep quality. Don't set an
alarm to eat mid-night.

**Quality** barely matters on a mixed diet hitting the total. Vegans are the real exception — eat
toward the top of the range and mix sources (expect to need 10–20% more), since plant proteins hit
the **limiting amino acid** problem. **Collagen has essentially zero leucine** and is useless for
muscle; "beef protein" supplements are often just collagen.

### Timing

[Full note](nutrient-timing-around-training.md). The window is real but it's **4–5 hours**, not 30
minutes — pre- and post-workout meals shouldn't be separated by more than that.

- **Pre-workout, 1–1.5 h out:** ~1 g/kg carbs (low-GI source plus a serving of fruit), ~0.5 g/kg protein, 0–20 g fat
- **Post-workout:** ~0.5 g/kg protein, plus 1–1.5 g/kg carbs (or ~0.5 g/kg when cutting)
- **Intra-workout:** optional. Worth it if you train fasted, are lean and in a deficit, train over an hour, or are elite — **0.5 g carbs per minute of training, starting 30 min in**

Fat is the timing lever: less fat the closer the meal is to training, more the further out.
Training fasted isn't disqualifying, but it makes intra- and post-workout nutrition much more urgent.

The underlying reason the window is generous: training **sensitises the muscle to dietary amino
acids for 1–2 days, with the longest documented MPS elevation at 72 hours.** You are effectively
always in a post-exercise state.

### Refeeds and diet breaks

[Full note](refeeds-and-diet-breaks.md). Net balance decides whether you lose weight, but **how you
distribute the deficit changes body composition** — Garthe found a slower diet lost the same weight
but more of it as fat.

- **Refeeds should run 48 hours, not 24.** One day is too short to raise metabolic rate, and full glycogen replenishment can need up to 10 g carbs/kg — far more practical as two days at ~400 g than one at 800 g. Campbell 2020 (n=27 trained lifters): a 5-days-deficit/2-days-maintenance structure **preserved fat-free mass and resting metabolic rate better** than a continuous deficit, with equal fat loss. Time them 1–2 days before your hardest session.
- **Diet breaks** (2+ weeks at maintenance) look spectacular in obese non-exercisers — Byrne 2017 found 50% more fat loss and twice-preserved resting expenditure — but a 2021 trial in **trained** subjects found **no physiological difference at all**. What it did find: every psychological outcome favoured diet breaks — less hunger, less desire to eat, more diet satisfaction. Which plausibly still matters for adherence.
- **Classic cheat days are out.** Too easy to erase the whole weekly deficit.

His single most consistent recommendation across the nutrition videos: **give the diet more time
than you think you need.**

### Metabolism and cardio

[metabolism](metabolism-and-energy-expenditure.md) · [cardio](cardio-for-fat-loss.md)

Metabolic rates differ enormously — in a 6,000-subject *Science* study, people at 80 kg ranged from
**~1,400 to ~5,700 kcal/day**. Only four things reliably move yours:

1. **Building muscle** — muscle burns ~6 kcal/lb/day vs fat's ~2. Adding **30 lb of muscle ≈ +180 kcal/day.** The biggest lever, and it takes years.
2. **Cardio** — but it's partly self-cancelling: every 100 calories burned yields only about **72 extra** because NEAT drops to compensate.
3. **Dieting slowly** — aggressive cuts tank metabolic rate.
4. **Smuggling in NEAT** — stairs, walking, standing, parking further away.

Green tea, sauna, cold plunges and meal frequency are worth **5–30 calories** and don't move the
needle. He's sceptical of reverse dieting: **jump straight to your new maintenance** rather than
creeping up.

**Cardio dosing:** HIIT **1–2×/week, 15–30 min**; LISS **0–5×/week, 20–40 min**. **Lift first,
cardio last** — a 2017 meta-analysis found cardio immediately before weights significantly worsened
1RM gains, with strength compromised for 6–8 hours after endurance work. A 2021 meta-analysis found
interference **only in well-trained** people, so this is an advanced-lifter problem. Real-world
weight loss from cardio is only **20–50% of what the calorie math predicts** — compensatory eating
and reduced NEAT. **Fasted cardio does not improve fat loss.**

## Supplements

[supplements](supplements-that-work.md) · [creatine](creatine.md)

The short list, and it really is short:

| Supplement | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Creatine monohydrate** | **3–5 g/day** | Optional load: 20–25 g/day for a week. Never cycle. Timing irrelevant |
| **Whey / protein powder** | as needed | Convenience for hitting the daily total; a whey/casein blend or mixing with milk is his preference |
| **Caffeine** | **4 mg/kg pre-workout** | Cycle off **3–7 days every 1–2 months** — tolerance is described as insurmountable |
| **Citrulline malate** | **4–10 g, 1 h pre** | Effect grows with each set; also reduced soreness at 24 and 48 h |
| **Multivitamin** | 1/day | Most valuable in a deficit, where micronutrient shortfalls are common |

**Creatine specifics:** raises muscle stores by 20–40% from a baseline that's only 60–80%
saturated. Expect **5–15% more maximal strength**, and in one study **30% more total reps across
five sets** even in six-year-trained subjects. Water goes **inside** the muscle, not under the
skin. Roughly **20–30% may be non-responders** (high meat eaters and older trainees most likely).
On hair loss: **never directly investigated.** The concern traces to one 2009 rugby study where
5 g/day raised DHT 40% over two weeks — but weight training raises DHT too, and DHT matters for
hair loss mainly in men already genetically predisposed.

**No supplement causes fat loss.** Trexler's rundown of the fat-burner categories finds every one
either habituating, poorly quality-controlled, side-effect-laden, or simply unsupported.

## Recovery, breaks and individual differences

**Sleep is the most underrated variable in the library.** In Wang 2018, dieters sleeping one hour
less on five nights a week lost the **same total weight** — but the well-slept group lost
**83% of it as fat** while the sleep-restricted group lost **85% of it as lean mass**. (Caveat: the
subjects weren't lifting, which would soften but not erase an effect that size.) Target 7–8+ hours.

**Muscle memory is real and mechanistically supported** ([note](muscle-memory-and-detraining.md)).
Two mechanisms: **myonuclei survive atrophy** (a 2010 Oslo mouse study — the muscle shrinks, the
nuclei stay, so on return you skip the satellite-cell donation step entirely) and **epigenetic
changes persist in muscle-cell DNA** (a 2018 human study confirmed these lasted through a seven-week
break). Most people don't lose meaningful size or strength until **2–3 weeks** of no training, and
in a 24-week study, periodic training (6 on, 3 off) and continuous training **ended at the same
point**. So: if you're injured, rest and heal. But breaks are permission, not a strategy — and be
sceptical of any program's results measured right after a layoff.

**Sex differences** ([note](sex-differences-in-training.md)) are minor tweaks to the same program,
not a different philosophy. Women build somewhere between **50 and 100%** of male hypertrophic
potential — the literature genuinely conflicts. **97% of the absolute strength difference is
attributable to muscle mass alone.** Women recover better and fatigue less (better perfusion,
greater proportional type I fibre area), so they can handle **more volume, higher reps and shorter
rest**; men are relatively better suited to explosive work.

**Genetics and leverages** set the timeline, not the destination. Short arms and a deep ribcage
favour the bench but hurt the deadlift; short femurs favour the squat. Leverage disadvantages are
surmountable — Layne Norton set a squat world record with unusually long femurs — they just change
how hard you have to work.

## Strength standards

[Full note](strength-standards-by-training-age.md). Bodyweight multiples for a 1RM, male:

| Level | Squat | Bench | Deadlift | Typical timeline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | 1.25× | 1.0× | 1.5× | 6 months – 2 years |
| Intermediate | 1.25–1.75× | 1.0–1.5× | 1.5–2.25× | 2–5 years |
| Advanced | 1.75–2.5× | 1.5–2.0× | 2.25–3.0× | ~5 years |
| Elite | 2.5–3.0× | 2.0–2.25× | 3.0–3.5× | 5–10 years |
| Freak | 3.0×+ | 2.25×+ | 3.5×+ | genetics |

Conditions: constant technique (full depth, paused bench), natural and reasonably lean, and cap
your input weight at 250 lb since the ratios skew above that. You don't need to test a 1RM — run a
hard sub-5-rep set through a calculator.

**The method changes at each level**, which is the actually useful part. Linear progression works
until roughly the 1–2 year mark. Then you need undulating work (a heavy day and a lighter
hypertrophy or speed day). Then specialisation phases with the other lifts at maintenance, plus
dedicated hypertrophy blocks once size becomes the limiter.

## Where he changed his mind, and where he's unsure

Worth reading before you treat any single number here as settled.

**Positions that moved over the decade:**

- **Volume framing.** The 2018 Fundamentals series presents 10–20 sets/week as the headline. The 2022 junk volume video adds the crucial per-*session* constraint (~6 sets) that reframes how those weekly sets must be distributed. Same weekly number, very different programming.
- **Frequency.** He publicly corrected himself: the 2016 bro-split video is explicitly an update to an earlier pro-bro-split position, and he flags his own bias toward low frequency and tries to correct for it.
- **Stretch position.** The 2024–25 tier lists rank almost entirely on tension in the stretched position — a criterion largely absent from the older videos. This is what moved dumbbell flyes from "C tier" to worth doing (as lengthened partials), and what promotes cables and machines over their free-weight equivalents.
- **Rest periods.** The old textbook 1-minute-for-hypertrophy prescription is now abandoned in favour of 3–5 minutes on compounds.

**Live disagreements he doesn't resolve:**

- **Beginners and effort.** Nippard thinks most general-public lifters need permission to push harder. **Israetel argues the opposite** — that the statistically more common beginner problem is going *too* close to failure with unstable technique, grooving bad motor patterns and risking injury in exactly the population that pays most for it. They talk past this on camera without settling it.
- **How far from failure.** Credible experts span 0–7 RIR. That's not a small range.
- **The volume ceiling.** Schoenfeld's study found growth still rising at 30–45 sets/muscle/week; Fisher and Steele's lab found 5–10 sets maxed it out. Schoenfeld's own explanation for the conflict is that the Fisher/Steele protocol crammed the volume into a bro split — which points back at the per-session junk threshold.
- **Whether MPS predicts hypertrophy.** Trommelen's case is that the measurement window has to match what you're studying: measure 4 hours post-session and you see no correlation; measure 24–48 hours and you do.

**Things to be careful with:**

- **EMG activation is not hypertrophy.** A large share of the exercise-selection content rests on activation data, and he flags the inference as "most plausible," not proven, nearly every time.
- **Tier lists are partly personal.** Several placements come down to "feels good," "annoying," or "awkward," and he says so. Machine rankings assume you have a *good* machine.
- **Small, short training studies deserve scepticism.** Trommelen's filter: under 10 weeks with fewer than ~20 subjects per group commands very little faith. About **80% of protein supplementation studies find no significant muscle gain** purely because they're underpowered — had the field relied on those alone, it would have abandoned protein fifty years ago.
- **Averages aren't people.** Studies report means. On the same program one subject gains 20% muscle and another gains zero.
