## If you read nothing else

Train each muscle **twice a week**, **10–20 hard sets a week**, no more than **6–8 of them in one
session**, each set taken to within **1–3 reps of failure**, mostly in the **6–15 rep** range,
through a full range of motion with a controlled — not slow — eccentric, resting **3–5 minutes** on
compounds. Add weight or reps over time and write it down. Eat **1.6–2.2 g of protein per kg**,
run a **5–10% surplus** to gain or a **15–25% deficit** to lose, and sleep **7–8 hours**. Take
**3–5 g of creatine** a day. Everything else in 279 notes is refinement.

The two failure modes he names most often, across the whole decade: people obsess over the bottom
of the hierarchy while neglecting the top, and **people do not train nearly as hard as they think
they do**. In the study he returns to most, lifters given their usual "10-rep" bench weight and told
to go to failure got **16 to 20+ reps** — **47%** of them — meaning six to ten reps left in the tank
on every set they'd been calling hard ([not training hard enough](not-training-hard-enough.md)).
Over **40% of gym-goers train at RPE 4 or below**.

## The hierarchy

The variables at the top decide your results; the ones at the bottom are rounding errors. Fix them
in order ([basics and theory](training-basics-and-theory.md)).

1. **Sustainability** — a program you'll run for years
2. **Effort** — sets genuinely close to failure
3. **Progressive overload** — more weight or reps over time, tracked
4. **Volume** — hard sets per muscle per week
5. **Frequency** — how those sets are spread
6. **Exercise selection** — what fills the slots
7. **Rest periods**
8. **Tempo** — explicitly last

By 2023 he collapses the whole ladder into one idea: **mechanical tension is the driver**. Muscle
damage and metabolic stress, the other two thirds of the old three-mechanism model, have both
failed to hold up ([how muscle grows](build-muscle.md)). Chase tension, not soreness and not the
pump.

## Training

Full detail in [programming and volume](SUMMARY-programming.md).

### Volume

**10–20 hard sets per muscle per week** held steady from 2016 to 2025, and in December 2024 he
revised the floor down to **8**. The floor is well supported — more than 10 sets a week roughly
**doubled** hypertrophy in Schoenfeld's 2017 meta-analysis. **The ceiling has never been
established**, and it is the single largest open question in the library.

The landmarks, from [Israetel](training-volume-deep-dive-israetel.md):

| Landmark | Sets/muscle/week | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **MV** maintenance | ~6–8 | holds what you have while you prioritise elsewhere |
| **MEV** minimum effective | ~10 | least that still grows you; where a block starts |
| **MAV** maximum adaptive | drifts | the sweet spot, which *moves* across a block |
| **MRV** maximum recoverable | ~20 | defined by failing to repeat last week's performance |

Count **hard working sets only** — warm-ups don't count and every counted set must be within about
three reps of failure. **Individual variation is the whole point, not a footnote**: Israetel's own
hamstrings cap at 10–15 sets while his side delts take the mid-to-high twenties. Budget about
**six months** to learn your own numbers.

**Junk volume is the most practically useful correction in the library**
([note](junk-volume.md)). Three ways sets get wasted:

1. **Too many in one session.** Growth per session plateaus around **six sets per muscle**.
2. **Sets that are too easy.** The most common and most damaging — see the 47% figure above.
3. **Ultra-high-rep sets.** Above ~40–50 reps you're buying fatigue, not growth.

The consequence: **20 chest sets on one chest day is at least half junk.** Ten on Monday and ten on
Thursday is better work for the same set count.

The counterweight is his own 2025 experiment — **100 days at 6.5 sets per muscle per week**, which
cost him **1.8 lb of lean mass** while dropping 5.5 lb of fat, with strength up
([100 days of low volume](cut-number-sets-half.md)). Low volume is not nothing; it is a trade.
The minimalist literature puts **1–4 sets a week at roughly 64% of the gains** and **5–9 sets at
84%** — with the honest caveat, which he supplies himself, that only two of fifteen studies used
trained lifters ([minimalist training](train-like-minimalist.md)).

### Effort

Failure is an **involuntary set endpoint** — you physically cannot move the weight — not "it felt
hard" ([proximity to failure](how-hard-to-train-proximity-to-failure.md)). **Target 1–3 RIR**, with
~2 RIR as Israetel's best-average stopping point, on the argument that hypertrophy rises roughly
*linearly* toward failure while fatigue rises *exponentially*.

Failure has a measurable price. Volume-matched, taking sets to failure impaired bench performance
for **48 hours** and squat for **24**, with creatine kinase back to baseline at 48 hours rather
than 24. So spend it where it's cheap: **compounds at 1–3 RIR, isolation to failure, intensity
techniques on the last set only.** His working target is about **30% of sets to true failure**.

This is one of the positions that moved most — see below.

### Frequency and splits

**2× per week per muscle beats 1×** at matched weekly volume. **Whether 3× beats 2× is genuinely
unresolved**, and he's careful about it: the strongest pro-frequency study used only three sets per
muscle per week, which is nothing like real programming.

The honest case for higher frequency isn't protein-synthesis theory — it's **volume spread**. The
same weekly sets across more sessions, each performed fresher, each session under the per-session
junk threshold.

**No split architecture grows more muscle than another.** Three questions decide it, in order: does
it let you hit your weekly volume, does it allow recovery, will you actually run it. When days are
scarce, **collapse rather than skip** — four days becomes upper/lower, two days becomes full body.
The bro split grades **C+, not F**: it works at matched volume, and its problem is arithmetic, not
architecture — progressing past intermediate means adding weekly sets, and stacking those onto one
day makes them junk.

He changed his own split roughly every two years across the decade and never claimed it cost him
anything.

### Load, range of motion, tempo, rest

**Hypertrophy is equivalent from about 3 to 35 reps** at matched effort and volume. **Strength is
not** — that needs heavy work. Put **~75% of volume in 6–15 reps** and keep load above roughly
**20–30% of 1RM**.

**Long-length partials** are the best-supported intensity technique in the library: they beat
short-length partials in **7 of 8 studies** and full ROM in **4 of 5**
([long-length partials](world-s-smartest-muscle-building-technique.md)). His own 2024 study on 30
trained lifters came back **null** — 6.6% versus 5.9%, not significant
([his own study](everyone-thinks-m-wrong-about-training.md)). He uses them more since, not less,
which he acknowledges is a judgement call rather than a finding.

**Tempo is the lowest-priority variable and he retracted his own position on it.** Anything from
**0.5 to 8 seconds per rep** works; past 10 seconds is worse. Slow negatives specifically were
dropped in December 2024 — three studies show 1–2 seconds matches 3–4.

**Rest 3–5 minutes on compounds, 1–2 on isolation, never under a minute.** Longer rest won on both
hypertrophy and strength, which reversed the old one-minute-for-hypertrophy prescription.

### The session

His recurring template, distilled from roughly 25 sample-workout videos:

5–10 min cardio and 2–4 min dynamic stretching → **one heavy overloadable compound** (low reps,
tracked, 1–3 RIR, not to failure) → **a back-off or second compound at ~75%** of that load →
a machine or cable compound at RPE 8–9 → a **stretch-position isolation** → a
**contracted-position isolation** with an intensity technique on the final set → calves, abs, neck.
Weak point first, heaviest first, alternate upper and lower on full-body days to buy rest.

Progression is matched to the lift: **rep double-progression** on bench, **set progression**
(2×5 → 3×5 → 4×5) on technical lifts because the extra sets buy skill practice, wave loading on
squat, reps-only on RDLs so load creep doesn't hand the work to the low back.

**Random exercise swapping is "the number one cause of lifetime intermediates."** Hold an exercise
1–2 months.

### Strength standards

Bodyweight multiples for a 1RM, male
([standards by training age](strength-standards-by-training-age.md)):

| Level | Squat | Bench | Deadlift | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | 1.25× | 1.0× | 1.5× | 6 months – 2 years |
| Intermediate | 1.25–1.75× | 1.0–1.5× | 1.5–2.25× | 2–5 years |
| Advanced | 1.75–2.5× | 1.5–2.0× | 2.25–3.0× | ~5 years |
| Elite | 2.5–3.0× | 2.0–2.25× | 3.0–3.5× | 5–10 years |

Cap your input weight at 250 lb — the ratios skew above that. **Linear progression dies at the
1–2 year mark**; after that you need undulation, then specialisation blocks.

## Exercise selection

Full detail in [exercise selection](SUMMARY-exercise-selection.md).

Since 2024, three criteria decide every ranking: **tension in the stretched position** (failing this
is an automatic F), **whether it feels good**, and **whether it progresses simply**. Resistance
profile is the most common reason something gets demoted — dumbbells and bands load the shortened
position, where the muscle is strongest and the stimulus is weakest.

The best-supported pick per muscle:

| Muscle | Pick | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Chest | Machine chest press | Incline over decline as the default angle |
| Back (overall) | Chest-supported row | Close-grip cable row or neutral pulldown for lats |
| Front delts | Machine shoulder press | |
| Side delts | Cable lateral raise | Cable at hand height |
| Rear delts | Reverse cable crossover | |
| Biceps | Bayesian cable curl or preacher curl | Hammer curl for brachialis |
| Triceps | Overhead cable extension with a bar | **~40% more growth than pressdowns** (Maeo 2023) |
| Forearms | Reverse-grip curl, thumbless | Top-half partials |
| Quads | Hack squat | High-bar squat if unavailable |
| Hamstrings | Seated leg curl + RDL | **~50% more growth than lying** |
| Glutes | Walking lunge | Hip thrust machine for mid-glute |
| Calves | Standing raise | Bottom pause mandatory |
| Abs | Weighted cable crunch + a leg raise | |
| Neck / traps | Plate neck curl, trap bar shrug | |

**Free weights versus machines is a wash for hypertrophy** if sets go close to failure (Haugen
2023). Use both; free weights first while you're fresh.

**Exercise selection is variable six of eight. Nothing is mandatory** — including squat, bench and
deadlift ([is any exercise essential](lateral-raise-only-must-exercise.md)). Cover the six movement
patterns, then fill the gaps isolation has to fill: biceps femoris short head, abs, neck, calves,
forearms.

**Treat the rankings with care.** He says so himself: S versus B means *slightly* more versus
*slightly* less effective, several placements come down to "feels good" or "awkward", machine
rankings assume a good machine, and — his own admission in December 2024 — **no study has ever
directly measured back hypertrophy**, so the back rankings rest on indirect evidence plus personal
experience.

## Technique

Full detail in [technique](SUMMARY-technique.md).

Technique has two jobs: **make the target muscle the limiting factor**, and **keep you healthy
enough to keep training**. Adding weight only counts as overload when form is unchanged; the named
failure mode is *progressive cheating*.

What generalises across every lift:

- **Hips back, not down**, initiates every hinge.
- **Set the scapulae before the rep** — retract and depress on bench, dip, fly and skull crusher;
  depress to initiate on pull-ups and pulldowns.
- **Brace 360° against the belt**, not by pushing the gut forward.
- **Bar over mid-foot** on squat and deadlift — explicitly not on bench, and not on good mornings.
- **Stack the joints**: vertical shins on hinges, wrist over elbow on bench.
- **Control the eccentric everywhere except the deadlift**, where resisting the negative costs more
  recovery than it returns.
- **Internal focus for isolation, external for heavy compounds** — roughly the 8-rep line.
- **Controlled cheating is defensible only when the muscle's strength curve, not its fatigue, ends
  the set.** Being ultra-strict is itself listed as an error on dumbbell rows.
- **Anatomy sets the ceiling.** Femur-to-tibia ratio drives how much you lean in a squat. Insertions
  don't change.
- **Film it.** Mirrors give you one angle.

**Going too heavy is the number one error by a wide margin** — named as the top fault on Pendlay
rows, curls, lateral raises, skull crushers and leg extensions, and the root cause of RDL knee
travel and deadlift rounding.

And the caveat he added in December 2024: **"perfect technique" is not itself science-based.** No
strict-versus-loose form study has ever been run. He is running one.

## Nutrition

Full detail in [nutrition](SUMMARY-nutrition.md).

**Energy balance governs net storage, not tissue** — the energy-density gap between muscle and fat
is exactly why recomposition works. Two numbers that recalibrate most people: a genuine
**10,000-calorie day deposits about 0.5–0.6 lb of actual fat**, and three pounds of *fat* in a week
would require a **10,800-calorie excess**. Run the arithmetic before panicking at the scale.

**Bulking.** Surplus **5–10%**; gain **1–2% of bodyweight a month** as a beginner, **0.5–1%** after
that. Garthe's trial is the one to remember: **+600 kcal/day produced no extra muscle and three
times the fat**. Nutrition is permissive, not causative — the surplus permits growth, training
causes it.

**Cutting.** **0.5–1% of bodyweight a week** from a **15–25% deficit**. Cutting faster costs lean
mass: across three prep case studies, **21%, 32% and 43%** of the weight lost was lean. And sleep is
the most underrated variable in the whole library — in Wang 2018, dieters sleeping an hour less on
five nights a week lost the same total weight but flipped it from **83% fat** to **85% lean mass**.

**Where to sit.** Men **10–20%**, women **20–30%**, and the argument is against living at the
bottom of that: getting to 4.5% dropped one subject's testosterone from **922 to 227 ng/dL**,
doubled cortisol, and raised mood disturbance **617%**. The model he lands on is the *dual
intervention point* — sit at the **lean boundary of your own range**, not below it.

**"Starvation mode" is a myth; metabolic adaptation is real.** In the Minnesota experiment a 55%
deficit over 24 weeks stalled nobody. RMR fell 40%, but only about **15% of that was truly
adaptive** — the rest was a smaller body and less movement. Real stalls are, in order:
**37% average under-reporting of intake**, water retention, then deficit size.

**Protein.** **1.6–2.2 g/kg bulking, 1.8–2.7 g/kg cutting** — and note he revised this *downward*
over the decade, from "0.8–1 g/lb" in 2019 to naming excessive protein one of the four worst
nutrition mistakes by 2023. **~20 g gives near-maximal MPS**; 40 g adds 10–20%, often
non-significantly. Absorption is a red herring — about **95% is absorbed, but only ~10% of a meal
reaches muscle**. Pre-sleep, **40 g** works where 30 g didn't. Bone-loss and kidney fears are
busted for healthy people; **4.4 g/kg** has been reported harmless.

**The anabolic window is 4–6 hours** spanning the session, not 45 minutes — though by 2021 he says
the science crowd overcorrected, and the pre/post gap should still stay inside 4–5 hours.

**Sugar, carefully.** This is the richest arc in the library and it does not resolve the way the
titles suggest. In 2018 a 68-study review found **isocaloric sugar swaps change nothing**, and the
video titled as a self-correction is mostly a defence with concessions — he softens "addictive" to
"addictive-*like*" and concedes the individual-overconsumption case. **The position actually
changes in 2019**, with Guyenet: sugar does matter, not as a metabolic poison but because it makes
food more seductive and raises total intake. The strongest evidence against sugar-as-cause is that
**US intake fell 15–23% since 1999 and UK intake ~22% while obesity rose** — an ecological argument
that carries more weight in the recaps than it can really bear.

**Diets as systems.** Keto loses its advantage once calories and protein are matched (Soenen, at one
year). Intermittent fasting, across a 40-study review, gives **better hunger control and no better
weight loss or body composition**. Fibre at **10% of carb grams, floor 20 g** — and its satiety
case is weaker than advertised, with only **43% of 104 RCTs** finding any appetite effect.

**Cardio** delivers only **20–50% of predicted weight loss**, and you net just **72 of every 100
calories** burned. Cap HIIT at 1–2×/week. Fasted cardio is neutral over 24 hours.

**Food quality and cost matter less than anyone selling either would like.** His own test:
**$8.92 a day beat $10,000 a day** at matched macros.

## Supplements, recovery and physiology

Full detail in [supplements and physiology](SUMMARY-supplements-and-physiology.md).

The list that works is short:

| Compound | Dose / timing | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Creatine monohydrate** | **3–5 g/day**, any time, forever | **High** — 5–15% more max strength; 30% more total reps across five sets |
| Protein powder | enough to hit the daily total | High for the total |
| **Caffeine** | **4 mg/kg** pre; cycle off 3–7 d every 1–2 months | High — negates the performance cost of lost sleep |
| Vitamin D | up to 3,000 IU/day if sun-deprived | Moderate — deficiency logic, not gains data |
| Multivitamin | 1/day, non-negotiable in a deficit | Moderate |
| Magnesium (not oxide) | daily | Moderate |
| Citrulline malate | 4–10 g, 1 h pre | Moderate and **disputed inside the library** |
| Ashwagandha | 600 mg/day | Low-moderate, for stress not testosterone |

**Beta-alanine** works only for 1–4 minute efforts, so it does nothing for a 6–15 rep set. **BCAAs**
failed across 12 studies. **Test boosters** do nothing at normal testosterone. **Turkesterone** has
zero human evidence and products tested at 0.15% of label. **SARMs**: only **52% of 44 products**
contained any SARM at all. **No supplement causes fat loss.**

**Creatine specifics**: loading is optional and only buys speed. Water goes *inside* the muscle.
Roughly 20–30% are non-responders. On hair loss — **never directly investigated**; the concern
traces to one 2009 rugby study, and training raises DHT too.

**Acute hormonal spikes from training don't build muscle.** The wood-chopping study is the worked
example of the inference error: testosterone rose, muscle protein synthesis didn't differ. And the
natural levers are all small — body fat and calories dominate everything else.

**Steroids, factually.** Bhasin 1996 is the number that reframes the whole debate: **600 mg/week
with no training at all (+7 lb) beat natural training (+4.4 lb)**. The natural ceiling sits around
**FFMI 22–24, maybe 25** — a trend, not a law, since pre-steroid Mr. Americas and average college
offensive linemen breach it. The 2024 cohort data: **2.8% mortality versus 1.0%** in matched
non-users, at an average age of 27. Enhanced lifters recover roughly **ten times faster**, which is
precisely why their programs are bad models for you.

**Detraining.** Two to three weeks off costs essentially nothing; two months costs about half.
**One-ninth of normal volume maintained mass for 32 weeks.** Muscle memory is real; the mechanism is
contested — myonuclear retention (shown in mice) versus epigenetic memory (Seaborne, humans), with
Snijders 2019 finding human myonuclei back at baseline within a year.

**Recovery modalities mostly don't.** Cold water immersion has a real cost — over 12 weeks it lost
to a ten-minute bike cooldown on *every* measure. Foam rolling is harmless and buys range of motion,
but has never been shown to beat a decent warm-up.

**Individual differences are large.** **50–80% of muscle-size variability is heritable**; on one
identical program, bicep growth ranged from **−2% to +59%**. For women, **97% of the absolute
strength gap is muscle mass**, and the tweaks are minor: better recovery at moderate loads, not at
85%+.

## How to read the evidence

Full detail in [myths and evidence](SUMMARY-myths-and-evidence.md), which also carries the
deduplicated list of **31 myths** with verdicts.

- **EMG is a proxy, not evidence.** Group averages, electrode-area limits, and studies that
  repeatedly fail to equate load. A large share of the 2015–2019 exercise content rests on it, and
  it is the material he has revised most.
- **Small, short studies deserve very little faith.** Trommelen's filter: under 10 weeks with fewer
  than ~20 per group. About **80% of protein supplementation studies find no significant gain**
  purely because they're underpowered — had the field relied on those alone it would have abandoned
  protein fifty years ago.
- **Averages aren't people.** Studies report means; on the same program one subject gains 20% and
  another gains nothing.
- **A mechanism is not an outcome.** Acute testosterone, acute MPS and activation all fail to
  predict growth on their own.
- **"You can find a study to prove anything" doesn't survive contact with the actual literature** —
  it describes cherry-picking abstracts, not reading a body of evidence
  ([note](addressing-find-study-prove-anything.md)).

## Where he changed his mind

The **December 2024 retrospective** ([lifting-over.md](lifting-over.md)) is the single most
valuable note in the library, because it is him retracting his own past positions:

- **Slow negatives — retracted outright.** Three studies show 1–2 s matches 3–4 s.
- **Technique cues have minor impact**, including the evidence-backed ones.
- **"Perfect technique" is not science-based** — the strict-versus-loose study has never been run.
- **The tier lists were misread, and he says that's partly his fault.** S versus B is a small
  difference; he'd personally pick his B-tier barbell row over his S-tier one-arm pulldown.
- **No study has ever directly measured back hypertrophy.**
- **Volume revised to 8–20 sets**, with 4 workable and 30 for a short specialisation block. He
  explicitly declines to follow a new 42-set meta-analysis because those trials ran 6–10 weeks.
- **Effort revised toward failure** — 1–2 RIR with the last set to true failure, against his 2019
  "failure only on the last isolation set."

The longer arcs, across the decade:

| Position | Then | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | 15–20 sets (2016), then "no proven ceiling" and 30–45 sets (2018–19) | 8–20, with a per-session cap, and a public reversal after his own low-volume experiment (2025) |
| Failure | use sparingly (2017–20) | last set to failure (2023), nearly every set at low volume (2025) |
| Frequency | bro-split defender (2016) | full body 5×/week himself (2020) |
| Range of motion | full ROM wins (2021) | long-length partials (2023), kept even after his own null study (2024) |
| Stretch position | largely absent from rankings before 2021 | the primary ranking criterion |
| Rest periods | one minute for hypertrophy | 3–5 minutes on compounds |
| Inter-set stretching | called bro science | publicly reversed (2019) |
| Reverse dieting | "the slow ramp buys nothing" (2016), "fine if lean" (2019) | **don't do it** (2023) |
| Leanness | sub-10% as the working range (2015) | find your range, sit at its lean edge (2022–25) |
| Protein | 0.8–1 g/lb (2019) | 0.7 g/lb break point; excess protein named a top-four mistake (2023) |
| Diet breaks | MATADOR as a physiological win (2017) | psychological only in trained lifters (2021) |
| Meal plans | five reasons a fixed plan beats IIFYM (2015) | audits that same plan and names rigidity its core flaw (2020) |
| Recomposition | four narrow groups qualify (2017) | nearly everyone (2019) |

He also loses arguments on camera and leaves them in, which is worth something. **Israetel** pushes
back hard on soreness — arguing the things that cause it (volume, effort, eccentrics, long muscle
lengths) are the things that cause growth, against Nippard's "soreness is just novelty" — and
neither of them settles it. **Contreras** treats the mind-muscle connection as one of two roads to
hypertrophy where Israetel calls it a quaternary variable. **Norton** and Nippard split on full ROM
and on creatine.

## Where the evidence is genuinely unsettled

Not hedging — these are places where the library contains contradictory findings and says so.

**Training**
- **The volume ceiling.** Pelland 2024 says 43 sets; Fisher and Steele say 10; Barbalho found 5
  beating 20. No mechanism reconciles them.
- **3× versus 2× weekly frequency**, and the novelty confound sitting inside every frequency study.
- **How close to failure.** Credible experts span **0–7 RIR** and two meta-analyses conflict.
- **Whether the per-session ceiling exists at all.** Quoted as 5, 5–10, 6–8 and ~10 across four
  years; Helms argues there is no threshold in either direction.
- Whether the eccentric is more anabolic; metabolic stress; delayed supercompensation; how to count
  indirect volume from compounds.

**Nutrition**
- Whether fast fat regain permanently raises a set point — Norton's case rests on **one rat study**.
- Refeeds in trained lifters rest on **one trial** (Campbell 2020, n=27).
- The usable per-meal protein ceiling; meal frequency (admitted underpowered); pre-sleep protein
  (acute positive, longitudinal null at high daily totals).
- Saturated fat, where biomarker and hard-outcome evidence diverge and Patel's answer is that
  nobody knows. Eggs, where neither the RCT nor the observational data has a moderate-intake group.
- **Spot reduction is an open question, not a bust** — three null studies against one positive, and

## Known problems in this library

Found during the summarisation pass. Recorded here rather than quietly patched, because a reader
hitting one of these notes needs to know.

**Wrong reference lists** — Nippard pasted the wrong sources into three video descriptions, so the
references in these notes do not support their claims. Each note flags it in place:
[training when sick](train-when-sick.md) (sexual-health papers),
[muscle imbalances](fix-muscle-imbalances-asymmetries-4-tips.md) (creatine papers), and a malformed
link on a progression video.

**A citation that doesn't support its claim** — the resolved Farina 2010 reference in
[the cable kickback note](build-bigger-glutes-perfect-training-technique.md) is about decoding
neural drive, and does not support the "pain inhibits muscle excitation" statement it sits beside.

**Misleading filenames.** [most-effective-push-workout-chest-shoulders-triceps.md](most-effective-push-workout-chest-shoulders-triceps.md)
is push day *one* and [most-effective-push-workout.md](most-effective-push-workout.md) is push day
*two* — the names read backwards, and the same inversion applies to the two pull days. Also:
[tell-if-someone-steroids.md](tell-if-someone-steroids.md) is an FFMI note, not a detection guide;
[most-effective-way-deadlift-muscle-strength.md](most-effective-way-deadlift-muscle-strength.md) is
the sumo tutorial; [our-best-work-yet.md](our-best-work-yet.md) is the EMG methodology note;
[body-recomposition.md](body-recomposition.md) is the 2019 video while
[build-muscle-lose-fat-same-time.md](build-muscle-lose-fat-same-time.md) is the 2017 one.

**The same study rendered differently across notes.** Maeo's seated-versus-lying leg curl result
appears as "~56% more", "1.6×" and "~50% more" in different notes. The fructose ceiling drifts from
60 g to ~50 g to a recalled "100–150 g". Ashwagandha's effect is given as "+96 ng/dL" in one note
and "15%" in another.

**Caption-garbled researcher names** still flagged as uncertain in some notes — Novella, Burke,
Siler, McLester, Raastad, Fagerli, Goto among them. These cluster in the 2016–2017 EMG-heavy notes.
Where a name is marked uncertain, treat the attribution as unverified.

**Nineteen videos have no usable English caption track** and were never written up. Injury recovery
and posture are the two worth chasing elsewhere — prehab is the thinnest area in the library.
