# Protein Dose Per Day and Per Meal: A Researcher's View on Totals, Distribution and Pre-Sleep Feeding

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How Many Grams of Protein Do You Need Per Day & Per Meal? ft. Jorn Trommelen" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wRehf1L231Q)) · 2018-04-04 · 83 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

Part two of the Jorn Trommelen interview (muscle metabolism / protein researcher, Maastricht, Netherlands). Part one covers MPS fundamentals.

## Bottom line
- **1.6 g/kg/day** is the average optimum from the Morton meta-analysis; **2.2 g/kg (~1 g/lb)** is the safe upper bound covering individual variation. **1.2 g/kg is definitively insufficient.**
- **~20 g of protein gives near-maximal MPS for 4-5 hours.** 40 g adds roughly 10-20% more — real but small, and not statistically significant in every trial.
- The night is 7-8+ hours, longer than a 20 g dose covers. **30 g pre-sleep did not significantly raise overnight MPS in Trommelen's study; 40 g did.** Aim for 40 g before bed.
- Trommelen's minimalist prescription: **20 g protein at breakfast, lunch and dinner plus 40 g before sleep.** His maximalist version: **at least 40 g per meal, at least 4 meals a day.**
- Up to ~4 meals per day is worth targeting; beyond four, likely no practical benefit.

## Carbs and fat around protein [02:02]

- **Insulin does not stimulate MPS** under normal feeding conditions — only under artificial experimental setups. The bodybuilding practice of slamming fast carbs post-workout to spike insulin is not supported.
- Insulin *is* mildly anti-catabolic — the only known inhibitor of muscle protein breakdown — but **only a tiny amount is needed for the maximal effect, and protein alone supplies it.** No carbs required.
- Fat has no clear direct effect. One study found full-fat milk slightly more anabolic than skim; Trommelen's own study adding milk fat to a protein shake found **no effect on MPS**. The whole-egg vs egg-white finding (Nick Burd's lab) is more likely driven by other nutrients (cholesterol is one candidate) than by fat itself.
- Where carbs and fat matter is **indirectly, by slowing protein digestion**, and via **total energy**: even **three days of a moderate caloric deficit already reduces MPS rates**. Notably, a deficit lowers synthesis rather than raising breakdown. Energy can come from carbs or fat, it does not matter which, and it does **not** need to be present in every meal — many studies feed protein alone after an overnight fast and still see maximal MPS.

## Total daily protein [14:28]

Trommelen's headline number comes from the **Morton et al. meta-analysis (Stu Phillips's lab)**: gains are optimised around **1.6 g/kg/day**, with the confidence interval extending to about **2.1-2.2 g/kg** to cover individuals who need more — essentially the 1 g/lb rule.

Factors pushing you higher:
- **Older adults** clearly need more than young adults (ironic, Trommelen notes, since it is the young who live on shakes).
- **People with obesity** appear to respond worse to protein and may need more.
- More total exercise, especially full-body over lower-body-only sessions, may raise needs.
- A caloric deficit may raise needs — evidence is weak, but 1.2 g/kg is definitely too low there; **1.6 g/kg is the absolute minimum in a deficit**, ideally a bit more.
- **Women respond essentially the same as men.** Their starting point is smaller, not their response.

Surprising finding: the one study testing it directly found **no difference in protein needs between low and high lean-body-mass groups**. Indirect evidence from meta-analyses (which express needs per bodyweight and find a clear cut-off) still supports bodyweight-based recommendations. In practice, protein intake tracks bodyweight anyway because caloric intake does — in Trommelen's study of 300+ athletes up to Olympic level, energy intake and protein intake were strongly correlated.

Jeff's imperial summary, which Trommelen endorses: **~0.7 g/lb for most people, 1 g/lb at the upper end, maybe up to 1.2 g/lb for the elderly, obese, or those doing lots of full-body work.** No need to go higher.

Argument for erring high: individual variation is large, protein is easy for a lifter to eat, and there is little downside. Jeff cites Jose Antonio's lab feeding as much as 3-4 g/kg/day with *less* fat gain in the high-protein groups. Trommelen adds a **metabolic-chamber protein overfeeding study in untrained subjects where overfeeding protein increased lean body mass**. Variation may be partly genetic and partly environmental — daily step count, general activity and sleep all plausibly shift requirements, and since you cannot measure your own, eating a bit more is the safe play.

## Protein quality and plant-based diets [28:24]

High quality = high in essential amino acids (EAAs) with a balanced spectrum. Animal proteins (all meat, all dairy) score well. **Collagen is an animal protein with essentially zero leucine** and very little of most amino acids — useless for muscle, though Burd's lab suggests it may support collagen/tendon synthesis. So-called "beef protein" supplements are often just collagen and are, in Jeff's words, the worst protein you can buy.

Plant proteins have lower EAA content and often lack one amino acid — the **limiting amino acid** problem (the house analogy: plenty of bricks and windows, zero doors, no functional house).

Two workable fixes, per Trommelen:
1. **Eat more of it.** His own study showed you can compensate by simply raising the dose; practically limited by fibre load from whole plant foods.
2. **Mix sources.** Not directly tested but strongly rationalised: corn is high in leucine but low in lysine, other plant sources are the reverse; combining gives a decent spectrum. Expect to need maybe **10-20% more total protein**.

**Supplemental leucine makes sense specifically for vegans** and for older adults with poor appetite (who need ~40 g per meal to optimise MPS, hard to hit at breakfast or lunch). Adding leucine on top of an already-leucine-rich meal is marginal at best. And leucine alone does nothing — it is caffeine telling the construction workers to start; without bricks (the other EAAs) no wall gets built.

## Per-meal dosing and the "leucine threshold" [34:37]

Trommelen dislikes the leucine threshold concept — the on/off-switch framing has no supporting evidence.

- The **smallest dose ever tested, 5 g of protein (~0.5 g leucine), already significantly stimulates MPS.** Any protein does something.
- **~20 g of protein gives near-maximal MPS for about 4-5 hours** — roughly 10 g of EAAs and ~3 g of leucine. **Plant-based, you are looking at ~30 g** for the same.
- Response is **roughly linear up to 20 g, then a sharp decline in incremental gains** — a turn dial, not a switch.
- **40 g gives about 10-20% more MPS than 20 g**, though often not statistically significant. Possibly larger when you have trained more muscle mass (full-body session). "You certainly don't need 40 g every meal to build muscle, but if you want to do everything you can, you probably want 40 g in each meal."

## Where eaten protein actually goes [48:34]

- **~95% of ingested protein is absorbed.**
- **~50% is retained by splanchnic/gut tissue** — the gut turns over fast and is a greedy organ.
- Of the ~50% reaching circulation, **~20% is taken up by muscle — about 10% of what you ate** (measured at rest; slightly higher with exercise).
- Uptake is not the same as incorporation into myofibrillar protein; that fraction is lower still. Most of the rest is oxidised. (Sanity check: if 20% of dietary protein became muscle, everyone would be Ronnie Coleman in weeks.)

## Concepts Trommelen rejects

**Muscle full effect [44:53].** Originally seen in amino acid infusion studies where MPS rose then fell despite sustained elevated amino acids. Every study supporting it was done **without exercise**. At rest, 20 g of protein elevates MPS for ~3 hours; **after exercise, 20 g elevated MPS for at least 5 hours — the full measurement window, possibly longer**. His view: muscle full explains why you cannot grow by eating protein alone, but once there is a training stimulus the body has a reason to keep responding — and lifters are in a post-exercise state (24-48 h) essentially all the time.

**Refractory period [53:21].** Popularised by Layne Norton. The proposed pattern — responsiveness switching off then back on — **has never been demonstrated in a study**. It is a hypothesis at best and conflicts with real-world and intermittent fasting data.

## Distribution and meal frequency [37:40]

Evidence is thin because frequency studies are near-impossible to run properly (you cannot verify free-living compliance; a proper trial needs to be enormous and long). Supporting lines: animal work, epidemiology, and one acute MPS study.

**The key distribution study (Areta / Phillips lab) [55:24].** After resistance exercise, MPS was measured over 12 hours with 80 g of protein split three ways:
- **2 × 40 g every 6 hours** (bolus)
- **4 × 20 g every 3 hours** (intermediate)
- **8 × 10 g every 90 minutes** (pulse)

The **intermediate pattern won**. Trommelen's reading: it is a proof of concept, not a real-world result. Everything fed was fast whey, which nobody does; the 40 g doses wasted everything above ~20 g, and the 10 g doses never reached peak plasma amino acid levels. **Repeat it with casein and he expects no difference**, because a slow protein gives continuous elevation in all conditions — and most real food is slow protein. He also notes the pulse result is *not* evidence for a refractory period: you would need a fourth group getting 20 g every 90 minutes to test that.

Related evidence for fast delivery: **whey drunk as a bolus is more anabolic than the identical whey sipped in small portions over the same period**, and **hydrolysed (pre-digested) protein raises MPS more**. Faster is better — up to the ~20 g point.

**Intermittent fasting.** Theoretically it should be bad, but the one IF-plus-exercise study looks fine. Trommelen's explanation: a large mixed meal (say an 80 g protein barbecue) is still digesting for many hours — "meat burps 10 hours later." You think you fasted 16 hours; you were digesting for 12 of them. He suspects IF done entirely on whey *would* be suboptimal.

**His recommendation: about four meals a day — breakfast, lunch, dinner, pre-sleep. Beyond four, probably little to no additional benefit.**

## Pre-sleep protein [63:36]

- Trommelen's own data: **30 g pre-sleep did not significantly raise overnight MPS across ~8 hours; an earlier study with 40 g did.** Reading the two together: a bigger meal is needed to keep MPS elevated over a longer window. The 20 g rule holds only when the next meal is 4 hours away — not the case before sleep. **He thinks going up to 40 g pre-sleep may be most effective.**
- A **12-week longitudinal study using 30 g pre-sleep did increase muscle mass gains** (using a fast + slow protein combination, i.e. a whey/casein blend like the milk protein isolate Jeff takes).
- **It does not make you fat.** In the long-term study the pre-sleep group (protein plus some carbs) gained muscle with **no increase in body fat**. Mike Ormsbee's lab found no detrimental effect of pre-sleep protein on overnight lipolysis, next-morning appetite, or insulin sensitivity. Trommelen would still avoid a huge 1,000-calorie meal right before bed given circadian/fat-storage evidence, but a modest protein-only feeding is safe.
- **Sleep quality:** measured in his studies with no detrimental effect, though not with gold-standard methods.
- **Do not set an alarm to eat mid-night.** Sleep quality drops drastically with unintentional waking. If you naturally wake, extra protein is fine — and it is only worth it if your pre-sleep dose was small. With 40 g of slow protein you are covered all night; after an 80 g dinner at 6pm you probably do not need a pre-sleep meal at all.
- Real-world case for it: in 300+ national- to Olympic-level athletes, **total protein averaged ~1.5 g/kg/day (close to the 1.6 target) but over 80% came from breakfast, lunch and dinner**, leaving a ~12-hour overnight gap. Adding pre-sleep protein raises the total *and* fixes the distribution — the biggest bang for the buck.

## Practical application

**Minimalist protocol (Trommelen's own summary) [75:03]:**
- Training: at least **10 sets per muscle per week**, spread over at least **2 sessions per muscle per week**; **rest at least 2 minutes** between sets; do not worry about rep range (6-12 is fine); use **full range of motion** — superior even when load is equated.
- Nutrition: eat **near maintenance** (argue against aggressive bulks and cuts), tilt slightly toward your goal. Protein **1.6 g/kg/day**, or if you hate arithmetic: **20 g at breakfast, 20 g at lunch, 20 g at dinner, 40 g before sleep.**
- Do that and protein quality mostly takes care of itself, though animal protein is better; if fully plant-based, eat somewhat more and mix sources within each meal.

**If you want the last few percent [77:40]:**
- Training: increase weekly volume (challenging sets) over time. If your lifts are not going up every couple of months, you are under- or over-training.
- Protein: **at least 40 g per meal, at least 4 meals per day.** No drawback to more.
- **Sleep more.** The classic study found dieters sleeping **8.5 h/night lost more fat and retained more muscle** than those sleeping 4.5 h. Even 7 hours is suboptimal for cognitive outcomes — and most people who think they get 7 hours are in bed 7 hours and asleep for closer to 6h20.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Meal-frequency and distribution research is badly underpowered; nothing here is settled. A definitive human trial would need to be huge and long, and nobody will fund it.
- The distribution study used only fast whey — conclusions may not survive a switch to slow protein, a different total dose, or a different time window.
- Only one study has tested lean-body-mass-based protein needs.
- The whole conversation repeatedly returns to *perspective*: total protein gets you ~80% of the result. Whether the remaining marginal gains are worth the effort depends on whether you are a recreational lifter or competing.
