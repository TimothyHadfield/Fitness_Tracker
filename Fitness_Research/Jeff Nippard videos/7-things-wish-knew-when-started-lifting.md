# Seven Lessons for New Lifters: Independence, Simplicity, Genetics and Tracking

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "7 Things I Wish I Knew When I Started Lifting" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emxEhfwori4)) · 2019-08-18 · 10 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The whole of training fits in a tweet: lift with good form, mostly 6-12 reps, mostly compounds, be consistent, train hard, progressively overload, eat enough protein, surplus to gain and deficit to cut.
- Genetic response to a given program varies enormously — one 12-week study saw a best responder gain 59% while some subjects lost muscle on the identical program.
- Give a program at least four months before judging your response to it, and at least a year of consistent hard training before claiming bad genetics (Greg Nuckols).
- Judge progress on monthly photos, average bodyweight trends and gym numbers — never on how you look in a given mirror at a given hour.
- Beginners are the most primed for growth they will ever be; don't spend that window only training chest and arms.

## 1. Don't let other people steer your training [00:30]
The worst form of this is lifting to impress people, which means loading more than you can control: squats cut short, bench not touching the chest, spotters curling the bar for you. Experienced lifters are more impressed by clean technique than by heavy sloppy weight. Ingraining bad habits early costs more to undo later than the temporary embarrassment of your friends progressing faster.

The subtler form is copying whatever the biggest guy in the gym or your favourite influencer does. Nippard says he fell for this hard. Healthy scepticism plus self-experimentation would have found what worked for him sooner.

## 2. It really is simple [01:31]
Influencers make the process sound more complicated than it is by piling on tip-of-the-iceberg detail, which makes it hard to see what actually drives progress. Asked to explain it in 280 characters, his answer: lift with good form, mostly (not exclusively) in the 6-12 rep range, mostly compound movements, be consistent, train hard, have fun, eat enough protein, be in a caloric surplus to gain size faster and a deficit to lose fat. He would add progressive overload — you need to be consistently *progressing*, not just consistently showing up.

Fancy techniques and novel workouts aren't wrong, they're just not what's driving the majority of the results, especially as a beginner.

## 3. Genetics matter more than people admit [03:02]
He cites a 12-week study (the auto-captions render the lead author as "who ball," which is likely garbled — treat the name as unverified) in which subjects trained biceps and triceps on an identical program. The spread of results was enormous: the best responder gained 59% muscle, while a few subjects actually lost muscle, possibly from overtraining or poor nutrition.

The right conclusion is not "I might be a non-responder." Everyone starts from a different place, so compare your physique only to your own starting point. Someone at the bottom of that distribution may simply have responded poorly to *that style* of training rather than to training in general — hence the case for self-experimentation.

The counterweight to experimentation is patience. In a blog post on genetics, **Greg Nuckols** argued you need at least four months of consistent training to reasonably judge how you respond to a given program, and that without a year of consistent, challenging training with a good attitude you aren't justified in claiming bad genetics.

Also: training changes muscle size, not muscle shape. Ab symmetry and biceps insertion distance from the elbow can't be altered. Anyone selling a trick for this is selling nothing.

## 4. Your physique looks wildly different hour to hour [05:04]
Lighting, pump and time of day change your appearance dramatically — he describes looking muscular in one room and flat in another purely from lighting. Don't assess progress hour-to-hour or even day-to-day; it's discouraging and destabilising.

## 5. Be analytical: track training and food [06:05]
Keep a logbook or app with weights and reps week to week. Track macros, or at least track food intake, so you know what you're actually eating and can adjust it. A meal plan works if the macros suit your goals, but a flexible approach is better for beginners so you don't become a slave to one fixed plan. Over time you learn your body well enough to stop tracking. You don't need to log every set and every bite — ballpark records still have real value.

## 6. You don't need steroids [07:07]
The belief that a lean, muscular physique requires drugs is false. Starting steroids while still new sets up a slippery slope toward serious physical and mental health problems, particularly when you cycle off, lose most of the size, and get pulled back on. There is also value in maxing out natural potential first, because it forces you to learn how your body responds to different training styles without drugs papering over the gaps.

## 7. The newbie window is the best growth you'll ever get [08:08]
New trainees are the most primed for growth they will ever be. He commonly sees guys train only chest and arms for their first year and end up disproportionate, then need a couple of years of hard leg and back work to rebalance. Train thoughtfully and in balance during this phase. There's no need for panic — nobody gets the newbie phase perfect, and consistent balanced training over years evens things out as you approach your natural ceiling — but the window doesn't come back unless you fully detrain.

## Practical application
- Rep range default: mostly 6-12, mostly compounds, with progressive overload logged week to week.
- Give any program a minimum of four months; give training a year before conclusions about your genetics.
- Progress markers: progress photos once or twice a month, average bodyweight trends, and getting stronger in the gym.
- Train legs and back from day one, not from year two.

## Caveats & unknowns
The 12-week biceps/triceps training study's lead author name is garbled in the captions and is not stated reliably here. The 59% best-responder figure is reported as it appears in the video.
