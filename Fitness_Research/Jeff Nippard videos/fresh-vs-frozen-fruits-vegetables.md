# Fresh vs Frozen Fruits and Vegetables: Is Fresh Actually More Nutritious?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "FRESH VS FROZEN Fruits and Vegetables | Which Are More Nutritious?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ahmDxbXCjBo)) · 2018-04-09 · 8 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The claim that fresh produce is across-the-board more nutritious than frozen or canned is busted.
- Most head-to-head nutrient comparisons show no significant difference; where differences exist they run in both directions depending on the specific food and nutrient.
- The consistent finding is that five days of refrigerated storage lowers nutrient content — so "fresh" that's been sitting in your fridge can be worse than frozen.
- Processing exists on a spectrum. Washing, packaging, canning and freezing are minimal; TV dinners and frozen pot pies are where processing becomes a genuine concern.
- Practical rule: the most nutritious produce is the produce you'll actually eat. Variety and consistency beat optimising individual nutrients.

## Why people assume fresh is better
The belief runs on a simple chain: fresh means less processed, and less processed means healthier. But while highly refined foods do contribute to calorie overconsumption and to displacing nutrient-dense foods, processing has also improved population health — it provides accessible nutrition for infants, the elderly and pregnant women, and enrichment/fortification can sharply reduce nutrient deficiencies compared with relying on naturally occurring nutrients alone.

Data from Weaver and colleagues shows that in the US, canned and frozen fruits and vegetables collectively contribute *more* to total nutrient intake than fresh ones — likely a function of convenience and affordability rather than superior per-gram nutrition.

## Levels of processing [01:35]
- **Minimal:** washing and packaging produce.
- **Simple preservation:** canning tuna, freezing fruit and vegetables.
- **Heavier processing:** TV dinners, frozen pot pies — the tier Nippard considers potentially problematic.

Freezing produce sits in the second tier, nowhere near the tier that drives health concerns.

## The evidence [02:07]
Nutritional differences between fresh and frozen genuinely exist, but which method wins depends on the specific food and nutrient. In a 2017 analysis (captioned as "Lim and colleagues" — Nippard also says "linen colleagues," so treat the surname as uncertain):
- Beta-carotene (a vitamin A precursor) was **higher in fresh broccoli** than frozen.
- Beta-carotene was **lower in fresh corn** than frozen, and **lower in fresh green peas** than frozen.

That same 2017 study compared three groups: fresh, frozen, and **fresh-stored** (fresh produce after five days in a refrigerator). The fresh-stored arm matters because produce often sits in the consumer's home for days before being eaten.

- **Vitamin C (ascorbic acid):** for broccoli, fresh and frozen came out essentially identical, with fresh-stored slightly worse. Same pattern for corn, green peas and strawberries.
- **Spinach vitamin C:** highest fresh, lower fresh-stored, lowest frozen.
- **Spinach folate:** the reverse — higher in frozen spinach than in either fresh group.

The authors' summary: the majority of comparisons yielded no significant difference, and where differences were significant, five days of refrigerated storage was consistently associated with lower nutrient concentration. Their conclusion was that the findings do not support the common perception that fresh produce is nutritionally superior to frozen.

A 2007 review reached the same place, noting that nutrient losses during fresh storage may be larger than consumers realise, that freezing and canning can preserve nutrient value, and that canned, frozen and fresh produce should all continue to be included in dietary guidelines.

## Why "fresh" isn't one category [02:37]
Fresh produce varies enormously by itself. Something picked at a farmers' market or roadside stand is not the same as something picked, trucked, then shelved in a grocery store. All else equal, longer transport and storage times mean worse nutrient quality — but even that rule is muddied by the specific crop, soil quality, season, weather, growing methods, ripeness at harvest, and post-harvest handling. A Harvard nutrition report puts it as: vitamin and mineral content depends on decisions and practices all along the food system, from seed to table, whether that system is local or global.

## Practical application
- Buy frozen without guilt. It's cheaper, more convenient, and nutritionally competitive with fresh — often better than fresh that's been in the fridge for days.
- If you buy fresh, eat it soon. Storage is the main measurable nutrient drain.
- Prioritise **variety** — different fruits and vegetables, different colours, different production sources — and **consistency** of intake over optimising any one nutrient.
- Let affordability and convenience drive your choice, because eating more produce overall matters far more than small differences in individual nutrients.
- Even the "worst" fruits and vegetables are more nutrient-dense than most other food categories (a point Nippard borrows from a Science-Based Medicine article by Dr Steven Novella).

## Caveats & unknowns
- Cooking method is not covered in this video and can meaningfully change nutrient retention.
- The nutrient differences reported are specific to particular food/nutrient pairings and don't generalise into a rule for all produce.
- Researcher surname for the 2017 analysis is garbled in the auto-captions and should be verified.
