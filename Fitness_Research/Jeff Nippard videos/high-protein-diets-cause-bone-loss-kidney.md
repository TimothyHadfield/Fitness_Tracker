# Do High Protein Diets Cause Bone Loss or Kidney Damage?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "High Protein Diets Cause Bone Loss and Kidney Damage? (MYTH BUSTED with science!)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=91CxhNLmO_o)) · 2018-01-01 · 5 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Both claims are busted. Neither bone loss nor kidney damage is supported in people with healthy kidneys.
- The bone myth rests on the **acid-ash hypothesis**, which is built on a 1970s–80s error: higher protein *does* increase intestinal calcium absorption, which explains the extra urinary calcium.
- The National Osteoporosis Foundation found **no adverse effects of higher protein intakes** on bone, plus positive trends between protein intake and bone mineral density at most sites, especially the lumbar vertebrae.
- The kidney myth generalises from diseased kidneys to healthy ones. In healthy people, more protein **increases GFR** — a sign of improved, not impaired, function.
- Higher protein may actively help: bone density, reduced age-related sarcopenia, body composition, weight management and appetite control.

## The bone myth: where it came from
- **1920, Journal of Biological Chemistry:** increased dietary protein led to increased urinary calcium excretion.
- **1970s–80s metabolic balance studies:** found that even with dietary calcium controlled, protein intake did *not* affect intestinal calcium absorption. Meanwhile studies going back to the 1920s kept showing higher protein → more calcium in the urine.
- The inference: if the extra urinary calcium was not coming from increased intestinal absorption, it had to come from somewhere — presumably the bones.
- This grew into the **acid-ash hypothesis**: extra amino acids from a high-protein diet create a state of acidosis; the body buffers it by releasing alkaline stores from bone; the result is calcium loss and eventually osteoporosis.

## Where the bone myth went wrong [01:34]
The 1970s–80s studies were simply wrong about intestinal absorption. A **2007 systematic review from the National Osteoporosis Foundation** reports that improved dietary intervention studies using **dual stable calcium isotopes** found increasing dietary protein was associated with a significant **increase in intestinal calcium absorption** — enough that nearly the entire rise in urinary calcium is accounted for by the improved absorption, with **no increase in skeletal catabolism** in the short term.

In other words, the extra calcium in the urine was coming from the diet, not the skeleton. The acid-ash hypothesis, as applied to protein causing bone loss, is founded on an outdated and erroneous finding.

Two further points:
- Even if protein-induced acidosis were real, more recent studies show these acid-base concerns are **minor compared with the alkalising effect of eating more fruits and vegetables**. Focus on adding produce, not cutting protein.
- **50% of bone by volume is protein.** The NOF concludes current evidence shows no adverse effects of higher protein intake, and finds positive trends between bone mineral density and protein intake at most bone sites, especially the lumbar vertebrae.

## The kidney myth [03:07]
The starting point is genuinely correct: in patients with **pre-existing kidney dysfunction**, increasing dietary protein contributes to further deterioration of kidney function.

The error is a recurring move in nutrition myths — assuming that because something is bad for people who already have a disease, it must help cause that disease. Jeff's parallel: because diabetics must regulate sugar intake, people conclude sugar plays a central causative role in developing diabetes.

The evidence against:
- **2015 review, *Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism*:** the belief that higher protein intakes lead to renal failure "has no foundation." In people with normal renal function, more protein actually produces an **increase in glomerular filtration rate (GFR)** — a marker of improved kidney function.
- **World Health Organization, 2007:** while there is clear evidence that high protein intakes in patients with kidney disease worsen kidney function, the idea that the age-related decline in GFR in **healthy** subjects can be slowed by reducing dietary protein "appears to have no foundation."

## Practical application
- If your kidneys are healthy, a high-protein diet carries no established bone or kidney risk. Eat protein according to your training and body-composition goals.
- Support bone health by eating **more fruits and vegetables**, not by cutting protein — the alkalising effect of produce dwarfs any acid load from protein.
- If you have **diagnosed kidney disease**, the caution is real and does apply: protein restriction is appropriate there, under medical supervision.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This video does not establish an optimal or upper protein intake — Jeff flags "how much protein you really need and how much is too much" as a separate future video.
- The kidney-disease exception is genuine, not a myth.
- Evidence cited is short-to-medium term for the calcium isotope work; very long-term skeletal outcomes are not addressed in detail.
