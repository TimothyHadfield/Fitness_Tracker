# Building Muscle At Home With No Equipment: Principles And Exercise Menu

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Build Muscle At Home: Science-Based Workouts (No Equipment Needed!)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-MRNjTr6xrE)) · 2020-03-15 · 19 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Four programming rules: 3-5 full body workouts per week; 1-2 exercises per body part; 3-4 sets per exercise; and — the one most people miss — train close to failure.
- Rep counts don't matter at home. Leave 1-3 reps in the tank on most sets and take the last set of each exercise to genuine failure.
- Eat at maintenance or a slight surplus if retaining muscle is the priority. If you must cut, push protein to at least 1 g per lb of bodyweight.
- Any tensile stimulus beats none. A short suboptimal stretch will not cost you your gains.
- With no external load, raise difficulty via unilateral variations, constant tension, longer range of motion, slower tempo, or intense voluntary contraction (no-load training).

## Why not to panic
[02:03] A 2013 study from Ogasawara and colleagues compared continuous training for 24 weeks against periodic training over the same six months (6 weeks on, 3 weeks off, repeated). During each three-week detraining block subjects lost some muscle, but regained it rapidly once training resumed — and over the full six months, triceps and pec growth ended up about the same in both groups. A few weeks of imperfect training won't rob you of your gains as long as you resume normal training, especially with some weighted loading.

**Diet.** Eat at maintenance or a slight surplus if retaining muscle is the main priority. Being in a deficit already raises muscle-loss risk, and training suboptimally on top of that compounds it — so if training at home with light weights, at least bump calories to maintenance. If fat loss remains the goal and a deficit is required, raise protein to at least 1 g per lb of bodyweight.

**On COVID diet claims** [00:31]: no named diet (vegan, carnivore, keto) prevents or cures COVID-19. One mouse study linked keto to influenza protection, but influenza and coronaviruses differ, so citing it is an enormous stretch. Warm water does not flush the virus into stomach acid. High-dose vitamin C appears (per Examine) to work only against the common cold and only if taken regularly before falling ill. Behavioural measures — handwashing, not touching your face, avoiding contact — are what matter. Otherwise eat a whole-foods, minimally processed diet with a wide variety of fruits and vegetables.

## The four principles
[03:34]
1. Three to five full body workouts per week.
2. One or two exercises per body part, depending on what you want to prioritise.
3. Three, maybe four sets per exercise, depending on volume needs.
4. Train close to failure. The common failure mode is writing "3 sets of 20" and stopping at 20 when 40-50 were available. The specific rep count doesn't matter; proximity to failure does. Reps will be high because external resistance is low. Leave 1-3 reps in reserve, then take the final set of each exercise to failure. This maintains muscle far better than circuit-style pump work.

Exercises are grouped into four categories — legs, push, pull, isolation (biceps, triceps, calves, abs) — and you pick one per category per full body workout. Sessions can be run as circuits rather than block-by-block, which keeps the pace up and reduces distraction at home. Warm up first with light cardio: skipping, basketball, jogging back and forth.

## Legs
- **Bulgarian split squat.** Far harder than bodyweight squats. Rear foot elevated, front leg forward, drive through the heel to 2-3 reps in reserve. Too easy? Use a constant-tension approach — go deep and pulse without locking out or resting at the top. Then add load (even 12 lb dumbbells make a real difference). No weight at all? Elevate the front foot too for a deeper bottom position and greater range of motion, keeping the constant-tension style.
- **Hip thrust (glute focus).** Bodyweight versions may need a million reps. Do them unilaterally with one leg lifted, keeping constant glute tension — never releasing at the bottom, squeezing hard at the top. This roughly halves the reps needed. Add a dumbbell in the lap if still easy.
- **Nordic ham curl.** The hardest of the bunch. Pin heels under a couch (have someone sit on it if it lifts) or have a partner hold your ankles. Critical cue: keep hips extended by squeezing the glutes — breaking at the hips is cheating. Lower under control until you can no longer hold it, catch with the hands, push back up about halfway, then finish with a hamstring squeeze. He'd expect around 8-12 reps.
- **Pistol squat.** Three-step progression: (1) sit-back-to-chair one-leg squat with hands out as counterbalance, at least 10-12 reps per leg; (2) assisted pistol with one hand on a surface for balance; (3) free-standing pistol. Balance is the initial limiter for most people but improves with practice.

## Push
- **Push-up.** Hits pecs and anterior delts, works from the knees if needed, and is a good high-rep exercise — he includes AMRAP push-ups in his own gym program and simply beats last week's number (he was at about 65). Harder variants: elevate the feet (more like an incline press, more upper pec and anterior delt, but only slightly harder); have a partner load a plate on your upper back or sit on you; wrap a resistance band around your back; or do deficit push-ups with hands on plates or stacked textbooks for extra range of motion and stretch.
- **Vertical (wall) push-up.** Feet up the wall, gradually increasing torso inclination toward vertical. Loads shoulders and upper pecs in a higher rep range with no weights. One of his favourites.
- **Lateral raise.** Obvious with bands or dumbbells. With nothing: stand in a doorway and press the backs of your hands against the frame — not maximally, but hard enough to feel the lateral delts switch on — holding 10-15 seconds with gradually increasing pressure. Then step out and do unloaded lateral raises, actively squeezing the delt through the range rather than letting the arms float. Co-contract the lats to antagonise the movement (think about packing the lats while pressing against an imaginary weight). There is research supporting no-load training, demonstrated in the biceps; the muscle still generates high tension without external load, so it should transfer to other muscle groups.

## Pull
- **Pull-up** is his number one back exercise and covers most bases — get a doorway bar if you can.
- **Rack chins / inverted rows under a desk.** Grip the desk edge (test that it holds your weight) and row your bodyweight up. Too easy for most, so elevate the feet, or have a partner load weight on your stomach.
- **Band pulls with a door anchor.** Thread the anchor over the door and close it. Keep the band fairly lengthened for more tension. Pulling from a higher angle mimics a high row (lats and traps); step closer to the door to make it more lat dominant; turn around to do band lat pulldowns; and do band face pulls, squeezing the rear delts at the top or shifting toward a high-row pattern for more trap.

## Isolation
- **Biceps:** any curl — seated dumbbell curl, band curl, or with no equipment the no-load method: flex the triceps and curl the biceps against that antagonistic resistance.
- **Triceps:** elbow extension from two shoulder positions — one overhead (skull crusher or overhead dumbbell extension) and one with arms at the sides (a band works well).
- **Abs:** bicycle crunch; planks, especially modified as long-lever posterior pelvic tilt planks; reverse crunches on the edge of a couch, curling the knees toward the chest — the closest substitute for a hanging leg raise, hitting lower and upper abs well.
- **Calves:** don't skip them. Use an elevated surface for extra range of motion, very high reps with no load, or hold weight if you have it.

## Practical application
Run 3-5 full body sessions a week. Each session: warm up with light cardio, then pick one exercise from each of legs, push, pull and isolation (one or two per body part), 3-4 sets each, most sets stopping 1-3 reps short of failure with the last set taken to failure. Circuit them to keep moving. Escalate difficulty with unilateral work, constant tension, added range of motion, slower tempo, improvised load (backpacks, bands, partners) or no-load contractions.

## Caveats & unknowns
The no-load training evidence he cites was demonstrated in the biceps; extension to other muscle groups is his inference. He offers no rep prescriptions deliberately, since proximity to failure is the variable that matters. Nippard notes he is not an infectious disease expert and defers to public health authorities on COVID-19 itself.
