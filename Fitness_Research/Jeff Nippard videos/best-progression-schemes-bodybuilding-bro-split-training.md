# Progression Schemes for a Bodybuilding Bro Split

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Best Progression Schemes for a Bodybuilding "Bro" Split + Training Footage" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xlMRwJUzW60)) · 2015-12-16 · 11 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The only non-negotiable across a training year is that you end up **capable of more work than you started with**. Individual blocks can raise volume, raise intensity while volume falls, or lower both for recovery.
- Within a mesocycle, progression means adding **a rep, a set, or some load** to what you did last week. Adding a set is the fastest way to add volume, since one set contributes more total reps than +1 rep or +5 lb.
- Intuitive progression works if — and only if — you **track everything**. Without a log there is no way to know whether volume is actually going up.
- Constrain intuition with two guardrails: a **fixed rep range per exercise** and an **RPE cap** (Jeff uses ~RPE 9, one rep in reserve, with the last set of an exercise often taken to failure).
- Structured schemes (percentage-based, linear, reverse linear) exist for people who want the decision made for them. Intuitive suits high intrinsic motivation and a desire for flexibility.

## Long-term planning without paralysis [01:31]
Long-term planning does not mean knowing what you will do on a specific day a year from now. It means setting goals, reviewing them regularly, and running short-term plans that move you toward them. The trap is analysis paralysis over the macro picture while the actual mesocycles drift.

Two prerequisites get skipped in progression discussions: **eating enough calories** and **working hard**. A sophisticated rep-target or %1RM scheme laid over lackadaisical sessions will not produce progress.

## Fully intuitive progression [02:32]
Jeff's own approach at the time. The mechanics:

- Open the previous week's training journal before the session.
- Keep most exercises the same week to week; if one is swapped, look back to the last time that movement was performed and beat it.
- Add **a rep, weight, or a set** — which one is decided by how you feel that day.
- No mandatory undulation or fancy periodization. If something got added across the mesocycle, progress happened.

**Rep-range constraint:** every exercise is assigned to 5-10, 10-15, or 15-20 reps. In this particular cycle roughly **half of total volume** came from the 10-15 range.

**RPE constraint:** generally leave about one rep in the tank (~RPE 9). The last set of an exercise usually goes to failure, though not always on squat, bench and deadlift.

**Arrow notation [05:05]:** after each set he logs an arrow — up means the weight was too light, increase next time; sideways means it was right, repeat it on the first set next week; down means it was too heavy and form got loose, back it off. Functionally close to an RPE rating, but it hands you a direction rather than a number.

## Autoregulated load selection [06:05]
He also uses a test-set method (described as the "DAP system" in the audio — the mechanics match **APRE-style autoregulation**; the acronym is likely garbled in the captions). After warm-ups, take one set to concentric failure with a load you estimate is your working-range max for the day. Then adjust:

- Target 6-8 reps, got **10** → you underestimated yourself; **add ~10%** for the working sets.
- Target 6-8 reps, got only **4-5** → **drop the load 5-10%** and run the working sets to failure with the adjusted weight.

This prescribes load from how you actually are on the day, which is part of the appeal of a bro split — the flexibility to do what you are capable of that session.

## Structured alternatives [07:39]
He credits **Eric Helms (3DMJ)** with a more detailed treatment and summarizes three:

**1. Percentage-based.** Establish a 1RM, prescribe loads as a percentage of it, undulate weekly if you like across ~4-8 weeks, then retest and plug in the new max. Higher maxes mean heavier working loads and automatically more volume.

**2. Linear progression.** Load up, reps down. Working in 6-8 reps for 3 sets: week 1 = 100 lb × 3×8, week 2 = 105 lb × 3×7, week 3 = 110 lb × 3×6. Volume falls, intensity climbs. Week 4 is a deload, then restart the cycle at **105 lb × 3×8** — the same shape, more weight. Repeat while it keeps working.

**3. Reverse linear (double progression).** Suits higher rep ranges where adding load every session is unrealistic. In a 12-15 range: week 1 = 3×12; week 2 = 3×13; week 3 = 2×13 + 1×14, and so on, adding reps set by set until you reach **3×15**. Only then does load go up, and you reset to 3×12 at the heavier weight.

## Practical application
- Keep a log with load, reps and a difficulty marker (arrow or RPE) for every set — intuitive progression is unusable without it.
- Assign each exercise one rep range and stay in it for the block. Bias total volume toward the middle range if hypertrophy is the goal.
- Each week, beat last week's entry by one variable. Reach for extra sets when you want a meaningful jump in weekly volume.
- Use a to-failure test set to set the day's load when readiness swings a lot: +10% if you overshoot the target reps, -5-10% if you undershoot.
- Pick structured schemes if motivation is inconsistent or you want proof the numbers are moving; intuitive if motivation is high and you value flexibility. The two can be blended.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This is a 2015 video reflecting Jeff's personal practice and reasoning; no studies are cited beyond a pointer to Eric Helms's video.
- The autoregulation acronym is garbled in the auto-captions; the percentages and logic above are as stated, the name is not reliable.
- Not every block needs more volume than the last — that is the point about non-linear macro planning, and it means "add something every week" applies within a mesocycle, not forever.
