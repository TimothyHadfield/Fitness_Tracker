# 8 More Fitness Myths Busted (Myth-Busting Season 1 Recap, Part 3)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "8 Common Fitness Myths Busted (What The Science Says)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HOAui_N-HFo)) · 2018-11-05 · 11 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- Breakfast has no metabolic advantage — eating or skipping is preference, but whichever your first meal is, make it large and high in protein.
- Spot reduction gets a skeptical question mark rather than a clean bust: one recent study found local fat loss, but it's small, new, and against consensus.
- Hydrate to thirst; coffee counts as water. Aim for pee that's clear or slightly yellow, about five times a day.
- Body types (ecto/meso/endo) come from 1940s eugenics pseudoscience — program by goal, training age and preference, not somatotype.
- Mind-muscle connection is real for isolation work: use it on single-joint exercises above 8 reps, external focus everywhere else.

## 1. You must eat breakfast
**Verdict: busted.**
[00:31] Research from Betts and colleagues found no difference in resting metabolic rate — the biggest chunk of total metabolism — between groups eating and skipping breakfast. Breakfast eaters did move around more and burn more calories through the day, but this was almost perfectly offset by eating about the same number of extra calories, i.e. the breakfast itself. Under free-living conditions there's little difference in total daily energy balance either way.

James Krieger's Weightology review summarises the practical position: breakfast is a personal preference; if you eat it, make it large and high in protein, and if you don't, make your first meal of the day large and high in protein. Skipping breakfast is simply one strategy to reduce caloric intake — you have to determine whether it actually makes you eat less overall.

## 2. Spot reduction (targeted fat loss)
**Verdict: uncertain — a skeptical question mark, not a bust.**
[02:04] Nippard calls this the most controversial one for him. Four studies:
1. Observational: the active swinging arms of tennis players weren't leaner than the non-swinging arms. Limited by being observational rather than interventional.
2. Interventional: 12 weeks of single-arm training produced no difference in fat volume between trained and untrained arms — but the study didn't produce much fat loss overall, so spot reduction may just not have been detectable.
3. A third study did see significant overall fat loss and still found no spot reduction between trained and untrained legs.
4. A newer study (published the year before the video) pushed back hard: an upper-body-only training group lost substantially more arm fat over 12 weeks, while a lower-body-only group lost substantially more leg fat, implying local fat reduction.

An important detail in the fourth study: all subjects did **30 minutes of light cycling after training**. That hints at a mechanism — the body may increase fat mobilisation from stores near the exercising muscle, and if that fat is burned as fuel immediately afterward, it could yield more net fat loss in that region.

But the sample size was small and the finding runs against the prevailing consensus. Nippard is not confident enough to recommend selectively targeting body fat, and leaves it flagged as a launching pad for future research.

## 3. There's one best cooking method for preserving nutrients
**Verdict: depends on the food, with weak general rules.**
[03:39] Like the fresh-versus-frozen question, it varies by food. Broadly, **steaming is slightly better for preserving nutrients and boiling is the worst**, especially for water-soluble vitamins like vitamin C — supporting the general advice to avoid high heat plus lots of water. There's no reason to avoid the microwave; it's safe technology and often has favourable nutritional effects. Best advice: eat a variety of fruits and vegetables and use a variety of cooking methods.

## 4. Drink 8 glasses of water a day
**Verdict: busted as a universal number.**
[04:09] The 8-glasses rule ignores that water requirements vary with body size, activity level, geography and climate. Nearly every academic source says most healthy people can meet daily water needs by **using thirst as a guide**. Coffee counts as a water source — it doesn't increase urine output or harm hydration status in people accustomed to caffeine.

Hydration does matter for lifting: dehydration as low as **3%** has been shown to impair athletic performance including strength and power. Alan Aragon's research review gives specific pre- and intra-workout recommendations, but Nippard prefers Lyle McDonald's simpler heuristic: **your urine should be clear or slightly yellow throughout the day, and you should be urinating about five times a day.**

Increasing water intake has a near-negligible effect on metabolic rate, so drinking past thirst does nothing extra for fat loss — unless it makes you feel fuller and cuts total intake.

## 5. Milk and dairy are bad for you
**Verdict: busted.**
[05:40] High-quality meta-analyses and systematic reviews — including one with over **700,000 participants** — consistently find no association between dairy and milk consumption and all-cause mortality, total deaths, coronary heart disease, or cardiovascular disease.

On the funding-bias objection: the potential for dairy industry funding bias is a legitimate reason for pause and for seeking datasets with less bias risk, but a well-conducted study can't be discredited on funding alone absent evidence of malpractice. In this case it's largely moot — high-quality data without funding conflicts shows the same findings. Meanwhile the counter-evidence he's seen comes almost without exception from vegan or vegetarian sources; he singles out the China Study as ideologically driven, pulling from 8,000 correlations, cherry-picking references and omitting data.

Verdict: setting aside ethical concerns about the dairy industry, the weight of evidence supports milk as a nutrient-rich beverage with health benefits for those who choose to drink it.

## 6. There are three body types (somatotypes)
**Verdict: busted.**
[07:11] The endomorph/mesomorph/ectomorph scheme comes from eugenics-riddled 1940s pseudoscience whose original goal was linking body type to personality, intelligence and future achievement. It later took root in fitness and bodybuilding.

The core problem: somatotype classifications imply you can't change your body composition or shape, which you plainly can. Bone structure and muscle insertions are fixed. But whether you currently look like an ectomorph or an endomorph, you will still lose fat in a caloric deficit and still build muscle through progressive resistance training with sufficient protein and calories. Results come more or less easily to different people for genetic reasons, but **training protocol differences should be based on specific goals, level of advancement and personal preference — not somatotype.**

## 7. Detox diets work
**Verdict: busted, and potentially dangerous.**
[08:44] They can be actively harmful, especially when they prescribe far more water than you need or excessively restrict food. The whole premise rests on the faulty physiological assumption that the liver and kidneys need help clearing toxins, which they don't. Any short-term rapid weight loss comes from extreme caloric restriction, is rarely sustainable, and typically leads to regain. Given the long list of weight-loss diets with adequate protein and micronutrition, **pretty much any weight-loss diet picked at random will beat the best detox diet available.**

## 8. The mind-muscle connection
**Verdict: NOT busted — the bros were onto something.**
[09:14] New data shows significantly more biceps hypertrophy when subjects focus on establishing a mind-muscle connection. The effect may be body-part specific: it didn't work for the quads in that study, though the subjects were untrained, so more experienced bodybuilders — better at mindfully activating lower-body muscles — might see quad growth too.

**Recommendation: don't use internal focus on everything. Reserve the mind-muscle connection for isolation, single-joint exercises with rep counts above 8. For everything else, focus on how the body executes the movement as a whole — proper technique and lifting tempo.**

## Practical application
- Eat or skip breakfast on preference; make whatever your first meal is large and high in protein.
- Don't buy fat-targeting programs. Overall deficit is still what drives fat loss.
- Steam rather than boil; microwave freely; vary your vegetables and your cooking methods.
- Drink to thirst, count coffee, and check the urine heuristic: clear-to-pale, ~5 times a day. Stay hydrated for training — 3% dehydration measurably hurts strength.
- Milk is fine and nutrient-dense if you want it.
- Ignore somatotype-based programs; program by goal, experience and preference.
- Never do a detox diet.
- Internal focus on isolation work above 8 reps; external focus everywhere else.

## Caveats & unknowns
Spot reduction is explicitly left open — the supportive study is small, recent, and contradicts prior work; the confounding 30 minutes of post-training cycling makes the mechanism ambiguous. The mind-muscle connection research was new at the time and the quad null result is unresolved. This is a compressed recap; each myth has a full-length original video with the detail.
