# How to Keep Making Gains as a Beginner, Intermediate and Advanced Lifter

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Make Gains At Every Fitness Level (ft. Picture Fit)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EbfdMaV7qqM)) · 2022-05-29 · 11 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Nutrition barely changes with experience: **1.6–2.2 g protein/kg/day**, and calories set by goal — **+10–20% over maintenance** to gain, maintenance for recomposition, **-10–20%** to lose fat.
- Beginners (year 1–2): master technique, run simple linear weight progression, and learn what failure actually feels like. Expect **10–25 lb (4.5–11 kg)** of muscle as a male, **6–15 lb (3–7 kg)** as a female.
- Intermediates (year 1–5): switch to rep progression, rotate exercises every 1–2 months, and calibrate proximity to failure. Expect another **10–20 lb** male, **6–12 lb** female across those years.
- Advanced (year 4–5+): specialization phases, intensity techniques, and frequency experimentation. Expect only **1–2 lb/year** male, **0.5–1 lb/year** female.
- Keep volume in the **10–20 hard sets per body part per week** window; adding sets is the last progression lever, not the first.

## Nutrition (all levels)
[01:01] Total daily calories is the dominant factor.
- **Muscle gain:** 10–20% above maintenance drives growth without excessive fat gain.
- **Recomposition:** calories at maintenance plus optimized training can build muscle and lose fat simultaneously — but this works best for beginners and early intermediates and gets harder as you advance.
- **Fat loss:** 10–20% below maintenance. Muscle gain in a deficit is possible but less feasible the more advanced you are.
- **Protein:** 1.6–2.2 g/kg bodyweight per day ([Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)).

## Beginner (first 1–2 years)
[02:32] Three priorities.

**1. Technique.** Learn proper form on a range of machines and free weights so tension actually lands on the target muscle. That means a range of motion taking the muscle through a reasonably full stretch and full contraction, controlling the negative, and correct breathing/bracing. Default: inhale on the negative, exhale on the positive. On heavy compounds like squats, progress to the Valsalva maneuver — breathe in before the rep, hold through the descent and ascent, release at the top.

**2. Linear strength progression.** Pick a handful of primary exercises and add weight every week at a fixed rep count, generally **6–12 reps**. Example: start bench with the empty bar and add **5 lb per week for 6 reps**. Doing only that, most people should bench bodyweight for a 1RM by the end of year one.

**3. Learn failure.** Once technique is solid, start finding out what true failure feels like on various exercises. You don't need to train to failure to progress, but if you've never been there you can't judge your own effort and will likely undertrain as an intermediate.

## Intermediate (roughly years 1–5)
[04:35] Three priorities.

**1. Rep overload, then set overload.** You can no longer add weight every session without form breakdown. Use a rep range and add a rep per week. Example on EZ-bar curls in the 6–8 range: 60 lb × 6 in week 1, 60 × 7 in week 2, 60 × 8 in week 3, then back to 6 reps with slightly more weight. If neither weight nor reps will move without form breaking down, *then* add a set (3 sets of 6–8 becomes 4 sets of 6–8). Save this for last — it spikes volume suddenly and costs more recovery than adding weight or reps. The goal is your personal volume sweet spot, not infinite volume: **10–20 sets per body part per week** is usually optimal. Well above that range, stop adding sets and swap the exercise instead.

**2. Periodic exercise rotation.** Haphazard constant switching makes progress impossible to track. Stick with a given exercise for **1–2 months**, progress weight/reps/sets on it, then swap. Mainly applied to isolation work — e.g. side delts: standing dumbbell lateral raise for a month or two, then lean-away Egyptian cable lateral raise, then chest-supported incline lateral raise, then a machine lateral raise.

**3. Fine-tune proximity to failure.** Get accurate at judging 1, 2 or 3 reps in reserve. General rule: **1–3 RIR on compounds** (squats, presses), **0–2 RIR on isolation**. Take the last set of isolation exercises to actual failure as a periodic recalibration of what failure feels like.

## Advanced (roughly 4–5+ years)
[07:39] Three strategies.

**1. Specialization phases.** Dedicate 1–2 months to one or two lagging body parts, raising their volume by **20–40%**. Example: 12 hard chest sets/week becomes 15–17 hard sets/week. If recovery suffers, cut back or drop to maintenance volume elsewhere — e.g. slash delt or triceps isolation while specializing on chest.

**2. Intensity techniques.** Drop sets (extend past failure by reducing the load and continuing), myo-reps (3–4 second mini-rests after failure to squeeze out more effective reps at the same load), and eccentric-accentuated reps (slow the negative, or have a partner add resistance to the eccentric). Best used on the last set of isolation exercises, especially machines, for stubborn muscles. They carry a greater recovery cost than a normal set, so don't overdo them.

**3. Experiment with frequency.** Consensus is that twice per week per muscle is usually enough to maximize growth on average, but optimal frequency varies individually. If you've trained each body part once a week for a long time, try twice. If you've been at twice a week a long time, try something higher — e.g. a high-frequency full-body split. In his experience higher frequency is at worst equal to moderate frequency, and for some people works considerably better.

Note: at the advanced stage you may need a more aggressive caloric surplus to build any further natural muscle.

## Practical application
- Beginner: 6–12 reps, +5 lb/week on primary lifts, protein at 1.6–2.2 g/kg, calories 10–20% over maintenance.
- Intermediate: fixed rep range, +1 rep/week, add weight on hitting the top of the range, rotate isolation exercises every 1–2 months, 1–3 RIR compounds / 0–2 RIR isolation, keep weekly volume 10–20 sets/muscle.
- Advanced: cycle 1–2 month specialization blocks at +20–40% volume for one or two muscles, add drop sets/myo-reps/eccentrics sparingly on final isolation sets, and test a different training frequency.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Muscle-gain ranges are wide because of large genetic variation between individuals.
- All rates assume natural training and that most other variables are already handled well.
- Optimal frequency is individual; not everyone responds better to higher frequencies.
- Recomposition and gaining in a deficit both become progressively less feasible with training age.
