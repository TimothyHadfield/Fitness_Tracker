# Should You Train When Sick?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Should You Train When Sick? (A Scientific Perspective)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CvPMbiND3Gw)) · 2017-12-21 · 5 min
**Topic:** Recovery

## Bottom line
- **Contagious → stay out of public gyms.** Common courtesy, regardless of how you feel.
- Use Lyle McDonald's **neck check**: symptoms **below the neck** (chest congestion, chest cough, wheezing) → rest. **Above the neck** (runny nose, headache) → grey area, judgement call.
- The grey-area tactic: **go to the gym and do 10–20 minutes of light-to-moderate cardio**, then decide. It filters laziness from genuine illness, and the cardio itself may help.
- **Moderate-intensity, moderate-volume cardio can improve immune function** and speed recovery.
- Detraining fear is overblown: it takes **2–4 weeks** to see meaningful losses in size or strength, and up to **3 weeks off** is unlikely to hurt you long term.

## Severity first
Pneumonia with fluid in the lungs is an obvious no. A common cold puts you in the grey area where it genuinely isn't clear either way — that's the case this video addresses.

## The detraining evidence [01:00]
Most studies show it takes **at least 2–4 weeks** to see significant losses in muscle size and strength.

A **2012 study from Ogasawara and colleagues** (name partly garbled in the captions) compared two groups: one trained continuously for 24 weeks, the other trained 6 weeks, took a 3-week complete break, trained 6 weeks, took another 3 weeks off, and so on. **Both groups finished in essentially the same place for muscle size.**

Muscle size did dip during the 3-week detraining blocks, but two caveats apply: the subjects were **untrained**, so possibly more susceptible to loss, and Jeff suspects much of the measured drop was **glycogen and hydration** rather than actual muscle tissue.

## The light cardio test [02:02]
Jeff's own approach, used the day before filming: go to the gym, do **10–15 minutes of cardio**, then honestly ask whether you're in shape to lift. In his case, after 15–20 minutes he was still lightheaded, lethargic and stuffed up, so he stopped at cardio for the day — and felt much better and ready to train the next day.

Two benefits:
1. **It removes self-deception.** Getting yourself to the gym puts you in a position to make a proactive decision rather than rationalising from the couch.
2. **There's no downside.** Moderate-intensity, moderate-volume cardio has been shown to improve immune function and expedite recovery, so the trip is not wasted even if you skip the weights.

## Looking smaller after time off
If your physique looks smaller, softer or flatter after a few days out, this is almost certainly **reduced muscle glycogen and hydration**, not real muscle loss. Both return to normal very quickly once you resume eating and drinking normally.

## Practical application
Decision sequence:
1. **Contagious?** Skip the public gym. Full stop.
2. **Symptoms below the neck** (chest congestion, chest cough, wheezing)? Rest up.
3. **Symptoms above the neck** (runny nose, headache)? Grey area — go to the gym, do **10–20 minutes of light-to-moderate cardio**, then reassess.
4. Still lightheaded, lethargic or badly congested after the cardio? Call it there and go home. You bought yourself an immune benefit anyway.
5. Feeling fine after the warm-up? Train.
6. **Don't panic about missed sessions.** Up to three weeks off is very unlikely to affect long-term muscle mass.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The neck-check rule comes from **Lyle McDonald** as an expert heuristic, not from a controlled trial.
- The Ogasawara detraining data is in **untrained subjects**; trained lifters may respond differently.
- The strategy assumes you are genuinely in the grey area and not using illness as an excuse — Jeff flags this as the main failure mode.
- No specific dose or intensity threshold is given for the immune-boosting cardio beyond "moderate intensity, moderate volume."
