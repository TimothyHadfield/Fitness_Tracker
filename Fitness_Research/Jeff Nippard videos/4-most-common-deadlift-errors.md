# The 4 Most Common Deadlift Errors And How To Fix Them

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The 4 Most Common Deadlift Errors (& How To Fix Them)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wr-iSaXPr5s)) · 2019-02-26 · 7 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Lower back rounding is usually a weak-glute problem, not a weak-lower-back problem. Train hip thrusts and pull-throughs.
- Some spinal flexion is normal on heavy pulls; keep any rounding in the *upper* back and minimise lower back rounding under heavy load.
- Hips shooting up early is a setup fault — push the hips back rather than sitting them down, and start with hips relatively high.
- Stack the joints: shins as close to vertical as possible from the side, and on sumo a straight vertical line from knees to heels from the front.
- Don't lean back and hyperextend at lockout. Stand tall, squeeze glutes, lift the chest, and stop.

## Error 1: Lower back rounding [00:33]

**Why it happens.** Rounding is genuinely controversial because almost every heavy deadlift involves some spinal flexion — it's a biomechanically stronger position for most lifters. As Bret Contreras showed in his biomechanics book, a rounded back lets the hips get closer to the bar, reducing the torque the glutes and hamstrings must overcome. Contreras' recommendation: if you round, keep it to the upper back only, and keep lower back rounding to a minimum with heavy loads.

**Fix 0: lower the weight.** The most obvious cause is simply training too heavy. But it isn't the only one.

**Fix 1: hamstring flexibility.** Before initiating the pull you want the hips in an anteriorly tilted position — think of slightly popping the butt out as in a bikini pose, to pull the lower back into neutral before you pull. Don't exaggerate it. If you can't hold that position and still reach the bar — you feel a huge, uncomfortable hamstring stretch trying — hamstring flexibility is the limiter. Address it with:
- Dynamic stretching and foam rolling before lifting
- Static stretching after lifting, if needed
- Moderate-rep stretch-based movements such as Romanian deadlifts to stretch the hamstrings under load

**Fix 2: strengthen the glutes.** Contrary to popular opinion, your lower back is probably not rounding because the lower back is weak. It's much more likely the glutes are weak, so the lower back rounds to compensate — reducing hip torque and therefore the load the glutes must handle. Train hip thrusts, cable pull-throughs, and other hip-extension movements so the lower back doesn't have to cover a strength deficit.

**Fix 3: paused deadlifts.** If you start in a good position but default to rounding once the bar clears the floor, train that specific point in the movement. Pause **1-2 seconds** as soon as the plates leave the floor, or just below the knees if the rounding happens later in the range. The pause forces a positional check and reinforces isometric spinal extension where you need it.
- **Prescription:** start at **50-60% of 1RM for sets of 4-6 reps**, then build from there. Go lighter than usual, especially if new to them.

## Error 2: Hips shooting up too early [03:36]

The usual culprit is setting up wrong. Many lifters set up with the hips very low and then try to squat the weight up — which almost always results in the hips shooting up first, leaving the lower back to handle much of the load.

**The fix is entirely in the setup:**
1. Rather than sitting the hips down, think about **pushing the hips back**, as in a stiff-leg deadlift.
2. Once the hips can't go back further without really stretching the hamstrings, let the **knees travel slightly forward until the shins touch the bar without moving it**.
3. Hold that relatively **higher hip position**.
4. On the pull, the hips shouldn't rise — they should **drive forward as the chest rises**.

Set up properly and the hips simply won't shoot up.

## Error 3: Joints not stacked [04:37]

**Sumo, viewed from the front:** you should be able to draw a perfectly straight vertical line from knees to heels. If the heels are outside the knees, either the stance is too wide or the knees are caving in.
- Fix 1: narrow the stance slightly.
- Fix 2: hip mobility work to keep the knees out — dynamic stretching and foam rolling for the inner thighs and glutes before training, static stretching for the same areas after.

**Both sumo and conventional, viewed from the side:** heels should be roughly stacked below the knees, meaning **shins as close to vertical as possible**. To achieve that you may need to adjust hip height, adjust stance width, and use a **flat-soled shoe or lift barefoot**.

## Error 4: Excessive lockout [05:39]

Very common, especially in male lifters who get excited and keep going at the top — leaning too far back and hyperextending the lower back.

**What lockout actually requires:** stand up with an upright posture, flex the glutes to lock out the hips, and lift the chest to lock out the shoulders. That's it.
- No squeezing the shoulder blades together at the top.
- No shrugging the weight up. Both waste energy and throw your position off.

Being explosive through the concentric is fine — but once you hit lockout, maintain control and don't take the lift past its intended range of motion.

## Practical application
- Diagnose rounding as a glute strength or hamstring flexibility issue before assuming your back is weak.
- Add hip thrusts and cable pull-throughs; add RDLs for loaded hamstring length.
- Run paused deadlifts at 50-60% 1RM, 4-6 reps, pausing 1-2s at the point where your position breaks.
- Rebuild your setup: hips back (not down), knees forward until shins touch the bar, hips high, chest and hips rise together.
- Check your stack: vertical shins from the side; on sumo, knees stacked over heels from the front. Wear flat shoes or go barefoot.
- Finish the lift standing tall with glutes squeezed. No lean-back, no shrug, no scapular squeeze.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This is the third part of a deadlift miniseries; the conventional and sumo setup tutorials cover the fundamentals assumed here.
- Nippard notes there are many more errors than these four.
- The "rounded back is stronger" point comes from Contreras' biomechanics book, not a named trial, and the safe amount of lower back flexion under load remains a debated area.
