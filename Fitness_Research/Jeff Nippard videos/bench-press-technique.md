# Bench Press: Full Technique Guide (Arch, Grip, Bar Path, Leg Drive)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Get A Huge Bench Press with Perfect Technique" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vcBig73ojpE)) · 2018-12-04 · 12 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- The powerlifting-style bench (arch, retracted and depressed scapulae, leg drive) is also an excellent mass builder — it maximises load, protects the shoulder and recruits the pecs well.
- Keep shoulder blades squeezed together and pulled down toward your butt for the entire setup and lift.
- Elbows tuck to roughly 45 degrees from the torso viewed from above; bar descends down and slightly forward to just below nipple level, then presses up and back toward the face.
- Maintain four points of contact — head, upper back, glutes, feet — and drive the heels into the floor.
- Only compare your bench to your own starting point: arm length, ribcage size, muscle origins and insertions, lumbar mobility and upper-back size all swing the numbers.

## Muscles worked [00:37]
In order of involvement: pec major (both the sternal/mid-lower and clavicular/upper heads), triceps brachii, anterior deltoid, and to a lesser extent the lats — the lats are only really active when the shoulder is behind the torso, i.e. at the very bottom of the press. The trained actions are horizontal shoulder adduction (pecs and front delts), some shoulder flexion (upper pecs and front delt) and elbow extension (triceps).

## Setup [01:39]
1. Check the plates match, the bar is centred, and a spotter is available if you're training near maximal.
2. Lie flat with your head back behind the bar and your feet up on the bench.
3. Squeeze the shoulder blades together and hold that retracted position through the whole setup and lift.
4. Build the arch: lift the arms and grab the bar (or reach back and push against the bench uprights — he prefers grabbing the bar), lift the hips high while thinking about pulling the shoulder blades down toward your butt to force scapular depression, and push the upper body forward with the feet in place. Going onto the toes is fine if it produces more arch.
5. You should now have your upper back planted and your eyes directly under the bar.
6. Set the legs for leg drive: lower one foot at a time, place them as far back as comfortable, keep the legs in close to the bench when viewed from the front. For regulation technique heels must be planted, so you may need to point the toes out / externally rotate the feet. Drop the hips so the butt touches the bench.
7. Take your grip.

**Grip width.** Research has found wider grips target the sternal pec head and anterior delts more, while closer grips target the triceps and clavicular (upper) chest more; experimenting with a variety of widths makes sense. Nippard uses a fairly wide grip because he's simply stronger there. A key constraint is joint stacking — the wrist should sit roughly over the elbow at the bottom, although some advanced powerlifters deliberately break that to go wider. A 2007 study suggests a maximum width of about 1.5 times the biacromial (shoulder) distance reduces injury risk without seriously compromising maximal strength ([grip width and risk of injury](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232096734_The_Affect_of_Grip_Width_on_Bench_Press_Performance_and_Risk_of_Injury)).

**Wrist position.** From the side, the wrist should be directly below the knuckles. Avoid excessive wrist hyperextension; the cue "punch the ceiling" corrects it.

Final check before the lift-off: crank the elbows forward to confirm the scapulae are retracted and depressed — he describes it as screwing his back into position.

## Bodybuilding-only variation [04:44]
Same general setup, but: less extreme arch, possibly a slightly narrower grip to increase range of motion, and feet a little further forward since the arch is smaller. Still keep the scapulae retracted throughout and some lumbar arch for shoulder health and stability.

He argues the range-of-motion difference between a big and a small arch is exaggerated, because bar travel distance is not the same as joint range of motion — comparing the two styles, the position of the humerus relative to the torso at the end range is very similar even though the bar travels further in a flat-back press. So build your arch and don't worry much about "losing" range.

## Execution — unrack, brace, descend, press [05:44]
**Unrack.** Have the spotter help you lift *out*, not up. Solo, keep the butt elevated for the lift-off then drop the hips once the bar is out. He prefers a spotter for near-maximal work.

**Brace.** Confirm four points of contact (head, upper back, glutes, feet). Deep breath into the gut, pressing air out against the belt if you wear one. Cue puffing the chest out to expand the ribcage. Grip the bar as hard as you can — optionally cue "bend the bar" or "rip the bar in half" to switch on the upper back and hold tightness.

**Descend.** Drop the elbows to about 45 degrees relative to the torso when viewed from above — Nippard says the best powerlifting coaches unanimously agree on this. Viewed from the side, the elbows shouldn't be far in front of the bar; aim to keep them stacked under it. The bar path is down and slightly forward, touching just below nipple level toward the bottom of the sternum, which should be the highest point on your chest. A ~1-second pause until the bar is motionless is required in competition; for bodybuilding you can simply tap the chest — but keep the touch controlled, no bouncing.

**Press.** Explode off the chest, driving the heels into the floor. Press the bar back toward your face rather than straight up; think about pushing the floor away with your feet to initiate leg drive and enforce that back-and-up path. Don't press so far back that you hit the rack or lose control — bring the bar back into balance over the shoulder joint. You may exhale once past the sticking point, or hold your breath to lockout. The bar finishes its arc back over the nipples. Re-brace and repeat.

**Sticking point.** Research shows it usually falls in the first 20-40% of the movement, roughly 3-4 inches off the chest, but it's individual: more advanced lifters tend to have a lower sticking point, newer lifters more mid-range. To get through it: press up and back rather than straight up; flare the elbows out a bit more to bring the pecs in to assist the triceps; lift off the chest with maximum speed so deceleration hits later. His own view is that the simplest fix is usually stronger pecs and triceps.

## Common errors [09:16]
- **Over-tucking or under-tucking the elbows.** The "tuck your elbows" cue originated in *equipped* powerlifting and is less useful without a bench shirt. Flaring completely out so the elbows are parallel with the bar is just as common and raises shoulder injury risk while forcing an inefficient straight up-and-down bar path — most people are weaker that way. A slight tuck is right, with more flare near the sticking point to stack the elbows under the bar. If you can't tell what your elbows are doing, do slow eccentric presses with light weight and pay attention through the whole negative.
- **Benching completely flat-backed.** Some arch confers at least three benefits: the lumbar and thoracic vertebrae and discs are safer in a lordotic position; the more declined pressing angle is safer for the shoulder; and you'll be stronger, meaning more total load on the target muscles.
- **No leg drive.** Feet parked wherever, out in front, or up on the bench wastes strength potential. Learning to use the legs takes time but is worth mastering.

## Practical application
Set up powerlifting-style even for hypertrophy: scapulae retracted and depressed, arch built, feet back and heels planted. Grip no wider than ~1.5x shoulder width with wrists stacked over knuckles. Brace, grip hard, lower with a 45-degree elbow angle to just below the nipples, then explode up and back with heel drive. Vary grip width across training blocks. If you bench purely for size, reduce the arch and narrow the grip slightly, but keep the scapular set.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The grip-width / muscle-emphasis findings are tendencies; he still recommends personal experimentation over a single "best" width.
- Sticking point location varies with training age and individual leverages.
- Bench numbers are heavily determined by anthropometry, so cross-lifter comparisons are not meaningful.
