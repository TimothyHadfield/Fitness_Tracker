# Myths and evidence

The rest of the library says what to do. This part says how to decide — what "evidence-based"
buys you, what it doesn't, how to read a study without fooling yourself, and where this
particular evidence-based channel got things wrong and said so.

The single most valuable note in this domain is the 2024 retrospective
([science-based lifting is over](lifting-over.md)), where he retracts a chunk of his own back
catalogue. Read that section first if you read nothing else.

---

## What "evidence-based" actually means

[Full note](evidence-based-mean.md) (2015). The term was borrowed from medicine, where
evidence-based practice named a 1990s movement to stop physicians cherry-picking research and
defaulting to inherited technique. The definition he prefers is David Sackett's, and the load-bearing
detail is that it has **two halves**: current best external evidence **and** individual practitioner
expertise. Someone doing only one of the two isn't meeting the standard.

That kills the most common objection to the whole enterprise — "there aren't good studies on
bodybuilders, so ignore the research." His analogy: nobody randomises stab-wound victims to
competing treatments, and nobody concludes from that gap that emergency medicine should be
improvised. The absence of a direct trial is exactly the situation the expertise half was written
for. It is not a licence to fall back on tradition.

**His own scorecard for the science-based fitness community**, graded against five standards of
good reasoning (attributed to the founder of Science-Based Medicine — the auto-captions render the
name as "Steven Nolla", almost certainly Steven Novella, and the note flags it as unverified):

| Standard | His grade | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Accounts for all available evidence | Good | Papers circulate constantly |
| Valid, internally consistent logic | Good **within the niche** | Poor in fitness at large |
| Thorough, rigorous, methodical | **Needs work** | Abstracts get read; methods and subject characteristics don't. He includes himself |
| Fair and unbiased | **Big problem** | Many people are financially tied to a belief; contrary research gets denigrated rather than absorbed |
| Ethics and professionalism | **Worst of all** | People who ace the first four are often the rudest |

The bias point is the sharpest one and it applies to him too: everyone in this space needs a
proprietary method, and a belief that is a revenue stream will be defended rather than updated. The
operative word in the standard is *reasonably* unbiased — nobody escapes bias, you just admit the
residue.

He closes on Harry Frankfurt's *On Bullshit*: bullshit is produced whenever someone's obligation or
opportunity to speak on a topic exceeds their knowledge of the facts, and it's worse than lying
because a liar at least tracks the truth well enough to steer away from it. That's a usable filter
for high-output fitness content, including his own.

## How to read a study

### The hierarchy, and why "you can find a study to prove anything" fails

[When to trust a study](when-trust-results-study.md) and
[the "study to prove anything" objection](addressing-find-study-prove-anything.md).

Evidence is ranked, not flat: anecdote and opinion at the bottom, then case studies, then
intermediate designs up through single RCTs, with **meta-analyses and systematic reviews at the
top**. Meta-analyses aren't infallible — they carry their own pitfalls — but one outranks any single
RCT.

The complaint splits into two claims and both dissolve:

- **"Studies can be spun to say anything."** True, and it's a *reporting* problem, not a science
  problem. Misreporting a study doesn't make its findings less true, and it doesn't make the
  misreport true. The fix is to distrust provocative headlines and read people who both do research
  and translate it — he names Aragon and Schoenfeld in this field.
- **"Different studies say different things."** Answered by the hierarchy. His worked example: an
  opinion piece claiming six meals beat three for fat loss, citing a single RCT that didn't even
  control calories between groups, versus a systematic review of 25 studies finding no association
  between eating frequency and weight. The review wins, and it isn't close.

He also objects to the wording: science doesn't set out to *prove* things, it collects evidence and
follows it. A single study contradicting a body of work is something to inspect, not grounds to
discard a field.

### Three quick trust signals for a single study

**Large effect size** — a large amount of actual muscle growth, not a marginal difference.
**Small p-value** — below 0.01. **A plausible known mechanism.**

His illustration of the third: a study where the Juicy Fruit gum group out-grows the spearmint gum
group fivefold. Nothing in that flavour should drive hypertrophy, there's no supporting literature,
and there's no mechanism — read it sceptically whatever the statistics say. He's explicit that these
three are a rough ballpark. The background reason they matter at all: **the publishing system
rewards positive findings**, so false positives are common.

### Why EMG is a weak basis for exercise selection

[Full note](our-best-work-yet.md) — filed under a video title that has nothing to do with the
content, and roughly half that video is travel footage; the EMG segment is the substance.

The implicit chain behind every "this exercise activates the muscle 30% more" claim runs: more
excitation, so more activation, so more motor unit recruitment, so more protein synthesis, so more
hypertrophy. Vigotsky and colleagues attacked the **last link**, and the follow-up paper's title says
it plainly: acutely measured surface EMG amplitude is not a validated predictor of muscle
hypertrophy.

Two things follow. **The words matter**: EMG amplitude measures **excitation** — a neuromuscular
signal — not **activation**, meaning how many motor units are firing, and Jeff committed on camera
to saying "excitation" from then on. And **activation isn't even necessary for growth** — stretch
research shows significant hypertrophy in its absence, a clean counterexample to the whole chain.

EMG became ubiquitous because it's cheap and fast — an afternoon with electrodes versus months and
money for a 12-week trial. That's a publication-incentive artefact, not a sign of quality, and he
admits to having used it to justify exercise choices himself.

He does **not** throw it out. With load, stretch, range of motion and effort genuinely held equal,
more excitation should still mean more activation and probably more growth. So EMG is a **tiebreaker
between similar exercises where no long-term data exists**, never something that overrides a training
study. Rank the evidence: long-term hypertrophy trials with direct muscle measurement, then
mechanistic and EMG data, then anecdote.

### What a mechanistic finding does and doesn't license

The recurring lesson across the domain. A mechanism tells you a thing is *possible*; only outcome
data tells you it *happens*.

- Post-exercise myofibrillar protein synthesis measured over ~6 hours **did not correlate** with
  hypertrophy across a 12-week programme (Mitchell 2014, cited in
  [the EMG note](our-best-work-yet.md)). The most-used mechanistic proxy in the field failed its own
  validation.
- Sauna activates heat shock proteins, which mechanistically can raise protein synthesis. RCTs on
  sauna show **no increase in lean body mass**
  ([Norton episode](overrated-explaining-controversial-fitness-topics.md)). Norton's line: the human
  outcome data is what counts, not the mechanism.
- Jeff's honest pushback in the other direction: net protein balance *is* synthesis minus breakdown,
  so some correlation almost has to exist, and milk-vs-soy comparisons point the same way across
  separate synthesis and hypertrophy studies. He flags this as same-day thinking after one read of
  the paper, not a settled position. That's the right register for a mechanism argument.

## Common reasoning errors in fitness

[Full note](gmos-chemicals-fallacies-productive-discourse.md) (2014). Three moves, and the third is
the interesting one.

**"Avoid chemicals" is a category error.** Everything is chemicals. He puts up an apple's full
constituent list; nothing on it is harmful for having a long name. His example:
**1,3,7-trimethylxanthine** sounds alarming and is caffeine.

**The appeal to nature fails on its own terms.** Sharks and cyanide are natural. Sucralose and
aspartame are synthesised and have extensive safety data. But he concedes the reasonable core of the
worry: *all else equal, mild extra wariness about something newly synthesised is rational*, because
apples have a tens-of-thousands-of-years track record. What doesn't follow is the conclusion —
**wariness is a reason to test, not a verdict**.

**Naming a fallacy is not an argument.** The methodological heart of the note, and it generalises
well beyond food. People label a claim "appeal to nature" or "straw man" and then don't engage with
what was said; the label becomes a way to win rather than a way to understand. Informal fallacies are
**context-dependent** in a way formal ones aren't: "appeal to authority" is fallacious when the
authority isn't legitimate and perfectly sound when your doctor gives you a diagnosis. His
prescription: restate the other person's actual concern and answer it first; name the fallacy only if
it's still doing explanatory work afterwards.

## Where the science-based movement got it wrong

[Science-Based Lifting Is Over (My Bad)](lifting-over.md), December 2024. The centrepiece. What he
retracts or revises about his own past positions:

**Slow negatives — retracted outright.** Three studies (preacher curls 1 s vs 4 s over 7 weeks;
squats 2 s vs 4 s; unilateral leg extensions 1 s vs 3 s on opposite legs) all found the same growth.
The technique review he co-authored concluded that as long as a whole rep lasts **2–8 seconds**
you're fine. Practical rule: under 1 s eccentric is too fast, 1–7 s is the band, 8 s or longer is too
slow. Notably, when he phoned Israetel — publicly associated with slow eccentrics — Israetel's actual
position was that slowing the eccentric does **not** directly cause more hypertrophy; he uses it
because it's safer and helps some people's mind-muscle connection, and his own eccentrics are 1–2
seconds. See [lifting tempo](lifting-tempo-fast-vs-slow-reps.md) for the fuller treatment.

**Technique cues and tweaks — demoted to rounding errors.** He built a table sorting every cue he
could find into directly-evidenced, indirectly-evidenced and unsupported, and the verdict was that
**every one of them, including the evidence-backed ones, has a relatively minor impact**. He still
uses several himself. They just aren't the lever.

**"Perfect technique" was never the science-based position.** No study has ever compared strict
technique to loose or cheating technique, so calling strict form "more science-based" was
misleading. He's helping run that study at Lehman College. The harmful version of the idea is
expecting rep 1 and rep 10 to look identical: ending a set because your range of motion shortened an
inch leaves several reps in the tank. Ugly late reps are fine — just keep technique roughly
consistent week to week so progress tracking stays honest.

**Tier lists were being misread, partly his fault.** His rankings mean "slightly more effective vs
slightly less effective," **not "science-based vs bro science."** Worked example: the half-kneeling
one-arm cable pulldown is S tier and the barbell row is B tier, but if forced to pick one exercise
for life he takes the barbell row without hesitation. The row is a compound that hits the whole
back; it's B tier only because better tools exist for the same specific job.

**And the rankings' evidence base was thinner than it looked.** *There has never been a single study
directly measuring back muscle hypertrophy.* Not one. Everything that exists is biomechanics and
EMG, and EMG's predictive value for growth is unestablished. His back rankings came from indirect
evidence plus personal experience, which he now says out loud. See
[choosing the best exercises](choosing-the-best-exercises.md).

**Volume revised.** He moves to **8–20 hard sets per muscle per week**, notes **4 sets can work** if
pushed hard, and allows **up to 30 sets for a month or two** to bring up a lagging body part with
volume cut elsewhere. A meta-analysis of 35 studies published weeks before the video found more
volume caused more growth **all the way to 42 sets per muscle per week** with diminishing returns —
and he declines to raise his recommendation on it, because most of those studies ran only 6–10 weeks.
That restraint is the epistemics working correctly.

**Effort revised in the other direction.** A meta-analysis of 15 studies found growth rises as you
approach failure. His stated rule stays 1–3 RIR, but he now deliberately errs closer: **1–2 RIR on
most sets, last set of an exercise to true failure where it's safe to fail** — because people aiming
to leave 3 in the tank routinely leave 5–6 without noticing. Compare the 2019
[beginner-myths note](5-training-mistakes-everyone-makes-when-they.md), where failure was reserved
for the last set of isolation work only. The direction of drift is real. Fuller treatment in
[proximity to failure](how-hard-to-train-proximity-to-failure.md).

**Two earlier self-corrections belong alongside these.** In 2018 he adopted "excitation" over
"activation" and admitted to over-leaning on EMG to justify exercise choices
([note](our-best-work-yet.md)). In 2015 he named cherry-picking-and-posting-the-abstract as the
community's methodological failure and explicitly included himself
([note](evidence-based-mean.md)).

## The consolidated myth list

Deduplicated from the three season-1 recaps
([part 1](10-fitness-myths-busted-10-minutes.md),
[part 2](7-fitness-myths-completely-busted-science.md),
[part 3](8-common-fitness-myths-busted.md)) and the
[beginner-myths note](5-training-mistakes-everyone-makes-when-they.md). Where a full-length note
exists elsewhere in the library, it's in the last column.

| Myth | Verdict | The one-line reason | Fuller note |
|---|---|---|---|
| High protein wrecks bones and kidneys | **Busted** | Osteoporosis scare rests on 1970s–80s methods; a 2017 National Osteoporosis Foundation review found protein's skeletal effect mildly favourable. Kidney risk applies to existing renal disease | [note](high-protein-diets-cause-bone-loss-kidney.md) |
| The 30-minute post-workout anabolic window | **Busted as stated** | Pre-workout protein is still being delivered post-workout, and MPS stays elevated ~48 h; the window is more like 4–6 hours | [note](post-workout-anabolic-window.md) |
| Sugar makes you fat and causes diabetes | **Busted, with a caveat** | It makes overeating easy but isn't independently causative. The supporting argument — US intake fell after 1999 while obesity rose — is ecological and thin on its own | [note](sugar-making-fat.md), [update](was-wrong-about-sugar-insulin-addiction-fructose.md) |
| Weight training stunts growth in kids | **Busted cleanly** | Traces to 1960s observational data on Japanese child labourers; every regulatory body now holds the opposite | [note](weight-training-stunt-growth.md) |
| You can only absorb 20–30 g protein per meal | **Busted on "absorb"** | Absorption is near-unlimited; the real question is use — 0.4–0.55 g/kg per meal across ~4 meals | [note](much-protein-absorb-one-meal.md) |
| Fasted cardio burns more fat | **Busted** | Burning more fat in the session means burning less over the following 24 h; net energy balance decides | [note](fasted-cardio-burn-more-fat.md) |
| Carbs before bed make you fat | **Busted** | Sleep metabolic rate isn't reliably lower than resting, and de novo lipogenesis is uncommon in humans | [note](carbs-before-bed-they-making-fat.md) |
| Foam rolling aids recovery and ROM | **Mixed — no strong case either way** | Findings conflict, and benefits may vanish once dynamic stretching is already in the warm-up. No detriment, so he still does 2–3 min | [note](foam-rolling-work.md) |
| Energy drinks are harmful | **Partly true, dose-dependent** | The concern is sugar and caffeine dose, not the category. Cap caffeine at 400 mg/day; diet versions sit far below sweetener ADIs | [note](energy-drinks-bad.md) |
| Starvation mode stalls fat loss | **Busted** | Metabolism adapts downward but never to zero loss. Look at under-reporting, water retention, or suppressed spontaneous activity | [note](starvation-mode-real-thing.md) |
| Cold water immersion / cryotherapy aids recovery | **Busted, and counterproductive** | No better than a 10-min bike cooldown, and two Roberts studies show blunted anabolic signalling and stunted growth over 12 weeks | [note](cryotherapy-work.md) |
| HIIT's afterburn burns far more calories | **Busted** | About 80 extra calories from 80 minutes of work. Cap at 1–3 sessions/week and take the rest of the deficit from food | [note](hiit-liss-which-better-fat-loss.md) |
| Keto is superior for fat loss | **Busted as a mechanism** | Roughly 1 kg advantage in meta-analysis, which the authors themselves call not clinically significant. Adherence decides | [note](ketogenic-diet-science-behind-low-carb-keto.md) |
| Fresh produce beats frozen | **Busted as a blanket rule** | Depends entirely on the commodity; freezing sometimes preserves more. The most nutritious produce is the produce you'll actually eat | [note](fresh-vs-frozen-fruits-vegetables.md) |
| There's one best cooking method | **Depends on the food** | Steaming edges it, boiling is worst for water-soluble vitamins, microwaving is fine. Vary both vegetables and methods | [note](cooking-affect-nutrition-food.md) |
| Knees shouldn't pass the toes | **Busted** | Blocking knee travel just shifts load to hips and low back (Fry 2003). Cue heels down, bar over midfoot, knees tracking over toes | [note](knees-go-over-toes.md) |
| Apple cider vinegar has powerful benefits | **Mostly hype, one modest real effect** | Real postprandial glucose effect, mainly relevant if already insulin resistant. The satiety effect was nausea, and it erodes enamel | [note](apple-cider-vinegar-s-all-hype-about.md) |
| Alcohol ruins all your progress | **True in excess, overstated in moderation** | Blunts fat oxidation and can cut post-workout MPS by up to 37%, but moderate intake with calories and protein controlled won't wreck you | [note](alcohol-impact-fat-loss-muscle-testosterone.md) |
| You must eat breakfast | **Busted** | No resting-metabolic-rate difference; breakfast eaters move more but eat back the difference. Make whatever your first meal is large and high-protein | [note](breakfast-most-important-meal-day.md) |
| Spot reduction | **Open — a sceptical question mark, not a bust** | Three studies find nothing; a small 2017 study found regional loss, confounded by 30 min of post-training cycling. He won't recommend targeting fat | [note](lose-bodyfat-from-specific-bodyparts.md) |
| Drink 8 glasses of water a day | **Busted as a universal number** | Needs vary with size, activity and climate; drink to thirst, and coffee counts. Heuristic: pale urine, ~5 times a day. 3% dehydration does impair strength | [note](don-t-need-8-glasses-water-day.md) |
| Milk and dairy are bad for you | **Busted** | Large meta-analyses, one over 700,000 participants, find no association with mortality or CVD — including datasets without industry funding | [note](science-milk.md) |
| Three body types (ecto/meso/endo) | **Busted** | 1940s pseudoscience about personality and "moral worth", and it carries no prescriptive information. Worked through below | [note](train-diet-bodytype.md) |
| Detox diets work | **Busted, potentially dangerous** | Liver and kidneys don't need help; any weight lost is extreme restriction and returns. Any random weight-loss diet beats the best detox | [note](detox-diets-work-fat-loss-health.md) |
| The mind-muscle connection is bro nonsense | **NOT busted** | Internal focus grew biceps significantly more; no effect for quads in the same study. Reserve it for single-joint work above 8 reps | [note](use-mind-muscle-connection-growth.md) |
| High reps tone, low reps bulk | **Busted** | "Toning" has no meaning; 25–35 rep sets matched 8–12 rep sets for growth when taken near failure. Practical band: 6–15 reps | [note](rep-ranges-and-training-intensity.md) |
| Lifting is a fat-loss tool | **Busted** | A session burns roughly 75–300 kcal — about a 10–15 min jog. Set the deficit with diet and cardio | [note](cardio-for-fat-loss.md) |
| One body part per workout (bro split) | **Works, but not optimal** | Splitting weekly volume across 2+ sessions beats dumping it in one day. Frequency meta-analysis finds no effect at equated volume, so the gain is indirect | [note](bro-splits-vs-higher-frequency.md) |
| You need to be sore | **Busted — but contested, see below** | Soreness marks novelty, not stimulus. The one useful signal is soreness in the *wrong* place | [note](not-training-hard-enough.md) |
| Muscle confusion | **Busted** | Progressive tension drives growth; constant rotation makes overload untrackable. Hold 1–2 anchor lifts per day for 8+ weeks | [note](using-muscle-confusion-scientifically.md) |
| No pain, no gain / always train to failure | **Busted, then partly walked back** | Failure roughly matches 1–2 RIR for growth while adding fatigue and injury risk — but see the 2024 revision above | [note](how-hard-to-train-proximity-to-failure.md) |

### Where they disagree with each other

Set the two episodes and the Contreras interview side by side and four fault lines open up.

- **Failure.** Norton and Israetel both call it overrated, but on different grounds and with
  different escape clauses — Norton because a couple of reps short gets nearly everything, Israetel
  because the pro-failure meta-regression only holds in low-volume, low-effort,
  lower-training-status populations. Contreras disagrees with Israetel's near-blanket stance
  outright. Nippard sits between them and moved *toward* failure in 2024.
- **Mind-muscle connection.** Israetel calls it quaternary and warns it costs you load and
  progression. Contreras calls it one of **two roads to hypertrophy** and co-authored the study
  everyone cites. Nippard's own 2018 recap called it "NOT busted — the bros were onto something,"
  and by 2024 he had it back among the minor variables.
- **Soreness.** Nippard's beginner note says soreness is novelty, full stop. Israetel argues the
  things that cause soreness are largely the same things that cause growth. Unresolved in the
  library, and the notes say so.
- **Supplements.** Israetel says there is no such thing as a natural test booster; Norton calls
  ashwagandha underrated partly on a small testosterone effect. They agree more than it looks —
  Norton immediately dismisses the testosterone part as irrelevant, so it does no work in his case.

## Body types: a framework that fails, worked through

[Full note](train-diet-bodytype.md). Worth its own section because it's the cleanest example in the
library of *why* a popular framework is wrong rather than just that it is.

Somatotyping comes from William Sheldon's 1940 *The Varieties of Human Physique* — 4,000 photographs
of University of Chicago students rated 1–7 on three axes. It was never about diet or training. It
was an attempt to link body type to personality, intelligence, "moral worth" and future achievement,
and it reads in retrospect as shaped by the eugenics pseudoscience of the period (Vertinsky 2007).

Three separate failures, and they hold independently of the ugly origin:

1. **It implies your type is fixed.** It isn't. Natural bodybuilder Alberto Nunez reads as a pure
   endomorph at his 250 lb bulked weight and as meso or ecto in stage condition — same person, and
   he diets on very high carbohydrate.
2. **The categories carry no prescriptive content.** "You're an ectomorph" doesn't yield a set count,
   a rep range, a calorie target or a macro split. A framework that can't generate a recommendation
   isn't doing any work.
3. **It's the wrong shape for the real variation.** Individual differences are enormous — they just
   aren't three boxes. 585 untrained subjects on an identical arm programme for 12 weeks produced
   biceps cross-sectional area changes from **−5% to +55%** (Hubal 2005). Overfeeding by 1000
   kcal/day for 8 weeks produced fat gains from **0.3 kg to 4.2 kg**, with NEAT changes from −98 to
   +692 kcal/day — some subjects nearly offset the whole surplus by subconsciously moving more
   (Levine 1999).

He grants one narrow legitimate use — some data suggests somatotype predicts suitability for
particular sports — and calls it near-trivial, since of course limb lengths suit people to different
events.

The replacement isn't "everyone is the same." Start from evidence-based defaults, then treat volume,
rep range, calorie level and macro split as **individual variables to test on yourself over weeks and
months**, tracking results. Individual variation is real and discovered, not looked up. See
[understanding genetics](understanding-genetics.md) and
[metabolism and energy expenditure](metabolism-and-energy-expenditure.md).

## Bro science vs real science

[Bret Contreras, 99 minutes](bro-science-vs-real-science.md), December 2018. The most useful thing
here is watching practical experience and research pull against each other without either side
winning cleanly.

**Where the bros were right.** Contreras and Schoenfeld ran the training study: internal focus grew
**biceps** significantly better, and made no difference for **quads** on leg extensions. Contreras'
model is **two roads to hypertrophy — progressive overload and mind-muscle connection, neither
sufficient alone**. External focus and load progression on the first one or two lifts of the day;
internal focus and quality of contraction on later, lighter work. He notes the external-focus
literature (100+ studies) genuinely favours external focus **for performance**, and that critics
conflate performance with hypertrophy.

**Where the research is right and the bros aren't.** A **4% EMG difference between exercises probably
means nothing**, and comparisons are only valid at matched range of motion, tempo and relative load.
Above roughly 85–90% of 1RM you can't meaningfully change EMG by focusing at all. Contreras defends
Vigotsky — his own former intern, and author of the papers that undercut the EMG-based content
Contreras is famous for — as careful rather than contrarian.

His rebuttal to EMG nihilism is a nice piece of reasoning: one group did **biceps curls**, one did
**calf raises**, and activation and growth tracked together — curls grew biceps and not calves, calf
raises the reverse. And **tension needs time**: a drop landing spikes EMG for a tenth of a second and
builds nothing, which is why the grinding late reps of a high-rep set are the productive ones.

**Where nobody knows.** Volume. Contreras/Schoenfeld/Krieger found **30–45 sets/week beat 6–9** over
8 weeks — and the authors deliberately *didn't* change their recommendations off one study, which is
exactly the behaviour the epistemics section is asking for. A 6-month study going the other way found
**10 sets/week best, 5 close behind, 15 clearly worse and 20 worst**, all sets taken to actual
failure — not a plateau, a reversal, with no clean mechanism proposed. Contreras' honest landing
point is that the working range is roughly 10–20 hard sets/week with brief higher-volume bursts, not
40 sets year-round. See [junk volume](junk-volume.md) and [volume roundtable](volume-roundtable.md).

**Failure, contextualised.** Contreras openly disagrees with Israetel's near-blanket anti-failure
stance: yes train to failure, but only on movements that don't load a deep stretch or huge muscle
mass, only on the last set, and not on lifts you're already training frequently. Three sets to true
failure on deadlifts three times a week will wreck you; the same on prone rear delt raises barely
registers. See [when to train to failure](when-train-failure.md).

**And the practical wisdom no study produced.** Why the big lifts hurt people isn't the mechanics —
it's **emotional attachment to a number**. Nobody injures themselves on goblet squats, because
nobody cares about the load there. At his gym he trains a couple hundred people a month with zero
injuries across 11 months by pushing hardest on the movements that are safest to push. That claim
has no controlled trial behind it and is probably right anyway, which is precisely what the
expertise half of "evidence-based" was written to cover.

## Reading notes and open problems

Things in this domain that don't fully hang together, flagged rather than smoothed over:

- **Soreness** is genuinely unresolved. The 2019 beginner note treats it as pure novelty; Israetel's
  2023 pushback is substantive and Nippard doesn't answer it.
- **Spot reduction** carries three different verdicts across three years — an open question with a
  sceptical lean (2018), a flat "fails in at least three studies" (2019), and "overrated, and the
  supportive studies are supplement-sponsored" (2023). The 2023 framing introduces a funding
  criticism the earlier notes never mention.
- **Volume** conflicts head-on between the Contreras/Schoenfeld high-volume trial and the 6-month
  reversal study, and the 2024 note's 42-set meta-analysis doesn't settle it. His recommendation
  stays conservative on purpose.
- **Mind-muscle connection** is called real (2018 recap and Contreras), quaternary (Israetel 2023),
  and a minor variable (2024) — with the *same* Schoenfeld 2018 study cited in all three places.
- **Auto-caption name garbling** is a recurring hazard the notes flag honestly: "Steven Nolla" for
  Novella, "Bernadot", "birkin" for Burke, "Siller" for Siler, plus the Vigotsky and Barbalho names.
  Verify before citing anything that appears only as a spoken mention.
- **The sugar/obesity ecological argument** — intake fell after 1999 while obesity rose — is thin
  evidence on its own and carries more weight in the recap than it can bear.
- **[our-best-work-yet.md](our-best-work-yet.md)** is filed under a video title unrelated to its
  content, and roughly half that video is vlog footage. The EMG material inside it is among the most
  important in the domain — don't skip it because of the filename.
