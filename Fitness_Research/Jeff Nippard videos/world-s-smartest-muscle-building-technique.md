# Long-Length Partials: The Best-Supported Intensity Technique

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The World's Smartest Muscle Building Technique" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ftpH4-xFGQI)) · 2023-11-16 · 11 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Long-length partials — half reps in the **stretched** portion of a lift — have more evidence behind them than drop sets, forced reps, supersets or cheat reps.
- **7 of 8 studies** favour long-length over short-length partials (1 tie, 0 against). **4 of 5 studies** favour long-length partials over full ROM (1 tie, 0 against).
- Expected magnitude, per Dr Milo Wolf: roughly **5-10% more growth** than full ROM — meaningful, not revolutionary.
- Two uses: (1) extend the last set past failure with 3-6 partials, best on **back and hamstrings**; (2) do every rep of the last set as a partial, per Dr Wolf's recommendation.
- Jeff's overall stance: still use full ROM most of the time, with long-length partials in these specific applications.

## What they are
[00:30] Split any exercise's full range in half. The half where the target muscle is more stretched is the long-length half; the other is a short-length partial. On a bicep curl the elbow travels from ~0° to ~150°, so the bottom half is the long-length portion. On squats, long-length means reaching a deep stretch in the quads and glutes at the bottom and only coming about halfway up — cutting out the squeezed part. On lat pulldowns the lats are most stretched with arms overhead, so a long-length partial is pulling only about halfway down to the chest. Short-length partials (top-half only) are not recommended.

## The evidence
[01:32] The emerging picture is that it is the stretched portion of the range that drives growth, not full ROM per se.

- Long-length vs short-length partials: 8 studies, 7 favour long-length, 1 found no difference, none favour short-length.
- Full ROM vs long-length partials: 5 studies, 4 favour long-length partials, 1 found no difference, none favour full ROM. In the study finding no difference, the partial group used only **10% of the full range** — half the subjects were doing tenth-reps in the stretched position and still matched the full-ROM group ([Werkhausen 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33465838/)).

[02:32] Jeff spoke to **Dr Milo Wolf**, the leading researcher on the topic, who has trained exclusively with lengthened partials for all his hypertrophy work for 9-10 months. Both are careful not to oversell: Wolf frames the expected improvement as **5-10% over full ROM** — worth having if maximising hypertrophy is the goal, but not groundbreaking.

Mechanistically this fits stretch-mediated hypertrophy, known for years: the classic quail study where loaded stretch of the wing produced dramatic growth ([Antonio 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8226539/)), plus human studies showing training at long muscle lengths is effective.

## Application 1 — extending the last set past failure
[03:33] Reserved mainly for **back and hamstrings**, because both have far more capacity left once full-ROM failure is reached.

**Back:** on pulldowns, most science-based lifters stop when the bar can no longer reach the chest. Instead, keep going with partial reps, pulling the bar as far as technique allows, and end the set once you can only get it about halfway down. Same on pull-ups (once the chest no longer touches the bar) and rows (once the full squeeze is gone, keep working the stretched part until the weight won't come halfway up). Typically yields **3-6 extra partial reps**.

**Hamstrings:** once you can no longer get the full squeeze at the top, another **5-6 partials** are usually available before you can't move the weight halfway.

You could in theory continue with quarter and eighth reps until the weight won't budge, but Jeff considers that overkill. Critically, **standardise your endpoint** week to week so progress remains trackable. He does not do this on every set of every exercise — that would cause a sharp performance drop-off later in the session, reduce total volume, and risk recovery problems if diet and sleep aren't dialled in.

## Application 2 — every rep as a partial
[05:35] On the last set of selected exercises, do all reps as long-length partials. This is Dr Wolf's recommended method — his logic being that you spend more total time in the most anabolic part of the range, so why water the set down with time in the less anabolic part.

Example: last set of chest-supported machine rows, every rep coming only halfway up, aiming to hit failure at rep 10 with an appropriate load. Expect to need **somewhat heavier weight** for partials.

Exercises that suit this especially well:
- **Dumbbell flies.** Their resistance curve already loads the bottom heavily and the top barely at all — a bias that now looks like a feature. Jeff previously argued cable flies were superior for continuous resistance; he has revised that. He still does cable flies for variety, but now performs dumbbell flies almost exclusively as long-length partials.
- **Smith machine press** — strong stretch and pump.
- **Leg press** — he finds partials more enjoyable here. They are not easier; arguably harder, because you lose the brief rest at lockout.

**Integrated partials** are a variant: rather than saving partials for the end, alternate throughout the set — one full rep, one half rep, one full, one half, to failure. Jeff does these on the pec deck. They raise total time in the stretched range without abandoning the contracted portion, keep the set engaging and produce a strong pump.

## Three common mistakes
[08:08]
1. **Losing control.** Many people treat partials as cheat reps and let form go loose. A partial rep is still a rep: control the negative, minimise momentum, feel the deep stretch at the bottom.
2. **Not shortening the range enough.** Lifters used to full ROM tend to just skip the lockout, ending up doing ~90% of the range. That is fine but is not a true long-length partial — in the studies, subjects did **half reps**. Cut the range substantially.
3. **Overhyping them online.** Jeff defended the cable triceps kickback in a short and was told it must be inferior because it loads the shortened position. He considers that over-extrapolation — the evidence is not strong enough to discard every shortened-bias exercise, especially ones with decades of good results, nor to eliminate the top half of every exercise.

## Practical application
Keep full ROM as the default for most work. Add long-length partials in two places: as an end-of-set extender on your last set of back and hamstring exercises (3-6 partials, consistent endpoint), and as an all-partial last set on stretch-friendly movements like dumbbell flies, machine rows, Smith press and leg press — going slightly heavier and hitting failure around rep 10. Try integrated partials (alternating full and half reps to failure) on pec deck or similar. Control every partial rep as strictly as a full one, and cut the range to a genuine half.

## Caveats & unknowns
Full ROM has decades of empirical support and, zoomed out, long-length partials are not miles ahead — the fairest reading is that they clearly beat short-length partials and are at least as good as full ROM, possibly better in some cases. The expected 5-10% edge is a researcher's estimate. Doing partials on every set risks late-workout performance drop-off, reduced volume and recovery issues. The evidence does not yet justify discarding shortened-position exercises or making every set partials.
