# Programming for the Beginner Powerlifter: Specificity, Overload and a Sample Week

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Smartest Way to Program for Powerlifting (Science Explained)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LDdx0YuDh-I)) · 2016-12-23 · 13 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Beginner = under roughly **six months** of intelligent strength-specific training, or (Rippetoe's definition) anyone who can still progress **workout to workout**.
- The whole job as a beginner: **add weight to the bar incrementally and non-randomly**, and fix technique before bad habits get grooved in.
- Train **each competition lift 2-3× per week**, and avoid taking many or any sets to failure.
- Hold reps fixed and add load — don't chase extra reps at the same weight.
- Success in powerlifting is mostly the ability to keep progressing **without getting injured**. Slowing progression to stay in the game is a win.

## Defining the beginner
[04:09] "Beginner" here means new to powerlifting — no meet done, no real training specifically for squat/bench/deadlift strength. Roughly **less than six months** of intelligent strength-specific training.

[04:39] Mark Rippetoe's framing from *Practical Programming*, which Jeff endorses:
- **Novice** — progresses from one workout to the next
- **Intermediate** — progresses week to week
- **Advanced** — progresses month to month or year to year

So as a beginner you should be able to get stronger every session; when you can't, you're moving into intermediate territory.

## Newbie gains don't mean any program will do
[05:09] Beginners can make substantial progress on almost anything, but that's not an argument for running any random program. Rippetoe's point, which Jeff quotes as nailing it: what separates a good program from a lesser one is its ability to **keep stimulating the desired adaptation**. A program that requires a **regular increase in some aspect of its stress** is effective for a novice, and nothing works as well as **moderately increasing some loading parameter each time** for as long as adaptation to that increase keeps occurring.

Two beginner priorities:
1. Incrementally add weight in a controlled, non-random way.
2. Practise and refine basic technique. Now is the time to fix, e.g., hips shooting up on the deadlift — otherwise you neurologically program a bad habit that has to be un-programmed later.

## Hierarchy of priorities: injury, specificity, overload
[06:10] Nothing in the hierarchy matters if you're injured. You do need to lift heavy to get strong, but going ultra-heavy all the time is a trap, because powerlifting success is basically a function of slowly progressing without getting hurt. If you're progressing at all, be happy with that.

[07:17] **Daily 1RM training** has solid empirical support but is appropriate for very advanced lifters and lifters on drugs — not beginners, and definitely not natural beginners.

## Specificity, and lifting heavy for strength
[07:48] Specificity: to get better at squat, bench and deadlift, do a lot of squatting, benching and deadlifting.

There is a real distinction between strength and hypertrophy adaptations. In a **2014 paper by Schoenfeld et al.**, a bodybuilding-style group (**3 sets of 10**) was compared to a powerlifting-style group (**7 sets of 3**). **Hypertrophy was roughly equal**, but the powerlifting-style group **gained significantly more strength**. The lesson: you must lift heavy to maximise strength.

Because much early strength gain comes from **motor learning**, beginners need frequent practice. Jeff's sweet spot: **each lift 2-3× per week**, provided you aren't taking many or any sets to failure.

## Daily undulating periodization (DUP)
[08:19] DUP simply means some programming variable changes within the training week — e.g. squat reps on day one differ from squat reps on day three. Jeff uses it for beginners even though it isn't required for progress, because:
- It has a good deal of general empirical support.
- Programs with identical set/rep structures all week (Starting Strength is his example) get stale and boring fast for new lifters, even if they'd progress fine on them.

## Sample beginner week
[09:20] Split: **Lower / Upper / Lower / Rest / Upper / Lower / Rest**

First half of the week:
- Squat — **3 × 6**
- Bench press — **3 × 6**
- Deadlift — **4 × 3**

Second half of the week:
- Bench press — **4 × 3**
- Squat — **4 × 3**
- Deadlift — **3 × 6**

Add assistance movements — rows, vertical presses, hip thrusts, rear delt flyes, whatever your muscular development needs — onto the appropriate upper or lower days.

## Progression and top singles
[09:51] **Keep the rep count fixed and add weight over time.** As a new powerlifter you want to get better at getting stronger and get used to handling heavier and heavier loads — not just adding reps at a given weight.

[10:21] Occasional **top sets**, especially on squat, build confidence. If you only ever handle 3s and 6s, a 1RM attempt feels intimidating on your back. So do occasional **single-rep sets at submaximal load, ~75-85% of 1RM**, *before* the volume work. Frequency: **about every other week, and certainly not more than once per week per lift.**

## Practical application
1. Run a 4-day lower/upper/lower/upper/lower-style week with each of squat, bench and deadlift trained 2-3×.
2. Undulate: heavier low-rep day and lighter higher-rep day for each lift (e.g. 3×6 and 4×3).
3. Add weight session to session while reps stay fixed. Don't grind to failure.
4. Every other week, work up to a submaximal single at **75-85% 1RM** before your volume sets, mainly on squat.
5. Spend beginner time fixing technique flaws now.
6. Listen to your body — slowing short-term progression to stay healthy and in the sport longer is the correct trade.

## Caveats & unknowns
- DUP is a preference, not a requirement: Jeff is explicit that beginners could progress fine on a fixed set/rep structure, and he uses DUP partly for adherence and boredom reasons.
- Daily max training works but is aimed at advanced and enhanced lifters, not natural beginners.
- The 6-month cutoff for "beginner" is a rough marker; the workout-to-workout progression test is the better one.
