# Four Shoulder Puzzles Answered: Press Mechanics, Grip Width and Rear Delt Grip

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "4 Shoulder Mysteries Resolved ft. Stephanie" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b9poVsJKLkM)) · 2017-06-14 · 5 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- The shoulder press hits the front delt rather than the lateral delt because the shoulder sits **externally rotated**, putting the anterior deltoid in the better position to oppose gravity.
- The front raise underperforms the press for the front delt because the anterior deltoid contributes maximum force **above horizontal** — exactly where the front raise stops.
- Grip width depends on bench angle: **narrower for decline and flat presses**, **slightly wider for incline and vertical presses** — but the differences on shoulder presses are small.
- A neutral grip beats pronated on the reverse pec deck because it lets the shoulder produce **external rotation as well as horizontal abduction**, using both deltoid functions.

Follow-up to the questions posed in `4-unsolved-shoulder-mysteries.md`.

## 1. Why the shoulder press doesn't primarily target the lateral deltoid [00:30]
You'd expect a frontal plane movement to hit the lateral delt, since the lateral raise is also a frontal plane movement and does exactly that. The reason it doesn't: during a shoulder press the **shoulder is externally rotated**, which places the **anterior deltoid** — not the lateral — in the better position to oppose gravity.

## 2. Why the front raise is less effective than the press for the front delt [01:02]
The front deltoid contributes its **maximum force above horizontal** — above shoulder height. A front raise stops at horizontal and reverses, so it never loads the delt where it's strongest.

**Why not just extend the front raise overhead?** Because the resistance path is variable/circular. At the bottom of a front raise there is effectively **zero resistance**, which increases as you rise. Above horizontal you can maintain **higher, more constant resistance through the entire motion** — which is what the overhead press does. Extending a front raise all the way overhead would therefore still not be optimal.

## 3. Grip width and deltoid activation [02:04]
The answer depends on **bench angle**:

- **Decline and flat bench press:** a **narrower grip** produces greater anterior deltoid activation (per [Barnett et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232217991_Effects_of_Variations_of_the_Bench_Press_Exercise_on_the_EMG_Activity_of_Five_Shoulder_Muscles), rendered "Baretta" in the auto-captions).
- **Incline and vertical (overhead) press:** not much difference, with a **wider grip slightly better**.

Because the differences on shoulder presses specifically are small, the recommendation is to **find a grip that feels comfortable and safe for your shoulder joint and use it**.

## 4. Why a neutral grip beats pronated for rear delt EMG [03:04]
In a **neutral hand position**, the shoulder can perform **horizontal abduction *and* external rotation**, and the deltoid contributes to both. With hands **pronated**, you lose the external rotation component, so you're not using that secondary function of the shoulder. Neutral engages both functions, which shows up as higher EMG ([Schoenfeld 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/23302754)).

**A possible confound** raised in the comments (credited to viewer Evan Godby): the rotator cuff muscles also externally rotate, and they sit in close proximity to the rear delt. Surface electrodes may have been picking up rotator cuff signal rather than pure rear deltoid activity — a genuine confounding variable rather than a true rear delt difference.

## Practical application
- For the **front delt**, prefer overhead pressing over front raises — the press loads the delt in its strongest range with more constant resistance.
- For the **lateral delt**, use lateral raises; the shoulder press won't cover it.
- **Grip width:** narrow on flat and decline presses, slightly wider on incline and overhead presses. On overhead work, prioritise joint comfort over the marginal EMG difference.
- **Rear delts:** use a **neutral grip** on the reverse pec deck.

## Caveats & unknowns
- All four answers are mechanistic reasoning applied to EMG data, not hypertrophy outcomes.
- The rear delt finding may be partly an artefact of electrode placement picking up rotator cuff activity.
- The researcher name for the grip-width data is garbled in the auto-captions; treat the attribution as uncertain.
