# Alcohol's Effect on Fat Loss, Muscle Growth and Testosterone

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How Does ALCOHOL Impact Fat Loss, Muscle & Testosterone? (What The Science Says)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vwn8E4juTts)) · 2018-05-28 · 9 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Alcohol doesn't break energy balance. In a calorie deficit you will lose fat while drinking; in a surplus you won't. The real question is what it does to *body composition*.
- Alcohol itself is burned off almost immediately — but while it's in your system, dietary fat oxidation is severely blunted and other calories (especially fat) are preferentially stored.
- Heavy drinking post-workout cut muscle protein synthesis by 37%, and by 24% even when optimal protein was consumed alongside it.
- Alcohol's damage looks sex-specific: it suppresses testosterone and mTOR signalling in men but appears to spare women.
- The biggest practical cost may simply be a wrecked training session the next day from dehydration, hangover and disrupted sleep.

## What happens when you drink [01:31]
Alcohol is absorbed into the bloodstream and goes straight to the liver. The body handles it metabolically much like a poison — metabolism of every other nutrient you've eaten is put on the back burner until the alcohol is cleared. Because there's no storage form for alcohol (unlike carbs and fat), as long as alcohol is in your system incoming carbs and fats are preferentially shuttled toward storage. This is most pronounced for **dietary fat**, whose oxidation is severely blunted after drinking ([Siler 1999](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/10539756)).

Crucially: alcohol isn't directly making you fat — it's burned off first. It's the *other* excess calories present alongside it that get stored so readily. **Practical implication: if you're going to drink heavily, lower your fat intake that day.**

## Why alcohol is bad news for a deficit [02:32]
- **Post-drinking binges** are the single biggest risk — a lot of new fat in a very short window, made hard to resist by lowered inhibitions. If your willpower while intoxicated is poor and fat loss is the priority, avoiding heavy drinking may simply be the better call.
- **No compensation.** Experimental studies show people generally don't compensate later for calories consumed as alcohol; they get tacked straight onto the day's total, making a surplus much easier to hit.
- **Terrible satiety.** A 2010 review on alcohol and appetite described alcohol as the *least satiating* energy source ([Yeomans 2010](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/20096714)), and some studies show alcohol magnifies food's ability to stimulate appetite — it doesn't fill you up and may make you hungrier once you start eating.

Despite all of that, it comes down to moderation. Assuming a net calorie deficit is in place, an occasional drink or even a splurge shouldn't derail fat loss. Nippard notes from coaching that an occasional night out can even help when someone is stressed or diet-bound, with many dieters (himself included) seeing a "whoosh" in scale weight in the days after.

## Testosterone [04:35]
- **Light drinking (2-3 standard drinks)** has been shown to *raise* testosterone by around **17%** in men ([Sarkola 2003](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/12711931)), which may explain the libido bump. The spike is very transient and unlikely to do anything meaningfully anabolic.
- **Higher doses** cause testosterone to decline steeply. One study drawing blood from intoxicated emergency-room patients found **45% lower testosterone in drunk men** versus controls — but about **fivefold higher testosterone in drunk women** ([Frias 2002](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/11912073)).
- The authors attributed this to alcohol's inhibitory effect on *testicular* testosterone production specifically, an idea supported by multiple lines of evidence. Practical summary: if you don't have testicles, alcohol's testosterone effect isn't your concern.

## Muscle protein synthesis [05:36]
The general trend of the research is that alcohol suppresses muscle protein synthesis, especially in the post-workout period. Much of this is rodent work, but it all points the same way.

A **2014 human study** (lead author sounds like "Parr" in the captions — reasonably likely but worth verifying) found that heavy post-workout alcohol consumption in the range of **8-9 drinks reduced muscle protein synthesis by 37%**. Even when protein was consumed in amounts shown to be optimally effective, alcohol still cut MPS by **24%** — meaning protein, one of the most anabolic tools available, can't fully rescue alcohol's anti-anabolic effect.

That study used only male subjects. A **2017 study** in 10 men and 9 women found that although resistance training produced similar mTOR signalling in both sexes, alcohol only attenuated the mTOR pathway **in men** ([Duplanty 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/27135475)). Women appear to have considerably less to worry about from drinking after training.

## Next-day performance [07:07]
Nippard argues this may be the biggest real-world downside. How much it hurts is individual and dose-dependent, but the combination of dehydration, hangover and interrupted sleep works against you in the gym the following day. Occasionally this won't derail progress — particularly if you schedule a planned rest day the day after and focus on rehydrating. If it becomes a regular habit, the performance losses stacked on top of suppressed testosterone and reduced MPS (especially for men) genuinely hold you back.

## Verdict
Not cleanly busted either way. Alcohol in moderation with calories controlled is unlikely to cause significant fat gain or impaired muscle growth. In excess it slows progress on every front.

## Practical application
- Drink inside your calorie budget — count the alcohol calories, since your appetite won't.
- On a heavy drinking day, cut dietary fat, since fat oxidation is blunted and fat is preferentially stored.
- Guard hardest against the post-drinking food binge.
- Don't drink heavily right after training if you're male; the MPS hit is large and protein won't fully offset it.
- Schedule a rest day after a night out and prioritise rehydration.
- Keep it occasional. Regular heavy drinking compounds performance, testosterone and MPS losses.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Much of the MPS evidence is rodent research; the human data is limited and acute.
- The sex differences come from small samples (the 2017 study used 10 men and 9 women).
- The emergency-room testosterone data is observational, drawn from intoxicated patients, not a controlled dosing trial.
- One study's lead author is uncertain from the auto-captions.
