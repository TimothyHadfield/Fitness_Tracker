# Picking Apart 11 Anti-Sugar Claims: What the Cited Studies Actually Show

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "11 Weird Things Sugar Probably ISN'T Doing to Your Body" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=80GmAG9n7LQ)) · 2014-10-05 · 10 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- A widely shared article listed 11 harms of sugar and cited **zero references**; Nippard tracked down the underlying studies himself, and most did not say what the article claimed.
- The recurring errors are the same few: extrapolating from extreme doses to normal intakes, treating correlation as causation, hasty generalisation from one disease to another, and reductionism (blaming one nutrient for a multi-factor outcome).
- The headline "fructose makes your organs fat" study used ~150 g pure fructose/day (25% of calories) on top of an ad libitum diet in overweight/obese subjects — roughly seven cans of Pepsi daily.
- His stated safe reference point: about **60 g of fructose per day** shows no negative effects on any measured health marker, especially in active people eating an otherwise good diet.
- Credit for much of the material goes to Alan Aragon's research review and Aragon's online debates with Robert Lustig.

## The claims, one at a time

**1. "Sugar makes your organs fat."** [01:33] Traces to a 2009 trial giving glucose or fructose as a soft drink supplying 25% of daily calories for 10 weeks. Fructose did increase de novo lipogenesis, worsen dyslipidemia, reduce insulin sensitivity and raise visceral adiposity. But the dose was ~150 g pure fructose/day *added* to whatever subjects already ate — in a group already gaining weight. The error is dose extrapolation: nothing about that design speaks to moderate intake in healthy, active people.

**2. "Sugar primes your body for diabetes."** [03:06] The PLOS paper behind it was authored by Robert Lustig — whom Nippard characterises as prone to cherry-picking observational data. The study is correlational: increased fructose availability tracks with diabetes prevalence. Correlation is not causation, and no causal chain is demonstrated.

**3. "Sugar hammers your heart."** [04:07] Pure hasty generalisation: because sugar is linked to type 2 diabetes, it is assumed to also cause heart disease and stroke. Two leaps at once — the warning applies only to people who already have diabetes, and it credits sugar rather than the cluster of other things diabetics tend to have (low physical activity, high overall calorie intake).

**4. "Sugar creates tense blood vessels."** [04:38] A 2012 systematic review found that fructose swapped isocalorically for other carbohydrate *lowered* diastolic and mean arterial pressure, with no significant effect on systolic. The evidence points the opposite way to the claim.

**5. "Sugar promotes cholesterol chaos."** [05:09] The claim rests on the undefined phrase "sugar overload." A study of over 25,000 subjects found fructose and non-fructose sugar intakes had no positive association with triglycerides, HDL, and similar markers *when consumed in typical amounts*.

**6. "It leads to type 3 diabetes."** [06:12] Nippard searched the full text of the cited paper for both "sugar" and "fructose" and got no hits for either. The citation simply does not support the claim.

**7. "Sugar turns you into a junkie."** [06:43] Addiction is hard to demarcate even within psychology. A 1997 review concluded the physiological evidence for carbohydrate addiction is weak relative to drug addiction; if it exists at all it more likely resembles a habitual, reinforcement-driven dependence (like gambling) than a substance dependence.

**8. "Sugar turns you into a ravenous animal."** [07:13] True in rats, not shown in humans. A 2009 review on fructose and satiety concluded that, given inconsistent experimental designs, the case for fructose having lower satiating efficacy as a driver of the obesity epidemic "is simply not compelling."

**9. "Sugar makes you an energy-starved zombie."** [07:44] Logically incoherent — sugar is the body's preferred energy substrate.

**10. "Sugar turns your smile upside down."** [08:16] The claim is a ~40% greater depression risk in the highest junk-food consumers. Depression is multi-causal; reducing it to a single nutrient is reductionism, and junk food is not the same variable as sugar.

**11. "Sugar wrecks your face."** [08:47] The cited paper does confirm that glucose and fructose cross-link the amino acids in dermal collagen and elastin, producing advanced glycation end products (AGEs), and that the process accelerates when blood sugar is elevated. But the paper contains no controlled trial of *dietary* sugar intake on AGE accumulation, and the one supporting citation only relates skin elasticity to age and BMI. The hidden premise — sugar is required to make AGEs, therefore more dietary sugar means more AGEs — is unsupported. Nippard leaves this one as an open question rather than a refutation.

## Practical application
- Before accepting a nutrition claim, check whether the article cites anything at all; an uncited list is low-credibility from the outset.
- When a study is cited, check the **dose**. Ask what intake the subjects actually consumed and whether it resembles a normal diet.
- Check the **population**. Overweight/obese sedentary subjects in overfeeding conditions are not a model for a trained person in energy balance.
- Roughly 60 g fructose/day appears benign; that is far above habitual intake for most people eating fruit and the odd dessert.
- Judge sugar within the context of the total diet and training, not as an isolated variable.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The AGEs/skin claim is genuinely unresolved — absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.
- Several studies are referenced only by year and topic in the transcript, so exact citations should be verified before reuse.
- Nippard's characterisation of Lustig as a cherry-picker is his assessment of a scientist's body of work, not a finding.
- This is a 2014 video reviewing pre-2014 literature; the fructose and cardiometabolic-health evidence base has grown since.
