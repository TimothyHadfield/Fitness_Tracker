# Should the Knees Travel Past the Toes When You Squat?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Should The Knees Go Over the Toes? (Proper SQUAT Technique Explained)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dCHLUtf--pg)) · 2018-04-23 · 6 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- The "knees must not pass the toes" rule is busted. Moderate forward knee travel is normal and safe in healthy squatters.
- Blocking forward knee travel cuts knee torque slightly but multiplies hip torque enormously — a bad trade.
- Judge a squat by three criteria instead: heels planted, bar over mid-foot, knees tracking over toes.
- How much forward lean and depth you get is largely dictated by femur and tibia length — skeletal, not fixable.
- The rule does still apply to lunges, where the knee/hip trade-off doesn't exist in the same way.

## Where the myth came from
It traces to a 1972 study of 12 subjects in which the subject with the greatest forward knee travel also had the greatest shear force at the tibiofemoral joint (the tibia-femur joint). Shear here means opposing forces — one pushing the tibia forward, one pulling the femur back — and extreme shear can produce knee pain or, at the far end, ACL injury.

More recent literature has attacked that dataset hard: it didn't control for squat depth and never described the squat technique used, raising the possibility that the shear was actually driven by a very upright torso position. The rule then survived as personal-training dogma handed down without anyone rechecking the evidence.

## Why the logic fails
Common sense first: walking down stairs puts the knees well past the toes and almost never hurts healthy knees. Olympic weightlifters routinely hit extreme knee flexion with the knees far past the toes without chronic knee problems.

The stronger argument is methodological: you cannot write technique rules for a multi-joint movement by looking at the forces at one joint alone and ignoring the hip and lower back.

## The key study [02:03]
A 2003 study by Fry and colleagues ([Fry 2003](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/14636100)) compared knee and hip torque in two squat variations taken to parallel:
- **Unrestricted (knees free to travel forward):** knee torque 150 Nm, hip torque 28 Nm.
- **Restricted (a wooden board blocked forward knee travel):** knee torque 117 Nm, hip torque 302 Nm.

So restricting the knees dropped knee torque modestly but raised hip torque more than tenfold. The authors concluded it is appropriate to permit forward displacement of the knees past the toes to allow proper torso positioning — while adding that the forward travel should be regulated rather than excessive.

Data from a second group (captioned as "lists and colleagues," name uncertain) supports this: there is less stress on the lower back in an unrestricted squat than a restricted one, and coaches should not be overly strict about coaching against anterior knee displacement. Keeping the knees back forces more forward lean, loading the hips and lumbar spine.

## What actually makes a good squat [03:35]
1. **Heels stay firmly planted.** Moderate forward knee travel is fine as long as your weight doesn't shift onto the toes.
2. **Bar stays centred over the mid-foot.** Viewed from the side, the bar should travel in a straight vertical line.
3. **Knees point the same direction as the toes.** No caving in, no forcing them out excessively (a few advanced cases aside).

Everything beyond that is individual anatomy. Forward lean is driven mostly by femur length, especially relative to tibia length — longer femurs necessarily mean more torso lean. Skeletal structure also affects how deep you can and should go.

## Practical application
- Let the knees travel forward as much as your depth and proportions require, provided the heels stay down and the bar stays over mid-foot.
- Do not chase ass-to-grass as a universal standard. Squat as deep as you comfortably can, treating **at least parallel** as the minimum for strength and size goals.
- Long femurs? Expect and accept more forward lean; it's not a technique fault.
- **Lunges are the exception** — limit forward knee travel there. You can stay upright much more easily in a lunge, so you don't pay a hip/lower-back penalty for keeping the knee back.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Recommendations apply to healthy, regularly active people. Anyone already dealing with knee pain should get assessed by a qualified professional rather than self-prescribing.
- The original 1972 paper had only 12 subjects and poor methodological control; the "excessive" threshold for forward knee travel is never quantified.
- The researcher name in the follow-up citation is garbled by the auto-captions and should be verified before being repeated.
