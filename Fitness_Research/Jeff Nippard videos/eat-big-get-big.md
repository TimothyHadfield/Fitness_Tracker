# "Eat Big To Get Big" and Other Bulking Myths, Dismantled

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Should You EAT BIG To GET BIG? (Responding To Dumb Diet Advice)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y3EUG4ivY_M)) · 2019-06-11 · 17 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- More protein is not endlessly better. The literature's plateau sits around 1.6 g/kg (~0.7 g/lb); Jeff prefers ~2 g/kg (~1 g/lb) as an easy, slightly buffered target.
- Per-meal anabolic response caps somewhere around 20–40 g of protein. You absorb far more than that, but absorption is not the same as use.
- Post-workout carbs are not urgent. Adding carbs to protein does not increase the anabolic response.
- Overfeeding heavily adds fat, not much extra muscle. Run a moderate surplus and let progressive training drive growth.
- Target roughly 0.5–1.5% of body weight gained per month.

## Protein: how much is actually useful
The RDA of 0.8 g/kg (~0.4 g/lb) is far too low for anyone lifting — it puts a 120 lb woman at ~50 g/day and a 200 lb man under 100 g/day, neither of which maximises the anabolic response to training. Most of the general public genuinely under-eats protein, so "eat more" is a reasonable starting nudge.

But the benefit plateaus. Current evidence puts the ceiling around 1.6 g/kg (~0.7 g/lb). Jeff finds that a touch low in coaching practice — for him it would be ~120 g/day — so he leans on an earlier 2011 paper arguing for up to 2 g/kg, i.e. roughly 1 g per pound of body weight. It is easy arithmetic (165 lb person → 165 g/day) and there is no apparent downside to the extra buffer. He is also open to going slightly above 1 g/lb during body recomposition, an area the literature hasn't pinned down.

The 450 g/day figure being parodied is far outside anything in the literature; for that lifter he'd prescribe 165–200 g/day.

## Absorption vs. use
You can absorb essentially all the protein you eat — absorption just means amino acids crossing the intestine into the blood, and that limit is enormous. The useful question is how much drives muscle protein synthesis, and there the per-meal cap sits somewhere around 20–40 g depending on which studies you read. Slamming 100–200 g in a single sitting doesn't buy extra anabolism past that point.

## The on/off switch is the wrong model
Thinking of yourself as either "fully anabolic" or "fully catabolic" is about the worst mental model available. Researchers describe it better as a dial: a little protein turns the response up a little, more turns it up further, and eventually the dial stops turning. In reality the two processes blend.

## Carbs, insulin and glycogen
- **"Clean carbs can't make you fat"** is false. A caloric surplus produces fat gain regardless of carb source.
- **Post-workout insulin spike.** The theory (insulin as a storage hormone forcing protein into muscle) has surface appeal but doesn't hold. Research shows adding carbohydrate to post-workout protein does not increase the anabolic response. Insulin does have an anti-proteolytic effect (blunting further muscle protein breakdown), but the blood concentration needed is low — protein alone reaches it, and a pre-workout mixed meal with carbs produces a sustained insulin response that carries into the post-workout window.
- **Glycogen.** Bodybuilding-style training isn't especially glycogen-depleting. Even after a brutal high-volume session in a deficit, replenishment measured over 24 hours is the same whether you slam carbs immediately or just eat normal carb-containing meals.
- **Two exceptions.** (1) Training the same muscle twice in one day — e.g. back in the morning, biceps in the evening — where rapid carb intake after the first session matters for the 7–8 hour turnaround. (2) Training fasted, where there is no pre-workout insulin response, making post-workout nutrition somewhat more urgent.

## Why "eat big to get big" fails
Overfeeding people substantially does not produce much extra muscle, but does produce considerably more fat. Jeff cites research from Garthe and colleagues (auto-captions render it "Garth"): small vs. large surplus with weight training gave near-identical muscle gain, with far more fat in the large-surplus group. A newer study covered in the MASS research review found the same pattern — heavy overfeeding gave slightly more muscle but much more fat. Eric Helms' write-up concluded this leaves a fairly bleak view of bulking for natural lifters: a moderate surplus is all that's required, and any substantial surplus beyond that is largely allocated to fat — the tortoise beats the hare. His stated exception: a lean novice, or someone who cares only about absolute strength with no regard for body composition.

The deeper problem is emphasis. "Eat big" implies calories cause growth. They don't — a progressive training stimulus does. Nutrition augments the training stimulus, not the other way round.

**Hard gainers** are the partial exception: very skinny, low-appetite people sometimes struggle to even hit maintenance, and calorically dense food may be needed to grow at all. But dirty bulking can still take them from lean-and-skinny to skinny-fat.

**"Bear mode"** is distinct from dirty bulking — a deliberate heavier look, accepting fat gain but still capping around 15–20% body fat. Even there, eating big isn't required; it's more a psychological approach to gaining weight.

## Practical application
- Protein: ~1 g per pound of body weight per day (2 g/kg). Spread it so meals land in the ~20–40 g effective range rather than one enormous serving.
- Calories: moderate surplus only.
- Rate of gain: 0.5–1.5% of body weight per month. A 150 lb lifter → ~1–2 lb/month, ~10–20 lb over a year. Scale down when advanced or wanting to stay lean; scale up slightly if brand new.
- Priority: get progressively stronger and increase workload over time. That's the driver.
- Don't chase post-workout carbs. Protein alone post-training is fine; carbs somewhere in the day covers glycogen.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The optimal protein intake for body recomposition specifically is not well established; Jeff's willingness to exceed 1 g/lb there is judgment, not settled data.
- The 20–40 g per-meal cap varies by study and has more nuance than a single number (meal composition, total daily intake, training status).
- The 2011 paper Jeff prefers over the newer 1.6 g/kg estimate is not named in the transcript.
