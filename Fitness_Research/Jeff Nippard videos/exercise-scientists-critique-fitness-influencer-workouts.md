# Two Exercise Scientists Critique Influencer and Pro Bodybuilder Training

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Exercise Scientists Critique Fitness Influencer Workouts" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ykreq4u_Egw)) · 2025-02-27 · 29 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- Exercise selection is a minor variable. Effort (proximity to failure), volume, technique/range of motion, and frequency are what actually drive growth — most common exercises work.
- Cranking load, intensity and volume all to the maximum simultaneously is what breaks people. Turn up two of the three, not all three.
- Getting bigger makes you stronger more than getting stronger makes you bigger — but you still have to add load over time to keep reps in a stimulative range.
- Body composition is calories and macros almost entirely; food sources barely matter once macros and sodium are handled.
- Cold plunges are the most overhyped recovery tool in fitness right now; hot exposure post-workout has better evidence.

## Ronnie Coleman and the "he did it right" argument
Both agree Ronnie could have had bigger quads with fewer injuries under a more systematic approach — not as a knock, but because no historical process is ever optimal in hindsight. Jeff's injury theory: Ronnie maxed load, intensity *and* volume at once. Dorian Yates trained just as heavy but with lower volume and got hurt less. Mike's illustration: if Ronnie's maximum recoverable volume for quads was ~25 hard sets/week and he averaged ~22, he lost a few percent. But recent literature suggests that when systemic fatigue isn't limiting, 20–40 sets per week in short blocks can out-grow lower volumes, so his true ceiling may have been ~10 sets higher than he ever trained.

## Steroids
[03:32] On the Tren Twins' "99.9% shouldn't take steroids": the most effective deterrents are the psychological ones. Mike adds two more — potential degradation of intelligence (especially in developing brains) and, under age ~22, premature closure of growth plates limiting adult height. Also acne and early balding.

On TRT: if clinically low, see a doctor. TRT restores a normal physiological range — you won't get huge from it. But two nuances: many TRT protocols put you at the *top* of the natural range, which most people never reach ([Travison 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28324103/)); and TRT holds levels stable, whereas natural testosterone dips with poor sleep, poor nutrition or stress — that stability subtly adds hypertrophic potential over time.

Response to steroids is highly individual, like response to training. Expecting a cycle to transform you is wrong 7–8 times out of 10, and disappointment drives escalating doses and more harm. Naturals in a public gym often look better than the enhanced guys.

## Technique critiques
- **Sam Sulek, Smith squat with 5 plates** — 9.5/10 set, but the load is needless connective-tissue risk; 365–405 would do the same job.
- **Barbell row** — decent, improvable: tighter core, less wobble, touch the floor and pause at the bottom, then raise the torso slightly on the way up to improve the peak-contraction angle (the movement is much harder at the bottom than the top). Lengthened partials at the end of the set are endorsed by both. Cheat-concentric plus a slow eccentric may work, but the stimulus-to-fatigue ratio is questionable — usually better to just do another clean set.
- **Chris Bumstead, incline dumbbell press with 140s** — excellent. Nitpicks: take the dumbbells to *outside* the chest rather than onto it for more stretch, and pause 1–2 s at the bottom. Jeff prefers a 1–2 s controlled eccentric rather than ultra-slow, partly because very slow negatives force a lighter load and once you're at 140s the heavier dumbbells become impractical to handle.
- **Decline dumbbell skull crushers** — Mike is unenthusiastic: at the deep stretch the lever arm isn't maximally loaded. A flat bench puts the upper arm parallel to the floor at the bottom, so force rises as you go deeper. For elbow comfort, try a thumbless grip and angle the dumbbells ~45°.
- **Top-squeeze pauses on rows** — on paper stretch beats squeeze, but the back is a web of muscles; a top pause with hard scapular retraction can be a legitimate way to target the mid-back retractors specifically. Going all-in on stretch to the point of negating the squeeze can cost mind-muscle connection.
- **Seated hamstring curl, leaning forward (Lean Beef Patty)** — both like it for the extra pre-stretch, but stability wins if leaning forward makes you unstable, and it depends heavily on the machine's chest pad. Test both on your machine.
- **"Twerker squats" (pulse in the hole, then a full rep)** — great for bodybuilding, more time in the stretched position for glutes and quads. For powerlifting, keep full-ROM reps for specificity; use these as assistance work if the bottom position is your sticking point.
- **Athlean-X "Omni crossover"** (one cable high, one low, simultaneously) — looks odd, nothing wrong on principle if you progress it. Jeff's preferred alternative is a crossover ladder: high cables set one, mid set two, low set three. Mike instead spends months biasing one chest region at a time.
- **Jay Cutler 2003 back workout** — outstanding control, near-identical reps, mini rest-pauses with a pause at the stretch. Nitpick: slightly slower eccentric. On the wide-grip pulldown with heavy lean-back and scapular retraction: this is really an upper-back/mid-back movement, and wide grip does not build a "wider" back. For lats, use a closer or neutral grip, drive the elbows down, stay upright at the top for a big lat stretch, then lean back only 10–15° to clear the face.
- **Greg Nuckols/Doucette-style leg press coaching (Eric Janicki cueing)** — good. Improvement: more knee *and* hip flexion, flaring the knees outside the ribcage toward the armpits/front delts to get deeper and add glute growth on top of quad growth. Low foot position is good for knee flexion and quad stimulus. Rising onto the toes costs neural drive and force production because instability makes the nervous system hold back — valid as a variant, but heels planted is better.

## On the "stop overcomplicating it" criticism
[18:27] Both largely agree with the critics. Adding a technique because you understand the reasoning behind it and slotting it into an already sound plan is good. Rewriting your program every two weeks for the influencer of the week is bad. Mike's specific irritation: novel exercises get labelled "science-based" when there is no science on them — there is not a single long-term study on back training, so everything said about back training is inference from biomechanics and anatomy.

## Strength, cold and calories
[20:28] On "get stronger and you'll get bigger": causality runs mostly the other way. Getting stronger without getting bigger just improves neural efficiency. Jay Cutler built his physique largely on sets of 10–15, rarely in the 5–10 range. But as you grow, the old load stops being stimulative, so you do have to add weight to keep sets under ~30 reps. Also: watching how varsity athletes lift is bad advice — technique and range of motion there are usually poor.

[22:30] Cold exposure ("11 minutes a week uncomfortably cold"): mechanism is a sympathetic spike with a parasympathetic rebound, and the "do hard things" argument. Mike's counter — hard training gives you all of that *plus* health, body composition and aesthetics. Jeff's numbers: an ice bath burns roughly 10 extra calories; walking in place beats it. New evidence favours hot exposure (hot bath) over cold after a workout for recovery ([Benoît 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38967392/)).

[24:00] On the "calorie myth": hunger signalling genuinely is the core driver of obesity and the reason people lose control of body weight — that part is correct. But calories are not a myth, they're the mechanism; hunger matters *because* it determines caloric balance. Keto and hunger management coexist with calorie balance. On eating breakfast cereal in a bodybuilding offseason or pre-contest: fine if macros and sodium are sorted. Restriction only becomes a problem at the level of real nutrient deficiency, and US cereals are heavily fortified anyway.

## Practical application
- Pick common, effective exercises and stop optimising selection. Spend the attention on effort, volume and range of motion instead.
- Don't max load, intensity and volume simultaneously. Pick two.
- Use lengthened partials at the end of sets; prefer an extra clean set over cheat-concentric reps.
- Pulldowns for lats: closer/neutral grip, upright at the top, elbows down, minimal lean-back.
- Leg press deeper by flaring knees outside the ribcage, heels planted.
- Test lean-forward vs upright on your specific hamstring curl machine.
- If you're clinically low on testosterone, see a doctor. If you're under 22–25, don't touch it.
- Skip the ice bath unless you enjoy it. Use hot exposure post-workout if you want a bath.

## Caveats & unknowns
Ronnie's actual recoverable volume is unknowable — the 25-set figure is an invented example. The very-high-volume findings come from short training blocks; long-term applicability is unclear. There is no study comparing strict to loose technique, and no long-term hypertrophy study on back training at all, so most exercise-selection claims are inference. Whether chronic cold exposure has real benefits when other variables are equalised is genuinely unsettled — Mike says he just hasn't seen convincing literature yet.
