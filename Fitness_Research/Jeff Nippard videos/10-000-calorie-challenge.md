# How Much Fat Can You Actually Gain in One Day? (10,000 Calorie Challenge)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "10,000 Calorie Challenge SCIENCE Explained | How Much Fat Gained in One Day?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FdaM3f_odqI)) · 2016-11-10 · 22 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Eating 10,000 kcal in a day on a ~3,000 kcal maintenance produced an estimated **0.5–0.6 lb of actual fat gain** — far less than the scale would show, but visually meaningful.
- Most of the surplus disappears into the thermic effect of food (~19% of the surplus with a mixed macro split) and into increased NEAT.
- **Macro composition matters acutely:** a pure-carb surplus stores far less fat than a pure-fat surplus over one day.
- You almost certainly absorb nearly all of it — absorption is not the limiting factor, appetite and gut tolerance are.
- None of the fat gain is permanent if you impose a deficit in the following days. Long-term accumulation comes from prolonged overeating, not a single day.

## Five factors determining how much fat you store from a day of overfeeding [02:11]
1. **Starting TDEE.** A lower expenditure puts you in a bigger net surplus for the same intake, so more gets stored.
2. **Starting body composition.** Lean people have fewer adipocytes; obese individuals can carry up to **four times as many fat cells**, so they arguably have more capacity to store triglyceride long-term.
3. **Glycogen depletion.** The more depleted you are, the less fat you gain, because incoming carbs are shunted into glycogen rather than fat.
4. **Macronutrient composition.** A **1995 paper by Horton et al.** put subjects in a 50% surplus from *either* pure carbs or pure fat. After **one day**, the carb group stored only ~**10%** of the excess as fat; the fat group stored ~**60%** ([Horton 1995](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/7598063)). The difference levelled out over the following two weeks, but acutely, high-carb overfeeding produces much less fat accrual. This fits the broader literature: de novo lipogenesis (converting carbs into fat) is not a major acute route in humans; instead, chronic carb excess suppresses fat oxidation, leaving dietary fat available for storage.
5. **NEAT response.** Non-exercise activity thermogenesis (fidgeting, walking more) varies up to **tenfold** between people. A **1999 paper by Levine et al.** overfed subjects **+1,000 kcal/day for 8 weeks** and found NEAT changes ranging from **−98 kcal to +692 kcal** — one subject burned off ~70% of the extra intake spontaneously ([Levine 1999](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/9880251)).

## The calculation, step by step [07:50]
- Intake 10,000 kcal − maintenance 3,000 kcal = **7,000 kcal surplus**.
- Assume 20% protein / 40% carb / 40% fat. Weighted average thermic effect of food ≈ **19%** → 1,300 kcal lost as heat. **5,700 kcal remain.**
- NEAT: rather than using Levine's high end, he assumes **+200 kcal burned per extra 1,000 kcal eaten** (conservative, because the Levine data was 8 weeks, not 12 hours; leptin, a key NEAT regulator, rises only ~40% in 24 hours versus ~300% over 5 weeks — [Kolaczynski 1996](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/8923877)). That is **1,400 kcal**. **4,300 kcal remain.**
- Assume ~50% of the remainder ends up as fat (protein and carbs contribute little; fat was ~40% of intake) → **2,150 kcal → ~239 g → ~0.5 lb of fat.**

**Cross-check using Horton et al. directly:** with a roughly even carb/fat surplus, split the difference between the 10% and 60% figures → ~**35%** of the excess stored as fat. 35% of 7,000 = 2,450 kcal → **272 g → ~0.6 lb of fat.**

Both methods converge on **half a pound of fat from a single 10,000 kcal day.**

## Absorption: do you actually take it all in? [19:58]
Yes, essentially. Absorption is limited by the transporters in the small intestine, and while each has a maximum rate (glucose uptake maxes at roughly **60 g/hour** — [Duchman 1997](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/9107630)), enormous meals slow digestion dramatically, so uptake spreads along the full length of the small intestine over more time. Expect the vast majority of the 10,000 kcal to be absorbed, with only a small amount passing through in stool. There is eventually a point where the body rejects further intake at one end or the other; he estimates that ceiling to be in the ballpark of ~10,000 kcal of food for most people.

## Practical application
- One extreme overeating day costs you roughly **half a pound of real fat**, not the 5–8 lb the scale shows the next morning (that is food mass, water and glycogen).
- If you overshoot on a holiday or a binge, run a modest deficit over the following days and the fat gain is fully reversible.
- If you are going to overshoot deliberately, **skew the excess toward carbohydrate rather than fat** — acutely it is stored as fat far less readily.
- Do not fear a single day. Fear a fortnight of them: sustained overfeeding is what produces persistent fat accumulation.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The whole thing is a back-of-envelope estimate using population averages for TEF, NEAT and partitioning; his own TDEE and NEAT response are assumed, not measured.
- The Levine NEAT data was collected over 8 weeks of chronic overfeeding; how much NEAT actually rises within a single 12-hour window is unknown, which is why the estimate is deliberately conservative.
- The Horton et al. design used *pure* carb or *pure* fat surpluses; a real-world mixed day is being interpolated, not measured.
- No body composition measurement was done — this is arithmetic, not a DEXA result.
