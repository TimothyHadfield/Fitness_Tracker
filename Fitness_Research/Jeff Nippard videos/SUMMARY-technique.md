# Technique

Thirty-three notes on how to execute the movements, organised by movement family rather than by
video: setup, the cues that matter, and the specific errors with their fixes. These span 2017–2022
and a few cues moved; where two notes disagree, the newer one is flagged in place.

---

## The general principles

**Technique exists to do two things: make the target muscle the limiting factor, and keep you
healthy enough to keep training.** Every error below is a failure of one or the other — load gets
shared out to muscles you weren't training, or a joint takes a position it can't tolerate for years.

**Going too heavy is the most repeated fault in the entire set.** It is named as *the* number one
error on the [Pendlay row](build-thick-back-perfect-rowing-technique.md),
[barbell curl](build-huge-biceps.md), [lateral raise](build-capped-shoulders.md),
[skull crusher](build-huge-triceps-optimal-training-technique.md) and
[leg extension](leg-extensions-perfect-technique.md), and it is the root cause of most of the rest:
knees drifting forward on the [RDL](romanian-deadlift-technique.md), shoulder movement on skull
crushers, lower back rounding on the [deadlift](4-most-common-deadlift-errors.md), excessive knee
bend on the [good morning](good-morning-exercise-build-glutes-hamstrings-squat.md). On the
[squat](squat-technique.md): prefer honestly lightening the bar for a few months to losing months
to an injury.

**Adding weight only counts as progressive overload when form is unchanged**
([rowing](build-thick-back-perfect-rowing-technique.md)). The failure mode is named *progressive
cheating* — a bit more hip drive on the curl each week, a bit less range on the skull crusher, while
tension on the target muscle stays flat ([curl](build-huge-biceps.md),
[triceps](build-huge-triceps-optimal-training-technique.md)). The
[overhead press](build-bigger-shoulders-perfect-training-technique.md) note puts it sharpest:
inconsistent range of motion turns progressive overload into "progressive decreases in range of
motion."

**Full ROM is the default, not an absolute.** Inadequate ROM is the top error on
[pull-ups](best-way-pull-ups-wide-back.md), the overhead press and the
[leg extension](leg-extensions-perfect-technique.md). But the [RDL](romanian-deadlift-technique.md)
note is blunt that lowering the plates to the floor produces lumbar rounding, not extra hip range;
a 2017 study in the [triceps note](build-huge-triceps-optimal-training-technique.md) found mid-range
work with **constant tension produced nearly twice the triceps hypertrophy** of full-range work that
lost tension at the top (possibly confounded by relative load); and the
[sumo note](sumo-deadlift-cheating.md) states the general principle — **hitting the hardest part of
the range matters more than maximising total range**. Which part you cut is the question.

**Control the eccentric.** About one second on the [squat](squat-technique.md), two on
[dips](dips-bigger-chest-shoulders.md) and the
[hip thrust](build-great-glutes-perfect-hip-thrust-technique.md). Treat the negative as a *failed
positive* — actively resisting, not dropping ([curl](build-huge-biceps.md),
[kickback](build-bigger-glutes-perfect-training-technique.md)). The deadlift is the exception: many
powerlifting coaches call it concentric-only because resisting the negative sharply raises the
recovery cost, and his compromise is a ~1-second descent only while learning or relearning the lift
([sumo](most-effective-way-deadlift-muscle-strength.md)).

**Controlled "cheating" is defensible in one situation: when the target muscle's strength curve,
not its fatigue, is ending the set.** The [dumbbell row](dumbbell-rows-build-thicker-back-proper-cheating.md)
is the worked case — near-zero moment arm at the bottom where you're strongest, peak tension at the
top where you're weakest, so you fail on the last few inches with the lats not actually fatigued.
**Moderate torso rotation** to generate speed at the bottom fixes that curve. More momentum on
close-grip cable rows, less on the [Pendlay row](build-thick-back-perfect-rowing-technique.md), none
on the chest-supported T-bar. On the [lat pulldown](build-v-tapered-back-lat-training-dos.md),
moderate concentric cheating is legitimate eccentric overload **provided you control the negative**.
Banned everywhere: leg drive, pushing off the planted arm, dropping the weight. Ultra-strict rowing
is itself an error — excessive rigidity cuts overloading potential.

**Mind-muscle connection: internal focus for isolation, external for heavy compounds.**
[The note](use-mind-muscle-connection-growth.md) rests on Schoenfeld 2018 — 27 untrained men,
8 weeks, 4×8–12 on barbell curl and leg extension. The internal group ("squeeze the muscle") got
**over 5% more biceps growth**; **no difference in quad growth**, no strength difference. So:
external cueing on squats, deadlifts and presses, especially under 8 reps; internal on curls, flyes,
leg extensions and leg curls, and above ~8 reps. Two named exceptions — **hip thrusts and RDLs**,
where pure external focus lets the lower back and quads crowd out the glutes and hamstrings. The
[pull-up note](best-way-pull-ups-wide-back.md) applies the split within one exercise: external on
heavy low-rep sets, internal on higher-rep ones.

**When load stops being the progression channel, technique becomes it.** The
[kickback](build-bigger-glutes-perfect-training-technique.md),
[lateral raise](build-capped-shoulders.md) and [leg extension](leg-extensions-perfect-technique.md)
notes give the same three replacements: better mind-muscle connection, better technique, better pump
— add a rep whenever form allows, add weight only after topping the rep range cleanly.

## Warm-up and mobility

Two notes: [the 2017 version](warm-up-mobility.md) and [the 2019 rebuild](warm-up-and-mobility.md).
Same architecture, different details; 2019 is current.

**What matters is raising core body temperature, not elaborate mobility work.** The Fradkin review
of five studies found three showed reduced injury risk — and the two nulls were both
stretching-weighted rather than temperature-raising. The performance case is stronger: a
meta-analysis of **32 studies** found warming up improved **79%** of criteria, with **17%** hurt,
usually by warm-ups too long, too hard, or mismatched to the activity.

**Phase 1 — general. 5–10 minutes** of easy cardio to a light sweat, **55–65% of max HR**
(~**100–120 bpm**). A 2013 study found 15 minutes beat 5 for leg press 1RM by only **3–4%**.
Time-pressed: **20–30 s assertive / ~1 min easy** intervals until you sweat, then stop.

**Phase 2 — specific. 3–4 minutes** lower body, less upper. Dynamic, not long static holds — Behm's
review found static stretching produced numerous performance impairments while dynamic had no effect
or helped. Two qualifiers from the older note survive: holds **under 30 seconds** per muscle don't
impair anything, and static *first* followed immediately by dynamic appears to cancel the penalty.

- **Foam rolling, 2–3 min:** ~15–20 s on quads; upper and lower back **with the abs flexed** so the
  lower back doesn't hyperextend over the roller; inner thighs and adductors; cross the leg over the
  knee to stretch glutes and piriformis before rolling them. **20–30 s** per spot is enough;
  lacrosse ball for very tight hamstrings or glutes. He calls rolling overhyped but downside-free.
- **Lower body dynamic:** a few walking lunges; **10–12** leg swings front-to-back; **10–12**
  side-to-side; **10–12** step-throughs with a 1–2 s pause, knee driven forward. Tight days:
  Scorpion stretch, knee-drive rotating squat.
- **Upper body:** **10–12** small-to-big arm circles, **10–12** arm swings, **~15** band pull-aparts
  (his go-to against bench-related shoulder pain), banded external rotations and face pulls. Wall
  slides and overhead dumbbell side bends if shoulder mobility lags.
- **Lift-specific:** sumo days — squat-and-reach held **10–15 s**, plus Cossack squats. Tight ankles
  limiting squat depth — slow eccentric calf raises. Before benching — roll the thoracic spine and
  hip flexors, which makes the arch easier to set and lets you place the feet further back without
  risking a hip flexor strain.

**Phase 3 — progressive pyramid**, on the day's main heavy compound only. Squat example to 330 lb
(77.5% 1RM): empty bar ×10–12, 135×4–5, 225×3–4, 275×1–2. Bench example: bar ×10, 30%×5, 50%×4,
60%×3, 70%×2, 75%×1–2. Three to four building sets covers the 4–10 rep range; drop reps as weight
rises. Total **10–15 min upper body**, more for lower. The
[405 lb bench note](coached-client-405-lb-bench.md) adds the rule that makes it work: **treat every
warm-up rep exactly like a working rep** — same setup, same technique, same cues.

## The squat

**Setup** ([note](squat-technique.md)). Rack at **armpit height**. Hands as close to the shoulders
as comfortable — a closer grip recruits the upper back for a stabler base; the hands only stabilise,
so mash the bar between palms and upper back rather than gripping it. **Unrack with the hips, not
the knees** (squatting it out adds quad fatigue and can contribute to knee pain). Walk out in
**three steps**: small with the non-dominant leg, medium with the other, mini to even up. Stance
**wider than shoulder width, toes flared 15–30 degrees**.

**Bar position.** The 2019 note calls the high/low-bar difference over-exaggerated and prefers high
bar (protects the lower back, more quad dominant; low bar more hip dominant). The 2021
[blow up your squat](fastest-way-blow-up-squat.md) note is newer and pushes the other way: only
about a quarter of his audience squats low bar despite most people being **5–10% stronger** with it
(Glassbrook), with an expertise effect, so expect an adjustment period. His reconciliation: run
both — low bar for heavy top sets, high bar for paused technique and hypertrophy work.

**Execution.** Even pressure through heel and big toe, **weight over mid-foot** — and **do not use
the "weight on your heels" cue**; it throws balance off and makes the hips shoot up early. Head
slightly extended, eyes straight ahead, elbows pointing slightly back rather than directly under
the bar (elbow pain). Big diaphragmatic breath pushed out against the belt in a full **360 degrees**,
not just inflating the gut forward. Break hips and knees together (high bar), knees forward and
slightly out tracking the toes, hips straight down. **~1-second descent**, driving the upper back
into the bar. **No pause in the hole** unless that's the drill — reverse off the stretch reflex and
explode as if jumping through the ceiling. He dislikes "chest up" (it makes some lifters lean back)
and prefers driving the back into the bar with the head slightly up. Hold the breath to about
two-thirds up, exhale, re-brace.

**Depth.** Parallel is the minimum for size and general strength; hip crease below knee for
regulation. The 2021 note says test both strategies — cut to the minimum your goal requires (less
ROM, more weight), or go as deep as you comfortably can for the bounce (his own camp). Heuristic:
**good ankle mobility → deeper and use the bounce; poor ankle mobility → cut to minimum depth.**

**Knees over toes** ([note](knees-go-over-toes.md)). Busted. Fry 2003 compared parallel squats with
and without a board blocking forward knee travel: unrestricted **knee torque 150 Nm / hip torque
28 Nm**; restricted **117 Nm / 302 Nm**. Blocking the knees cuts knee torque modestly and
**multiplies hip torque more than tenfold**. The myth traces to a 1972 study of 12 subjects with no
depth control and no described technique. Judge a squat on three things instead: **heels planted,
bar over mid-foot, knees tracking the same direction as the toes**. Forward lean is driven by femur
length relative to tibia — skeletal, not a fault. **Lunges are the exception**: you can stay upright
there, so keeping the knee back costs no hip or low-back penalty.

**Errors.** **Knee valgus**, usually poor hip external rotation — fix with adequate toe flare,
actively driving the knees out toward the toes, a mini band or hip circle, and a heeled shoe (foot
pronation is a known contributor). **Too much weight plus not enough depth**, which arrive together
— the squat starts with the eccentric, so it's easy to cheat by overloading and going halfway.
**Butt wink**: if excessive, cut depth slightly or change stance width either way; mild and painless
is basically inevitable and not a concern.

**Adding weight fast** ([note](fastest-way-blow-up-squat.md)). **Heavy walkouts at 95–105% of 1RM**
— walk out, brace, walk back in without squatting, safeties high — **once or twice a month** before
squat work. **Pin squats** to start with the concentric if descending under load is what scares you;
pins at **half-squat depth**, lowered over time, or **65–75% of 1RM for 4–6 reps** at parallel.
Stance **2 inches wider per side** with more toe flare (Nuckols: hits the stretch reflex without
excess depth, adds adductors, eases the back). Gear: belt **~5%**, knee sleeves **2–5%**, hard-soled
shoe over a running shoe.

**Front squat** ([note](front-squat-build-bigger-quads-stronger-squat.md)). The bar in front forces
an upright torso — slightly more quad, dramatically more upper back. You usually fail because traps
and rhomboids can't hold the spine extended, which makes it the best accessory for back squat
strength. **6–8 reps.** Olympic grip first: bar just behind the shoulders **touching the front of
the neck** (a slight choke feel is about right), resting on the first two or three fingers, hands
just outside the shoulders, elbows up until **the humerus is parallel to the floor**. Straps are a
temporary workaround; cross grip a distant third. **Sit straight down, not back** — sitting back
drops the elbows and destroys balance. Expect more forward knee travel than in a back squat. Errors:
**elbows dropping** (elbows up before you unrack; forward or slightly upward gaze) and **heels
lifting** (ankle mobility work, more toe flare, a heel-elevated shoe — but don't over-correct into
sitting back on the heels).

## The bench press

**Setup** ([note](bench-press-technique.md)). Head back behind the bar, feet up on the bench.
Squeeze the shoulder blades together and hold that retraction for the whole setup and lift. Build
the arch: grab the bar, lift the hips high while pulling the shoulder blades **down toward your
butt**, push the upper body forward with the feet in place; eyes end up directly under the bar. Then
set the legs one foot at a time, as far back as comfortable, in close to the bench, heels planted
(you may need the toes turned out). Drop the hips. Final check: crank the elbows forward to confirm
the scapulae are retracted and depressed — "screwing my back into position." **Four points of
contact — head, upper back, glutes, feet — throughout.** Deep breath into the gut, chest puffed,
grip the bar as hard as you can ("bend the bar," "rip it in half") to switch on the upper back.

**Grip width.** The 2018 note gives the ceiling: about **1.5× biacromial (shoulder) distance**
reduces injury risk without seriously compromising max strength, wrist stacked roughly over the
elbow at the bottom. Wider biases the sternal pec and front delts, closer the triceps and upper
chest. The newer 2019 [bigger bench](fastest-way-blow-up-bench-press-sample.md) note is more
aggressive: **widen about one finger-width per workout** toward max-legal (index fingers on the
rings), because wider means shorter ROM, an easier midpoint and lockout, and a tighter upper back.
Read the 1.5× as the injury-risk consideration and the finger-width creep as the strength method;
shoulder comfort is the real limit. Wrist directly below the knuckles — cue "punch the ceiling."

**Bar path.** Down and slightly forward to just below nipple level at the bottom of the sternum,
then **back toward your face and up** — not straight up. Lowering down and forward creates a moment
arm between bar and shoulder; pressing straight up holds it there, forcing the front delt into a
partial front raise at the hardest point. Shoving the bar back kills it fast. McLaughlin's data:
most benchers lower correctly, but **only elite lifters drove the bar back then up** — novices
pressed up first, then back. Film from the side.

**Elbows.** About **45 degrees** from the torso viewed from above (unanimous among the best
powerlifting coaches, he says), stacked under the bar rather than far in front. The
[405 lb note](coached-client-405-lb-bench.md) turns this into a tempo cue: **tuck on the negative,
touch at the bottom of the sternum, flare as you press** — rejecting both the wide-flared
near-guillotine (fine for hypertrophy, cranky shoulders for some) and the extreme tuck (only optimal
in a bench shirt). **Leg drive:** heels into the floor, think about pushing the floor away.

**Sticking point.** Usually the first **20–40%** of the lift, roughly **3–4 inches** off the chest,
lower in advanced lifters. Press up and back, flare a bit more there to bring the pecs in, come off
the chest at maximum speed — though his own view is the real fix is stronger pecs and triceps.

**Errors:** over- or under-tucking (the "tuck your elbows" cue came from *equipped* lifting);
benching flat-backed (some arch is safer for spine and shoulder and lets you move more load — and
the ROM you "lose" is exaggerated, since bar travel isn't joint ROM); no leg drive. **Bodybuilding
variant:** less arch, slightly narrower grip, feet further forward, but keep the scapular set.

**Gear and programming** ([405 lb note](coached-client-405-lb-bench.md)): heeled shoe for planted
heels and leg drive; belt to make the Valsalva brace a rigid column; wrist wraps so the bar stays
over the wrist joint instead of settling back into the upper hand; chalk, potentially the biggest of
the four on a wide grip. None is worth 30 lb alone. **Bench 3×/week** as daily undulating
periodization — 8s hypertrophy, speed work, 5s strength — plus **one heavy top set of 2–3 reps at
~90% 1RM once per week**, before your lightest day.

**Dips** ([note](dips-bigger-chest-shoulders.md)), the bench-adjacent press. **6–12 reps**, not a
bodyweight finisher; add load once you hit **12 clean bodyweight reps** (30+ rep sets are fine as a
finisher). Grip just outside shoulder width, **scapulae retracted and slightly depressed**, **torso
leaning slightly forward**, knees pinched and hips extended, **~2-second** descent to roughly
**90 degrees at the elbow** (upper arm parallel to the floor for most healthy shoulders), no bottom
pause. Both errors cause shoulder pain: **anterior scapular tilting** — the most common, and the
reason dips got their reputation, since people carry the retract-and-depress cue to bench but not
here — and **staying too upright**, which adds shoulder stress and removes pec contribution. Being
less technical than bench or overhead press, dips can be pushed closer to failure.

## The deadlift

**Conventional setup** ([note](deadlift-technique.md)). Shins **half an inch behind the bar**, bar
over the tongue of the shoe. Stance **slightly narrower than shoulder width**, **toes straight
ahead** (no flare, unlike sumo). Get to the bar by **setting the hips back as in a stiff-leg
deadlift**; once they can't go further, let the knees drift forward until the shins lightly touch
the bar without knocking it. Arms hang straight down at shoulder width, hands just outside the
shins. Dig the bar into the base of the hand and drive the pinky in to reduce grip fatigue. **Pack
the lats** by thinking about pulling the bar in close. Lower back to neutral, hamstrings stretched,
held for the whole lift; some thoracic rounding is more acceptable, but don't let it round *further*
under the pull. Check: **a straight line through scapula, middle of the bar, middle of the foot**.

**Grip.** Alternate pulls the most but creates imbalances unless you switch which hand supinates
(his choice). Double overhand is safest but grip limits you. Hook grip works with a painful
adjustment period. Straps are fine, but **do at least one heavy set per session without them**.

**The pull.** **Pull the slack out** — light upward tension until the bar rises a millimetre and
comes softly against the inside of the plate holes, before the plates move. Never grip and rip: you
collide with the weight before it moves, waste energy and lose position. He calls this the step most
inexperienced lifters miss. Conventional is **explosive off the floor** (sumo pullers should be
patient there instead) because lockout is the hard part. Lead with the chest, drive the **hips
forward** — thinking about driving the feet down turns it into a squat and shoots the hips up. Hold
**three isometric contractions** throughout: lumbar spine, scapulae, and shoulders keeping the bar
tight to the legs. Bar travels straight up over mid-foot, dragging the shins.

**Lockout.** Knees straight, glutes squeezed, chest lifted, shoulders slightly back. **No** backward
lean, **no** lumbar hyperextension, **no** scapular squeeze, **no** shrug.

**Descent.** **Hips back first with straight knees**, stiff-leg style; only once the bar clears the
knees do they bend and the bar drops straight down the shins. Never push the bar out in front to
clear the knees. Full dead stop between reps — no bouncing — then reset.

**The four most common errors** ([note](4-most-common-deadlift-errors.md)).

1. **Lower back rounding.** Usually **weak glutes**, not a weak lower back — the back rounds to
   bring the hips closer to the bar and cut the hip torque the glutes must overcome. Contreras'
   guidance: if you round, keep it upper back, minimise lumbar rounding under heavy load. Fixes:
   drop the weight; hamstring flexibility (dynamic stretching and rolling before, static after, RDLs
   for loaded length) if you can't hold an anterior tilt — "pop the butt out like a bikini pose" —
   and still reach the bar; **hip thrusts and cable pull-throughs** for the glutes; and **paused
   deadlifts**, **1–2 s** at the point position breaks, at **50–60% of 1RM for 4–6 reps**.
2. **Hips shooting up early** — purely a setup fault. Push the hips **back**, not down; knees
   forward until the shins touch; hold the **higher hip position**; on the pull the hips drive
   forward as the chest rises rather than rising first.
3. **Joints not stacked.** From the side, both stances: heels under knees, **shins as close to
   vertical as possible** — adjust hip height and stance width, and use a flat shoe or lift barefoot.
   Sumo from the front: **a straight vertical line from knees to heels**; heels outside the knees
   means the stance is too wide or the knees are caving.
4. **Excessive lockout.** Stand tall, squeeze the glutes, lift the chest. That's it.

**Sumo** ([note](most-effective-way-deadlift-muscle-strength.md)). Feet as wide laterally as
possible while keeping heels in line with knees, toes flared **30–40 degrees** toward the front end
of the plates. Hands inside the legs, arms hanging at roughly shoulder width (some go slightly wider
so the hands clear the quads at lockout). If full sumo isn't accessible, start conventional, move to
semi-sumo, widen over several weeks while stretching and rolling the inner thighs and groin.
Everything else is the same. Train either style **1–2× per week maximum**, **5–10 reps for
hypertrophy, 1–5 for strength**; the recovery cost is the highest of any lift.

**Is sumo cheating?** ([note](sumo-deadlift-cheating.md)) No. It's legal in every federation, and if
it were a genuine shortcut the elite would all use it — most 93 kg IPF world-championship men pull
**conventional**, whereas **100%** of them arch on bench, which *is* a universal advantage. Adoption
tracks size: roughly **75% sumo** in the second-lightest men's class, flipping to about **25% sumo /
75% conventional** in the superheavyweights. ROM reduction is only **~7–15%** (his measurements:
conventional 19 in vs sumo 16 in; a taller lifter 22.5 vs 21), and it removes neither sticking
point. Escamilla's 3D analysis found **no significant difference in hip extension demands at any
point**, and EMG found **no difference in glute or hamstring activation**. The only real differences:
**sumo taxes the quads more, conventional the spinal erectors more** — which is why conventional
*feels* harder. Heuristic: under **~180 lb / 81 kg male** or **~140 lb / 65 kg female**, sumo may
suit you. Give each style **1–2 months**.

**RDL** ([note](romanian-deadlift-technique.md)). Bar racked **just below deadlift lockout height**,
grip shoulder width just outside the thighs, three-step walkout like a squat, stance shoulder width
with **~10 degrees of toe flare**. Brace by lifting the chest and stomach. **Push the hips straight
back with the shins completely vertical** — picture a wall directly in front of the knees; bar over
mid-foot, tight to the legs. **Stop when the hips can't go further back without the lower back
rounding or the torso pitching forward** — for most people **just below the knee to mid-shin**, and
the plates should not touch the floor. Reverse by driving the hips forward and lifting the chest.
For glute emphasis, squeeze hard at the top into posterior pelvic tilt; he uses it for hamstrings
and skips that. He says people botch this lift more than any other in the series. Three errors:
**over-extending the range** (deeper gives lumbar rounding, not hip range), **knees travelling
forward** (too much load — it becomes a top-half conventional deadlift), and **dropping the hips
down instead of back**, which cascades into the bar drifting forward and losing spinal position.
The [leg day note](coach-leg-day.md) adds the dumbbell version's signature error — **pushing the
dumbbells out in front to clear the knees**, proof the hips never went back; keep them travelling
**straight up and down, close to the body**. It also offers a foot experiment: **toes dorsiflexed is
usually stronger, toes pointed down usually gives a better hamstring feel** — start toes-down, switch
to toes-back near failure to extend the set.

**Good morning** ([note](good-morning-exercise-build-glutes-hamstrings-squat.md)). Set up exactly
like a **low-bar squat** — the bar on the rear-delt shelf is what gives squat carryover an RDL
doesn't have. Grip slightly wider than shoulder width, **hands above the bar** with the weight fully
on the back (letting it sit in the hands strains wrists and elbows). Stance wider than shoulder
width, **10–15 degrees of toe flare**, even pressure across the whole foot. Break at the **hips
first**, near-vertical shins, only a slight knee bend; unlike the squat, **the bar does not stay
over mid-foot** — it ends out in front of the toes. Stop just before the spine would round. Load
**6–12 reps**, and even when advanced stay **under 40–50% of squat max** — heavier turns it into a
"squat morning." Errors: **too much knee bend** (the main fault, it strips the hamstrings out),
**lack of upper-body tightness** (a sliding bar means you aren't mashing it into the shelf), and
**not knowing where to stop** — set the safety pins and start from the bottom to cap the range while
you learn it.

## Pulling technique

**Pull-ups** ([note](best-way-pull-ups-wide-back.md)). **Pronated double overhand at ~1.5× shoulder
width**; wider is counterproductive (shorter ROM, harder loading, wrist pain). Alternative check:
make a 90-degree angle between upper arm and forearm, raise the arm straight up, grip where the
hands land. A 2016 Massey study found **no significant activation difference between four grips**
except the pronated wide grip being slightly better for the **mid traps** — so **consistency beats
variety: keep one grip for at least 8 weeks** and progress by reps or load first. **Don't jump to
the bar**; use a riser **1–2 feet behind** it so your feet don't catch. The rep: near dead hang,
**initiate by depressing the shoulder blades** ("pull your elbows into your back pocket"), raise the
chest slightly (a puff, not an inverted row), keep pulling the elbows **down and in** until they're
as close to your sides as possible — chin over bar minimum, which for most means the **upper chest
touches**. **Hips extended, glutes squeezed** to prevent kipping. Controlled negative, feeling the
lat stretch; cue "pull the bar apart" both directions. **5–10 reps**, weighted once 10 clean
bodyweight reps are banked; elite standard is bodyweight plus **100+ lb**. Errors: inadequate ROM at
both ends, and loss of full-body rigidity as reps get hard. Doma 2013 found **equal lat activation
between pulldown and pull-up but more biceps and spinal erector activation on the pull-up**.

**Lat pulldown** ([note](build-v-tapered-back-lat-training-dos.md)). A different job: pull-ups are
the heavy overload movement at **5–10 reps**, pulldowns the feel-and-contract movement at **8–12**.
**Adjust the knee pads first** — if you're on your toes to brace, your hips lift every rep; stack
10s or 25s under your feet if needed. Grip **double overhand ~1.5× shoulder width** (thumbless helps
many people feel the lats, with no safety risk here). **Lift the chest into thoracic extension and
depress the scapulae before every rep.** Pull the elbows down and in at only **10–15 degrees viewed
from above** — forward at 45 degrees converts it into shoulder extension. Bar to **light contact
with the top of the sternum**. Resist the negative. Moderate lean-back is fine provided **the cable
still travels in a roughly straight line from the side**. On the supinated version grip **closer,
about 1× shoulder width**, and still drive the elbows down like a pullover rather than pulling with
the biceps. If you can't do a bodyweight pull-up, **assisted pull-ups beat pulldowns** for building
pull-up strength.

**Pendlay row** ([note](build-thick-back-perfect-rowing-technique.md)). **8–12 reps**, with just
under **1× bodyweight** as a high-end standard for that zone. Set up like a conventional deadlift:
shoulder-width stance, hips back, shins vertical, bar over **mid-foot to toes**. **Double overhand
slightly wider than shoulder width** — pick the width where upper and lower arm make a **90-degree
angle at the top of the pull**. **Anteriorly rotate the pelvis** (bikini-pose cue), because a
totally flat lower back is actually slightly flexed. Can't reach the floor without rounding? Elevate
the bar on bumper plates or risers. Start each rep with the scapulae **slightly protracted**, then
pull the blades together and drive the elbows up and back at **45–65 degrees** from the torso (more
flare = more traps, more tuck = more lats). Bar touches the **lower sternum, around nipple level**.
Chest roughly parallel to the floor — **if you can read the lettering on your shirt at the top of
the rep, your chest is rising too much.** Dead-stop and controlled touch-and-go both work. Reference
point: Mike Israetel, ~250 lb with outstanding lats, uses **235 lb** with identical-looking reps.
**Helms row** substitute if you can't stop cheating: upper chest on an incline bench, dumbbells,
**12–15 reps**, more lat-dominant — but you lose the isometric erector work, so add direct erector
work if it fully replaces the Pendlay.

**Dumbbell row** ([note](dumbbell-rows-build-thicker-back-proper-cheating.md)). **8–12 per side**,
starting with the **non-dominant side**. Torso **0–20 degrees above parallel** to the floor —
upright allows heavier loads but badly cuts lat range. Let the scapula **protract** at the bottom
and the dumbbell drift slightly forward rather than stopping under the shoulder, then drag it back
in an **arc toward the hips**, elbow close to the torso. Moderate torso rotation to get through the
top; no leg drive, no pushing off the planted arm. If grip or biceps fail first, put the pinky toward
the back end of the handle or use straps.

## Isolation technique

**Cable fly** ([note](best-way-isolate-chest-growth.md)). Cables beat dumbbells and the pec deck as
the only version letting you add both **shoulder flexion** (upper pec) and **internal rotation** (a
lesser-known pec function). **Set cables low** for upper chest, arms out at **15–30 degrees**, step
forward one or two steps into a **staggered stance** until you feel a slight stretch. **Retract and
depress the scapulae** exactly as when benching, then **externally rotate** the shoulders for the
most lengthened position. Draw the cables up and across thinking about **pulling the insides of your
elbows toward each other**, internally rotating as you go, finishing with **thumbs facing one
another**. **15–20 reps.** Dominant error: **not controlling the movement** — bending and extending
the elbows to muscle the weight instead of squeezing the pecs. The arms needn't be locked straight,
but **the elbow angle should be identical top to bottom**. Second: losing scapular retraction, which
weakens pec recruitment and can cause shoulder pain.

**Lateral raise** ([note](build-capped-shoulders.md)). Possibly the most important isolation
exercise for physique, since pressing barely touches the side delt. **10–20 reps, sometimes up to
30**; most people can't hold form past **15–30 lb dumbbells**, and past that progress comes from
technique, connection and pump. Retract the scapulae, **initiate by leaning out and up with the
elbow** rather than lifting the dumbbell, raise in the **scapular plane — about 15–30 degrees
forward** — and **stop when the elbow reaches shoulder height**. Cue: **"sweep the weight out," not
"lift the weight up"**, which recruits the side delts over the traps. Index finger against the inside
head of the dumbbell. The pinky-up internal rotation cue past the first half of the concentric may
shift emphasis onto the side delt, but **research suggests it may raise impingement risk** — if you
use it, keep the blades retracted, stay in the scapular plane, end at shoulder height. Nuckols'
suggestion: split reps across internally rotated, neutral and externally rotated positions. Errors:
**going too heavy** (fatigue should show as an inability to raise the arms as high, not as body
English) and **turning it into a front raise**.

**Upright row** ([note](fix-upright-row-avoid-shoulder-pain-maximize.md)). Possibly the only
multi-joint compound that heavily targets the **lateral** delt. A 2011 NSCA report noted **36% of
weight training injuries occur at the shoulder complex** and flagged this lift for **subacromial
impingement** — but concluded it can be safe with three precautions: **keep the bar close to the
body**, **pull through the elbows out and up rather than straight up**, and **stop below shoulder
height** (most studies place impingement between **70 and 120 degrees** of elevation, so stay just
below **90**). Grip **1 to 1.5× shoulder width** (McAllister 2013: wide beat shoulder-width and
close for lateral delt EMG). **8–12 reps** — heading toward 8 or lower invites lower-back and hip
cheating. His favourite variation is the **standing rope upright row**, where you can cue **pulling
the rope apart** on the concentric.

**Barbell curl** ([note](build-huge-biceps.md)). **8–20 reps**, light to moderate. Grip roughly
shoulder width and **loosely** — opposite of the bench cue — to cut forearm elbow-flexor
involvement. Before rep one, flex the shoulders about **10 degrees** and **posteriorly tilt the
scapulae** (the biceps short head attaches on the front of the scapula). **Press through the pinky
and ring fingers** to force active supination, the biceps' unique job since the brachialis can't
supinate. **Curl out in an arc, not straight up**: the arc increases the moment arm between elbow
and bar and generates far more torque at the same load. Neutral wrist, knees and hips motionless,
reset each rep at the bottom. **Up to 10–15 degrees of shoulder flexion to initiate is fine**; once
hips, knees and ankles join in it's a different exercise. Second error: **no active supination** —
underhand grip but forgetting the pinky drive, so the forearms do the work. For the long head, use
the upper arm **behind** the body: incline dumbbell curls, behind-the-body cable curls.

**Skull crusher** ([note](build-huge-triceps-optimal-training-technique.md)). **8–20 reps**,
moderate weight to spare the elbow soft tissues; **EZ bar preferred** for the wrists. Take the bar
overhead off the top of the bench using the lats — if you can't, it's too heavy — and shift back so
the head is slightly off the end. Retract and depress the scapulae. **Lower back in an arc past the
forehead, not straight down to it** — more elbow torque, more triceps overload, less elbow pain.
Start each rep with the bar slightly back and **stop just short of full lockout** to hold constant
tension. Depth minimum: **forearm contacting the biceps**. Movement must be **pure elbow extension**
— shoulder movement brings in lats and pecs. The **floor** variation enforces an identical ROM every
rep, a hard checkpoint against progressive cheating. Cover the triceps with **three arm positions**:
overhead (skull crusher, overhead cable extension), at the sides (pressdown), behind the torso
(kickback).

**Leg extension** ([note](leg-extensions-perfect-technique.md)). **12–20 reps.** Three cues: **set
the seat back so the hips sit at 45–60 degrees of flexion**, not 90 — at 90 the rectus femoris is
already shortened at the hip and can't contract forcefully at the knee; **pull your butt down into
the seat** (a butt that pops up means you're cutting the bottom of the range); **toes straight ahead
or slightly in**, since two independent EMG studies found toes-in produced more vastus lateralis
activation with no meaningful effect on the other heads. Errors: **half reps** (too much weight, or
pins set too high) and **not trying hard enough** — the lift is misleading for RPE, since the quads
burn ferociously with 5–6 reps still in the tank. Push to within **1–2 reps of failure**. Not
dangerous for healthy knees, per Schoenfeld; pair with hamstring isolation to balance the knee.

**Hip thrust** ([note](build-great-glutes-perfect-hip-thrust-technique.md)). **8–15 reps for
hypertrophy, 4–8 for strength.** Bench matched to **tibia height** and braced so it can't slide, pad
round the bar, bar centred on the hips, upper back against the bench. Stance slightly wider than
shoulder width, **~15 degrees of flare**, feet set while warming up so that **tibia and femur form a
90-degree angle at the top of each rep**. Unlike the squat, **don't think about driving the feet
into the ground** — squeeze the glutes and fully extend the hips. **Gaze straight ahead, not up**;
chin and ribcage tucked down. At the top, squeeze the glutes as if holding a coin between the cheeks
and **posteriorly rotate the pelvis by contracting the abs**. Lower over about **2 seconds**. Two
errors: **failing to fully lock out** — the most common, usually because it burns, and **the hips
must finish above the knees** — and **extending the lower back instead of the glutes**. Self-test:
arch your lower back and squeeze the glutes hard, then flatten it with the abs and squeeze again;
the contraction is noticeably stronger with a flat lower back. Optional **1–3 second top pause** as a
hypertrophy intensity technique. Feeling it in the quads? Shift the feet further forward.

**Cable glute kickback** ([note](build-bigger-glutes-perfect-training-technique.md)). **12–20 reps.**
Velcro ankle strap (other attachments cause pain, and pain inhibits muscle excitation), leg lined up
with the cable, torso leaned forward **45–60 degrees** — standing fully upright loads the glutes only
at the bottom of the range. Brace against the frame, knees slightly bent. **Think "swing back and up
in an arc," not "kick"** — kicking recruits the quads via knee extension. Cue: pull the lower glute
toward the upper glute, hamstrings soft. Keep rep speed identical from rep 1 to rep 15. Errors:
kicking too much, and using torso momentum as a counterbalance. Also usable as a pre-activation
drill before heavy hip thrusts.

**Overhead barbell press** ([note](build-bigger-shoulders-perfect-training-technique.md)). **5–10
reps** barbell, **8–15** dumbbell. Bar at **armpit height**, grip slightly wider than shoulder width,
two to three steps back, feet shoulder width with **~15 degrees of flare**, elbows externally rotated
to about **45 degrees**. **Flex the glutes** — loose glutes push the hips back behind the bar, cut
stability and strain the lower back. Press in a **straight vertical line**: tilt the head back to
clear the bar, push it forward once the bar passes. **Don't shrug** to initiate off the chest — it
helps short term but weakens bottom-end pressing and caps max strength. At lockout, a straight line
through **barbell → shoulders → hips → mid-foot**. On the eccentric, elbows down and out at
**45 degrees**, and let the bar come to a **full dead stop** on the chest before the next rep.
Errors: partial ROM (the most common), touch-and-go reps, loose glutes. It's front-delt dominant — a
2013 comparison found **dumbbell presses beat barbell for side delts, and standing beat seated in
both cases** — so add a standing dumbbell press and lateral raises if side delts are the priority.

## Safety and error correction

**Left/right imbalances** ([note](fix-muscle-imbalances-asymmetries-4-tips.md)). Reality check
first: most imbalances are far less visible than the person thinks, and **muscle shape and insertion
points cannot be changed** — only strength and size differences are in your control. His view is
that left-right discrepancies are largely **neural**, so the fix starts with the mind-muscle
connection on the weak side.

1. **Cue the weak side during the set** — literally "left side, left side" each rep as you fatigue.
2. **Pre-activate the weak side only** before the main lift: single-arm lat pull-in for lats, a
   **10-second isometric flex** for biceps, a unilateral cable fly for pecs. (Support exists for
   glute isolation carrying over to squats; extending it elsewhere is his own reasoning.)
3. **Don't train only bilaterally.** A pure barbell program has no mechanism to correct a
   side-to-side difference and entrenches it. For obvious asymmetries, progress **unilateral →
   iso-lateral → bilateral**.
4. **Film your sets.** Mirrors give one angle and you shouldn't be craning your neck anyway. The
   fault is either a mobility restriction or an ingrained motor pattern; the latter needs the ego
   checked, the weight stripped right back, and **light loads with slow eccentrics** until you can
   control limb position on the way down.

Bonus: **1–3 extra sets per week for the weak side only**, at the end of the session or on a
separate day. Don't exceed that — you can overshoot and reverse the imbalance.

**Crunches and spinal flexion** ([note](footnote-crunches-dangerous.md)). The anti-crunch case rests
almost entirely on **cadaveric pig-spine** studies: no fluid flow, smaller flexion range than human
spines, all musculature stripped, and thousands to tens of thousands of flexion cycles in one bout.
Schoenfeld and Contreras' review rejected the extrapolation and pointed to evidence that discs
**adapt and strengthen** to progressive flexion and that genetics drive most spinal degeneration.
Guardrails if you want them: **at most 60 total spinal-flexion reps per workout**, **at least 48
hours** between crunching sessions, and **1–2 hours after waking** before flexion-based ab work,
since discs become more elastic as the day progresses.

**Impingement.** Two places it comes up — the
[upright row](fix-upright-row-avoid-shoulder-pain-maximize.md) (stop below shoulder height, elbows
out and up, bar close) and the pinky-up cue on the [lateral raise](build-capped-shoulders.md). Both
reduce to the same mechanism: **raising the arm above shoulder height in an internally rotated
position** reliably jams the subacromial space. The [dip](dips-bigger-chest-shoulders.md) note's
anterior scapular tilt is the same category of problem at the other end of the range.

**A lower body day, coached live** ([note](coach-leg-day.md)). Leg press **8–10 reps**, depth to
parallel, knees tracking out — load found by deliberately starting too light and adding plates
rather than guessing. Walking lunge: **step length sets the bias** — short step quad-dominant, longer
step far more glute and hamstring; **drive the front heel through the floor and treat the back leg as
if it isn't there**; place the foot slightly to the side, not directly in front, for a wider base.
Calf raises: **pause at the bottom of every rep** so the Achilles' stored elastic energy doesn't do
work the calves should be doing, and **train barefoot** for range and a solid base. He flags that
this intensity suits an early intermediate, not a beginner.

One cue conflict worth naming. This note (2017) tells the athlete to **keep the weight in the heels**
on RDLs — right for a hinge, where the bar drifting forward shifts you onto the toes. But the
[squat](squat-technique.md) and [front squat](front-squat-build-bigger-quads-stronger-squat.md) notes
(both 2019) explicitly warn *against* the heels cue, because it throws balance off and makes the hips
shoot up early. Squats want **even pressure triangulated across heel, big toe and pinky toe**; hinges
want the hips going back. Don't carry the cue across.
