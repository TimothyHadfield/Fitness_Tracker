# Back Exercises Ranked S to F

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Best And Worst Back Exercises (Ranked By Science)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jLvqKgW-_G8)) · 2024-02-05 · 12 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- **Best back exercise overall: the chest-supported row** — it smashes both the lats and the midback, and there are many effective ways to do it.
- **Worst: the renegade row** — too unstable to deliver meaningful tension to the back.
- Six movements reach S tier: **wide-grip lat pulldown, neutral-grip lat pulldown, half-kneeling one-arm lat pulldown, Meadows row, chest-supported row, and cable rows (close and wide grip).**
- Ranking criteria: (1) **big stretch under high tension** — fail this and it's automatic F tier; (2) **feels good** — no pain, smooth resistance profile, good pump and mind-muscle connection; (3) **simple progression** — you can add weight or reps.
- The **deadlift is only C tier as a back builder** — great for total-body strength, but it barely stretches the lats or midback and the tension they receive is isometric.

## The full ranking

### S tier
- **Wide-grip lat pulldown** [03:37] — big lat stretch, smooth even tension throughout, seated and stable, an excellent combination of lat *and* mid-trap activation, and dead simple to overload by moving the pin.
- **Neutral-grip lat pulldown** [04:07] — same upsides but biased toward the lats over the mid traps, especially if you think about driving the elbows down.
- **Half-kneeling one-arm lat pulldown** — high stretch, high tension, easy overload (add a rep weekly, then weight). Slightly less stable than a pulldown machine, but you can brace against your knee or a bench, and working one lat at a time helps prevent asymmetries.
- **Meadows row** [06:09] — named after John Meadows. Huge stretch, absolutely smashes the midback with very high tension, stable once you brace against your knee, and the midback pump is unmatched. Unilateral. A personal favourite.
- **Chest-supported row** [08:41] — all the positives of the freestanding T-bar row with a far more stable and secure feel; the chest support lets you direct all the tension into the back. A staple that should be in every bodybuilding routine. Substitute the dumbbell Helms row (chest braced on an incline bench), a T-bar setup, or whatever machine engages your back best. **Crowned best of the best.**
- **Close-grip cable row** [09:11] — possibly the single best exercise for feeling a deep lat stretch, especially if you lean forward on the negative. Crazy lat pump, easy to add reps or weight.
- **Wide-grip cable row** — same benefits, with emphasis shifted to the mid traps if you squeeze the shoulder blades together.

### A tier
- **Wide-grip pull-up** [02:35] — high tension throughout, deep stretch at the bottom, feels good, good pump, and easy progression (add a rep each session, then load with a belt). Docked slightly for a tricky resistance profile: very easy at the bottom, very hard at the top, so not the smoothest feel.
- **Neutral-grip pull-up** — all the upsides of wide grip with slightly more lat emphasis.
- **Cross-body lat pull-around** [04:38] — twist the torso 90° and pull the cable across your body for an even bigger lat stretch. Held out of S tier only because many people find it awkward at first.
- **Deficit Pendlay row** [06:09] — a Pendlay row standing on a bumper plate, so the negative goes deeper and the lats get more stretch.
- **One-arm dumbbell row** [07:10] — much maligned online by Athlean-X, but Jeff notes no other physiotherapist or exercise scientist he's aware of shares the concern, and he's used them with clients for over a decade without an issue. Big lat stretch at the bottom, nice smooth stable feel. Only downside: strong lifters max out the dumbbell rack, usually at 100 lb.
- **Croc row** [08:11] — a dumbbell row with deliberately loose form, using leg drive and momentum on the positive. Normally he dislikes loose technique, but braced against the bench the momentum is controlled and consistent, and it leans into the back's natural strength curve — assistance through the hard top half while still giving a nice stretch at the bottom.
- **Lying or seated rope face pull** [10:12] — removing the balance requirement lets you overload with much more tension going directly into the back. Close to S tier but not quite.
- **Cable lat pullover / lat prayer** [10:43] — huge lat stretch if you bend forward on the negative, and a strong mind-muscle connection. Better suited to higher reps so it's less easily overloaded, but you can add a rep here and there. Triceps taking over is common until you learn to engage the lats by driving the elbows down. Great for extra lat isolation volume without total-body fatigue.
- **Dumbbell lat pullover** [11:14] — same movement, with much more tension at the bottom and very little at the top. Not a problem, since **the stretch is most likely the most anabolic part of the range** — just cut out the very top end.

### B tier
- **Chin-up (palms up)** [03:06] — still a great upper body exercise, but the supinated grip shifts back tension to the biceps, so it's B tier for the back specifically.
- **Barbell row** [05:09] — stretches the back well with high tension, but it isn't stable, so tension gets dispersed to calves, glutes and low back keeping balance. Your back still gets hammered and you can overload it, but it isn't the best row — or even the best barbell row.
- **Pendlay row** [05:39] — the opposite of the Yates row: more bent over, so more stretch and tension on the lats, and tighter form allows more consistent progression. He'd nudge it to A tier if strength development counted, but as a pure hypertrophy exercise it isn't stable enough. Do them with a controlled negative.
- **Freestanding T-bar row** [08:11] — epic looking, nice lat stretch, high back tension, progresses well with reps or load — but he finds it annoying. Balance becomes an issue as you get strong and the far end of the bar pops up without a landmine attachment. He was thinking C tier, bumped to B "for Ronnie."
- **Standard standing rope face pull** [09:42] — stretches the midback well if you pull the shoulder blades apart on the negative, and gives a strong midback mind-muscle connection. Unstable, though: the cable constantly pulls you forward, so tension is lost to balance.

### C tier
- **Deadlift** [01:33] — one of the best total-body strength exercises, but a poor back *hypertrophy* exercise. It barely stretches the lats or midback, doesn't take the lats or traps through an active range (any tension is isometric), and gives no back pump. It's easy to add weight, but usually because your hamstrings and glutes got stronger. Kept out of D/F tier because it will beef up the spinal erectors and thicken the lower back. **On a lower body day he'd rank it A or B tier**, especially with a well-controlled negative.
- **Yates row** [05:39] — a barbell row with a more upright posture and looser form. The loose form lets you load more weight but doesn't guarantee more tension because hip momentum takes over, and the upright posture actually reduces the stretch on the lats and mid traps.
- **Inverted row** [06:39] — amazing stretch at the bottom, feels great, strong locked-in mind-muscle connection. Killed on progression: loading a partner-placed plate in your lap feels awkward, and without it you're stuck doing very high reps, which becomes suboptimal above 30-40 reps.

### F tier
- **Renegade row** [00:32] — you bottom out against the floor so there's no stretch, and so much work goes into balancing that the back doesn't get maximum tension or a pump. **Crowned worst of the worst.**
- **All two-in-one combination rows** — row + curl, row + press, row + kickback [01:03]. The principle: the smaller muscle in the pair is always far weaker than your back, so it fails first and leaves the back totally understimulated. They burn calories and grow the weaker muscle, but they are not back builders.
- **Above-the-knee rack pull** [02:04] — basically a deadlift with less range of motion, which puts it at D tier; he drops it to F because it damages gym equipment, hogs plates, and feels terrible.

## Practical application
- Anchor the back day around a **chest-supported row**, a **lat pulldown variation** (wide or neutral grip), a **cable row**, and either **pull-ups** or a **Meadows / one-arm dumbbell row**.
- Use neutral grip and drive the elbows down when you want lats; use wide grip and squeeze the shoulder blades when you want mid traps.
- Do face pulls **seated or lying**, not standing, to stop losing tension to balance.
- Keep deadlifts in the program for strength and erector thickness — just don't count on them as your lat or midback stimulus.
- On dumbbell pullovers, cut out the top of the range where tension disappears.
- On a Pendlay or deficit Pendlay row, control the negative deliberately.
- Skip anything that combines a row with a second exercise.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This is a tier list built on his three criteria plus coaching experience, not on head-to-head hypertrophy trials; several placements ("feels good," "annoying," "awkward") are explicitly personal.
- Rankings shift by goal: the deadlift jumps from C to A/B tier if you're ranking it as a lower-body or strength exercise, and the Pendlay row would move up if strength development counted.
- The one-arm dumbbell row's bad online reputation is acknowledged and dismissed on the basis of an absence of concern from other professionals, not on positive evidence.
- The dumbbell pullover ranking rests on the premise that the stretched position is the most anabolic part of the range — stated as "most likely," not settled.
- The T-bar and inverted row placements hinge on equipment: a landmine attachment or a good loading method would improve both.
