# Fixing Muscle Imbalances and Left-Right Asymmetries: 4 Tips

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How to Fix Muscle Imbalances & Asymmetries: 4 Science-Based Tips" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zQTmfOsSXN0)) · 2018-12-10 · 16 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Most imbalances are far less visible than the person thinks, and **muscle shape and insertion points cannot be changed** — only strength and size differences are in your control.
- Jeff's view is that left-right discrepancies are largely **neural**: the dominant side simply fires better. Fix the mind-muscle connection on the weak side first.
- Include **unilateral work**; a purely bilateral program (squat, bench, deadlift, row, pull-up) has no mechanism to correct a side-to-side difference and will entrench it.
- **Film your sets** or use a coach/training partner — mirrors show you only one angle and you shouldn't be craning your neck to look anyway.
- Bonus: add **1-3 extra sets per week for the weaker side only**, at the end of the workout or on a separate day.

## Reality check
People's arms are usually far more even than they believe. Some differences are structural — one bicep with more roundness, one lat inserting further down — and nothing will change that. Building more size on one side may slightly alter its peak or shape, but insertions are fixed. The goal is to minimise strength differences and equalise size as far as possible.

## Tip 1 — Improve the mind-muscle connection on the weaker side [04:07]
Self-test: flex your dominant bicep hard, then the non-dominant one. The dominant side contracts harder and more easily — the motor units fire more readily. Jeff's own left side is bigger and flexes harder; on stage his right quad shows detail far more easily than his left.

Two methods:
1. **Mark the rep during the set.** As you fatigue, consciously pull with the weak side. Saying "left side, left side" in your head each rep reinforces it. (He demonstrates the same on trap shrugs — "right side, right side" while squeezing.)
2. **Pre-activation before the set.** Do an isolation exercise for the weak side only, then move to the heavy compound. Empirical support exists for glute isolation before squats carrying activation over to the compound; Jeff extends the logic to other muscles (his own extrapolation, and he flags "pre-activation" is not a term from the literature).
   - **Lats:** single-arm lat pull-in or pull-over on the weak side.
   - **Biceps:** a **10-second isometric flex** of the weaker arm, actively contracting plus a stretch, before heavy barbell curls.
   - **Pecs:** unilateral cable fly, or press the weak-side pec against a solid object for ~10 seconds with a stretch.
   - Only pre-activate the weak side — the strong side doesn't have a firing problem.

## Tip 2 — Don't train only bilaterally [08:11]
A minimalist bilateral-only program gives you no way to isolate a side, so any movement-pattern discrepancy (e.g. one elbow tucking more on bench) gets more ingrained with every rep. Include **dumbbell presses, single-arm lat pull-ins, single-arm/dumbbell rows, dumbbell curls** alongside the barbell work.

**Progression for obvious asymmetries** — go unilateral → iso-lateral → bilateral. Using the pulldown as the example:
1. **Unilateral:** one handle, one arm at a time.
2. **Iso-lateral:** both handles set up, both arms working at once but each loaded and moving independently.
3. **Bilateral:** the standard bar. By this stage both sides have been pre-programmed to fire evenly, so the strong side shouldn't take over.

He recommends this progression only for people with genuinely obvious asymmetries or movement-pattern problems. It works both correctively and preventatively.

## Tip 3 — Film your lifts or get outside feedback [10:12]
You shouldn't be staring into the mirror while lifting (keep a neutral head position), and the mirror only gives you a side view — it won't show one elbow tracking down faster than the other on a pulldown, or one elbow drifting forward on hammer curls. Film the set, or have a partner watch. Jeff says he's repeatedly spotted faults in his own footage he had no idea about.

Once a fault is identified, it usually comes from one of two sources:
- **A mobility restriction** — e.g. one scapula won't retract as far. Address with specific mobility work or see a qualified professional.
- **An ingrained motor pattern** — a slight elbow tuck that grew over years until the bench press bar is visibly tilted. This needs full reprogramming: **check the ego, strip the weight right back, use lighter loads with slow eccentrics**. If you can control and master limb position on the way down, most of the asymmetry corrects. Then reapply the unilateral → iso-lateral → bilateral progression from Tip 2.

Standard to aim for: from every viewing angle, all joints and moving parts should be symmetrical on a bilateral lift.

## Tip 4 (bonus) — Add a little extra volume to the weak side [13:44]
Add **1-3 extra sets per week for the weaker side only**.
- Don't exceed that — you can overshoot and reverse the imbalance.
- Don't create excessive fatigue in that body part such that it interferes with the rest of your training.
- Do it **at the end of the workout** or on an unrelated day, so it doesn't reduce the load you can handle on your main work.

## Practical application
Sequence for an arm or lat imbalance:
1. Confirm it's real (film it, measure it) rather than assumed.
2. Pre-activate the weak side (10-second isometric or single-limb isolation) before main sets.
3. Cue the weak side out loud or internally during every rep of bilateral work.
4. Add dumbbell/unilateral versions of your main movements.
5. If the pattern is badly distorted, deload hard and rebuild with slow eccentrics through unilateral → iso-lateral → bilateral.
6. Finish with 1-3 extra weekly sets for the weak side only, at the end of the session.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The neural explanation for asymmetry is Jeff's opinion, not a cited finding.
- "Pre-activation" carry-over is supported for glutes-before-squats; extending it to lats, pecs and biceps is his own reasoning.
- Nothing here changes muscle shape or insertion points.
