# Science-Based Leg Workout: Squat, RDL, Lunges, Unilateral Isolation and Calves

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Effective Science-Based LEG WORKOUT | Science Applied (12 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y4Vv2ASsyhs)) · 2018-05-31 · 13 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Warm up 5-10 minutes — a 2006 review found 3 of 5 studies showed warming up reduced injury ([Fradkin 2006](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/16679062)); the two that didn't over-emphasised stretching instead of raising core temperature.
- The squat is the single best overall lower-body mass builder despite the hip thrust beating it on glute activation, because of its far larger range of motion and total musculature involved.
- The squat is dead last for hamstring activation among lower-body lifts, so hamstrings need both a hip-extension movement (RDL) and a knee-flexion movement (leg curl).
- Isolation work is done unilaterally throughout to prevent left-right asymmetries.
- Two calf movements: a straight-leg one and a bent-leg high-rep one for the soleus.

## The routine

| # | Exercise | Sets × reps | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Warm-up | 5-10 min | Elliptical, dynamic stretching, light foam rolling |
| 1 | Back squat | 3 × 4-6 | Leave 2-3 reps in reserve; straight sets at one working weight |
| 2 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | Fixed weight, add 1 rep per week |
| 3 | Walking lunges (optional) | 2-3 × 20 total strides (10/leg) | Skip if beginner / early intermediate |
| 4 | Single-leg leg extension | 3 × 10 per leg | |

| 6 | Single-leg press calf raise | 3 × 10-12 | Gastroc + soleus |

## Squat technique and the evidence [01:00]

**Bar placement.** High bar (on the traps) versus low bar (on the rear delts). Nippard uses high bar because it strains his lower back and wrists less. Biomechanical analysis suggests high bar emphasises the quads via a greater moment arm at the knee, and low bar emphasises the glutes via a greater moment at the hip — supported by a 2017 review recommending low bar for posterior-chain development and high bar for quad contribution ([Glassbrook 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/28570490)). High bar's drawback is that almost everyone is weaker with it, since the upper back becomes limiting. But since progressive overload drives hypertrophy, the absolute load matters less than getting stronger over time. **Coaching recommendation: use whichever placement feels most comfortable.**

**Setup and bracing.** Take two or three shallow steps back to minimise energy loss on the walkout. Take a deep breath into the stomach and use the Valsalva manoeuvre — hold it on the descent and through most of the ascent, exhaling once you break through the top half of the concentric. This raises intra-abdominal pressure and force transfer, allowing heavier loads. Caveat: some research shows it raises blood pressure during lifting, so use at your own discretion.

**Depth [03:05].** A 2013 study compared 12 weeks of shallow quarter squats against deep squats and found the deep squat outperformed at *every quad site measured* ([Bloomquist 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/23604798)). For quads, squat deep. An earlier paper from Caterisano and colleagues found glute activation rose in step with depth, partial → parallel → full ([Caterisano 2002](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/12173958)). But that study used absolute loading — the same weight across all three variations — so deep squats may simply have been harder. A 2016 study using relative loading found little difference between parallel and full ass-to-grass squats ([Contreras 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/26252837)). **Recommendation: squat as deep as you comfortably can; going past parallel isn't worth sacrificing technique or fighting your mobility for.**

**Stance width [04:32].** Quad activation is similar between narrow and wide stances, but glute activation does increase with a wider stance ([McCaw 1999](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/10188748)). If glutes are the priority, consider going wider. For quads it doesn't seem to matter. Use the stance that's comfortable and lets you lift the most; how wide depends mostly on bone structure and requires experimenting.

**Butt wink [05:03].** Whether the lower back rounding and pelvis tucking at the bottom is dangerous isn't settled. The *flexibility theory* camp blames tight muscles (usually hamstrings or calves) pulling the pelvis posteriorly, fixable by stretching. Nippard leans toward the *structural theory*: butt wink is determined by skeletal anatomy — the femoral head runs out of room in the hip socket, forcing posterior pelvic rotation. Consensus among strength professionals is that the fear is overhyped; a little butt wink doesn't seem to meaningfully raise injury risk, though "how much is too much" is unanswerable from current literature. **His advice: limit depth to the point where the lower back just starts to round. If you struggle to hit depth, try different stance widths, a heel-elevated shoe, or calf stretches — calf work helps a lot of people's ankle mobility.**

## Hamstrings [06:34]
A 2009 study ranked six lower-body movements for hamstring activation and put the squat dead last ([Ebben 2009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/19417230)), hence the RDL. Isolation movements tend to top the hamstring activation lists, but a hip-extension lift is still worth including because of the large hamstring stretch, with stretch being a plausible driver of anabolic signalling.

Combine both movement patterns: a 2013 study found the **leg curl** slightly better for the **outer** hamstrings and the **RDL** better for the **inner** hamstrings ([Zebis 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/22736206)), so both are needed for complete development. Also, the short head of the biceps femoris doesn't cross the hip joint at all — it can only be trained through knee flexion, so RDLs cannot reach it. You have to curl.

**RDL progression [07:04]:** keep the weight identical and add one rep per week. Rationale: past a certain load the lower back and glutes take over and the hamstring stretch and mind-muscle connection disappear — which is the whole point of the lift. Find the heaviest weight you can do 8 reps with while still feeling a solid hamstring stretch, then add a rep weekly up to 12 or beyond. Progressive overload without loading yourself out of the target muscle.

## Lunges, leg extension, leg curl
**Walking lunges [08:05]** — optional; beginners and early intermediates can skip them as redundant volume. Not heavily studied, but in the same 2009 study they had a slight edge over the squat for vastus lateralis (outer sweep) activation ([Ebben 2009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/18975260)), are clearly great for glutes, and are decent for hamstrings. Drive the leading heel through the floor and minimise the back leg's contribution so each rep is intentional and forward momentum is limited. Great glute stretch through a big range of motion. Soreness isn't a driver of hypertrophy but can serve as an activation indicator, and nothing hits his glutes like high-rep walking lunges.

**Single-leg leg extension [09:06]** — done one leg at a time because when both work together one usually contributes more; unilateral guarantees equal tension. Research shows vastus lateralis activation is higher with toes pointed in ([Stoutenberg 2005](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/16437818)), so consider that if chasing the outer sweep — but the differences are small, so prioritise a comfortable foot position and controlled, mindful contractions.

**Single-leg lying leg curl, 8 + 4 [10:07]** — a mechanical drop set. Do the first 8 reps **plantarflexed** (toes pointed down), which takes the gastrocnemius out and forces the hamstrings to do the work. At or near failure, immediately **dorsiflex** (toes up) so the gastroc assists knee flexion and buys roughly 4 more reps.

## Calves [11:09]
- **Single-leg press calf raise, 3 × 10-12** — straight leg, hits both soleus and gastrocnemius.
- **Seated calf raise, 3 × 20** — bent knee preferentially targets the deeper but actually larger soleus. Evidence that high reps better target slow-twitch fibres isn't conclusive, but there's a strong theoretical basis, and the soleus is typically slow-twitch dominant. **Key cue: pause deliberately at the bottom of every rep** so elastic energy in the Achilles tendon dissipates and the calf muscles actually do the work.

## Practical application
- Never skip the leg-day warm-up, and make it about raising core temperature, not just stretching.
- Pick bar placement and stance by comfort unless you have a specific glute or quad priority (low bar / wide stance for glutes).
- Squat as deep as technique allows, no deeper.
- Train hamstrings with both a hip-extension and a knee-flexion movement every leg day.
- Use rep progression, not load progression, on RDLs to keep the hamstrings as the limiting factor.
- Do all isolation work one leg at a time to stop asymmetries developing.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Much of the supporting evidence is EMG activation data, which is acute and not a direct measure of hypertrophy.
- The depth/glute-activation finding is confounded by absolute loading; relative-load research contradicts it.
- Butt wink safety thresholds are genuinely unknown.
- Whether high reps preferentially develop slow-twitch fibres is theoretical rather than established.
- The author name garbled by the captions resolves to Caterisano and colleagues (2002), now corrected above.
