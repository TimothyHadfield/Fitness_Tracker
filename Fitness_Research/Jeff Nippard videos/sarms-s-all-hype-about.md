# SARMs: What's All the Hype About?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "SARMS: What's All The Hype About? (Worth Taking? Side Effects? Legal?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t-IWhZgrV1c)) · 2018-05-21 · 6 min
**Topic:** PEDs

## Bottom line
- SARMs = selective androgen receptor modulators, marketed as delivering steroid-like muscle gain without steroid-like side effects because they act on similar receptors without being chemically identical.
- Product quality is the biggest immediate problem: a 2017 JAMA investigation bought 44 products sold as SARMs and found **only 52% actually contained any SARM at all**.
- There is no body of high-quality evidence supporting SARMs for muscle building in healthy trainees. Both speakers consider them overhyped.
- They are not legal for supplement companies to sell — they're sold under a "research purposes" fig leaf — and are banned by drug-tested organisations including WADA.
- Core reasoning: anything powerful enough to drive aggressive muscle growth should be expected to carry a higher incidence of side effects. "Extreme muscle builder, extremely safe" is not a plausible package.

This is a conversation between Nippard and **Ben Carpenter**, who has worked in the supplement industry for seven or eight years and produces science-based content. Neither uses SARMs, and Carpenter is explicit that it's not an area he researches deeply.

## The contamination and dosing problem [01:31]
Carpenter's central objection is manufacturing quality. If a company is selling an ingredient it isn't legally allowed to sell — effectively operating as a research-chemical outfit — there's little reason to believe it follows quality control to the letter.

Testing analyses back this up. Products labelled as containing a given SARM have been found to:
- contain none of the labelled compound at all,
- contain a **different** compound instead,
- contain a combination of the labelled compound and something else,
- contain the right ingredient but at a **dose that didn't match the label**.

**The source [02:01]:** a 2017 investigation published in the *Journal of the American Medical Association*. Researchers bought 44 products marketed as SARMs off the internet and ran lab analysis. **Only 52% of the 44 products contained a single SARM.** Some results were extreme — one product claiming 10 mg of a substance actually contained only 0.1 to 1 mg.

## The safety-data argument [01:01]
Carpenter is sceptical of people who cite safety data for SARMs and then use **doses higher than those used in the research studies that generated the data**. The safety evidence, such as it is, does not transfer to recreational dosing.

## Do they work? [03:02]
Carpenter doesn't class SARMs as the "holy grail" they're marketed as. He finds it genuinely interesting that they have medical literature and are under investigation for medical purposes — but his position is that even if a compound has an **anti-catabolic** effect and is being investigated for **muscle-wasting disorders**, it doesn't follow that taking a much larger dose converts it into a powerful anabolic. Nippard adds that from his own quick scan of the literature there isn't much high-quality evidence supporting SARMs for muscle building.

## Why they're everywhere [03:33]
Marketing. Companies are selling them and largely getting away with it, and "legal steroids" is an easy pitch — search the term and you get a flood of sellers promising the holy grail. The appeal: steroid-like anabolism you can buy at a supplement shop without injecting anything.

## Legality and testing [04:03]
Not legal for a supplement company to sell — they exist commercially under the pretence of being for research purposes, which is a large part of why the market is so sketchy. They are banned in most drug-tested organisations, including WADA, which is why Carpenter (a tested athlete context) hasn't looked into them.

## The M1T precedent [04:34]
Nippard's comparison: when he first got into bodybuilding, the hype product was **M1T (methyl-1-testosterone)** — sold as delivering steroid results without being a steroid and without the side effects. It turned out to have genuinely nasty side effects and then fell out of popularity. SARMs follow the same marketing template.

Carpenter's summarising principle: if something promises to do something physiologically aggressive enough to create very rapid muscle growth, expect a higher incidence of side effects to come with it. People want a safe muscle builder; an extreme muscle builder that is also extremely safe is a big ask.

## Practical application
- Treat any product marketed as a "legal steroid" or SARM as roughly a coin flip on whether it contains what the label says.
- Medical-literature existence is not evidence of bodybuilding efficacy, and a therapeutic anti-catabolic dose does not scale into an anabolic one.
- If you compete in any drug-tested federation, SARMs will fail you.
- Weigh the side-effect expectation, not the marketing claim: potency and safety trade off against each other.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Neither speaker is a SARMs specialist; Carpenter explicitly says he hasn't researched them deeply because he can't take them and they don't interest him personally.
- The discussion is deliberately general — no specific compound (ostarine, LGD-4033, etc.), dosing, or side-effect profile is covered.
- The video is from 2018; the evidence base and legal status may have moved since.
- The transcript garbles "SARMs" as "Psalms"/"sums"/"serums" throughout; the JAMA figures above are the reliable numbers in it.
