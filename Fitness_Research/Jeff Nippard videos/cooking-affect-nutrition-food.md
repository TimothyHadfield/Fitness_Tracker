# Does Cooking Method Change the Nutrition in Vegetables?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How Does Cooking Affect Nutrition in Food? (What The Science Says)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RoVVNX74E6Y)) · 2018-07-09 · 4 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The claim that some cooking methods are clearly superior for retaining nutrients is **mostly busted** — the effect depends on which vegetable and which nutrient.
- The one consistent finding across the literature: **boiling usually performs worst**, especially for vitamin C.
- Steaming is, on average, slightly better than other methods — but the evidence for that is thin and driven largely by broccoli data.
- Microwaving is safe and actually has a favourable nutrient profile; it is not the nutrient-destroyer it's rumoured to be.
- Practical advice: vary your cooking methods, and above all eat a wide variety of fruits and vegetables. That matters far more than method.

## Where the myth comes from
Two mechanisms are cited [00:31]: **heat** is claimed to degrade vitamins such as vitamin C, and **water** is claimed to leach water-soluble vitamins out into the cooking liquid. Both have some truth. The problem is the evidence base — most studies test a single vegetable against one or two cooking methods, then get generalized into a universal rule. That assumes a given vitamin or phytochemical behaves the same way in every vegetable, which it does not.

## The 2015 multi-vegetable study
A 2015 paper (researchers not named in the video) measured **folate, carotenoids and vitamin C** across **13 frozen vegetables** after boiling, steaming, pressure steaming and microwaving [01:01]. The results were nutrient- and vegetable-specific:

- **Whole leaf spinach:** beta-carotene (a vitamin A precursor) was best preserved by **steaming** — it actually *increased* by **24%**.
- **Chopped/hashed spinach:** pressure cooking beat steaming.
- **Sliced carrots:** beta-carotene *increased* by **12%** after **microwaving**.
- **Vitamin C:** boiling retained the least, in all 14 vegetables examined.

The authors' own conclusion, which Jeff endorses: aside from boiling, the absence of any general result across components and vegetables makes it very hard to give the cook a simple recommendation.

Note the apparent increases: heat can break down cell walls and release bound carotenoids, so measured content can go *up* after cooking.

## Microwaves and steaming
Per a Science-Based Medicine article on microwaves and nutrition [02:02], microwave cooking is a safe technology, fast, convenient, and has a favourable profile for food nutrition. That still doesn't make it best across the board.

A separate 2015 review concluded **steaming** is the best method for maintaining nutritional quality — but it only examined pinto beans, peas, broccoli, onions and potatoes, and the conclusion appears to lean mostly on the broccoli results [02:02].

## Practical application
- Don't reorganize your cooking around nutrient retention — the differences are small and inconsistent.
- **Avoid boiling as your default**, particularly for vitamin C-rich vegetables. If you do boil, consider using the cooking liquid (e.g. in a soup or sauce).
- Steaming is a reasonable default if you want one; microwaving is perfectly fine and convenient.
- **Rotate cooking methods** rather than trying to optimize per vegetable per nutrient — that's impractical to the point of uselessness.
- The highest-leverage action is simply eating a **broad variety of fruits and vegetables**, cooked however you'll actually eat them.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Neither 2015 paper's authors are named in the video, so the citations can't be verified from the transcript alone.
- The 13-vegetable study used **frozen** vegetables; results may differ for fresh.
- The steaming-is-best review covered only five foods and was heavily influenced by broccoli.
- Only a handful of nutrients were tracked (folate, carotenoids, vitamin C); minerals, polyphenols and others weren't covered.
