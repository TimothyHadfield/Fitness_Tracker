# Lyle McDonald on Female Physiology: Cycle, Hormones, Fat Loss and Training

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Interview With Lyle McDonald | Part 1 | Nutrition and Training for Women" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o7eIZWKkBq0)) · 2016-03-27 · 81 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Much of the supposed male/female fat-loss gap is body size, not sex hormones — normalise for lean mass and most early "gender difference" findings shrink [05:09].
- What is genuinely different: the monthly hormonal cycle, where fat is stored and mobilised, which fuel is burned during and after exercise, and how fast the leptin-driven shutdown arrives [09:17–14:25].
- Because women's systems adapt faster, McDonald argues they need refeeds and full diet breaks *more* often than men, not fewer — the leaner you get, the more often you must go off the diet [39:32].
- Losing the menstrual cycle is a health event, not a cosmetic one: bone density starts going and may not come back [41:34].
- Training: no meaningful sex difference at heavy loads; a real one at 10–15 reps, where women recover faster and can take more volume, shorter rests and higher frequency [58:26–62:01].
- Individual variability in women is far larger than in men on almost every variable here — his repeated instruction is that a woman and her coach have to track their own pattern rather than trust a generality [24:41].

## Why fat loss is harder — size before hormones [03:35–09:17]
The "women are screwed" line McDonald credits to a friend, a female bodybuilder, whose name the captions render as **"Elie Vul" (uncertain — likely garbled)**. The distal explanation is evolutionary: female bodies are better at surviving energy deprivation, shown decades ago in animals and observationally in famine and concentration-camp data, where men lose fat faster and die sooner.

The proximate explanation surprised him when he dug into it — it is largely **body size**. Women average ~10% or more lighter, ~10% less lean mass, ~10% more fat (men typically 12–15% body fat, women 22–27%). That means lower RMR, lower thermic effect of food (they eat less), fewer calories burned in a session (smaller, weaker, slower). Same 45-minute session, smaller deficit, less fat lost.

- One researcher's studies in **overweight** women equalised the calorie burn — both sexes worked up to ~**600 kcal/day** — and fat loss was **identical**. But the women had to exercise **20–35% longer** to get there.
- Diet arithmetic: pulling 500 kcal from a woman at 2,000 kcal/day maintenance is far harsher than from a man at 3,000. Set it as a percentage instead — 20% — and he cuts 600 while she cuts 400, so she loses slower anyway.

Two hedges he flags himself: the equalised-burn result was in **overweight** women, where these adaptations largely don't fire, and much of the older "women don't lose fat with exercise" literature confounded sex with body weight.

## The real physiological differences [09:17–14:25]
- **Hormonal pattern.** Male testosterone across a month is a flat line (slow decline with age). A woman not on birth control has estrogen and progesterone changing week to week, affecting appetite, metabolism, fat storage and fat mobilisation.
- **Fat depots.** Men carry more **visceral** fat (testosterone-driven), which comes off very fast — hence the rapid early male weight loss. Women carry more **subcutaneous** and more **lower-body** fat, which is harder to mobilise; men have more upper-body fat, which is easier.
- **Fuel use.** Even when men and women burn similar grams or percentages of fat in a session, women draw more of it from **intramuscular triglyceride** — up to half — sparing body fat, so the actual body fat burned in a moderate session is trivial. Afterwards the pattern flips: men use less fat during and more fat for the rest of the day; women burn a higher fat percentage during and shift **back to carbs** for the other 23 hours.
- **Adaptation speed.** Leptin can fall **up to 50% within a week** of dieting, long before any real fat is lost, and women's leptin falls **up to ~20% more** than men's. There is also some indication women's brains respond to leptin differently. On aggressive deficits, active thyroid hormone can drop significantly **within five days**.

The leptin study he leans on: subjects dieted to 10% below body weight had suppressed expenditure and thyroid and raised hunger; **injecting leptin back to pre-diet levels** normalised hunger, expenditure and thyroid, and they kept losing fat with nothing else changed. Leptin injection has also reversed diet-induced loss of the menstrual cycle. Ghrelin rises on a diet and adds to the hunger signal.

A six-month half-marathon-training study is the exercise-only case: weight differences were trivial (four pounds vs two), but women's hunger and food intake climbed in the second half — which is why "exercise without dietary control is ineffective for women" got written. He notes a typical low/moderate-intensity session is only ~**300–400 kcal** for a 120 lb woman, trivially offset by appetite.

## The menstrual cycle, water and measurement [21:06–30:51]
Average **28 days**, split by ovulation. **Follicular** (weeks 1–2): estrogen dominant, sweeping up to a spike then crashing. **Luteal** (weeks 3–4): progesterone high, estrogen back to roughly half.

- **Estrogen** he calls mostly beneficial — it aids muscle remodelling and blunts hunger for about **three days before ovulation**.
- **Progesterone** is "the problem hormone": directly stimulates **thigh fat storage**, stimulates hunger and carb/fat cravings, raises body temperature (~1 degree, usable as an ovulation marker) and raises energy expenditure slightly, and **blocks the androgen receptor**.
- **Water spikes** come in two places: the few days before ovulation, when estrogen changes sodium handling, and the last luteal week, where progesterone has been blocking the water-retention receptor and its fall produces a rebound. It can take a couple of days into menstruation for the weight to drop off.
- **PMS variability is enormous** — from nothing at all to premenstrual dysphoric disorder (depression, suicidality, incapacitating cramps), driven by the relative levels of the two hormones.

Measurement consequence: never compare consecutive weeks. Compare **week 1 to week 1, week 2 to week 2**, cycle to cycle. Onset of menstruation is the reliable anchor; two or three days in, the water is gone. It can therefore take **a month** to know whether a diet has actually stalled. Cycle length itself varies (26/28/30 days, or irregular), so even a fixed 7-day week may not land on the same phase. Sodium down and potassium up mitigates the water somewhat; it will still happen.

## Refeeds and diet breaks [31:21–52:18]
He defines a **refeed** as a short high-carb period. In *A Guide to Flexible Dieting* (2004) he described them from 5 to 48 hours; he now thinks a **5-hour refeed is purely psychological plus a little glycogen**, with no hormonal effect, and that 24 hours done more frequently does have a metabolic effect.

The frequency logic, worked out with **Eric Helms** as "intermittent caloric restriction": the brain takes about **four days** to register the leptin drop (hunger starts at day 4–5), so a **one-day refeed every 3rd–4th day**, or a **two-day refeed every 5–7 days**, is a better structure than dieting a fortnight and then eating.

Book schedules he previews (keyed to starting body fat and deficit size):
- **20% deficit (mild):** two-day refeed every **2 weeks**.
- **Over 30% deficit:** two-day refeed every **week**; generally somewhere in the 7–14 day band, placed on weekends for convenience.
- **High training volume on a small deficit:** a one-day refeed on day 7 to refill glycogen for performance, then a two-day refeed the following week.

He defines "lean" for women as under **24% body fat** — roughly a man at 10–12%, since women run 10–12 points higher. Female bodybuilding/physique requires **10–11%**; fitness around **20%** and far fewer problems; male bodybuilders 4%. He suspects his *Ultimate Diet 2.0* 7-day cycle (4.5 days hard dieting, 2.5 days refeeding) may actually suit women **better** than men, though few women have run it and his evidence is his own small caseload plus Helms' observation of fewer stalls, later loss of menstrual cycle, and less physical and emotional strain.

**Diet break:** two weeks at (slightly under) estimated maintenance, initially every **8–10 weeks**, more often as you get leaner. His striking supporting study is military recruits in a multi-stressor environment — ~2 hours sleep, near-zero calories, ~8 hours of intense training daily — who reached ~4% body fat with **testosterone at castrate levels, cortisol off the map, thyroid in the toilet**. **One week at maintenance calories reversed many of the hormonal adaptations even with the training load unchanged.**

Caveats he volunteers: you will **never** reset metabolism to pre-diet levels — at best to normal for the new body weight — and adaptation restarts quickly. The Minnesota study's adaptive RMR component ranged from about **50 to 500 kcal/day** — a fivefold individual spread.

## Why not to just diet harder [36:59–46:07]
- Start earlier: estimate the time to goal, then **double it**. A light woman may lose half a pound of fat a week at best; a beginner may need six months. The 12-week contest prep is unrealistic for naturals.
- Start with a **small** deficit so you have somewhere to go. A 120 lb woman at 1,000–1,200 kcal ("poverty macros") who stalls has no lever left — 800 is not an answer.
- **Cortisol water retention** masks fat loss: cortisol rises with dieting, over-exercise and psychological stress, and women's baseline is higher. He notes women who lose their cycle from psychological stress alone, without dieting or over-training. Raising carbs and calories to maintenance, or taking a day off, drops cortisol and produces the "whoosh" — three pounds lighter and tighter overnight.
- **Survivorship bias:** the competitors who make the stage are the ones who made it, sometimes on undisclosed clenbuterol, thyroid and Winstrol. Most people who diet the same way fail and are never heard from.
- **Amenorrhoea costs bone density and may be irreversible**, with raised later-life osteoporosis risk. He is explicit this is not hyperbole.
- For genuine non-responders his last resort is a protein-sparing modified fast — but he concedes there is a real genetic component and some people should pick a different goal.

## Training differences [56:23–72:19]
Under a microscope male and female muscle is identical; women have less of it, distributed more toward the lower body, with **maybe 5% more type I fibres when a difference is found at all** — never enormous — and somewhat different neuromuscular control.

- **Reps at a given intensity:** at **80% 1RM and above** the difference essentially disappears. At **65%** a woman might do 30 reps to a man's 15.
- **Elasticity:** women use more elastic recoil in the lower body until maximal strength requirements dominate — an early depth-jump study he attributes to a researcher the captions render as **"Henan" (uncertain; possibly Häkkinen)** found women rebound a larger percentage until the drop height gets high. Men may be more elastic in the upper body.
- **Recovery, two studies by the same researcher:** 20 sets of maximal singles — **no** sex difference in recovery; 10×10 at 70% — women recovered **faster**. It is an intensity effect: lower forces mean less occlusion, less lactate and acidosis, less fatigue. Chinese Olympic lifting coaches report women handling heavier, more often, with more volume and faster recovery — but Olympic lifting is explosive and elastic at low percentages of max. Sprint coaches report female sprinters tolerating **~20% more** high-speed repetitions.
- **Practical:** shorter rest intervals, and he'd argue **higher frequency per muscle** suits women better than the once-a-week-hammer-it model. He also thinks most women already do too much volume, but at genuinely submaximal loads that don't count.
- **Cycle-phase periodisation:** "five or six studies" now. One clustered **14 workouts a month** — 10 follicular + 4 luteal, versus 10 luteal + 4 follicular, versus spread evenly. The **follicular-focused group made better strength and muscle gains than both** others. Mechanisms offered: estrogen aids remodelling; progesterone blocks the androgen receptor in the luteal phase.
- **Anatomy/biomechanics:** wider hips, typically weaker glutes, knees caving on squats and leg presses, more hypermobile shoulders, and an upper body that can't shoulder or brace the loads the legs could move — hence more upper back work. He credits **Bret Contreras**' glute bridges partly for removing the upper body as the limiter, letting women load an exercise they enjoy.
- **PMS week:** coordination can collapse (his trainee could hit Olympic-lift PRs the day bleeding started but couldn't power clean during PMS, limited to ~60% machine work), ligament laxity and injury risk rise, mood may not permit hard training. PMS week makes a natural **deload**. Early follicular is usually the strongest week.

Tracking method: anchor on menstruation, then log mood, energy, RPE and whether last week's load feels heavier at the same reps — over a couple of months, before dieting, so you know her normal weight-spike pattern.

## Cardio and general-population points [76:58–80:01]
Both HIIT and steady state have a place. Higher intensity raises cortisol more but depletes muscle glycogen usefully; too much low-intensity work raises cortisol perfectly well too. The common mistake is going straight to an hour of hard cardio a day — the hormonal system "can literally shut down" in **5–7 days**. Rule of thumb: **big deficit or lots of cardio, not both at once**, and keep low intensity genuinely low.

He accepts the argument that women are relatively better suited to endurance work: in most sports women are ~10% behind men, mostly explained by muscle mass, but **beyond about 50 km women outperform men** — more fat for fuel so they don't bonk, more elastic so less fatigue and muscle damage.

On the general population, his complaint is behavioural, not physiological: men gravitate to protein and the weight room, women to carbs and the cardio deck. His two non-negotiables for anyone are **sufficient protein and resistance training**. His proposed mechanism for why women transform when they start lifting and doing intervals: depleting muscle glycogen (which their aerobic training doesn't do) shifts them toward men's pattern of burning more fat across the whole day. He offers this as a reasoned extrapolation, not a demonstrated finding.

## Points of disagreement
Mostly they agree; two moments of real friction:

- **Does water retention explain stalls?** [46:07] Nippard pushes back directly: he sees women lose 3–4 lb in month one, stall, get cut from 1,500 to 1,300 kcal, and stay stalled — if it were only water, the scale would still be drifting down. McDonald concedes the point, says he has been "occasionally misrepresented," and agrees metabolic adaptation is real and independent. The exchange resolves into both mechanisms operating.
- **Are refeeds physiologically necessary or just psychological?** [38:31] Nippard presents the hardheaded competitor's position — skip the refeed, bank the deficit — and asks whether there is any physiological case in the final four weeks. McDonald answers unambiguously yes for 24-hour-plus refeeds done frequently (reversing the leptin → thyroid, LH and FSH suppression), while conceding the 5-hour refeed is psychological only. Nippard doesn't contest the answer, so this is a probe rather than a standing disagreement.

Nippard's suggestion to time refeeds and diet breaks to cycle phase [69:46] McDonald endorses enthusiastically and says it's already in his book.

## Practical application
1. Track the cycle before dieting. Menstruation onset is the anchor; log weight, mood, energy and session RPE for a couple of months at maintenance.
2. Compare like week to like week across cycles. Expect a month before you can call a stall.
3. **Start the diet in the follicular phase**, never the luteal — starting when hunger and cravings peak sets you up to fail.
4. Put a refeed in the last 3–4 days of PMS, when cravings and low mood are worst, then train hard in the follicular phase on full glycogen.
5. Cluster more training volume into the follicular phase; use PMS week as a deload.
6. Refeed frequency by deficit: 20% deficit → two days every two weeks; >30% → two days weekly; or one day every 3rd–4th day.
7. Diet break of two weeks at maintenance every 8–10 weeks, more often as you lean out.
8. Start small on both deficit and cardio and escalate; leave yourself room to cut.
9. Lower sodium, raise potassium around the predictable water spikes.
10. Non-negotiables for the general-population woman: enough protein, and resistance training.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The equalised-exercise study showing identical fat loss was in **overweight** women; he says most of these adaptations don't apply at high body fat and get worse as women get leaner.
- Much of the older sex-difference literature confounds sex with body size — he explicitly says the difference "had more to do with the differences in body weight than anything else."
- The cycle-phase training result rests on "five or six studies"; he gives one design in detail and does not claim the effect size is settled.
- The type I fibre difference is "maybe 5%… when they do find it at all."
- The UD2-suits-women-better claim is a hunch from mechanism plus a handful of clients, plus Helms' uncontrolled observations — not data.
- The glycogen-depletion-shifts-female-metabolism argument is his own inference from fuel-use data, not a tested intervention.
- **Individual variability is the recurring caveat**: PMS severity, cortisol feedback sensitivity, cycle length, adaptive RMR drop (50–500 kcal/day), performance change across the cycle, and whether a woman notices any of it at all. Women with elevated testosterone or PCOS may see none of these effects.
- **Birth control largely erases the cyclic picture** — no major hormonal swings to work with or around. He notes six application types and eight synthetic progestins that all behave differently, and does not attempt to generalise across them here.
- Perimenopause, menopause and ageing change the hormonal baseline again; flagged but not covered.
- Two researcher names are garbled in the auto-captions and are flagged above rather than guessed: the "women are screwed" attribution and the depth-jump researcher.
