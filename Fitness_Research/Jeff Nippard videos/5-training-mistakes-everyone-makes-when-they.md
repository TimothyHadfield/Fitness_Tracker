# Five Training Myths Beginners Fall For

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "5 Training Mistakes Everyone Makes When They Start Lifting" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dp3truKibtc)) · 2019-10-12 · 12 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- Rep range doesn't decide "toning" vs "bulking" — all rep ranges build muscle when taken near failure. Practical zone: **6-15 reps**.
- Don't use lifting as a fat-loss tool. A session burns only ~75-300 kcal; set the deficit with diet and cardio.
- Splitting weekly volume for a muscle across 2+ sessions beats dumping it all in one bro-split day.
- Soreness is a marker of novelty, not of an effective stimulus. Judge workouts by your logbook.
- Keep the core lifts constant week to week; "muscle confusion" has no support. Leave 1-3 reps in reserve on most sets.

## Myth 1: high reps tone, low reps bulk
"Toning" has no scientific meaning — what people mean is losing fat so existing muscle shows. Fat loss comes from a calorie deficit plus adequate protein; muscle comes from progressive tension. Spot reduction fails in at least three studies, and per the MASS research review a typical weight session burns only about 75-300 calories. A 150 lb person burns roughly as much in a 10-15 minute jog as in an hour of lifting.

On the low-rep side: a 2015 study compared **25-35 reps per set** against **8-12 reps per set**, both on a 3x/week full-body routine. After 8 weeks, ultrasound showed equal muscle growth. Growth is similar across rep ranges as long as sets are taken to or near failure.

Why he still recommends **6-15 reps** in practice: above 15 reps costs disproportionate recovery for no extra growth; below 6 reps adds injury risk from heavy loading. Dip above or below occasionally, but live in that band.

## Myth 2: one body part per workout
The classic Mon-chest / Tue-back / Wed-legs / Thu-shoulders / Fri-arms split assumes a muscle needs a full week to recover. Research suggests there is a **per-session volume threshold** — 12 chest sets on one day is worse than 6 sets on each of two push days.

Mechanism: training spikes muscle protein synthesis for roughly 24 hours, then it falls back toward baseline around **48-72 hours** post-workout (figure from Damas and colleagues). In trained lifters the response is shorter still — MPS starts dropping after roughly 10 hours and is near baseline within a day or two, which is a theoretical argument for higher-frequency full-body work in advanced lifters.

Counterpoint he makes himself: a 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis of 25 studies found **frequency does not significantly affect hypertrophy when weekly volume is equated**. Bro splits work — he built muscle on one for 3-4 years and knows natural competitors who use them. The real problems are practical: performance on later sets in a huge single session degrades, and higher frequency usually ends up producing more total weekly volume, which does drive growth.

## Myth 3: you need to be sore
Soreness signals a novel stimulus, not an effective one. A marathon would make him very sore with zero hypertrophy; stabbing his leg would make him sorer than a good leg day. Soreness can actively hurt progress by degrading performance on the next session two days later — and it's that next session's performance that matters.

The one useful signal: soreness in the wrong place. If hip thrusts leave you sore only in the lower back and hamstrings, you executed them wrong and missed the glutes.

Better gauges: did you lift more weight, get more reps, improve technique, or focus better than last time?

## Myth 4: muscle confusion
Progressive tension drives growth, not variety. Constantly rotating lat pulldowns → Hammer Strength with drop sets → weighted pull-ups makes it impossible to progressively overload anything. Beginners should do the opposite: keep the core of the routine identical week to week to master form and add weight. Accessory exercises, rep schemes and intensity techniques can rotate freely — but hold **1-2 main heavy movements per training day for at least 8 weeks** (e.g. a pull-up and a Pendlay row on every pull day).

## Myth 5: no pain, no gain
Growth from a set taken to failure is very similar to one leaving 1-2 reps in the tank, while failure adds fatigue, injury risk, form breakdown, and cuts the volume you can do later in the session.

## Practical application
- Train mostly in **6-15 reps**, dipping outside occasionally.
- Spread each muscle's weekly sets over **2-3 sessions** rather than one; pick whatever split lets you hit your weekly volume while recovering and performing.
- Save **failure for the last set of isolation exercises**; leave **1-3 reps in reserve** everywhere else.
- Use diet and cardio for the deficit; use lifting to build and keep muscle.
- Track weight, reps, form and focus in a log — not soreness.
- Keep 1-2 anchor lifts per day fixed for 8+ weeks.

## Caveats & unknowns
The meta-analysis showing frequency doesn't matter at equated volume sits awkwardly beside the MPS-timecourse argument for higher frequency; his resolution is that frequency matters mainly because it makes higher weekly volume and better per-set performance achievable. The 2015 low- vs high-load study and the three spot-reduction studies are not named by author in the video.
