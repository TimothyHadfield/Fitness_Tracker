# Full Body Five Days a Week with Eric Helms: Volume Spread, RPE and Fatigue Management

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Why Training Full Body 5x Per Week Is Smart: Science-Based Workout ft. Dr. Eric Helms" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k1Xr8rMK-Gw)) · 2019-07-08 · 18 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Helms trains **full body five days a week**: typically **1-2 leg movements and 3-4 upper body movements per day**, with weekly volume equated to what he'd previously done on other splits.
- The case for high frequency is **not** a bigger MPS response — that argument is largely theoretical and weakly supported. The real benefit is spreading volume so you can complete it with less fatigue.
- There is likely **no per-session minimum volume threshold**: study volumes of ~10-20 sets per muscle per week split over five days is only ~2 sets per muscle per session, and those studies match or beat much higher per-session volumes.
- Progression is autoregulated: a rep range plus an **RPE target**, not a fixed rep number. Three sets at RPE 8-10, where RPE 8 genuinely means two reps left.
- Helms spent about a year and a half trialling splits, and over a year refining this one. Individualisation is the point, not the template.

## Why full body five days a week [13:38]
Helms tested different splits over about eighteen months with volume held constant — the same equated sets per muscle group — trying upper/lower and others. Full body five days a week is what felt best and allowed the most volume.

He explicitly dismisses the usual justification: the idea that an advanced lifter has a short muscle protein synthesis response that needs re-stimulating is "largely theoretical," has very little direct fibre-level data behind it, and if there's any effect it's neutral to only slightly positive.

The actual benefit is **volume spread and fatigue management**. In a prep, energy is finite. Doing RDL, front squat, leg curl, leg press, leg extension and calf raise in one session would wreck him. Splitting that same weekly volume across five sessions — leg press and calf raise plus upper body work — leaves him far more energy and lets him distribute identical volume with less fatigue.

## Is there a minimum per-session volume? [15:08]
Nippard raises the objection: all they did for legs that day was three sets of leg press. Two answers:

1. Those three sets were genuinely hard. Helms means RPE 8-10 literally — RPE 8 means if there were a gun to your head you could get two more reps.
2. Helms' logical argument: if a per-session minimum volume existed, higher frequency would produce *worse* results as sets per session dropped. Instead the literature finds higher frequency neutral or beneficial. Weekly volumes used in the literature are relatively low — around 10-20 sets per muscle per week — so ten sets split across five days is only two sets per muscle per session, and that has been done and comes out equal to or better than programs with three times the per-session volume.

Physiologically, the only candidates for a session minimum are getting a pump or generating muscle damage, and Helms regards those as indicators that you trained the muscle rather than causative drivers of growth. Nippard concedes he used to make the per-session-volume argument himself and now considers it possibly invalid, worth self-testing.

## Session walkthrough
**Leg press superset with calf raise [04:59].** Three working sets in the **6-10 rep range** targeting **RPE 8-10**, reached via three to five progressively heavier warm-ups. On each set he assesses RPE and lands in the target zone. The superset is done by finishing the leg press then sliding the toes down on the same platform at the same weight for calf raises. Second benefit: doing calf raises there improves dorsiflexion range of motion, which then gives you more depth on the leg press itself. Working weights that day were 425 kg on the press and 115 kg for calves. Helms logs everything in an app with years of history so he can compare against past mesocycles.

**Flat dumbbell press with seated cable row [08:09].** Run as a superset but with rest taken between each exercise. Same RPE-driven approach: hit the target RPE, then either keep the weight and cut a rep or drop the weight slightly on subsequent sets.

On rowing mechanics: leaning forward gives more lat stretch, but you stop generating force purely from shoulder extension. It's a trade-off, and the answer depends on intent — are you treating the row as a lat/shoulder-extension movement or as a scapular retraction/trap movement? Helms does both: he doesn't pull high, he pulls to the centre. Pulling low biases shoulder extension and lats.

**Barbell upright row with barbell curl [10:25].** Three rounds, roughly **9-10 reps**, resting a little between the two movements rather than true no-rest supersetting. Upright rows pulled to about a 90-degree shoulder angle, then push down and come back.

Helms on upright row safety [11:29]: he rejects sorting exercises into "good/bad" or "safe/dangerous" camps — the body moves in many ways, and load, range of motion and biomechanics all have to be considered. Pulling very high does raise impingement risk, but it's a low-load exercise and he says he has never met anyone who hurt themselves doing bodybuilding-style, good-form upright rows. His recommendation: stop at about 90 degrees, at which point it's essentially a lateral raise with different rotation. And if it hurts, don't do it.

## Autoregulated progression in a prep [17:12]
In contest prep, strength isn't as consistent as in the offseason. So instead of a fixed rep target, Helms sets a **rep range plus an RPE target**. Example: 6-10 reps; if the first set stops at 8 reps at the target RPE, hold the weight and try to match it for two more sets without exceeding the RPE cap.

Benefits: progression comes automatically when it's available, strength builds when the body allows, and on a bad day he simply does what he can — which itself is useful feedback on whether he's under- or over-recovered.

Both agree that rigid full body programs fail: "three sets of 15" means you stop at 15 even when far from meaningful effort, and even with an 8-10 prescription you can leave a lot of stimulus on the table by stopping purely because the sheet says so. Helms also varies rep ranges by movement, and uses exercises he's performed for 15+ years where his mind-muscle connection is established.

## Neck training [02:17]
Asked at the seminar, essentially nobody trains neck directly. Helms puts the neck in the same category as calves and forearms: nobody wins a bodybuilding show on them, but between competitors of equal calibre they help tie a physique together. A reasonable strength standard is working up to about a **45 lb plate for sets of 20** — but don't jump straight to that.

## Practical application
- Full body 5x/week: 1-2 leg movements and 3-4 upper body movements per session.
- Keep weekly volume equated (~10-20 hard sets per muscle per week) and let it spread across sessions; 2-3 hard sets per muscle per session is enough.
- Prescribe a rep range plus an RPE target (e.g. 6-10 reps at RPE 8-10), not a fixed rep count. RPE 8 = two genuine reps in reserve.
- Superset leg press with calf raises on the same machine and weight; the calf work also improves the dorsiflexion that limits leg press depth.
- Upright rows: stop around 90 degrees of shoulder elevation.
- Neck work, if included, worked up toward a 45 lb plate for 20s.
- Expect to spend months, not weeks, dialling in exercise selection and distribution.

## Caveats & unknowns
Helms is explicit that this is highly individual — over a year of trial to find the right exercises, distribution across days and mesocycle versions — and much of the video is his personal contest-prep experience rather than a general prescription. He is also explicit that the MPS-frequency rationale is weakly evidenced. The claim that no per-session volume threshold exists is an inference from frequency literature, not a direct test. No studies are named individually; the "10-20 sets" figure is quoted approximately from the training-volume literature.
