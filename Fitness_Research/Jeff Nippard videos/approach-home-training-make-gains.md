# At-Home Training By Experience Level: Who Actually Needs To Worry

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Approach Your At Home Training To Actually Make Gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rhI5C_hHY8Y)) · 2020-03-22 · 11 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Beginners to early intermediates (0-2 years training) have little to worry about. Training hard at home should maintain muscle for at least 3-4 months, and may still add muscle.
- Late intermediates and advanced lifters (3+ years) lose progress more easily because they need far more volume and stimulus to hold what they have.
- Doing *something* beats doing nothing by a wide margin: a 2011 study found one-ninth of previous training volume maintained muscle for months, while a group doing nothing returned to baseline.
- Advanced lifters: buy basic equipment if you can (a barbell, plates, dumbbells), accept that you will lose strength, and eat in a caloric surplus.
- Strength is highly specific and needs heavy loading. Let it go, focus on hypertrophy, and rely on muscle memory to bring strength back quickly.

## The two categories
[01:06] With gym closures looking like months rather than weeks, the honest answer depends on training age.

**Beginner to early intermediate (0-2 years).** The literature is reassuring. The relevant evidence isn't the three-week complete-detraining study he covered previously — this scenario is extended *suboptimal* training, not detraining. The better fit is a 2011 study in which subjects reduced training volume to a fraction of normal (one-ninth) and maintained the muscle they had built for months, while a group that did nothing at all returned to baseline. It is far better to do something than nothing.

He goes further: beginners can actually keep progressing at home, especially if the muscle is being stimulated in an unfamiliar way. If you've never done hard sets of 50-60 reps, that novel stimulus can spark new growth.

**Late intermediate to advanced (3+ years).** Harder to keep progressing and easier to lose progress, because the volume and stimulus required to move the needle is so much higher — ease off that stimulus and the muscle is more likely to go.

## Three recommendations for experienced lifters
[08:07]
1. **Get basic equipment if you can afford it.** High-rep bodyweight work probably won't maintain all your muscle mass past the first few weeks at this level. A minimal setup — power rack or barbell, plates, a range of dumbbells, optionally a pull-up bar — is enough. Nippard and a friend split roughly $2,300 CAD on a rack setup plus about $250 for dumbbells, so around $2,500 between two people. Adapting a machine-based program to barbells and dumbbells is straightforward.
2. **If you can't, don't write yourself off.** The "screw it, I'll take the time off" mentality guarantees large losses. Genuine, motivated effort at home should let even advanced lifters maintain most of their size — particularly training hard and in a caloric surplus.
3. **Enter a caloric surplus.** Accept some fat gain. It attenuates muscle loss, and when you return to normal lifting the muscle memory effect puts you in an excellent position for recomposition — drop back to maintenance or a very slight deficit and lose fat while regaining muscle, likely surpassing your previous peak.

## Accepting strength loss
[09:38] Strength is highly specific: you have to lift heavy to stay strong. If you can't, accept that squat, bench and deadlift numbers will fall, do the higher-rep home work, and focus on maintaining muscle mass instead. Muscle memory research says strength rebounds quickly once regular lifting resumes, so don't let it demotivate you.

## Practical notes from his own garage setup
- Cold training space (around 4°C) is a real problem — a heater helps, since warming up may not be enough to keep body temperature up.
- Cheaper plates can be undersized: theirs measured 15 inches versus a standard 17.7-inch barbell plate, meaning deadlifts were effectively pulled from a 1.35-inch deficit. Options are to build a raised platform for the plates or simply accept the longer range of motion, which is harder and arguably a training advantage.

## Practical application
Under two years training: train hard at home, chase closeness to failure, and stop worrying — 3-4 months of maintenance or better is realistic. Over three years: acquire minimal barbell and dumbbell equipment, train hard, eat in a surplus, write off strength temporarily and protect hypertrophy. Everyone: any tensile stimulus beats none.

## Caveats & unknowns
The 3-4 month maintenance window for less experienced lifters is his projection, not a measured result. The advanced-lifter advice is explicitly his opinion, not a research finding — he hedges that high-rep bodyweight work "might not" be enough. The one-ninth volume study's duration and generalisability to trained lifters is not fully specified here.
