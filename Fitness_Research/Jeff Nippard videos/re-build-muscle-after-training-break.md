# How To Rebuild Muscle And Strength After A Training Layoff

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Re-Build Muscle After A Training Break" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LiyDfoUkbdo)) · 2020-05-31 · 11 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Do not resume at your old volume and intensity. Build a 2-4 week "bridge": an intro phase (1-2 weeks) then a transition phase (1-2 weeks), longer bridge for a longer layoff.
- Intro phase: compounds at RPE 5 or lower — roughly 50-60% of your old 1RM for sets of about 4-6 reps, leaving 5+ reps in the tank. Isolation work can go to RPE 7-8, still not failure.
- Volume: start at 7-10 hard sets per muscle per week, 2-3 sets per exercise, each muscle trained twice weekly. Push/pull/legs 6 days or upper/lower 4 days.
- Pick low-muscle-damage exercises. Soreness is the enemy on a comeback — it adds nothing to hypertrophy and blocks performance and recovery.
- Expect strength regain to take roughly half the length of your layoff (two months off ≈ one month back).

## Why not just jump back in
[01:09] The instinct after a break is to restore old volume immediately and take most sets to failure to "shock" the body. This only addresses the stimulus half of the equation. Growth comes from stimulus plus recovery; too much stimulus too fast overwhelms recovery capacity and you progress *slower* despite doing more work. After a layoff, recovery ability has taken a significant hit, so the balance point has moved.

## Principle 1: expect newbie-like gains, but write the plan first
[02:10] Muscle memory makes rebuilding lost muscle far easier than building it from scratch — research repeatedly shows this — so a returning lifter can be in an even better position than a true beginner, depending on how much was lost. But the plan has to be written down before you walk into the gym. Freestyling makes overdoing it almost inevitable and wrecks the stimulus/recovery balance.

## Principle 2: accept the strength loss
[03:12] More than two or three weeks off and you will be weaker; the longer the break, the more is gone. Strength is a skill — like a jump shot after months without a basketball. Reset expectations to where you are now, forget the old numbers, and start logging weights again; they climb back quickly.

**Rule of thumb:** regaining most of your strength takes about half the time you took off. Two months off ≈ one month to return; six months off ≈ up to three months. Good programming or good genetics can speed this up. The rule applies on the scale of months and starts breaking down past roughly six months off.

## Principle 3: choose low-damage exercises
[04:12] The selection criterion is minimising soreness. You will get some just from training again, that's fine, but exercises that load in a highly stretched position — walking lunges, Romanian deadlifts — should be cut from the comeback plan entirely. Not because they're dangerous, but because other movements stimulate glutes and hamstrings just as well at a much lower recovery cost while recovery is already compromised.

Borrowing Mike Israetel's term: prioritise a high **stimulus-to-fatigue ratio** — movements you feel working the muscle without being wrecked for days. Cables and machines are your best friends here, being lower impact and less damaging than free weight equivalents. Don't abandon squats, deadlifts and presses; just reintroduce their loading gradually, since compounds carry high fatigue alongside high stimulus.

## Principle 4: the bridge — intro and transition phases

**Intro phase (1-2 weeks).** Goal is neural: reteaching the body and brain to work together. Compounds are for technique mastery with very light loads.
- Compounds: RPE 5 or lower, at least 5 reps left in the tank. Concretely 50-60% of your old 1RM for sets of about 4-6 reps. Example: a 275 lb bench 1RM before a two-month break means starting at 135-165 lb for 2 sets of 5.
- That feels absurdly light, but detrained lifters, like beginners, don't need to approach failure to grow. Nippard cites a MASS research review covering a study where a group stopping 7-8 reps shy of failure (RPE 2-3) grew similarly to a group training to failure over eight weeks. Not optimal long-term, but proof that solid progress happens with 5+ reps in reserve.
- Nearly all early strength regain comes from the nervous system relearning the pattern, so light weight is genuinely useful — and heavy loading can be actively harmful while joints, soft tissue and nervous system are unaccustomed.
- Isolation: RPE 7-8, still avoiding failure. Use it to rebuild the mind-muscle connection and get reacquainted with a pump.
- Volume: 7-10 sets per body part per week — the range Schoenfeld and Grgic gave in their 2017 paper outlining an intro phase for a one-year periodisation scheme. Especially reasonable if you did bodyweight work or other activity during the break. Cut per-exercise sets from your usual 3-4 down to 2-3.
- Frequency: twice per week per muscle is the sweet spot. Once weekly gives too little motor-learning practice to rebuild strength quickly; more than two (maybe three) outpaces recovery until the repeated bout effect kicks in. Use PPL six days a week or upper/lower four days a week.

**Transition phase (1-2 weeks).** Gradually turn the dials up. Compounds to RPE 6-7 by adding weight; isolation to RPE 8-9, provided soreness is falling and recovery is improving. If recovery holds, take per-exercise sets from 2 up to 3, especially on priority exercises.

## After the bridge
[10:19] You're back to normal training. Most people can run simple linear progression for at least the next month or two — adding roughly 5-20 lb week to week without varying rep ranges — until a plateau, at which point start methodically manipulating volume, intensity, frequency and exercise selection.

## Practical application
Write the bridge before your first session. Weeks 1-2: light compounds at 50-60% 1RM, RPE ≤5, 2 sets of ~5; isolations at RPE 7-8; 7-10 sets/muscle/week; each muscle twice weekly on PPL-6 or upper/lower-4; cables and machines favoured; no stretch-loaded high-damage lifts. Weeks 3-4: add load to RPE 6-7 on compounds, RPE 8-9 on isolations, 3 sets per exercise. Then linear progression.

## Caveats & unknowns
The half-the-time strength regain rule is a general heuristic and unreliable beyond about six months off. Bridge length depends on layoff length and is 2-4 weeks only. The sub-failure growth finding is from a single study over eight weeks and is explicitly framed as sufficient, not optimal, for the long term.
