# High-Frequency Full Body, Day 2: Chest-Focused Workout

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Effective Full Body Workout: CHEST Focused" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7USunyFGITk)) · 2020-02-12 · 8 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Day 2 of a five-day full-body split. Chest is prioritised simply by placing it first — exercise order early in a session has been shown to increase strength and performance for that muscle.
- Heavy triples at 85% 1RM belong in a hypertrophy program: low-rep work builds muscle directly and, more importantly, lets you overload heavier in moderate rep ranges later.
- RDLs stay 2-3 reps shy of failure — stretch-loaded hamstring damage ruins subsequent sessions.
- Spare the spinal erectors wherever possible on high-frequency work: chest-supported rows, Smith machine shrugs.
- Biceps and triceps need only about 6-10 isolation sets per week when compound work is high-effort; alternate arm isolation day to day.

## The workout

**1. Bench press — 3 x 3 @ 85% 1RM**
[00:31] Not max-effort sets, but heavy. Justification for low reps in a hypertrophy plan: strength is specific, so a stronger bench requires heavy lifting; and getting stronger in low rep ranges lets you handle more load in moderate ranges, producing more cumulative tension over time. As you advance, continued strength gain matters for continued progress.

Direct evidence: **Schoenfeld and colleagues (2014)** split subjects into 3 sets of 10 vs. 7 sets of 3 and found equal muscle gain after eight weeks of full-body training when volume was matched ([Schoenfeld 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24714538)). Caveat from that study — the heavy workouts took over four times as long to complete and had a higher dropout rate, so heavy low-rep training builds muscle but isn't the practical way to accumulate it.

*Cueing:* Use **external** cues here, not the mind-muscle connection. Despite recent evidence supporting internal focus on some isolation exercises ([Schoenfeld 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/29533715)), over 15 years of research consistently finds an external focus on the movement itself improves motor performance, including strength. Sequence: squeeze the bar as hard as you can before lowering; think about bending the bar forward (this cues the elbows to tuck and maintains upper-back tightness); near the bottom actively puff the chest up; then push the floor away as you press up and back.

*Progression:* Alternate heavier triples with lighter fives week to week. Strength on threes carries to fives, which carries to eights later on.

**2. Low-to-high cable fly — 3 x 15 @ RPE 9**
[03:04] An isolation exercise, so it can be pushed closer to failure without much fatigue cost. Cue all three biomechanical actions of the pec at once by imagining hugging a very tall tree — you hug *and* lift the arms up. Palms facing up at the bottom and down at the top takes the pec from fully stretched to fully contracted. Together this trains horizontal shoulder adduction, shoulder flexion and internal rotation simultaneously.

**3. Romanian deadlift — 3 x 12, 2-3 reps from failure**
[03:34] RDLs cause heavy muscle damage by loading the hamstrings in a stretched position, and sore hamstrings badly degrade later training until the repeated bout effect kicks in around week 2 or 3. So bracket any RDL workout with low-impact hamstring work: lying leg curls on day 1, no direct hamstring work at all on day 3.

Similarly, no direct quad work today — day 1's squats are disruptive enough to warrant a day of rest before the leg press on day 3. The principle: you don't need to hit every muscle every day; take the weekly volume you need and space it across the week in a way that serves performance and recovery.

Treat RDLs as a lighter mind-muscle-connection movement — loaded too heavily, the lower back takes over. Set the hips straight back; for most people the endpoint is just below the knees, and going deeper usually means lower-back rounding.

**4. Chest-supported row — 3 x 15**
[04:34] T-bar machine or anything else, as long as it's genuinely chest-supported. The program alternates a vertical pull with a horizontal pull day to day, and today is the row. Chest support spares the lower back — on high-frequency training it's important not to overtax the spinal erectors, since they stabilise most free-weight compounds. Bracing the chest relieves them of that role while still hitting mid traps and lats. Exaggerate scapular protraction and retraction: let the weight pull the mid back apart at the bottom, then retract fully at the top.

**5. Standing Arnold press — 3 x 12**
[05:34] Standing adds a stabilisation demand, likely increasing lateral delt involvement. He emphasises lateral delts heavily in programming because in his experience they tolerate a lot of volume and are central to the broad-shouldered X-frame. Initiate the press by sweeping the dumbbells out, almost like a reverse pec deck, to involve the rear delts, then press up so the side and front delts take over.

**6. Single-arm tricep pressdown — 3 x 15**
[06:04] Bicep and tricep isolation alternate day to day — tricep days have no direct bicep work and vice versa. Both arms get plenty of indirect work from the compounds, so **about 6-10 isolation sets per week each** is enough, especially at high effort. Work one arm at a time starting with the weaker side. Let the elbow drift forward at the top to stretch the triceps, then hyperextend the shoulder at the bottom, turning it into a pushdown-kickback hybrid that hammers the long head.

**7. Smith machine shrug — 3 sets**
[06:34] Same logic as the chest-supported row: less stabilisation demand, less lower-back load. Free weights work fine if there's no Smith machine; he prefers it as a precaution given a history of back issues. Use a wider grip to emphasise scapular upward rotation — upper traps both elevate and upwardly rotate the scapula — and think about shrugging up *and in*, lifting the shoulders toward the ears. A shrug isn't a contest for maximum load; in a high-frequency plan, controlled reps with lighter weight and a strong mind-muscle connection are smarter.

## Practical application
- Prioritise a muscle by putting it first in the session, not by adding a whole extra day.
- Keep heavy compounds submaximal; save proximity to failure for isolation work.
- Plan the week's exercise sequence around recovery: what you damage today constrains tomorrow.
- Use external cues on compounds, internal cues on isolation.

## Caveats & unknowns
Written for intermediate to advanced lifters running five full-body days per week; the recovery-sparing exercise choices (chest-supported row, Smith shrug, lying leg curl over glute ham raise) are compromises specific to that frequency. He notes low-rep heavy training is unlikely to be sustainable long term as the primary method, given the time cost and dropout rate in Schoenfeld's data.
