# What "Science-Based Lifting" Actually Gets Right (And What Jeff Nippard Retracts)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Science-Based Lifting Is Over (My Bad)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pi0PQZFFo4A)) · 2024-12-23 · 17 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- Slow negatives do not build more muscle. Three studies show a 1–2 s eccentric matches a 3–4 s one. Control the weight; that's it.
- Technique cues, tweaks and exercise selection are all minor variables. Effort and volume are the two things hypertrophy science actually supports.
- Train 1–3 reps shy of failure on most sets, and push the last set to failure when the exercise allows it safely.
- Volume sweet spot: **8–20 hard sets per muscle per week.** As little as 4 sets works if pushed hard; up to 30 sets for a month or two to bring up a lagging body part (reduce other muscles' volume to compensate).
- His tier lists mean "slightly more effective vs slightly less effective," not "science-based vs bro science."

## Slow negatives: retracted
[01:34] Three studies:
- Preacher curls, 7 weeks, 1 s vs 4 s eccentric — same growth.
- Squats, 2 s vs 4 s eccentric — same growth.
- Unilateral leg extensions, 1 s vs 3 s eccentric on opposite legs — same growth.

Jeff called Mike Israetel, who is publicly associated with slow negatives. Mike's actual position: slowing the eccentric does **not** directly cause more hypertrophy. He uses it because (a) it is substantially safer, so it's an upside with no downside, and (b) some individuals get meaningfully better mind-muscle connection from it, which produces small but real differences. Mike's own eccentrics are 1–2 seconds — just enough to control the load.

A 2024 scientific review of lifting technique Jeff co-authored found that as long as a **whole rep lasts 2–8 seconds**, you're fine ([Androulakis Korakakis 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38249086/)). His practical rule:
- Under 1 s eccentric — too fast, slow it down.
- 1–7 s — the sweet spot; go by preference.
- 8 s or longer — too slow, speed up.

## Technique tips and tweaks
[05:09] He still uses several himself (setting the seat back on leg extensions, staying in the stretched half on calf raises, raising cable height on lateral raises), and he built a table sorting every cue he could find on social media into three buckets: cues supported by direct peer-reviewed evidence, cues with only indirect evidence, and cues with no science behind them. His verdict: **every one of them, including the evidence-backed ones, has a relatively minor impact**, and none matters as much as pushing hard and doing enough volume.

## "Perfect technique" is not the same as science-based
[06:40] No study has ever compared strict technique to loose/cheating technique, so calling strict form "more science-based" is misleading. He is helping run that study at Lehman College in New York.

Basic guidelines that do matter on a squat: keep the bar over midfoot so you don't fall forward or backward, keep a relatively neutral spine (a little butt wink at the bottom isn't worth panicking over), and squat as deep as you comfortably can, because deep squatting appears to cause more quad and glute growth. Toe angle, exact stance width, and thumb position have very small effects. Same for the lat pulldown: grab the bar, lock into the seat, pull to the chest, control the way up, push the set hard. Everything beyond that is educated guessing — coaching as art, not science.

[08:43] The harmful version of the "perfect form" idea is expecting rep 1 and rep 10 to look identical. If your range of motion shortens by an inch on the last rep, ending the set there leaves several reps in the tank. He endorses Sam Sulek's style of continuing pulldowns past the point where full reps are possible. Ugly late reps are fine. Do keep technique roughly consistent week to week, though, so progress tracking stays honest — if form degrades over time you'll still grow, but you're loading the target muscle less than your numbers suggest.

## Exercise selection: the tier-list correction
[09:45] Example: the half-kneeling one-arm cable lat pulldown (his S tier) vs the barbell row (his B tier). He clarifies he does **not** think the one-arm pulldown builds a bigger back than the barbell row — if forced to pick one exercise for life he'd take the barbell row 100%, because it's a compound that hits everything in the back, while the one-arm pulldown isolates the lats.

- **Barbell row:** best for time-limited lifters wanting bang-for-buck, and for anyone whose priority is getting stronger.
- **One-arm cable pulldown:** for someone who has already done their compounds and needs extra lat volume — it adds lat volume for almost no systemic fatigue.
- **Why the barbell row is only B tier:** better tools exist for the same job — the deficit Pendlay row (same job, bigger stretch, more standardised ROM) and the chest-supported machine row (same job, more stability, less fatigue).

Critically: **there has never been a single study directly measuring back muscle hypertrophy.** Not one. All that exists is biomechanics research and EMG studies, and it isn't known whether EMG predicts hypertrophy ([Vigotsky 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35006527/)). His rankings came from indirect evidence and personal experience. Any exercise taken to or near failure will build muscle; fine-tune from there using stretch, tension, overload and what feels good.

## Effort
[12:48] The latest meta-analysis pooled 15 studies comparing training to failure vs stopping short. Growth increases as you get closer to failure, but there's a point where too much failure training causes recovery problems. His rule: **1–3 reps shy of failure**. The practical hazard is that people who aim to leave 3 reps in the tank often actually leave 5–6 without realising, and that under-exertion costs gains. So he deliberately errs closer to failure: 1–2 RIR on most sets, last set of an exercise to true failure when he can fail safely.

## Volume
[13:19] Definitions he uses — 5 sets/muscle/week is low, 10 is moderate, 20+ is high, 30+ is ultra-high.

A volume meta-analysis published a few weeks before the video pooled 35 studies and found more volume caused more growth **all the way up to 42 sets per muscle per week**, with diminishing returns. The crucial caveat: most of those studies ran only 6–10 weeks, so long-term behaviour is unknown.

His recommendations:
- **8–20 sets per muscle per week** for most people.
- **Up to 30 sets/week for a month or two** to bring up a lagging body part, provided you cut volume elsewhere so you recover.
- **4 sets per muscle per week** still gets the job done if you're short on time and push those sets hard.

## Practical application
- Eccentrics: 1–7 seconds, whole rep 2–8 seconds. Don't chase ultra-slow.
- Stop hunting for cue-level optimisations; they're rounding errors next to effort and volume.
- Push past the point where reps get slightly shorter — don't end the set on an inch of lost ROM.
- 1–2 RIR on working sets, final set to failure where safe.
- 8–20 sets per muscle per week, with a 30-set specialisation block if something is lagging.
- Pick exercises you'll actually train hard on. Fun and sustainable beats optimal on paper.

## Caveats & unknowns
The volume meta-analysis's high-set findings come from 6–10 week studies; whether 30–42 sets/week holds up long term is unknown, which is why he keeps the recommendation at 8–20. No strict-vs-loose technique study exists yet (his is in progress). No direct hypertrophy study exists for back training at all. EMG's predictive value for hypertrophy is unestablished. And his overall framing: the best program is the one you'll stick to, not the most optimal one — occasional suboptimal-but-enjoyable workouts, cheat reps and dropped RPE discipline are not in conflict with smart training.
