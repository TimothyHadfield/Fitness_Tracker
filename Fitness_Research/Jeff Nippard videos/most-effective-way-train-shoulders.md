# Building the Delts: Evidence-Based Shoulder Training

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Effective Way to Train Shoulders | Science Explained (12 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KyTAraGimfE)) · 2017-06-07 · 10 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Pressing alone covers the front delt; dedicated front raises are largely redundant.
- Side and rear delts are where nearly everyone has room to grow, and both need direct isolation work.
- Reverse pec deck is the standout rear delt movement (~90% EMG across three separate studies).
- Minimum 8 isolation sets/week each for side and rear delts; 16-22 sets/week is the productive range for intermediate/advanced lifters.
- Train shoulders at least 2x/week; lagging side/rear delts tolerate 4-5x/week in Nippard's coaching experience.

## Anatomy [00:31]
Three deltoid heads, all inserting on the humerus:
- **Anterior** — origin on the clavicle; primary action shoulder flexion (front raise pattern).
- **Lateral** — origin on top of the scapula; primary action abduction (lateral raise pattern).
- **Posterior** — origin further back on the scapula; primary action horizontal abduction (reverse fly pattern).

The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint with a huge range of freedom, so the delts assist in many actions beyond these. Four rotator cuff muscles stabilise the joint and handle internal/external rotation. They add nothing visually but keeping them strong supports training longevity — cable internal/external rotations at the start of a session are a cheap insurance policy.

Fibre type: multiple lines of research put the deltoid at roughly a 50/50 split of type I and type II fibres, so a mix of higher and lower rep work is the sensible default rather than committing to one range.

## Pressing and the front delt [02:03]
Mark Rippetoe calls the overhead press the most useful upper-body exercise for sports conditioning, and Nippard would pick a press if limited to one shoulder movement.

**Bench/press angle.** A 2010 study (name garbled in captions, sounds like "Trebs et al.") found a flat 0° bench produced the *worst* anterior delt EMG compared with 28°, 44° and 56° inclines, which were all similar to each other with a mild trend favouring steeper angles. This matches earlier work from Barnett et al. and a 2015 paper (name unclear in captions). Practical read: anything from roughly 28° to 90° hits the front delt hard; no single angle is clearly superior, though more incline trends toward more delt.

**Dumbbells vs barbells.** A 2013 study compared standing barbell, standing dumbbell, seated barbell and seated dumbbell presses at 80% of 1RM for 5 reps. Dumbbells beat barbells for front delt activation in both standing and seated versions. Side and rear delts were far less active in all vertical presses, but the standing dumbbell press topped those two heads as well.

**Front raises.** Largely unnecessary — redundant with pressing and weaker at the job. Sweeney (2014) measured front raises at 57% of maximum voluntary contraction versus 74% for the dumbbell shoulder press. One horizontal press plus one vertical press per routine is enough for the anterior delt.

## Side delts [04:06]
Author Michael Gundill observed that bodybuilders carry roughly 5x the front delt development of untrained people, but only 3x the side delts and a mere 10-15% more rear delt size — so the side and rear heads are where the upside sits.

Pressing barely touches them. A 2013 paper found ~20% activity in the side delts during a Smith machine shoulder press versus ~70% for the front delts; a 2014 paper agreed. Free-weight dumbbell pressing recruits the side delts more than the Smith machine, likely because they take on a stabilising role that the fixed bar removes — but it still won't isolate them.

Best abduction exercises (2013 EMG study, name garbled — likely "Botton et al."): **dumbbell lateral raise, cable lateral raise, reverse pec deck**.

Technique points:
- Internally rotating the shoulder (pinkies up) should increase lateral delt involvement on the basis of fibre orientation and line of pull, supported by EMG data from a trio of researchers whose names the captions garble. But many experts think it raises impingement risk, so lifters with shoulder history may want to skip it. Nippard has used the cue for 11 years without issue, stopping at or just below shoulder height and keeping the scapula retracted.
- Cables give a more constant resistance curve. Dumbbells peak at the top of the range with essentially zero tension at the bottom.
- Leaning away into the direction of the raise shifts emphasis off the supraspinatus and onto the side delt. Nippard's preferred variation is the lean-away between-the-legs cable lateral raise.

**Upright row.** A 2013 study by McAllister et al. compared grip widths and found a wide grip produced more side delt EMG than shoulder-width — and more rear delt and trap activity too. Because bad upright row technique is linked to impingement, the authors recommend keeping elbow elevation below shoulder height. Nippard prefers a rope (freer shoulder position, more comfortable end range) and cues pulling the rope apart during the row.

## Rear delts [06:41]
Nearly silent in shoulder presses, and barely active in bench press, push-ups and pressing generally. Bret Contreras: isolation work for the rear delts vastly outperforms compound movements.

Top three from the same 2013 EMG study: **reverse pec deck, incline lat pulldown, seated row**. Three separate studies (Franke, "Botton", Schoenfeld) independently put the reverse pec deck around 90% EMG activity for the rear delts.

Grip: a 2013 Schoenfeld study found a neutral (palms facing) hand position raised mean rear delt activity, but co-author Bret Contreras later noted some subjects did better with a pronated grip — test both. Similar horizontal-abduction movements (reverse flies, reverse cable crossovers) likely recruit well too, though direct data is limited. Rows work, but the traps can take over, so include true isolation work on shoulder or back days.

## Volume and frequency [07:41]
Dr. Mike Israetel's position: no front delt isolation needed, since horizontal plus incline/vertical pressing covers it. For side and rear delts, 8 isolation sets/week is the floor for progress, with roughly 16-22 weekly sets being close to optimal for most intermediate to advanced trainees. Caveat: many "isolation" exercises hit both side and rear delts and count as a set for each.

Frequency of at least 2x/week is backed by the current training-frequency literature. Nippard's own coaching experience is that side and rear delt isolation can be run 4-5x/week without recovery problems, especially when those heads are lagging.

## Practical application
- Anchor the session on pressing — one vertical, one horizontal per week. Prefer dumbbells over barbells and inclines over flat if front delt growth is the aim. Skip front raises.
- Add direct abduction work: dumbbell or cable lateral raises, ideally leaning away on cables for a fuller resistance curve. Optional wide-grip rope upright row with elbows kept below shoulder height.
- Add direct horizontal abduction work: reverse pec deck first choice; reverse flies or reverse cable crossovers as substitutes. Try both neutral and pronated grips.
- Aim for 8 sets/week minimum, 16-22 for a real push, for each of side and rear delts, spread over at least two sessions.
- Mix rep ranges — the delts are roughly half slow- and half fast-twitch.
- Cable internal/external rotations at the start of the workout to keep the cuff healthy.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Almost all of this rests on acute EMG activation data, not long-term hypertrophy trials. High activation is a proxy for growth, not proof of it.
- Several researcher names are mangled by the auto-captions and should not be cited without verification: the 2010 incline study ("Trebzatal"), the 2015 follow-up ("Laatal"), the 2013 exercise-ranking study ("Button Natal", probably Botton), the internal-rotation EMG trio ("Bohack, Bearhands and Busky"), and "Frank" (possibly Franke). Rippetoe, Barnett, McAllister, Schoenfeld, Contreras, Gundill and Israetel are confidently identified.
- The pinkies-up lateral raise cue is a genuine risk/reward trade-off with no consensus. Nippard's 11 years without injury is a single anecdote, not evidence.
- Upright rows carry documented impingement risk when performed with the elbows above shoulder height.
- The 16-22 set recommendation is an expert opinion applied to intermediate/advanced lifters, not a directly tested dose-response finding, and double counting across side/rear delt exercises makes it easy to overshoot.
- Video is from 2017; frequency and volume literature has moved since.
