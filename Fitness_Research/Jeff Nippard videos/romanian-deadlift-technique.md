# Romanian Deadlift (RDL): Technique for Hamstring and Glute Development

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "HOW TO DO ROMANIAN DEADLIFTS (RDLs): Build Beefy Hamstrings With Perfect Technique" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_oyxCn2iSjU)) · 2019-09-10 · 6 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- The RDL starts out of a rack and ends just below the knees; the stiff-leg deadlift starts from the floor and lets the plates touch down. For glute and hamstring development he prefers the RDL; the stiff-leg has more carryover to the conventional deadlift off the floor.
- Push the hips straight *back*, don't drop them down, and keep the shins vertical — imagine a wall in front of your knees.
- Stop the descent when your hips can no longer travel back without the lower back rounding or the torso pitching forward. For most people that lands between just below the knee and mid-shin.
- More range of motion is not better here. Lowering to the floor usually adds lumbar rounding, not hip range.
- The hamstrings cross both hip and knee, so a good program includes both a knee-flexion movement (leg curl) and a hip-hinge movement (RDL).

## What it trains [01:07]
Essentially pure hip extension — the glutes and hamstrings contract to straighten the hips. There is also a strong isometric lumbar extension contraction as the spinal erectors hold the lower back neutral.

## Setup [01:38]
- Set the bar in the rack just below the height of your deadlift lockout.
- Take a roughly shoulder-width grip, hands just outside the thighs.
- Grip: because the RDL is loaded lighter than a conventional deadlift, double overhand should be fine. An alternate grip can be used against grip fatigue, or straps on heavier sets — just don't become dependent on straps for every lift.
- Stand up with the bar and take three steps back, the same walkout as a squat.
- Stance roughly shoulder width, toes pointed out about 10 degrees, legs just inside the hands.
- The sumo-stance RDL is something he has used to assist sumo deadlift strength, but he finds it awkward and most people lack the flexibility to do it properly, so he recommends the conventional stance.
- Brace before the negative by lifting the chest and stomach up, which keeps the spine extended — hold that spinal position for the whole lift. You can also pack the lats by pushing back slightly against the bar.

## Execution [02:38]
**Descent.** Push the hips straight back with the shins completely vertical. A slight knee bend is good, but the knees must not travel forward the way they do in a squat — picture a wall directly in front of the knees. The bar stays centred over the middle of the foot and tight to the shins.

**Depth.** Lower only to the point where you can no longer set the hips further back without the lower back rounding or the torso coming forward. Unless you have extremely long arms or exceptional mobility, that means finishing somewhere between just below knee level and mid-shin. The plates should not touch the floor on an RDL.

**Ascent.** Reverse by pushing the hips forward and lifting the chest, keeping the bar over the mid-foot.

**Glute vs hamstring emphasis.** To involve the glutes more, squeeze them hard at the top, posteriorly tilting the pelvis and moving the bar slightly forward. Nippard personally uses the RDL for hamstring emphasis and ends at or near full hip extension without that extra posterior tilt — the glutes contribute to hip extension anyway and stay highly active without the added squeeze.

**Advanced variation.** Putting a 5 lb plate under the toes enhances the felt hamstring stretch for some people. He has seen no research on this, but reports a stronger mind-muscle connection with the distal (lower) hamstrings and suspects there may be regional activation differences.

## Common errors [04:09]
He says people botch technique on the RDL more than on any exercise covered in the series to that point.
1. **Over-extending the range of motion.** People carry over pride in deep squats and full-range pull-ups and assume more range is always better. Lowering the plates to the floor typically produces lumbar rounding rather than extra hip range. Standing on a bench or jump box to go even deeper only raises tear risk without adding meaningful hamstring tension. Some women with strong hamstring flexibility and hip mobility can touch the plates down without losing spinal position, but for the vast majority of men and women the lift should end just above mid-shin.
2. **Knees travelling forward.** Usually caused by loading too heavily, which turns the lift into a top-half conventional deadlift. More dangerous, and it shifts tension off the hamstrings and glutes onto the quads.
3. **Dropping the hips down instead of setting them straight back.** This cascades into the bar drifting too far forward, the knees bending too much, and a loss of upper-body rigidity and spinal position.

## Alternatives [05:40]
- **Dumbbell RDL** — identical movement pattern with dumbbells instead of a barbell.
- **Good morning** — another hip-hinge, hip-dominant option.

## Practical application
Rack the bar just below deadlift lockout height, walk it out like a squat, feet shoulder width with 10 degrees of toe flare. Brace with the chest and stomach lifted, hips back with vertical shins, bar shaving the legs over the mid-foot, and stop at the point spinal position starts to go — usually just below the knee to mid-shin. Drive the hips forward to stand. Pair it with a leg curl so the hamstrings get both hip-extension and knee-flexion work. Keep the load light enough that the hips lead and the knees stay put.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The plate-under-toes trick is explicitly unsupported by research he's aware of — it's his subjective feel plus a hypothesis about regional activation.
- Correct end range is individual, depending on arm length and mobility; the knee-to-mid-shin guidance is a default, not a fixed standard.
- The RDL vs stiff-leg deadlift distinction is his own definition in an ongoing semantic debate.
