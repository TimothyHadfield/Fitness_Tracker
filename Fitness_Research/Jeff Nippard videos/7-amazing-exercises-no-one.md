# Seven Underrated S-Tier Exercises Almost Nobody Does

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "7 Amazing Exercises No One Does (ALL S TIER)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qpRGnTTcLpo)) · 2025-09-27 · 16 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Seven movements he does weekly: dead hangs, Zottman hammer curls, incline dumbbell Y raises, sideways one-arm rear delt flies, dragon flags, Kelso shrugs, and seated cable deadlifts.
- The common theme: most of them fix a tension-profile or range-of-motion problem in a standard exercise — usually by adding load in the lengthened position.
- Training hard and consistently still matters more than exact exercise selection.

## 1. Dead hang
Improves shoulder health, grows forearms, builds core strength and grip endurance, and decompresses the spine. Opens the shoulder joint so rotator cuff tendons and muscles move more freely, which can help with shoulder pain. The spinal decompression evidence is mixed, but he gets relief from it, especially in the days after heavy squats or deadlifts.

His own max: 2:45 (advanced, not quite elite). He coached a friend from a 6-second max to 60 seconds in 30 days.

**Technique:** chalk your hands (liquid chalk if the gym has none — makes a huge difference). Grip just outside shoulder width. Start with a *looser* grip so the forearms don't fatigue early, and grip harder only once you start slipping — pace it like a race. For max time, brace the core but don't flex hard enough to tire yourself. For decompression, start braced then let the hips sink and stretch out. Legs straight down, feet together, toes pointed down to stop swinging. Keep a consistent breathing rhythm; don't hold your breath. If you can't hang with bodyweight, loop a band over the bar and step into it, or use an assisted pull-up machine.

## 2. Zottman hammer curl
Named after 1800s strongman George Zottman, modified. Rationale: you are 40–50% stronger eccentrically than concentrically, so a standard curl always overloads the positive and under-loads the negative — and the negative is crucial for growth.

**Technique:** curl up with a hammer grip (letting brachialis and brachioradialis assist), stop just before the arm is vertical, rotate to a supinated palms-up grip, and lower under control in a wide sweeping arc so the biceps take the full load. Rotate back to hammer at the bottom and repeat. Grip the middle of the handle — don't let your thumb rest against the dumbbell head. **8–10 reps.**

He does not think it adds inches over standard curls, but it makes biomechanical sense. If you prefer normal dumbbell curls, just slow the negative to even out the strength curve.

## 3. Incline dumbbell Y raise
A standard dumbbell lateral raise has zero tension at the bottom and peak tension only at the top, because gravity pulls straight down. New research shows dumbbell laterals still grow side delts fine if trained hard, but he finds the lopsided profile makes the mind-muscle connection harder.

**Technique:** bench at roughly **20–30° incline** (it should feel like you're leaning quite far back). Brace the upper back against the bench, chest up. From the sides, lift the dumbbells up and out in a Y. Think about squeezing the side delt fibres together on the way up and feeling them stretch apart on the way down. Stop at your sides at the bottom — don't let the dumbbells swing back to hanging vertically or you lose tension.

Lifting in a Y hits the fibres between the front and side heads, adding shape from the front. Many people also find the scapular plane (~30° forward of straight lateral) more comfortable for the shoulder.

## 4. Sideways one-arm rear delt fly on the pec deck
The standard reverse pec deck is effectively half reps in a shortened position — the rear delts never get lengthened, the equivalent of doing top-half-only bicep curls. Rear delts don't approach a stretch until the arm reaches across the body.

**Technique:** turn sideways rather than facing the pad, one arm at a time, and sweep the arm out across the body. Cue from Dr. Mike: imagine a big pile of money on the ground and however much area you sweep is how much you keep — this makes you sweep with the rear delt instead of yanking back with the back and biceps. You don't need to go all the way around.

**Alternatives:** cables (still reach across the body), or lying on a bench with a dumbbell stretched across the body and lifted back up.

## 5. Dragon flags
Bruce Lee's go-to core movement. Hits the lower abs hardest since you're lifting the legs, but the whole six-pack contracts isometrically to keep the spine extended. Obliques stabilise, serratus anchors the back and shoulders, and there is some lat activation. Expect brutal upper-ab soreness lasting 5–6 days the first time.

**Technique:** grab the top of a bench, keep hips and knees straight, lift your whole body including the glutes off the bench until the legs are at roughly **60–80°**, then lower back down under control keeping the hips straight. **4–8 reps** or as many as you can get.

**Progression:** lying leg raises → 8–10 reps → bent-knee dragon flags (shorter lever, much easier) → 8–10 reps → full dragon flags.

He prefers these to hanging leg raises, where there is nothing stabilising the torso so you swing and lose tension. A Roman chair fixes most of that but your back, arms and shoulders still support your bodyweight; the dragon flag avoids both problems.

For visible abs you also need to be around **10–12% body fat as a male and around 20% as a female**.

## 6. Kelso shrug
Underrated back thickness builder. Completely isolates the mid traps and rhomboids without fatiguing the lower back or involving the biceps. Despite the name it's more of a row than a shrug: on a normal row you bend the elbows and squeeze the back at once; here the elbows stay locked and you isolate the scapular squeeze.

Rationale: the elbow flexion portion of a row does nothing for your back — that's biceps. Research shows rows do grow biceps to some extent, but curls grow them about twice as well. Since the biceps are far smaller than the back, they limit the tension the back receives. Evidence: after hitting failure on a normal row you can usually crank out another 4–5 Kelso shrugs — your biceps and rear delts failed, your mid back didn't.

**Technique:** on a chest-supported T-bar row machine, chest against the pad, grip roughly **1.5x shoulder width**. Unrack and with completely locked arms squeeze the shoulder blades together as hard as possible. **Pause ~1 second at the top**, then let the shoulder blades peel apart on the negative. Done right the weight only travels about **4–5 inches** — that is the real full ROM of the mid back.

**Alternative:** brace against an incline bench with dumbbells.

Rows are still a great overall back developer; Kelso shrugs are for isolating mid-back thickness specifically.

## 7. Seated cable deadlift
A glute and hamstring builder that gets the stimulus without the fatigue cost of a heavy barbell hinge. Hips fixed with glutes planted on the bench means more stability and much less strain on back, core and quads.

**Technique:** grip just outside shoulder width, feet planted on the platform with toes turned slightly out to engage the glutes, chest tall. Squat the weight up and extend the hips to a full lockout — glutes stay planted throughout. Begin each rep by unlocking hips and knees, lowering the bar tight to the body. You can go far lower than a barbell allows since no plates limit the ROM — go as low as you can without the lower back rounding (for him, about ankle level). Squeeze the glutes hard to extend the hips, shoulders back and chest up to lockout. It's a deadlift, not a row — don't yank with the arms. A hard glute squeeze at the top improves the connection.

**Variations:** keep the knees straighter for a Romanian deadlift version with more hamstring emphasis. Single-leg works well since balance isn't a factor — for left/right imbalances, start with the weaker side, go to or near failure, then match the reps on the stronger side.

Not better than barbell deadlifts, but easier to recover from, and the added stability improves the mind-muscle connection.

## Practical application
- Add these as substitutions or accessories, not replacements for the fundamentals — he still does barbell RDLs every week and still rates rows highly.
- The three highest-leverage fixes here: use the sideways rear delt fly if all your rear delt work is shortened-position half reps; use Kelso shrugs if your rows always fail on the biceps; use incline Y raises if you can't feel your side delts on dumbbell laterals.
- Test your dead hang time as a shoulder-health and grip benchmark.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The spinal decompression benefit of dead hangs has mixed scientific evidence; the shoulder-mobility rationale is stronger.
- He explicitly does not claim the Zottman variation or the deep rear delt stretch will dramatically increase gains — the framing is "not leaving gains on the table," not "double your growth."
- Dumbbell lateral raises still work fine per recent research; the Y raise is a preference-driven improvement, not a correction of something broken.
