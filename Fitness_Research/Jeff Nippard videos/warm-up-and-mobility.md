# Science-Based Warm-Up and Mobility Routine (Upper and Lower Body)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Effective Science-Based Warm Up & Mobility Routine (Full Body)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E81GN-3A8XM)) · 2019-07-03 · 9 min
**Topic:** Recovery

## Bottom line
- Actually raising core body temperature is the most important part of a warm-up — not elaborate mobility work.
- Three phases: general warm-up (5-10 min cardio to a light sweat), specific warm-up (foam rolling plus dynamic stretching, 3-4 min lower body, less upper), then progressive pyramid loading up to your working weight.
- Use dynamic stretching, not long static holds, before lifting.
- Only main heavy compound lifts need a full pyramid; most other exercises you can walk straight into.
- Total time: roughly 10-15 minutes for upper body, somewhat more for lower.

## Why warm up [00:00]
Two purposes: reducing injury risk, and increasing strength performance in the session (which may indirectly improve size gains over time).

- **Injury:** a systematic review by Fradkin and colleagues examined five studies on warming up and injury reduction. Three of five showed reduced injury risk. The two that showed no benefit had warm-ups weighted toward stretching rather than raising core body temperature — implying that literally warming the body up is the key factor. People who spend half their gym time on elaborate mobility drills may not be preventing much extra injury.
- **Performance:** a paper by McGowan and colleagues outlined the established benefits of a higher body temperature, including increased muscle metabolism and better phosphocreatine and ATP utilisation, leading to better power output.
- **Psychological:** rehearsing the workout mentally and running through the cues for the muscle you're about to train primes you to lift.

## Phase 1 — General warm-up [01:31]
Goal: get blood flowing and raise core body temperature. The literature recommends reaching 55-65% of max heart rate; for most young active people that's roughly 100-120 bpm. His practical guide is simply to break a light sweat. Moderate-pace incline treadmill or Stairmaster usually gets you there in 5-10 minutes — faster if you wear a baggy hoodie, which he recommends for cold climates, naturally cold people, or early-morning training when core body temperature is at its lowest.

You can shorten it with moderate-intensity intervals: 20-30 seconds assertive, about a minute easy, repeat until you feel a light sweat. Stop once you get there — this isn't cardio and isn't meant to burn calories. Do it before every session. He describes it as clear upside with no downside: better mood, clearer head, better performance, less injury risk.

## Phase 2 — Specific warm-up [02:32]
Goal: loosen the muscles and take the joints through the range of motion the session will demand. Differs for upper and lower body.

**Lower body foam rolling (2-3 min).** Focus on tight, active muscles. He notes foam rolling is somewhat overhyped, but has been shown to reduce soreness and improve range of motion without hurting strength performance — a modality with potential benefit and no obvious downside is worth doing if you have the time. His routine: ~15-20 seconds on the quads; upper and lower back with the abs flexed (don't let the lower back loosely hyperextend over the roller — keeping the abs tight loosens the whole back better); inner thighs and adductors; then cross the leg over the knee to stretch the glutes and piriformis before rolling them. A lacrosse ball works for very tight hamstrings or glutes.

**Lower body dynamic stretching.** Dynamic stretching has been shown to improve performance in several studies, unlike static stretching, which has been associated with performance detriments — though static stretching is probably not as bad as once thought: the negative effect only seems to matter for intense holds longer than about 30 seconds per muscle. His sequence:
- A few light walking lunges
- 10-12 leg swings front to back (hamstrings)
- 10-12 leg swings side to side (hip adductors and groin)
- 10-12 step-throughs with a 1-2 second pause, knee driven forward (glutes)
- Optional on tight days: Scorpion stretch, knee-drive rotating squat

**Lift-specific additions.**
- Sumo deadlift days: squat-and-reach stretch to open the hips and adductors for the wide stance — grab your toes with a slight knee bend, squat down and lift the chest until you feel a strong stretch in the inner thighs and hamstrings, hold 10-15 seconds. Cossack squats also work here (squat down to one side, stretching the adductors); start with hands on the floor or holding something in front if balance is an issue.
- Trouble hitting squat depth from tight ankles or calves: slow eccentric calf raises focusing on the stretch at the bottom.

This phase should take about 3-4 minutes depending on how many drills you include.

**Upper body version (shorter).** After the general cardio: 10-12 small arm circles building into big arm circles, 10-12 side-to-side arm swings, ~15 band pull-aparts (great for preventing bench-related shoulder pain in his experience), then banded external rotations and banded face pulls to warm the rotator cuff. If shoulder mobility lags, add wall slides as a prehab drill (elbows pinned to the wall, arms lifted overhead) and overhead dumbbell side bends to help get the arms overhead for any vertical pressing. He usually skips foam rolling on upper days, but before benching will sometimes roll the thoracic spine and hip flexors — this makes the arch much easier to set and lets you place the feet further back without risking a hip flexor strain.

## Phase 3 — Progressive pyramid loading [05:35]
Pyramid up to the working weight for the day. His worked example: three sets of six on the squat at 77.5% of 1RM, which is 330 lb for him.
- Empty bar x 10-12, rest ~30 seconds
- 135 x 4-5
- 225 x 3-4
- 275 x 1-2
- Then three working sets of 6 at 330

For most sets in the 4-10 rep range, three to four progressively building pyramid sets are enough. Decrease reps as the weight rises so you don't exhaust yourself — the point is psychological and physical preparation for the working weight, not fatiguing the muscle. Expect another 5-10 minutes depending on how heavy the target is.

You only need a full pyramid for the main heavy compound lifts; you can jump into most other exercises without one. On upper body, working weights are lighter, so two or three pyramid sets usually suffice — and if you open with a row or a pull-up, one or two may be enough.

## Practical application
Every session: 5-10 min cardio to a light sweat, then the dynamic sequence for the body part you're training, then pyramid up on the first heavy compound only. Total upper-body warm-up around 10-15 minutes. To cut time, drop the foam rolling (it isn't mandatory) and pick exercises that don't need a long pyramid, such as Bulgarian split squats or lunges. The three principles that matter: raise body temperature, take the active joints through their full range of motion, and progressively work up to your working weight.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Only three of five studies in the Fradkin review found reduced injury risk from warming up; the benefit appears tied to temperature, not stretching.
- Foam rolling is described as overhyped — worth doing for soreness and range of motion, but optional.
- Static stretching's performance penalty appears limited to intense holds over ~30 seconds per muscle.
- Nippard acknowledges some people will find this total time investment excessive, and offers the shortcuts above.
