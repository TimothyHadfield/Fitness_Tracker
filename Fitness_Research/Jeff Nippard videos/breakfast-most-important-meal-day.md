# Is Breakfast the Most Important Meal of the Day?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Is BREAKFAST The Most Important Meal Of The Day? (What The Science Says)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tDzpOdhAygA)) · 2018-06-11 · 9 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Busted. Breakfast eaters and breakfast skippers show no difference in weight change, and no convincing metabolic or health effect of eating breakfast has been documented.
- Eating breakfast does not "stoke the metabolism" — resting metabolic rate is identical between groups.
- Skippers do burn fewer calories through spontaneous physical activity, but they also eat fewer calories; the two roughly cancel out.
- The one clear case for breakfast: if you train in the morning and performance that day matters, a carbohydrate-rich breakfast is genuinely important.
- For muscle, total daily protein (roughly 0.8-1 g per pound of bodyweight) is what matters; extreme delays of the first meal may cost you some muscle protein synthesis opportunity, but moderate delays don't.

## Where the myth came from [00:31]
*Good Health* magazine ran an article in 1917 asserting outright that breakfast was the most important meal of the day, should not be rushed, and should be eaten by the whole family together. No evidence was offered. John Harvey Kellogg — inventor of corn flakes — was an editor of the magazine at the time, which likely helped push the idea into the public mind. It also sounds intuitive: eat first thing to fuel the day.

## Defining breakfast
Literally, "breakfast" means breaking a fast, so any first meal qualifies — not a useful definition. The video uses the operational definition from Betts and colleagues (2016): **any meal of at least 50 calories eaten within two hours of waking**.

## Metabolism and energy balance [01:32]
A 2014 study from Betts and colleagues examined breakfast skipping and metabolism:

- **Resting metabolic rate:** no difference between groups. Diet-induced thermogenesis is too small a slice of total daily energy expenditure for differences here to matter.
- **Physical activity thermogenesis:** the skipping group burned significantly fewer calories through activity. This is described elsewhere as a compensatory feedback mechanism — the body defends against a perceived energy deficit by unconsciously reducing movement.
- **Intake side:** skippers did overeat later, but only by ~161 calories on average after skipping a 700-calorie breakfast — leaving them **539 calories per day lower** than the breakfast eaters.

So skippers eat less but also burn less. Netted out, the difference between the two approaches is small, and the authors concluded that these observations "begin to explain why a resolution to start skipping breakfast may not predict the degree of weight loss one might expect."

## Muscle, training and protein timing [04:33]
Two potential concerns:

1. **Training performance.** Some people train well fasted, but in Nippard's coaching experience most are stronger and more fatigue-resistant with a pre-workout meal. As Betts's review put it, if your morning involves exercise and that day's performance is a priority, a carbohydrate-rich breakfast *is* the most important meal. Train later in the day, or thrive fasted, and it matters much less.
2. **Muscle protein synthesis.** Based on MPS data, roughly **4-5 meals per day with at least 20 g of protein each** appears best. Delaying breakfast to noon and eating a final meal before bed still fits 4-5 meals in comfortably. But progressively narrowing the eating window raises the risk of missing that maximum MPS stimulation. (He points to his interview with protein researcher Jorn Trommelen for detail — captions garble the name.)

Bottom line for muscle: as long as total daily protein is sufficient (~0.8-1 g/lb), how you distribute it probably has no measurable impact. Extreme first-meal delays are the only likely problem.

## Health claims [06:35]
Epidemiological research has consistently associated infrequent breakfast consumption with higher risk of adiposity, diabetes and cardiovascular disease. But correlation isn't causation, and habitual breakfast eaters also tend to be non-smokers, drink less alcohol, eat less fat, eat more fibre and micronutrients, and be more physically active. It remains unestablished whether breakfast is causal or simply a marker of a healthy lifestyle.

Skipping breakfast could plausibly reduce intake of certain vitamins and minerals if they aren't made up later in the day. Assuming nutrient intake is matched, Nippard's view is that the health effects of skipping breakfast don't extend far in either direction.

**Exception:** he would not recommend that children or growing adolescents intentionally skip breakfast or any meal — a position mirrored by the Canadian Paediatric Society, which discourages teenagers from fasting and skipping meals. The reasoning is body image and encouraging a diet built on including nutrient-rich foods rather than eliminating foods or mealtimes.

## Practical application
- Base the decision on appetite, schedule, and whichever pattern lets you hold a calorie deficit most consistently.
- Wake up ravenous? Eat breakfast to blunt hunger, then moderate intake across the rest of the day.
- Low morning appetite (Nippard included)? Work with it — skew calories later in the day to match your hunger pattern.
- Training in the morning with performance on the line? Eat a carb-rich breakfast.
- Hit your daily protein target (~0.8-1 g/lb) across roughly 4-5 feedings of 20 g+; don't compress the eating window so far that you can't.
- Children and adolescents: don't skip meals.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Meal-timing and MPS research was still developing at the time of the video; the exact cost of a very narrow eating window is not pinned down.
- The observational health data on breakfast is heavily confounded by healthy-user effects and cannot establish causation.
