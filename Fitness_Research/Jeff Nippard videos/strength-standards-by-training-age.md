# Strength standards from noob to freak

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How Strong Should You Be? (Noob To Freak)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LrDJXIQ_-eg)) · 2021-11-09 · 14 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Standards are expressed as multiples of bodyweight for a **1RM** on squat, bench and deadlift.
- Rough timeline: beginner by 6 months–2 years, intermediate by 2–5 years, advanced around 5 years, elite somewhere in the 5–10 year range. Freak requires genetics, not just patience.
- The method for getting to the next level changes at each level. Linear progression works until roughly the 1–2 year mark, then stops.
- Genetics move the timeline hard in both directions — outstanding genetics can reach advanced in a couple of years, below-average genetics can take five to ten.

## The standards

Bodyweight multiples, male:

| Level | Squat | Bench | Deadlift |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beginner | 1.25× | 1.0× | 1.5× |
| Intermediate | 1.25–1.75× | 1.0–1.5× | 1.5–2.25× |
| Advanced | 1.75–2.5× | 1.5–2.0× | 2.25–3.0× |
| Elite | 2.5–3.0× | 2.0–2.25× | 3.0–3.5× |
| Freak | 3.0×+ | 2.25×+ | 3.5×+ |

Noob (first 3–6 months) isn't a ratio — it's roughly an empty bar up to 135 lb on squat and deadlift, 45–95 lb on bench. Deadlift is the noisy one; some people pull 225 on day one. Getting a plate per side on the bar is the marker for leaving noob territory.

Female standards run lower across the board; at the top end, freak is about 2.25× squat, 1.25× bench, 3.0× deadlift.

For scale, his own lifts at the time: 480 lb squat, 380 lb bench, 530 lb deadlift at 180 lb bodyweight — 2.6× / 2.1× / 2.9×. That puts his squat and bench at the bottom of elite and his deadlift, his weakest lift, in advanced.

## Three conditions on using them [01:32]
1. **Technique is assumed constant.** Full-depth squats, and a paused bench — no cutting range of motion to jump a tier.
2. **Bodyweight ratios break down at the extremes.** They're built for natural, reasonably lean lifters: under 20–25% bodyfat for men, under 30–35% for women. Above 250 lb / 113 kg the ratios skew against you, so plug your weight in as 250 lb regardless of whether you're 250, 300 or 350.
3. **These are one-rep maxes**, but you don't need to test one. Take a recent hard set under about 5 reps taken near failure and run it through a 1RM calculator. It's an estimate — maxing is its own skill — but it lands you in the right neighbourhood.

## How to get to the next level

**Noob → beginner.** Work on technique and add the minimum increment to the bar every single session, generally in the 3–6 rep range for strength work.

**Beginner → intermediate.** Still linear: +5 lb or +1 rep per session in the same 3–6 rep range. Keep squat, bench and deadlift fixed and progressing steadily; vary the accessory work freely to keep it enjoyable. Technique should be consistent rep to rep, though still shaky near a max. When PRs stop coming every session without wrecking technique or recovery, you've moved up.

**Intermediate → advanced [06:36].** Drop pure linear progression for undulating work. Example on squat: one heavy day at 3–5 reps and high exertion, plus one lighter day that's either hypertrophy work at 6–10 reps or speed/technique work at 2–4 reps at lower exertion. This is daily undulating periodization (DUP). The lifts still climb on average, but the curve is flatter and less linear. This is where the "lifetime intermediate" gets stuck.

**Advanced → elite [08:39].** Programming has to get deliberate:
- **Specialization phases** — focus one lift while holding the others at maintenance. His example: Alex (Alpha Destiny) hit a 405 lb bench, but only by dropping the other lifts to maintenance for recovery.
- **Weak-point work** — e.g. for a failing deadlift lockout, chains, hip thrusts and block pulls.
- **Dedicated hypertrophy blocks** — once technique is handled, muscle size becomes the ceiling. Several months to a year of moderate-to-high-rep training in a calorie surplus.

## Where the standards came from
His own coaching experience, competitive powerlifting meet data, and existing scales from Mark Rippetoe and Tim Henriques.

## What "freak" looks like
- **Taylor Atwood**, drug-tested raw, 163 lb class: 668 lb squat (~4× bodyweight), 430 lb bench (2.6×), 750 lb deadlift (4.6×).
- **Jessica Buettner**, drug-tested raw, 167 lb / 76 kg class: 464 lb squat (2.77×), 231 lb bench (1.4×), 546 lb deadlift (over 3.25×).

## Caveats & unknowns
Strength is heavily genetic and leverages matter — short arms and a deep ribcage favour the bench but hurt the deadlift; short femurs favour the squat. Leverage disadvantages are surmountable with programming and technique changes (Layne Norton set a squat world record with unusually long femurs), but they change how hard you have to work. Tendon resilience, neural drive and starting muscle mass vary too.

Elite is not available to everyone, and he's explicit that reaching it may demand a level of sacrifice many lifters reasonably won't want to make. Freak requires genetics outright. His realistic framing: with time, good programming and dedication, a lot of natural lifters can hit elite on one or two of the three lifts — not necessarily all three.
