# Free Weights vs Machines for Delts, and the Best Press Angle (6 Studies)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Free Weights vs Machines for Big Delts (6 Studies Explained) | Vegas Hotel Room Tour" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E9KnHyYizd4)) · 2016-10-01 · 12 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- **A ~40° incline press with a wide grip** is the best single pressing choice for front delts — nearly as much delt activation as a vertical press, plus far more pec involvement.
- **Free weights vs machines makes little difference for anterior delt activation.** Free weights show a small edge for the **lateral** delt, attributable to stabilisation demand.
- That free-weight edge appears mainly at **lighter loads (60% 1RM)** and **disappears at 80% 1RM**.
- Use **both**, and put the free-weight movement **early in the session** when you are fresh.
- **Preacher curls and standing curls complement each other** — opposite activation profiles across the range of motion, so pair them.

## Press angle and grip width: front delt activation [05:19]
**Barnett et al. 1995** is the main study. It compared four bench angles:
- **−8° (decline)**
- **0° (flat / horizontal)**
- **40° (incline)**
- **90° (vertical / military press)**

Findings:
- **Anterior deltoid activation was greatest with the incline and vertical presses**, well above flat and decline.
- **A wide grip produced more delt activation than a narrow grip** on these movements. "Wide" was defined in the study as **twice the biacromial distance**.
- There was a **trend** toward the vertical press slightly beating the 40° incline for delt activation.

His conclusion: because the incline press also recruits **both the sternocostal and clavicular heads of the pecs** far more than a strict vertical press does, the **40° incline with a wide grip gives more bang for your buck** — near-maximal delt activation plus substantial chest work.

Two supporting studies:
- **Trebs et al. 2010** — inclines beat flat for delt activation.
- **A 2015 paper (author garbled in captions, uncertain)** — same result.

## Free weights vs machines for the delts [06:51]
Three papers:
1. **Schick et al. 2010** — compared **flat barbell bench press vs Smith machine bench press**. **No difference in anterior deltoid activation.** However, **medial/lateral delt activation was higher with the free-weight version**, presumably because the lateral delt takes on more of a **stabilisation** role.
2. **Saeterbakken et al. 2011** — essentially the same result: **no difference between machine and free-weight pressing.**
3. **A 1994 paper (author garbled — possibly "McCaw et al.", uncertain)** — in contrast, found **deltoid activity much higher for the free-weight press.** Two things distinguish it: it was **the only one of the three to test a vertical press** rather than a horizontal press, which may matter; and the result was **load-dependent** — at **60% of 1RM free weights produced more activation**, while at **80% of 1RM there was no difference** between machine and free weights.

**Mechanism proposed by the authors:** the anterior deltoid resists lateral rotation of the humerus and the lateral deltoid resists adduction of the humerus, so the extra activity on free weights likely reflects their contribution to **stabilising the shoulder**, not extra prime-mover work.

**His take:** there is nothing particularly special about free weights beyond a **potential and small** stabilisation-driven increase. Use a **combination**, or simply use whichever fits your programme and your body. He personally uses both, and puts the **free-weight movement earlier in the session**, when he is fresh and can generate more force.

## Biceps: pairing preacher and standing curls [09:26]
**A 2009 paper (author garbled — sounds like "Oliveira et al.", uncertain)** compared biceps activation in the **preacher curl** vs the **standing curl**:
- **Preacher curl:** activation **highest in the bottom third** of the range, then **drops off** through the rest.
- **Standing curl:** the **opposite** — low in the first third, **increasing through the remainder** of the range.

They **complement each other**, so pairing the two covers more of the range than either alone.

## The workout as programmed
Incline barbell bench press → machine shoulder press → one-arm dumbbell upright rows → **dumbbell lateral raises, 4 sets of 12–15 reps** for extra side delt volume → machine preacher curl → standing dumbbell curl.

On **one-arm dumbbell upright rows vs rope upright rows**: the dumbbell gives more degrees of freedom, letting him shift emphasis **away from the traps and external rotators and into the side delts** by initiating the movement as he would a lateral raise.

## Practical application
- Choose an **incline press at roughly 40°** as your main delt-and-chest press; use a **wide grip (~2× biacromial width)** if delt involvement is the priority.
- Do not agonise over free weights vs machines for delt growth — the difference is small. **Do both**, free weights first.
- If you only train heavy (80%+ 1RM), the free-weight stabilisation advantage largely vanishes.
- **Pair a preacher curl with a standing curl** to cover the full biceps activation curve.
- **4 × 12–15 dumbbell lateral raises** is his side-delt accessory dose here.
- On volume philosophy: he is explicitly **not a minimalist** — if extra work yields even slightly more progress despite diminishing returns, he does it.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Everything here is **EMG activation data**, which is a proxy for hypertrophy, not a measure of it.
- The three free-weight-vs-machine studies **disagree**, and the outlier used a different press direction (vertical vs horizontal) and different loads — the discrepancy is not resolved.
- Three of the six researcher names are garbled by the auto-captions (the 2015 incline paper, the 1994 press paper, the 2009 curl paper) — verify before citing.
- The claim that machine press handles force greater front-delt bias is his reasoning, not a tested finding.
