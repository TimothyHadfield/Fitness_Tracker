# Shoulder Exercises Ranked S to F

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Best And Worst Shoulder Exercises" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SgyUoY0IZ7A)) · 2024-09-10 · 13 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- **Best shoulder exercise overall: the cable lateral raise** — set the cable at about hand height and it puts maximum tension on the side delt in the stretched position.
- **Best per head:** front delts — **machine shoulder press**; side delts — **cable lateral raise**; rear delts — **reverse cable crossover**.
- **Worst: the front raise** — not harmful, just redundant, because any pressing already trains the front delts heavily.
- Bias your program toward **side delts**: they create the X-frame taper, and the front delts are usually already overdeveloped from chest work.
- The recurring problem with dumbbell raises is the resistance curve — **zero tension at the bottom where the delt is stretched, peak tension at 90° where it's shortest.** Cables, machines and leaning variations fix this.

## Criteria and anatomy
[00:00] S tier requires: high tension **especially in the stretched position**, a good feel (no shoulder pain, smooth resistance profile), and simple progression by adding weight or reps.

Three delt heads, treated almost as separate muscles: **anterior** (raises the arm to the front), **lateral/side** (lifts the arm out to the side), **posterior/rear** (pulls the arm back).

## The full ranking

### S tier
- **Cable lateral raise** [07:37] — maximum tension on the side delt at its most stretched, because tension peaks when the arm and the cable form a 90° angle; set the cable around hand height. Excellent smooth even tension throughout. He prefers wrist cuffs so his forearms don't pump up and steal focus, and holds a lacrosse ball so his hand isn't just floating. Works fine with a standard D handle too. **Best of the best, and his number one side delt exercise.**
- **Cable Y raise** [08:39] — sweeping the cable up, out and back gives an even bigger range of motion. Only kept off the top spot because some people find it awkward to learn.
- **Behind-the-back cuffed lateral raise** [09:09] — his most gimmicky-looking lift, but it trains both arms at once, which is twice as time-efficient as a one-arm cable raise. Setup: hook the wrists into the cuffs, optionally grab lacrosse balls, step over one cable while spinning around so the cables cross behind your back, then step forward and raise out and slightly forward.
- **Reverse pec deck, done sitting sideways** [10:10] — consistent tension throughout, and the braced chest sends all the stack's tension into the rear delts. Sitting sideways gives a much deeper stretch; the cost is training one arm at a time, which you can offset by supersetting left and right with no rest between.
- **Reverse cable crossover** [11:41] — amazing rear delt stretch, especially crossing the arms over at the top; enormous range of motion with even tension. Hold the cables themselves rather than the handles so nothing clangs as your arms cross. Alternate which arm crosses on top set to set to avoid left/right imbalance. Focus on **sweeping the cables out rather than yanking them back**. **His number one rear delt exercise.**

### A tier (and A+)
- **Machine shoulder press** [05:37] — **A tier plus**, and his **number one front delt exercise**. If the machine gets the delts into a deep stretch it feels better than dumbbells, and because it needs less technique and coordination you can take the delts all the way to failure without losing control. Still very front-delt dominant. His go-to vertical press.
- **Atlantis standing machine lateral raise** [06:37] — **A tier plus**. Extremely smooth with even consistent tension across the range, easy weekly progression, and he does it every week. Two snags: you can't move your arms across your body so the stretch isn't as good as cables or the Arnold lying raise, and he's close to maxing out the stack.
- **Lean-in dumbbell lateral raise** [02:32] — leaning against a bench or rack modifies the resistance curve so there is more tension on the side delt in the stretched position. **The most effective way to do lateral raises with dumbbells.**
- **Dumbbell overhead press** [05:07] — better than the barbell version on three counts: faster to set up, more range of motion at both the bottom and top (especially letting the dumbbells converge), and more side delt involvement since the delts have to stop the dumbbells falling forward or backward.
- **Arnold-style side-lying dumbbell raise** [06:07] — high tension while the delt is stretched, locked in against a bench for stability, huge range of motion, great side delt pump.
- **Rope face pull** [10:40] — hits the rear delts well if you set the rope a little lower, take an underhand grip, and externally rotate the shoulders as you pull, like a rear double biceps pose. Big rear delt stretch, feels good, solid for overload. Gripe: the mid traps take over no matter what cue you use, so the rear delts may not be the limiting factor — not a disaster, since you want midback growth anyway.

### B tier
- **Standing dumbbell lateral raise** [01:31] — the most time-efficient shoulder exercise (grab dumbbells and go), and clearly effective — plenty of bodybuilders built huge side delts on them, and he still uses them when pressed for time. But there's **zero tension at the bottom when the side delt is most stretched, and peak tension at 90° when it's most shortened.**
- **Lean-away dumbbell lateral raise** [02:32] — may shift emphasis away from the rotator cuff and onto the lateral delt depending how you read one study, but it still fails to load the stretched position.
- **Super-ROM lateral raise** [03:03] — picked up from Dr. Mike Israetel; genuinely lit up his delts. A rare movement that gets the side delt **fully contracted**, which he values because the evidence on stretch-vs-squeeze is converging on the stretch but there's very little direct shoulder data. Worth trying, but it has the same resistance curve problem as a standard dumbbell raise.
- **Standing barbell overhead press** [03:33] — provides high tension in the stretched position and overloads extremely well; a stubborn lift, but you can usually add weight, a rep, or improve technique, and it's very gratifying. Two downsides: activation data show it's **very anterior-delt dominant** with little side or rear delt involvement, and standing means the lower body and core steal tension keeping balance. He rates it **higher than most science-based hypertrophy people and lower than powerbuilding purists** — "overhated by the science crowd, overrated by the barbell crowd."
- **Seated barbell overhead press** [05:07] — fixes the stability problem, but it's still front-delt dominant and less satisfying than the standing version.
- **Seated machine lateral raise** [07:37] — he includes it, but with the pads higher on the arms it doesn't feel as good, possibly because the shorter moment arm to the shoulder forces more load, which cranks the shoulders. Genuinely between A and B; he picks B.
- **Upright row** [09:40] — overhated. Hits upper traps and side delts with some biceps. He prefers cables with a rope so he can sweep the handles out as he rows. **As long as your shoulders are healthy there's no evidence they cause shoulder problems.** Held to B tier as a delt builder because the upper traps take over before the delt fibres reach true exhaustion. A great overall yoke builder.
- **Bent-over reverse dumbbell fly** [11:11] — same flaw as dumbbell laterals: zero tension at the bottom when the rear delts are stretched. Still a good option without cables or when time-crunched, and his rear delts always get very sore from them. Would be A tier if not for the missing stretch tension.

### C tier
- **Banded lateral raise** [09:09] — his least favourite lateral raise and rarely programmed. Like the dumbbell version, zero tension in the stretch and maximum at the top. Only use it if a band is all you have.

### D tier
- **Front raises of every kind** — dumbbell, cable and machine [06:37]. If you press at all, your front delts already get more than enough work, so no extra isolation is needed. **Named the worst of the worst, but deliberately kept out of F tier** because it isn't bad for growth, just redundant. It's the go-to for the rare lifter with underdeveloped anterior delts or anyone who can't press.

## Practical application
- Make **cable lateral raises** (or the Y raise / behind-the-back cuffed version) the backbone of side delt training, cable set at hand height.
- Use a **machine shoulder press** as your vertical press; dumbbell overhead press is the next best. Barbell OHP is fine and impressive, just not the best hypertrophy tool.
- Pick a **reverse cable crossover** or a **sideways reverse pec deck** for rear delts; add face pulls with a low rope, underhand grip and external rotation.
- If you only have dumbbells, **lean into a bench or rack** on lateral raises, or do the Arnold side-lying raise, to load the stretched position.
- Skip direct front raises unless your anterior delts are genuinely lagging or you can't press.
- Superset left and right sides on unilateral rear delt work to keep it time-efficient.
- Wrist cuffs (with or without a lacrosse ball to hold) can keep forearm pump from stealing focus on cable laterals.

## Caveats & unknowns
- There is **very little direct evidence on stretch vs. contraction for the shoulders specifically**, which is why he keeps a fully shortened option (super-ROM lateral raise) on the table.
- The lean-away raise's rotator cuff / lateral delt claim depends on how you interpret a single study.
- Machine rankings depend entirely on machine quality — the Atlantis lateral raise and a good press machine are not interchangeable with whatever your gym has.
- Several placements are admitted preference calls: the seated machine lateral raise sits between A and B, and the seated OHP loses points partly for being "less satisfying."
- Upright rows are cleared only for people with healthy shoulders.
