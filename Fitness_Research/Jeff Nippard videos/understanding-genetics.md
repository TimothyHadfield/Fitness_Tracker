# Muscle and Fat Loss Genetics: How Much Is Actually Heritable

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Understanding Your Genetics | Muscle Genetics Science Explained feat. My Mom!" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XVrTgULSAAY)) · 2017-08-26 · 9 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Twin studies estimate **50-80% of the variability in muscle size** between individuals is genetic. Height is roughly **80% heritable**; the metabolic factors behind weight and fat gain around **40%**; fat storage location **22-61%**.
- Fat loss is therefore **slightly more within your control** than muscle gain or height.
- Response to an identical training program varies enormously — one study found bicep growth ranging from **-2% to +59%** over 12 weeks.
- True "non-responders" probably don't exist, or are rare — more likely different people respond to different training methods.
- There is **no optimal macronutrient split**. Caloric restriction is the shared mechanism of every successful weight loss diet; the best diet is the one you'll adhere to.

## Heritability, properly interpreted [01:02]

A 2016 report covering over **140,000 twin pairs** put height at ~80% heritable. Important nuance on what that means: it does *not* mean 80% of your height comes from genes. It means 80% of your **deviation from average** is genetic. Nippard's worked example — average male height ~5'10", he's 5'5", so of that 5-inch gap, roughly 4 inches are genetic and 1 inch environmental/dietary.

## Variability in muscle-building response

**Hubal et al., 2005** ([Hubal 2005](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/15947721)). 585 untrained men and women trained **biceps and triceps only for 12 weeks** on a simple linear progression, with MRI measuring muscle cross-sectional area before and after. Results ranged from **-2% to +59%** change in bicep size: the best responders grew nearly 60%, the worst actually lost size. Limitation Nippard raises: total calorie and protein intake weren't controlled, so some of the spread may be nutritional rather than genetic.

**McMaster University, 2007** ([Hartman 2007](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/17684208))**.** 56 young men on a push/pull/legs split for 12 weeks, comparing soy vs milk protein post-workout. DEXA showed **milk produced greater fat-free mass gains than soy or carbohydrate alone** — but individual responses were highly variable, with some soy-group subjects outperforming the average milk-group subject and vice versa.

**Follow-up four years later** ([Davidsen 2011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/21030674))**.** Researchers and geneticists took the top and bottom **15%** of that milk-vs-soy cohort. High responders had gained roughly **4x the lean mass** of low responders. They identified **21 microRNAs** (molecules that regulate muscle protein synthesis), of which **4 changed expression** over the 12 weeks of training. **miR-378** was most tightly correlated with lean body mass change: its expression **fell only in the low responders**, not the high responders. Maintaining miR-378 expression may therefore be a distinctive marker of the ability to build muscle. Nippard is explicit that turning this into training advice is well ahead of the current literature.

**Satellite cells, 2008.** Satellite cells surround muscle fibres and repair or form new muscle cells. Previously identified **high responders had roughly twice the satellite cell count of non-responders**, and after 16 weeks of training satellite cell number rose **50% in high responders but not at all in non-responders**.

Nippard's own read: he's unconvinced genuine non-responders exist. Putting everyone on one program and measuring response mostly identifies who responds to *that* protocol — it does not rule out that "non-responders" would do well on an individualised program found through trial and error.

## Genetics of fat gain and fat loss [05:08]

**Bouchard et al.** ([Bouchard 1990](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/2336074)) overfed **12 pairs of twins by 1,000 extra calories per day for 100 days**. Weight gain ranged from **9.5 to 29 lb** across pairs. More striking: the most metabolically favoured subjects showed **no increase in abdominal visceral fat at all**, while the least favoured showed roughly a **200% increase**. Authors estimated **~40% heritability** for the metabolic factors driving differences in weight and fat gain.

**Where you store fat** is also genetically regulated, with identified genes and a heritability of **22-61%** ([Schleinitz 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24632736)). Most research contrasts subcutaneous fat with the more health-relevant visceral fat, but individual differences in subcutaneous distribution exist too. Men typically store abdominally and around the love handles; women at the hips and glutes; and even within one sex, storage patterns differ. There is **no convincing way to manipulate this** other than losing fat overall — stubborn areas eventually come off last.

**Diet response phenotypes.** Studies as early as 1999 identified "high-fat" and "low-fat" phenotypes ([Blundell 1999](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/10817143)): some people increase fat burning in response to a **higher-fat diet**, others lose fat better on a **lower-fat, higher-carb diet**. A major regulator is **insulin sensitivity**, which is largely driven by lifestyle (regular exercise, diet quality, body fat percentage) but also has a large genetic component. A 2017 paper in the *Journal of Gastroenterology* ([Thom 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/28214525)) concluded there is no optimal macronutrient distribution for weight management and no one-size-fits-all approach — caloric restriction is the fundamental premise of every successful strategy, whether reached via lower fat or lower carbs.

## Practical application

1. **Don't assume you lost the genetic lottery and use it as an excuse.** Do that and you're guaranteed to fall short. Train as if your genes are working for you.
2. **Use the variability as a reason to experiment.** Try different exercises and training styles; periodically alter your carb-to-fat ratio at matched calories. Be your own scientist.
3. **Compare your current self only to your own starting point.** Role models are fine for inspiration; comparing your results to theirs is not.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The 585-subject study didn't control calories or protein.
- The miR-378 finding is a correlation from a small high/low responder subgroup — not actionable yet.
- Heritability estimates for muscle size (50-80%) are wide because few studies adequately control training and diet.
- "Non-responder" status may be an artefact of a single fixed protocol rather than a real biological category.
