# Periodization for Bodybuilding: The Four Models and What to Steal From Each

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Periodization for Bodybuilding (With Examples)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1KWsgdDX79w)) · 2016-02-12 · 16 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Periodization has no single agreed definition; treat it broadly as "how you plan and organise training over time."
- The research supports *variation* more strongly than it supports any specific periodization model. Vary methodically, not sporadically.
- No model should be run in isolation. Build a program that borrows from all four: linear, reverse linear, undulating, conjugate/block.
- The two ideas bodybuilders can use most: exercise variation (conjugate) and block-style body-part specialisation with maintenance phases in between.
- Progressive overload sits above all of it — if load or reps aren't climbing over time, the periodization scheme is decoration.

## Does periodization actually work? [01:02]
A review of 15 studies found 13 showed a positive effect of periodizing training on strength and hypertrophy. The two null studies were both short and used untrained subjects — beginners respond to almost any non-ridiculous program, so they're poor tests.

The caveat: it may not be periodization *per se* doing the work. A 2012 review by John Kiely argued that because of logistical constraints, studies compare "regularly varied training" against "minimal or no variation." What they demonstrate is that **variation** matters, not that periodization models are the optimal way of delivering it ([Kiely 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230756715_Periodization_paradigms_in_the_21st_century_Evidence-led_or_tradition-driven)). On the current literature, periodization as a specific methodology isn't strongly evidence-based; variety is.

Limit on that: if you vary too frequently, or swing too violently between extremes, your adaptive capacity gets spread too thin and gains suffer. Controlled, methodical variation only.

## Linear periodization [04:05]
Intensity increases, volume decreases over time. The original 1950s model from Russian physiologist Leo Matveyev.

- **Peaking example:** week 1 = 3x6 @ 80% 1RM, reps drop and %1RM climbs each week, finishing with heavy singles near max, then a taper/deload in meet week.
- **Progression-scheme example (6–8 rep range):** week 1 = 100 lb for 3x8; week 2 = 105 lb for 3x7; and so on. Volume drops within the block, but at the end of the block you restart the progression with heavier loads — so across the macro picture total volume actually rises.

## Reverse linear periodization [05:37]
Volume increases, intensity decreases. Most head-to-head studies favour standard linear over reverse linear ([Prestes 2009](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/19057409)), but reverse linear has specific uses — mainly building work capacity / muscular endurance.

Example squat work-capacity block, all at RPE 9:
- Week 1: 3x4–6
- Week 2: 3x6–8
- Week 3: 3x8–10
- Week 4: 3x10–12

Because load is prescribed by RPE, weight self-adjusts downward as reps rise — and often not by much, since work capacity is improving concurrently. Useful for a powerlifter's off-season; the endurance and recovery adaptations get converted into max strength in the following block.

A "pseudo reverse linear" scheme where load stays flat while volume climbs across a mesocycle works similarly, though it isn't strictly reverse linear.

## Undulating periodization [08:13]
"Undulating" just means wavy — sets, reps and load move up and down over time. **Daily undulating (DUP)** waves within the training week; **weekly undulating** waves week to week while staying fixed inside each week.

Most studies find undulating produces superior strength gains versus linear, though not universally. It also has a psychological argument: 3x8 squats Monday and 3x8 again Thursday gets boring faster than 3x10 Monday and 4x6 Thursday.

DUP example for lagging biceps trained 3x/week:
- Day 1: 3x4–6
- Day 2: 3x15–20
- Day 3: 3x8–12

Repeat the same structure in weeks 2 and 3, adding weight within each rep range — DUP layered over linear progression.

## Conjugate periodization [11:17]
Two premises: (1) different qualities are developed concurrently — e.g. max effort bench on day 1, dynamic/speed work with bands and chains on day 2; (2) **exercise variation matters**.

Study cited: Fonseca et al. (2014) — one group did only squats; the other did squats plus deadlifts, lunges and leg press. The squat-only group grew only some heads of the quadriceps; the varied-exercise group grew **all four heads** ([Fonseca 2014](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24832974)). Practical read: for symmetrical, proportional development, don't run a minimalist one-exercise-per-muscle program.

## Block periodization [13:21]
Built on **training residuals** — an adaptation persists for a while after you stop training it hard. So focus a block (roughly 1–2 months) on one quality or body part, then drop it to maintenance while a different target gets the focus.

Advanced bodybuilder example with a lagging chest:
- **Block 1 (4–6 weeks):** specialise chest — frequency 3–4x/week, high (but sensible) volume, lots of exercise variation.
- **Block 2:** chest to maintenance — about 2x/week at roughly half to two-thirds of the previous volume — while legs or another body part takes the specialisation slot.
- **Block 3:** return chest to high frequency and volume.

This is how advanced lifters keep moving stubborn body parts once general progress stalls.

## Practical application
- Don't pick one model. Combine: undulate reps/sets within the week, run linear progression on the main lifts within a block, rotate exercises conjugate-style, and use block-style specialisation for weak points.
- Use at least a handful of exercises per muscle group, not one, to develop all heads.
- Specialise a lagging body part for 4–6 weeks at 3–4x/week frequency, then maintain it at ~2x/week and half-to-two-thirds volume while you specialise elsewhere.
- Check every block against one question: is the weight on the bar or the reps going up over time?

## Caveats & unknowns
- The evidence base supports variation more confidently than it supports any named periodization model — Kiely's critique is unresolved.
- Reverse linear generally loses to linear in direct comparisons; it is a tool for specific goals, not a default.
- Undulating beats linear in most but not all strength studies.
- Beginners respond to nearly anything, so most of this matters more as training age increases.
