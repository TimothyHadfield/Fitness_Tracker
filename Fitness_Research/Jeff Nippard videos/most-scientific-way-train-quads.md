# Quad Training: Anatomy, Exercise Selection and Volume

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Scientific Way to Train QUADS | Quad Training Science Explained" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KMnp7y6_sMA)) · 2017-11-30 · 8 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Squats are the single best quad exercise — not because activation is uniquely high, but because they load heavily and progress well. No exercise is mandatory though.
- Depth matters more than stance width: deep squatting beat shallow for hypertrophy at every site measured; stance width made no measurable difference to activation.
- Front squats bias the quads over back squats (more knee torque, more VL and VM activation) at lower knee compressive load.
- 12-18 hard sets per week, spread over ~1.5-3 sessions, is the working target for most people.
- Mix rep ranges: the three vastus muscles are fiber-type neutral, the rectus femoris skews fast-twitch and likes heavier loads.

## Anatomy [00:31]
Four muscles. **Vastus lateralis** on the outside (the "outer sweep"), **vastus medialis** on the inside (the "teardrop"), **vastus intermedius** sandwiched between them. All three run from the femur to the tibia via the patellar tendon and only extend the knee. Lying on top is the **rectus femoris**, which crosses the hip as well as the knee — it extends the knee *and* flexes the hip, making it biarticular. Cadaver work has flagged possible fifth and sixth muscles in the group ([Woodley 2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/22218383), [Grob 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/26732825)); not practically relevant.

Fiber type: the vastii are roughly an even type I / type II split, so use both high and low reps. Multiple studies put the rectus femoris as more type II dominant, suggesting it favours heavier loading.

## Squat variations [01:32]
Front squats raise quad demand because the more upright torso increases knee torque. A **2016 study from Contreras and colleagues** found vastus lateralis activation ~21% higher in front squats than back squats to parallel ([Contreras 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/26252837)). A **2015 study from Yavuz and colleagues** found greater vastus medialis activation with a 1RM front squat than a 1RM back squat ([Yavuz 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/25630691)). Because the front squat reaches comparable or better activation with lighter absolute loads, and knee compressive force is lower, it's a reasonable pick for people with cranky knees.

## Depth and stance [02:04]
**Bloomquist et al., 2013**: 12 weeks of shallow vs deep squatting, MRI-measured. The deep group grew more at every single site; the shallow group showed no growth at all at four of six quad sites ([Bloomquist 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/23604798)). The Contreras data trended the same way — both peak and mean quad activation were higher in the full squat than the parallel squat, though it didn't reach statistical significance.

Nippard's read: going to at least parallel captures most of the available growth, and ass-to-grass is suggested rather than mandatory. Squat as deep as you can with good form for your mobility and experience.

Stance width appears to be a non-factor. A **1998 Illinois State University study** found identical EMG patterns across all quad heads at 75% and 140% of shoulder width ([McCaw 1999](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/10188748)). Follow-ups in **2001 and 2009** found no difference between 1x and 2x shoulder width ([Escamilla 2001](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/11404665), [Paoli 2009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/19130646)). So pick the stance that lets you hit depth comfortably and safely — same logic applies to the leg press.

## Other exercises [03:36]
Lunges and step-ups produce activation very similar to the squat, and one **2009 study** had the step-up beating the squat for vastus lateralis ([Ebben 2009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/18975260)). On EMG alone the leg extension would win outright, especially for the rectus femoris, but its ceiling for progressive overload is low — treat it as an isolation add-on after a compound.

**Foot position on leg extensions:** two independent studies found VL activation higher with toes pointed in, and rectus femoris activation higher with toes pointed out. Vastus medialis was similar across in, neutral and out ([Stoutenberg 2005](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/16437818)). Toes-in is a defensible cue for outer sweep, but use whatever gives the best mind-muscle connection.

**Leg extension safety [04:37]:** shearing force and ACL stress are genuinely high. **Dr. Brad Schoenfeld's** position is that this shouldn't harm healthy knees when the exercise is performed properly, and may even strengthen those structures more than alternatives. It is contraindicated for people with existing knee issues, particularly ACL-related. Train the hamstrings as antagonists for injury prevention.

## Volume and frequency [05:07]
Per **Dr. Mike Israetel**: most trainees respond best to **12-18 weekly sets** including squat volume, with above ~20 sets typically creating recovery problems. Frequency **1.5-3x per week**, where 1.5 means one very high-volume session plus one low-volume mini session.

Nippard's own preference: **2x per week** — one heavy strength day in the **4-8 rep range**, one higher-volume lighter day in the **8-20 rep range**.

## Practical application
- Anchor the week on a squat pattern, front squat if you want the quad bias or have knee issues.
- Go to at least parallel; deeper if mobility allows. Stop worrying about stance width.
- Add lunges, step-ups, hack squat or leg press as secondary work; finish with leg extensions as isolation, not as the main driver.
- 12-18 sets/week across two sessions: heavy 4-8s, then volume 8-20s.
- If you replace squats with machines, add posterior chain work — back extensions, hip thrusts, deadlifts — to cover the spinal erectors the squat would have trained.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Much of the exercise-comparison evidence here is EMG activation, which is a proxy for growth, not a measure of it. Bloomquist is the one direct hypertrophy trial cited.
- The 2015 front squat vs back squat citation has a garbled researcher name in the auto-captions; it matches Yavuz et al. 2015 in the reference list.
- The Contreras depth finding did not reach statistical significance.
- Volume and frequency numbers come from Israetel's practical recommendations, not from a single trial; individual recovery capacity varies.
- Nippard notes he personally squats little due to a previous injury, and that for hypertrophy there are no mandatory exercises.
- 2017 video — the evidence base on depth, volume and exercise selection has moved on since.
