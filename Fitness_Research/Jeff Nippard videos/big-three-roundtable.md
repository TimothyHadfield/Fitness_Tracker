# Big Three Roundtable: Competition Planning, Max Testing and Weight Cutting

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "BIG THREE Roundtable feat. Brett Gibbs, JP Cauchi, Josh Hancott" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SdQniL8vEVY)) · 2015-12-29 · 49 min
**Topic:** Programming

> **What this actually covers.** Despite the title, this is not a technique breakdown of the squat, bench and deadlift. "Big Three" refers to the three elite raw powerlifters on the panel — **Brett Gibbs** (NZ, world champion, ~830 kg total), **JP Cauchi** (Melbourne, coach at Obsidian Strength) and **Josh Hancott** (Newfoundland, 74 kg class). The subject matter is competition scheduling, off-seasons, how advanced lifters gauge strength without testing, top sets, water cutting and the mental side of the platform.

## Bottom line
- **All three test their 1RM almost never.** Gibbs and Cauchi both say they save maxing for the platform; Hancott does it maybe once a year. They track progress by bar speed, form quality and how a known load felt.
- **Cauchi's rule on testing:** beginners *should* test every 6–10 weeks because their response is unpredictable. Advanced lifters shouldn't — "if six weeks ago my max was 225, I guarantee it's not 200 now."
- **Take genuine time off, not just deloads.** All three take **2–3 weeks of no training at all** after their biggest meet of the year, then lighter work. Gibbs settles on **three competitions and about three months off per year** as his ideal.
- **Top sets before volume work are endorsed by all four** — for mental preparation in comp prep, and as an "over warm-up" via post-activation potentiation in the off-season. Nippard explicitly rejects the standard criticism that they cost you volume work.
- **Nobody on the panel counts macros or keeps a detailed training log any more.** All manage weight and load intuitively, having done the meticulous version earlier in their careers.
- **Disagreement on why elite lifters cut weight well:** Nippard floats a genetic explanation; Cauchi argues it's a *mental* trait that co-selects with being good at the sport.

## Competition scheduling and off-seasons [07:09]

**Cauchi**, who had done **15 competitions in three years**, was taking **six months away from competing** to actually build strength. His argument [08:11]: peaking wipes advanced lifters out — Gibbs' peak into Perth was **13 weeks of high-intensity work for a single meet** — and backing meets up against each other means you go up and down without net progress. *"You might get 5 kilos here or 10 kilos there,"* but you won't get genuinely stronger.

**Gibbs' cautionary data point** [08:41]: in **2014 he did four meets** (March, June, September, December), all similar in structure. He opened well, progressing from a **750 kg to a 770 kg total** across the first two raw meets, then did an equipped meet, and by December had his **worst meet of the year — 4 successful attempts from 9, and the lowest total of the year**. He had run no dedicated off-season block, and describes the burnout as physical *and* mental — the specificity required for powerlifting is hard to sustain. In 2015 he built properly from his first meet, had a small injury before Worlds, then finished strong three months later, and was taking **another three months off before even starting to peak again**. His settled view: **3 months off / 3 comps a year** is right for a lifter at his level.

**Hancott** describes **post-meet blues** — after months of mental preparation, hitting the goal leaves him unwilling to even think about training for about **four weeks**. His preferred structure going forward: **two big meets a year (Nationals and Worlds) plus one lower-priority international**.

**The federation constraint** [10:45]. Cauchi's point: powerlifters are often *forced* to compete when they don't want to — qualifying paths require doing Nationals to reach Worlds, in both raw and equipped. This is easier for dominant lifters (Hancott could win his Nationals comfortably without peaking hard) and brutal for average lifters, who must peak maximally at Nationals and then repeat it ~10 weeks later at Worlds. Hancott adds that federation calendars vary enormously — he had done **five or six World Championships to only two national events**.

**What "time off" actually means** [15:22]. Cauchi: after your biggest comp of the year, **genuinely no training for 2–3 weeks**, not just reduced intensity/volume, and says there's good research supporting it. After a secondary meet, 2–3 weeks of lighter training is enough. Hancott: about **two weeks a year of doing absolutely nothing**, plus perhaps four more weeks spread out doing only conditioning. Gibbs finds it much easier to take time off while travelling than at home — his best example was **four weeks off in Bali in December 2013**, refusing to go to the gym even when his friends did, coming back very refreshed; in 2015 he took **10 days** completely off.

**Nippard's framing:** the lifts are like riding a bike — you won't forget them in a couple of weeks, and it takes a great deal of bed rest to see meaningful atrophy. **Cauchi's supporting anecdote** [19:28]: he took **eight weeks off sumo deadlifts**, pulling only conventional, sumoed once, competed the following week, and lost no strength — while building a good foundation for the next eight weeks.

## How advanced lifters know where they're at [18:25]

**None of them max out regularly.**
- **Gibbs:** does the occasional heavy triple or "10 RPE on a three or four", rarely a true 1RM. His end-of-2015 max testing day was a deliberate one-off after three good months of training, to know where his strength sat entering 2016.
- **Cauchi:** avoids it as much as possible and saves it for competition — competitions are taxing enough without blowing himself up in the gym for nothing.
- **Hancott:** used to max often as an intermediate, precisely because he had nothing to train for. Now at most **once a year** in the gym.

**Gibbs' method for assessing progress** [21:31]: recall of specific sets. He remembers that **272.5 kg for a triple** was previously absolutely maxed out — one set only, form breaking down. When **272 moved well for three** and he had another set in him, he knew his max was well above the previous one. The variables he tracks: form, bar speed, how fatigued he was afterwards, and whether another set was available.

**Cauchi on why testing gets pointless** [22:34]: he doesn't like AMRAPs and doesn't use them as a form of testing. As progress slows in advanced lifters, you know you aren't adding 30 kg in six weeks. His best squat being 225 kg, he knows he isn't squatting 250 at the next Worlds no matter what he wants. He'll be *genuinely in the dark* about his max midway through a volume block and considers that fine — you might drift 5 kg either way on fatigue, but there are no sudden changes. His programming question is instead: **where am I now, what's realistic in 12–20 weeks, and how do we get there?**

**Cauchi's beginner exception** [23:34]: beginners *do* need to test every **6–10 weeks**, because you never know how a beginner will respond to training.

**Logging** [24:04]. Cauchi used to be meticulous; now he writes his own programs, they fit on a page, and he doesn't record numbers. Hancott stopped years ago. Both attribute it to experience producing an intuition for where you are — you can predict your meet numbers without the outlandish overestimates beginners and intermediates make.

**The beginner-impatience problem** [25:05]. Cauchi coaches at Obsidian Strength Powerlifting Club in Melbourne, where coaches stop new lifters maxing constantly — his current example is an 18-year-old at 93 kg who spent six weeks asking to bench 140 and to add 5 or 10 kg to every session. All three agree it's a lesson that can only be learned by making the mistake. Cauchi's own version: in 2013 he attempted **500 lb / ~227.5 kg on deadlift roughly 50 times** before getting it, never doing the actual work to earn it — *"that's when people plateau."* He was, at recording, **chasing a 300 kg deadlift for about a year and a half**, having pulled **277.5 kg** at his last meet (forced by the competitive situation) and **660 lb in training** on bumper plates with straps.

## Effort management and RPE [27:40]

**Cauchi is not a fan of pre-planned RPE targets** [28:10]: *"a lot of people get carried away with the at-8, at-9 thing because it's always at-8 or at-9."* His working sets of five are often with a weight he could do for 10 or 15 — but they still feel hard. His illustrative session: **5×5 deadlift at 225 kg / 495 lb**; after three sets he decided to do the last two sets as one, and got **10 reps**. Nominally that made the earlier sets ~5 RPE; they still felt difficult. That's where he pushes — squeezing an extra 2.5 kg, an extra rep, or combining sets — but he confines it to off-season blocks and avoids it inside a specifically written comp prep.

**Gibbs programs the same idea deliberately** [29:41]: RPE-based training with **alternating intense and non-intense days**. The difference between a non-intense and an intense squat day at the *same nominal RPE* is **30–40 kg**, purely because of how he approaches the bar. He'd program the "get 10 with the set-of-five weight" day in, and it worked well — it forces him to switch on mentally for every rep.

**Hancott errs toward undertraining** [31:43]: he adjusts by feel every day, accounting for sleep and life stress, but when he feels good he **holds himself back rather than adding weight**. He'd rather be slightly undertrained going into a meet than burnt out. His progression is straightforward and weekly — e.g. benching 350 in week three, adding about 5 lb a week to hit the target.

## Top sets before back-off volume [30:11]

**Hancott is a strong advocate**, specifically in comp prep: it's hard to predict a single from a 5×5 or 6×4, so hitting a relatively smooth double or single first tells you where you are, and — his main point — **builds confidence**. *"Okay, next week I only have to do 5 kg more and this was easy."* You can watch linear progression accumulate into your meet attempts.

**Cauchi separates two distinct uses** [32:44]:
1. **Comp prep** — top singles are mental rehearsal. He tells his lifters to treat the top set as their third attempt: *approach the 250 kg squat as if it were 300, so that the 300 feels like 250.*
2. **Off-season** — what he calls an **"over warm-up."** In a block of 5–6 sets at 70%, a single at 80% costs nothing: 80% for one is normally just a warm-up rung, it isn't fatiguing, and it won't interfere with the volume work. Ramp 50/60/70/80% for a single, then drop to working sets. No mental-imagery component — just thorough warming up.

**Nippard rejects the standard criticism** [33:45] that a heavy single fatigues the CNS and blunts the volume work that actually drives progress. His experience is the opposite — the volume work felt *lighter* and he was more confident (his analogy: shooting hoops with a medicine ball, then picking up a basketball). Hancott adds the caveat that it's a double-edged sword: it only works if the top set is **crisp and auto-regulated**; a 10-out-of-10 grinder will wreck the working sets. That requires lifter intuition.

**Cauchi supplies the physiology** [34:47]: **post-activation potentiation** — a heavy single switches the nervous system on so subsequent lighter work genuinely feels and moves lighter, not just psychologically. His supporting anecdote from his exercise-science studies: an **Eastern-bloc track and field team (he thinks Bulgarian) at a 1970s Olympics** demanded a full weight set in the warm-up room, so high jumpers would **squat 220 kg and go out to jump 20 minutes later**. *He is explicitly unsure who it was — treat the anecdote as uncertain.*

## Weight cutting [36:20]

**Cauchi's extreme case** — the one the others find remarkable. He competed in **Uzbekistan on 16 December at the 66 kg class (145 lb), weighing in at 65.9 kg**. Ten days later, on the Sunday before recording, he competed in a local bench-only meet at **75.2 kg (~164 lb)** — up **10 kg in 10 days**, jumping from the 66 to the 83 kg class in a week and a half. The bench-only meet was equipped and, by his own description, unprepared-for and terrible.

**Gibbs and Hancott both cut water only, intuitively.**
- **Gibbs:** water cuts are straightforward and always take off a couple of kilos. He loses appetite going into meets, cuts calories slightly, and the weight falls off. **He weighs himself every morning but does not count macros.** He has done full macro counting before — it worked and he got leaner — but he wanted to be bigger, and finds cutting 4–5 kg easy if you know your body.
- **Hancott:** has never tried macro counting; has water cut for essentially his whole career. In one 2013 contest he cut nothing at all and still weighed in at **74.9 kg**, eating the night before trying to *gain*. He now trains around **77–78 kg** and water cuts into the 74 kg class.

**Why elite lifters cut well — the panel's genuine disagreement** [37:53]. Nippard's observation is that lifters at the highest level seem to drop water unusually easily, and he floats a **genetic/individual** explanation. Hancott says it's genuinely easy for him and he doesn't know whether genetics are involved, but finds Cauchi's *mental capacity* to cut so much extremely impressive; he reckons he could get to 68 kg and end up in hospital. **Cauchi's counter-argument is that it's a mental trait, not a physiological one:** getting good at any sport requires mental strength, and cutting weight is a mentally punishing task. A beginner who gets anxious easily will find it the worst thing they can do. He also notes it is **easier for men than women**.

His description of what it actually costs [39:26]: **four hours in a sauna at ~110 degrees, 24 hours without food, 18 hours without water, 5 kg / 11 lb lost** — and then you weigh yourself with an hour to go and you're still a kilo over. He nearly broke down in Uzbekistan at exactly that point, and got through it because a friend cutting alongside him slapped him on the back and reminded him he was the champion. His summary: *it is not glamorous, it is painful, and it is not a coincidence that mental strength predicts both good cuts and good lifting.*

## The platform: what goes through your head [40:59]

**Gibbs** — family, sometimes the music, but always something internal. Never the crowd. He rehearses for days beforehand: after weigh-in, with the venue empty, he visualises walking up to the bar and every small step, on repeat. (Hancott filmed Gibbs' max testing day video Nippard praises; Nippard also recalls an IPF lifter who deliberately brought himself to tears before lifting — the panel identifies him as "George", *name uncertain*.)

**Hancott** — appears calm walking out, but describes himself as internally ferocious. He uses being doubted as fuel, then deliberately balances it with thinking about the people supporting him so his mindset isn't purely negative. He has cried before lifting. His framing: a peak isn't three months, it's a lifetime of unseen work, and outsiders don't understand that.

**Cauchi** — the outlier. He deliberately doesn't do any of that; he stays in the moment and is very relaxed. At Junior Nationals he chatted with the chief referee while setting up under a squat. He is more switched on at big meets. His recent change: he now gives the bench **more respect** — he used to write it off as a lift he was bad at and give it no focus, which he now thinks was a mistake.

**Contrast on meet-day approach** [46:03]: Hancott arrives with headphones on and refuses to speak to anyone, including at weigh-ins, sometimes for two or three days beforehand. Cauchi doesn't even bring headphones and chats with friends in the crowd between squat attempts. Gibbs is closer to Hancott. All three note this is purely individual style with no right answer.

**Music** [47:05]: Hancott likes Eminem specifically for competing, and both he and Nippard find **freshly discovered music** more motivating — Nippard deliberately loads new tracks for a meet, and reports better gym sessions on a new playlist.

## Points of disagreement
- **Why elite lifters cut water well.** Nippard proposes a genetic/individual component; **Cauchi argues it's a mental trait** that co-selects with being good at the sport. Hancott sits between them — easy for him, cause unknown.
- **Pre-planned RPE.** **Cauchi dislikes assigning RPE targets in advance** and thinks people get carried away with "at-8, at-9"; **Gibbs programs RPE-based intense/non-intense days deliberately** and found it worked very well.
- **AMRAPs.** Cauchi flatly doesn't like them or use them for testing. Gibbs does use heavy triples/"10 RPE on a three or four" as a proxy.
- **The top-set criticism.** Nippard raises the common objection (CNS fatigue eating into the volume work) and then **disagrees with it** on the basis of his own experience; Hancott qualifies it — the top set has to be crisp, not a grinder.
- **Meet-day psychology.** Cauchi's relaxed, in-the-moment approach is directly contrasted with Hancott's and Gibbs' intense, visualisation-heavy, don't-talk-to-me approach. All three treat this as individual rather than one being correct.
- **Frequency of meets.** Cauchi is taking six months off competing to build; Gibbs' answer is three meets a year; Hancott wants two big meets plus one lower-priority international — three answers to the same question.

## Practical application
- **Stop testing your max.** If you're past the beginner stage, gauge progress from a known heavy set: what load, what form, what bar speed, how much was left. Test every 6–10 weeks only while you're still a beginner.
- **Schedule about three meets a year and take a genuine off-season.** Two to three weeks of *no training* after your biggest meet, then lighter work — not just a deload.
- **Don't let the federation calendar dictate your year** if you can avoid it; peaking twice in ten weeks is what breaks average lifters.
- **Use a top single before back-off volume**, but for different reasons by phase: in comp prep as third-attempt rehearsal (approach 250 as if it's 300); in the off-season as an 80% over-warm-up that costs nothing and potentiates the working sets.
- **Alternate intense and non-intense days.** The same nominal RPE can differ by 30–40 kg depending on how switched on you are — so make some days deliberately switched-on.
- **Bias toward undertraining into a meet.** When you feel great, hold back rather than adding load.
- If you cut water, **weigh yourself every morning through the peaking block** — that alone is enough for most lifters to manage a 4–5 kg cut without counting macros.
- Time away from a lift doesn't cost you: eight weeks off sumo and one session back was enough for Cauchi to compete unaffected.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This is **experience and coaching opinion from four competitors**, not evidence. Only two research claims are made at all — that time off is supported by research (Cauchi, uncited) and post-activation potentiation (Cauchi, correctly named but supported only by an anecdote).
- Everything here is calibrated to **elite raw powerlifters** peaking for IPF-style meets. The "don't test, don't log, don't count macros" advice is explicitly framed as something you earn with years of experience — all three did the meticulous version first, and Cauchi carves out beginners as needing the opposite.
- **The intuitive weight-cutting approach carries real risk.** Cauchi's own description — 4 hours of sauna, 18 hours without water, near-collapse — is presented candidly as unglamorous and painful, not as a template.
- **Name flags from auto-captions:** the Arnold competitors Gibbs mentions are rendered "Kelly Branson and leay nordon" — most likely **Kelly Branton and Layne Norton**, uncertain. The crying IPF lifter is given only as "George". The track-and-field anecdote's team and decade are explicitly uncertain in Cauchi's own telling. "Robin" (a previous podcast guest) is unidentified.
- Totals and bodyweights are quoted conversationally: Gibbs is described as totalling ~830 kg and sitting at 86–88 kg (peak 88, heaviest ever ~90); Hancott is 5'9" in the 74 kg class training at ~77 kg. Treat as approximate.
