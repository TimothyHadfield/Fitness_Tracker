# What "Evidence-Based" Actually Means

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "What does "evidence-based" mean?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4qK0GgpzHq8)) · 2015-06-05 · 10 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- "Evidence-based" and "science-based" fitness are the same thing, and the term was **borrowed from medicine**, where it named a 1990s movement to stop physicians from cherry-picking research and defaulting to inherited technique.
- The standard definition **already includes practitioner expertise**. The common complaint that evidence-based people "only look at studies" misreads the term — integration of expertise with external evidence is built into it.
- "There aren't good studies on bodybuilders, so ignore research" is a bad argument. The absence of a direct trial is where expertise plus the adjacent evidence you *do* have takes over — it is not permission to abandon evidence.
- Measured against a five-part standard of good reasoning, the science-based fitness community does well on **breadth of evidence** and **logical consistency**, and poorly on **thoroughness**, **freedom from bias** and, worst of all, **ethics and professionalism**.
- Financial ties to a position are the main engine of bias in this space: research that contradicts a paid-for belief gets denigrated rather than absorbed.

## Where the term comes from [01:03]
Evidence-based medicine emerged in the early 1990s as a systematised approach to putting scientific research into clinical practice, replacing arbitrary selection of research plus subjective application of personal preference and handed-down technique. Fitness took the term from there.

Jeff's preferred definition comes from **Dr David Sackett**: evidence-based medicine is the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients — integrating **individual clinical expertise** with the best available external clinical evidence from systematic research.

Two components, not one. The expertise half matters most where hands-on judgment dominates, e.g. surgical technique, where a clinician necessarily draws on their own experience and that of practitioners before them.

## The "not enough research on bodybuilders" objection [02:36]
The medical analogy [03:06]: there are no randomised controlled trials randomising stab-wound-to-the-heart victims to competing treatments. Nobody concludes from that gap that emergency medicine should be improvised on vibes. Clinicians integrate expertise, lean on whatever evidence does exist, and derive best practice.

Bodybuilding works the same way. An individual athlete's preferences, or a specific need arising from injury, will regularly land outside anything directly studied. That is precisely the situation the expertise-plus-best-practice component was written for.

## Five standards of good reasoning [04:08]
He puts up a list attributed to the founder of Science-Based Medicine — the captions render the name as "Dr Steven Nolla", **almost certainly Dr Steven Novella, though the transcript is garbled and this should be verified**. The standards: reasonably accounts for all available evidence; uses valid and internally consistent logic; is intellectually thorough, rigorous and methodical; is reasonably fair and unbiased in judgment; and adheres to standards of ethics and professionalism.

His grading of the science-based fitness community:

**1. Accounts for all available evidence — good.** No shortage of papers circulating; people share and discuss new research constantly.

**2. Valid, internally consistent logic — good within the niche.** Poor in the fitness community at large, but the science-based subset generally draws conclusions that actually follow from the evidence presented.

**3. Thorough, rigorous, methodical — needs work.** The failure mode is cherry-picking a single study, posting the abstract, and treating it as proof. Abstracts get read; methods and subject characteristics do not. He includes himself in this criticism.

**4. Reasonably fair and unbiased — a big problem.** Many people in this space are **financially tied to a set of beliefs** — it is how they are paid, and everyone needs a proprietary method. When research conflicts with those convictions, the defensive move is to denigrate the study rather than update. Everyone has cognitive biases and nobody escapes them; the operative word in the standard is **reasonably**. Strive for reasonably unbiased while admitting residual, sometimes subconscious, bias.

**5. Ethics and professionalism — the biggest gap.** People who satisfy the first four perfectly are often the worst on this one. If your primary method of spreading good information is being rude, elitist and deliberately confrontational online, that is where your effort should go.

This is not a ban on criticism. Bad ideas should be criticised, ideas should be examined critically, and ridicule is sometimes warranted — especially for people **speaking from a position of authority** who broadcast nonsense to a mass audience.

## Frankfurt on bullshit [08:16]
He quotes **Harry Frankfurt's *On Bullshit***: bullshit is unavoidable whenever circumstances require someone to talk without knowing what they are talking about; its production is stimulated whenever a person's obligations or opportunities to speak about a topic exceed their knowledge of the relevant facts.

Frankfurt's argument is that this is a **greater threat than lying**. A liar at least tracks the truth well enough to steer away from it; the bullshitter is indifferent to whether what they say is true and simply says whatever serves their interest. Jeff treats this as the central obstacle in the fitness space — which justifies criticism, while making the professionalism failure worse when criticism is the *only* mode of engagement.

## Practical application
- Treat "evidence-based" as a two-part standard: systematic research **and** informed practical judgment. Someone doing only one of the two is not meeting it.
- When no direct study exists, do not default to tradition or to "what works" — reason from adjacent evidence plus experience, and say which part you are leaning on.
- Read past the abstract. Methods and subject population decide whether a finding transfers to you.
- Ask who is paid to hold a position — including your own. Expect defensiveness where a belief is a revenue stream.
- The "obligation to speak exceeds knowledge of the facts" test is a useful filter for high-output content: volume-driven output on topics outside the speaker's knowledge is where bullshit reliably comes from.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The name behind the five standards is garbled in the auto-captions ("Steven Nolla"); Steven Novella is the likely referent but this is not certain from the transcript.
- This is a 2015 methodology video with no empirical claims about training or nutrition to verify; it is an argument about how to reason, not a summary of findings.
- Jeff's grading of the community on each of the five standards is his subjective assessment, offered as such.
