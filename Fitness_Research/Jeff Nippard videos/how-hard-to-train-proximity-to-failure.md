# How Hard To Train: Proximity To Failure, RPE and Rep-Speed

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Tell If You're Training Hard Enough (Using Science)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=deDlhPmT2SY)) · 2021-04-26 · 18 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- Failure isn't "it feels hard" — it's an **involuntary set endpoint** where you physically can't move the weight (Fischer & Steele definition).
- You don't need failure for growth: Gergich et al. pooled 15 studies (~400 participants) and found **similar muscle growth with or without training to failure** — and the last two reps before failure are disproportionately fatiguing.
- Since more weekly volume drives more growth (Krieger meta-analysis: **10+ sets/muscle/week beats 5-9, which beats <5**), the optimal play is to leave a little in the tank so you can do more total sets.
- Target **1-3 RIR (RPE 7-9)** for most hypertrophy work, with a good chunk at ~1 RIR. On lighter sets (12-15+ reps) leave **no more than 1-2 reps**.
- **Rep speed is the most objective cue available**: if the concentric doesn't slow at all near the end, you're probably not close to failure.

## Defining failure
[01:03] Fischer and Steele: muscular failure is when, despite greatest effort, a person cannot meet the demands of the exercise, causing an involuntary set endpoint. You stop because the muscle can't produce enough force, not because it feels unpleasant.

Two coaching sub-definitions:
- **Absolute failure** — you can't move the weight at all, even with altered form or other muscles helping.
- **Technical failure** — you can't do another rep with unchanged form.

[01:33] Most coaches prefer the technical definition, but it has a flaw: on free weights, form always drifts somewhat as you approach failure, so a zero-deviation standard ends sets before the target muscle has reached its force-generating potential. Nippard sits in the middle — **minor form deviation is acceptable if it buys real reps**, but once you're inventing a new exercise to move the load, that's not failure. Because machines make form easy to hold constant, tolerate **little or no** deviation there.

## Rep speed as an objective marker
[02:35] A 2019 bench press study found that once subjects were halfway through a set, reps had already slowed by **30%**; on the last possible rep the bar moved about **80% slower**.

[08:11] One research group built equations predicting reps in reserve from velocity loss within a set. Example from that data: bench at **80% 1RM** with reps slowed by **50%** corresponds to about **1.6 RIR** (i.e. 1-2 reps left).

How much slowdown you see is highly individual and exercise-dependent. But if reps don't slow at all, you're most likely not at failure; noticeable slowing means you're at least somewhat close.

## The volume argument against always training to failure
[03:35] **Approach 1 — go to failure every set.** Simple, no risk of undershooting, and there is peer-reviewed support for maximising intensity of effort to recruit as many motor units and fibres as possible. But that recommendation always sits inside a **low-volume, single-set-to-failure** paradigm. It's a reasonable option for people very short on time who want stimulus per minute. It is not optimal long-term because it forces weekly volume down.

[04:37] Krieger's meta-analysis: of 15 included studies, **11 fell on the higher-volume side** of the centre line — not the random scatter you'd expect if volume didn't matter. Broken down by sets per muscle per week: **≥10 sets beats 5-9 sets beats <5 sets**.

[05:07] Gergich and colleagues' systematic review and meta-analysis (15 studies, ~400 participants) found **similar hypertrophy regardless of whether sets went to failure**. Other research shows the final two reps before failure are disproportionately fatiguing. Failure cost is exercise-dependent — failure on lateral raises is nowhere near as costly as failure on deadlifts — but per set, failure is clearly harder to recover from.

Conclusion: same stimulus, less fatigue, more weekly sets. Which means you need a way to quantify effort without going to failure.

## RPE and RIR
[06:08] **RPE** = rating of perceived exertion, 1-10. **RIR** = reps in reserve. RPE 10 = 0 RIR (failure), RPE 9 = 1 RIR, RPE 8 = 2 RIR, and so on.

How far from failure you can be and still maximise growth is, in Nippard's words, "the million dollar question." Expert positions cited:
- **Dr. Mike Zourdos** — comfortable setting the threshold low in certain contexts, suggesting you can grow fine at **5-7 reps from failure (RPE 3-5)**.
- **Dr. Eric Helms** — **0-5 RIR (RPE 5-10)** is a good hypertrophy range.
- **Dr. Brad Schoenfeld** — higher bar: **1-3 RIR (RPE 7-9)**, plus taking the last set or two to failure on certain exercises.

[07:10] Nippard's reconciliation is load-dependent: with **sufficiently heavy weight** you can get a solid stimulus leaving 4-5 reps in the tank, because most motor units are recruited from the first rep — especially in advanced trainees. But with **lighter loads (sets over 12-15 reps)** you should leave **no more than 1-2 reps**.

## Calibrating your RIR estimates
[07:41] RPE/RIR is subjective, and bad estimators risk falling under the threshold. Two fixes:
1. **Anchoring sets** — pick an exercise you can fail safely on, go all out with good form, call out when you think you have two reps left, then keep pushing to see what a true RPE 10 feels like and how far off your call was.
2. **Film a set and watch it back** — observe whether rep speed changes near the end. If it doesn't slow at all, you're not working as hard as it felt.

## Worked examples from the video
- **Nippard's own set** (11 reps with Eric Helms, target RPE 8): rep 1 = 0.80 s, rep 2 = 0.90 s, rep 9 = 1.10 s, rep 10 = 1.13 s, last rep = 1.23 s → **28% velocity loss**. Plugged into the Rodríguez-Rosell squat data at ~70% 1RM, that's ~**3.5 RIR — an RPE 6-7, not 8**. He should have done another one or two reps.
- **Chris Bumstead (RPE 10)**: rep 5 = 1.27 s, rep 6 = 1.23 s, rep 7 = 1.9 s, rep 8 = 1.9 s, rep 9 = 2.5 s, last rep = **3.2 s**. A clean 0 RIR set. Nippard would save this exertion for the **last set of an exercise**, or stop two reps shy and push like this only in the **last week of a training phase**.
- **John Meadows (1 RIR)**: explosive, consistent rep speed, then an abrupt wall on the last couple of reps — one more grinding rep available, no more. This is where **a good chunk of hypertrophy training should live**.
- **Kai Greene (RPE 7-8)**: ~2-3 reps left based on bar speed — still a perfectly stimulative working set given the load.
- **Ronnie Coleman pulldown set**: no slowdown, no grind, likely **5-10 reps left** — insufficiently stimulative on a per-set basis for the average person (though it may have been a warm-up).
- **Alberto Nunez pulldowns (RPE 9-10)**: no technique breakdown, final concentric takes a full **4.17 s**. Perfect effort for the last back set on a pull day.
- **Greg Doucette squat set — too much effort.** Working around an injury, pushing RPE 10 at high risk and recovery cost. Better: strip the weight back to ~RPE 7 and ramp RPE up over weeks as technique locks in; save RPE 10 for machines or isolation work.
- **Matt Ogus squat (1-2 RIR)**: dialled technique, visible slowdown, no back-breaking grind — the model RPE 8-9 set, best placed toward the end of a training cycle.

## Where rep speed misleads
[15:23] **Stefi Cohen deadlifts** — her first rep looked like it might already be RPE 10; she went on to do ten reps, only slowing around rep 9 with a near-five-second final rep. You cannot judge RIR from a single rep in isolation; individual differences are large.

[16:27] **Sunny Andrews elevated dumbbell squats** — deliberate mind-muscle connection, constant tension, smooth tempo both directions. The rep-speed research used explosive concentrics; if you're intentionally modifying tempo, velocity-based RIR estimation gets much harder.

[16:57] Same caveat resolves the opening example: Jay showed less slowdown (0.57 s per rep, rising to 0.70 s then 1.03 s on the last two) but uses a strong mind-muscle connection and consistent cadence, hitting the wall abruptly. Nippard judges that set **maximally stimulative** despite the modest velocity loss.

## Practical application
- Put most hypertrophy sets at **1-3 RIR**, with a good share at ~1 RIR.
- Heavy sets: 4-5 RIR can still be productive. Light/high-rep sets (12-15+): 1-2 RIR maximum.
- Save RPE 10 for the **last set of an exercise**, or the **last week of a block**, and preferentially on machines and isolation work where failure is safe and cheap.
- Do periodic **anchoring sets** to recalibrate your RIR calls.
- **Film sets** and check for concentric slowdown. No slowdown = not close.
- Never chase RPE 10 on a lift whose technique isn't dialled in — drop to RPE 7 and ramp up over weeks.

## Caveats & unknowns
- How many reps you can leave and still maximise growth is actively debated; credible experts span 0-7 RIR.
- Velocity-loss equations were built on specific lifts and loads — Nippard explicitly says he doesn't think the data extrapolates cleanly across exercises.
- Rep-speed reading breaks down for deliberately slow or mind-muscle-focused tempos.
- Individual differences in how much reps slow before failure are large; some lifters hit the wall abruptly with little warning.
- Cost of failure varies hugely by exercise: lateral raises vs. deadlifts are not comparable.
