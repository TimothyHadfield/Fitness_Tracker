# When Should You Train To Failure?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "When Should You Train To Failure? | Showing You Our New Home in Canada" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PNDpzfmj5dQ)) · 2017-11-03 · 14 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- Failure training costs far more fatigue than stopping short, even when total volume is matched — performance and muscle-damage markers stay suppressed roughly twice as long.
- A 2016 meta-analysis found no meaningful strength or hypertrophy advantage for failure vs. non-failure, regardless of exercise type (isolation vs. compound) or training experience.
- Since gains look similar but recovery is worse, failure should be used sparingly rather than as the default on every set.
- MASS's recommendation: reserve failure for isolation exercises, and only on the last set of the last exercise for a given body part.
- Brad Schoenfeld's angle: failure can be periodised — intensity-focused blocks with more failure sets, alternating with volume-focused blocks that stop short.

## The two competing arguments [05:08]
**Against failure:** going all-out early in a session accumulates fatigue that eats into later sets. Worked example on bench press, 3×8-10 at 225 lb — taking every set to failure might give 10, then 7, then 5 reps (≈22 total). Leaving 1-2 reps in reserve might give 9, 8, 8 (25 total). More total volume by not going to failure.

**For failure:** you need to reach failure, or get sufficiently close, to recruit the full spectrum of motor units and stimulate the larger, higher-threshold fibres. Stopping too far short leaves growth on the table.

## The 2017 acute study [06:42]
Ten trained young men, averaging **eight years** of training experience — unusually well-trained for exercise science. Crossover design (every subject did all three protocols) with **three-to-four-week washout periods** between them. Smith machine bench press and Smith machine squat.

Three protocols:
- **High volume to failure:** 3 sets of 10 reps with a 10RM (every set to failure, RPE 10).
- **High volume, not to failure:** 5 reps with a 10RM (5 reps in reserve, ~RPE 5), 6 sets per exercise — volume-matched to the failure group.
- **Low volume, not to failure:** 3 sets of 5 reps with a 10RM.

Measures: countermovement jump height (general performance proxy), mean propulsive velocity on bench and squat at a fixed load, plus blood markers — creatine kinase (indirect muscle-damage marker), growth hormone, testosterone:cortisol ratio. Time points: pre, immediately post, 6 h, 24 h, 48 h and 72 h post-session. Acute design only — no long-term strength or hypertrophy outcomes.

**Results [09:49]:** the two non-failure protocols barely differed from each other; the only significant difference was higher growth hormone in the high-volume non-failure condition immediately post-session. The failure vs. non-failure gap was much larger:

| Marker | Not to failure | To failure |
|---|---|---|
| Countermovement jump | Impaired immediately post only | Impaired for 24 h |
| Squat performance | Not impaired | Impaired for 24 h |
| Bench press performance | Not impaired | Impaired for 48 h |
| Creatine kinase | Back to baseline by 24 h | Back to baseline by 48 h |

Volume-matched, failure training produced significantly more fatigue and longer-lasting performance impairment.

## Practical application
- Default to leaving reps in reserve on most working sets. Non-failure training lets you recover faster, accumulate more total volume, and train each muscle at a higher frequency for similar gains.
- When you do go to failure, save it for **isolation exercises**, on the **final set of the final exercise** for that body part.
- Programme failure in blocks rather than continuously: high-intensity phases with more failure sets alternating with volume phases that stay short of it.
- The opposite error is also real — many lifters overestimate how close to failure they actually are. If you are habitually stopping 4-5 reps short on everything, push harder before blaming programming.
- Expect a genuine failure session on compounds to cost you 24-48 h of impaired performance; schedule accordingly.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The 2017 study is acute only, n=10, and measures fatigue markers rather than muscle growth — it says nothing directly about long-term hypertrophy from failure training. Jeff does not name its authors, and the transcript does not name them.
- The 2016 meta-analysis is likewise unnamed in the video.
- The MASS reviewer is referred to only as "Eric" in the auto-captions (almost certainly one of the MASS authors, but the surname is never stated — treat the attribution as uncertain).
- Jeff explicitly withholds his own position, noting the scientific community is split.
- The Schoenfeld periodisation point comes from an unreleased interview and is described as an informal remark, not a study finding.
