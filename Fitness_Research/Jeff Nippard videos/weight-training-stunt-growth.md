# Weight Training Does Not Stunt Growth in Kids and Teens

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Does Weight Training Stunt Your Growth?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6HXIw75JFlE)) · 2018-01-22 · 3 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- The "lifting stunts growth" idea traces to a 1964 Japanese observation of short-statured child labourers — correlational, and confounded by undernourishment.
- Growth-plate damage from resistance training has only ever been demonstrated in the case of actual bone fracture, which is far more likely in contact sports than in the weight room.
- A 2014 international consensus position statement (243 references, endorsed by bodies from the American Academy of Pediatrics to the NSCA) states fears of growth-plate injury from resistance training are unsupported.
- Loading may actually *help* bone development: mechanical stress on growing plates appears osteogenic.
- Conditions: proper technique instruction, sensible progression, adult supervision.

## Where the myth came from
In 1964 a Japanese research team noticed that children doing heavy manual labour tended to be short. They hypothesised the lifting and carrying was the cause. Over the years this hardened into a proposed mechanism: excessive external load damages the growth plates at the ends of long bones, causing them to close prematurely and capping final height.

Two problems with that story:

1. **Correlation, not causation.** Those child labourers weren't just overworked — they were also undernourished. Nutrition is far more central to reaching your genetic height potential than lifting is.
2. **The mechanism runs the wrong way.** Premature growth-plate closure from loading has only been shown to occur where a genuine bone fracture happened. Fractures of that kind come mainly from contact sports (football, hockey), not from controlled resistance training.

## What the evidence actually says [01:36]
The 2014 international consensus position statement on youth resistance training — drawing on 243 scientific references and endorsed by essentially every relevant regulatory body, including the American Academy of Pediatrics and the NSCA — concluded that fears resistance training injures the growth plates of youths are not supported by scientific reports or clinical observation. It went further: the mechanical stress placed on developing growth plates by resistance exercise, or by high-strain sports such as gymnastics and weightlifting, may be *beneficial* for bone formation.

A more mechanistically focused review in *Sports Medicine* notes that high loads play a critical role in bone mass acquisition during and before puberty. Under the **mechanostat theory** of bone formation (the auto-captions garble this as "Makino stat" / "mccanna stat" — mechanostat is the near-certain intended term, and the reviewers' names are not given in the transcript), tensile, compressive, shear, bending and torsional stresses applied to bone during sport trigger osteogenic — bone-building — responses during growth.

## Additional benefits for young lifters [02:37]
Beyond being safe, youth resistance training has been shown to help with:
- Bone health
- Injury prevention
- General strength
- Perceived sports competence and overall self-esteem

Nippard also makes a behavioural argument: getting kids lifting early helps build habits they're likely to carry into adulthood.

## Practical application
- There is no height-based reason to keep children or adolescents out of the weight room.
- Do require three things before a young person trains: **proper instruction on technique**, **sensible progression**, and **an adult supervising**.
- Treat resistance training as bone-*positive* for this age group, not bone-negative — loading during and before puberty is a window for building bone mass.
- If growth-plate injury is the worry, note that contact sports carry the higher fracture risk, not supervised lifting.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The safety verdict is conditional on supervision and technique. The consensus statement is not a licence for unsupervised maximal lifting by untrained kids.
- The original 1964 observation is epidemiological and cannot establish causation in either direction.
- Nippard names no individual researchers here; the claims rest on the 2014 consensus statement and an unnamed *Sports Medicine* review. He treats this topic as effectively settled within the scientific community, with converging evidence and near-unanimous agreement.
