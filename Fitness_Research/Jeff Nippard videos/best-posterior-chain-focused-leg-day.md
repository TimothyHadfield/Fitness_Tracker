# Posterior-Chain Leg Day: Glute, Hamstring and Erector Focused Session

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Best Posterior Chain Focused Leg Day (Glutes, Hamstrings, Lower Back)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GgW5EtBrCuE)) · 2019-05-19 · 10 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- This is the second lower-body day of a 4-day upper/lower split, biased toward glutes, hamstrings and spinal erectors rather than quads.
- Session: deadlift 2x5 (adding a set per week to 4 sets by week 3), wide-stance low-bar box squat 3x8 (adding a rep per week to 10), constant-tension barbell hip thrust 3x12, leg extension / seated leg curl superset 3x20 each with 30 s rest between every set, seated calf raise, ab wheel rollout 3x8.
- Four modifications turn a normal squat into a posterior-chain squat: low bar position, sitting back onto a box, stance up to 2x shoulder width, and up to 45 degrees of foot flare.
- Deadlifts belong on a lower-body day, not a back day — the upper back only contracts isometrically as a stabiliser, while quads, glutes and hamstrings work through full lengthening and shortening under load.
- Two sessions of this kind is enough volume for beginners and most intermediates; advanced lifters may want a third leg day targeting their weakest area.

## Deadlift — 2x5, volume-based double progression [01:02]
Week 1 is 2x5, adding one set each week to 4x5 in week 3, then back to 2x5 in week 4 with more weight. Nippard uses this volume-focused double progression specifically for technical lifts — deadlift, overhead press, pull-up — because extra sets buy more skill practice, which drives strength quickly as proficiency improves, while the accumulated volume also drives size.

Sumo or conventional both work. If you always pull one way, spending a month or two on the other can expose and fix weak points; sumo is a good accessory for conventional strength and vice versa. Activation differences: sumo hits the quads a bit more, conventional hits the spinal erectors a bit more, and glute/hamstring activation is essentially the same between them ([Escamilla 2002](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/11932579)).

On upper-back involvement [02:05]: an EMG study (researcher name garbled in the captions — do not cite) found lats are highly active early in the pull, with a large drop-off by roughly 70% of the way up, so they mainly stabilise the spine and stop the bar drifting forward. A 2002 paper found upper traps peak as the bar passes the knee and mid traps peak at the start of the lift ([Escamilla 2002](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/11932579)). All of these are isometric stabilisers, which is why the deadlift is a lower-body exercise.

## Box squat — 3x8, four posterior-chain modifications [03:06]
Add one rep per week to 10 in week 3, then back to 8 in week 4 with more weight.

1. **Low bar** — bar on the rear delts rather than the upper traps. A 2017 review from Glassbrook and colleagues (captions say "glass broke et al") concluded that lifters seeking posterior-chain hip development (glutes, hamstrings, erectors) may want the low-bar squat ([Glassbrook 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/28570490)). Keep the bar centred over midfoot on the way down and up; initiate by sitting the hips back without letting the chest collapse forward.
2. **Box** — set at roughly kneecap height, or wherever sitting on it puts you at parallel. It removes the fear of falling backwards and reinforces sitting back, shifting emphasis from knee to hip musculature.
3. **Wide stance** — up to 2x shoulder width if comfortable. Research from Paoli and colleagues found a wide stance is necessary for greater gluteus maximus activation in the back squat ([Paoli 2009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/19130646)).
4. **Foot flare** — rotate the feet out up to 45 degrees, since hip external rotation is a primary glute function.

Nippard notes specificity still matters for squat strength (if you want a big squat, squat often), but box, front and pause squats remain highly specific while training weak points, so they serve both hypertrophy and long-term strength. Start lighter than your usual load for the rep range; it feels awkward at first.

## Constant-tension hip thrust — 3x12 [05:08]
Work only the top half of the range. Research from Worrell and colleagues found the glutes fire hardest at or near full hip extension, so the top of the thrust gives the most glute stimulus per rep ([Worrell 2001](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/11767248)). Many lifters use the bottom of the range to let the bar rest passively, which isn't wrong if it lets you load heavier, but it means the glutes aren't firing maximally throughout the set. Emerging research also hints at a benefit to constant tension generally — not letting the muscle rest between reps ([Goto 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/31034463)) — which suits an exercise like this where an internal mind-muscle focus helps. Stop at neutral hips; do not hyperextend the lower back at lockout. Go lighter and chase the mind-muscle connection.

## Superset with 30-second rests — leg extension / seated leg curl, 3x20 each [06:40]
Rather than zero rest, take 30 seconds between every single set: 20 extensions, rest 30 s, 20 curls, rest 30 s, repeat until 3 sets of each are done. There is no rule that supersets require zero rest — the short rest simply keeps the pace up. Splitting a one-minute rest into two 30-second chunks lets you push harder on every set instead of being needlessly winded. With the heavy work already done, the purpose here is added volume in a metabolic-stress rep zone.

## Calves and abs [08:14]
**Seated calf raise:** 8 reps with a full pause at the bottom, a squeeze at the top and a slow negative, then 8 more reps pulsed in the middle of the range — no bouncing out of the bottom, no pause or squeeze.

**Ab wheel rollout, 3x8:** think of it as a dynamic plank that gets harder as the arms extend and the lever lengthens. A 2008 EMG study from Youdas and colleagues (captions say "Judas et al") found the ab wheel produced the highest activation of the four exercises tested, including the crunch ([Youdas 2008](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/18978615)). Squeeze the glutes throughout, actively brace the abs to stop the spine extending, and shorten the range if the lower back dips.

## Practical application
- Run the two lower-body sessions inside a 4-day upper/lower split; that is enough weekly leg volume for beginners and most intermediates.
- Advanced lifters: add a third leg day and put the extra volume into your weak point (hamstrings, glutes, quads, calves or abs).
- Use the technical-lift progression pattern (add a set per week for 3 weeks, then reset with more weight) on deadlift, overhead press and pull-up.
- Use the rep-based progression (add a rep per week for 3 weeks, then reset heavier) on the box squat.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The auto-captions garble several researcher names. The 2017 low-bar study, the glute-activation study behind the constant-tension hip thrust and the 2008 ab EMG study have been matched to the papers linked above; the deadlift lat-activation study has not, so that finding is stated as given in the video and remains unverified.
- Nippard says the constant-tension idea is supported by research that has not specifically studied the glutes — it is an extrapolation.
- The workouts shown are not identical to those in his upper/lower program, only built on the same principles and similar volumes.
