# How Much Training Volume Do You Really Need?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How Much Training Volume Do You Really Need? (Science Explained)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qwv3JqOUqWs)) · 2018-07-11 · 6 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- 10+ hard sets per muscle per week is the well-supported floor: the meta-analysis data has it producing roughly double the growth of under 10 sets.
- The upper limit is genuinely unknown. Unpublished data James Krieger describes suggests 20-30 sets can still beat 10 sets, contradicting the "plateau around 20" picture Jeff previously drew.
- No study has ever shown muscle *loss* from adding volume. Some show a plateau; none show a reversal.
- If you're not growing and your recovery has room, adding volume is the first lever to pull.
- Past ~20 sets it becomes a risk/reward balance: marginal extra gains vs. an injury that could cost you far more time than those gains are worth.

## Where the 10-20 set range came from
Jeff's earlier video recommended 10-20 sets per body part per week, sketched as a curve where most of the available growth arrives by 10 sets, 20 sets adds a diminishing amount, and 20-30 sets risks overreaching and blunting gains. He got heavy pushback that this was too low.

The 10-set floor comes from a 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis by Brad Schoenfeld, Dan Ogborn and James Krieger [01:01] ([Schoenfeld 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/27433992)). It found that more than 10 weekly sets per muscle roughly **doubled** hypertrophy compared with fewer than 10. What it did *not* establish was an upper bound — the 10-20 "sweet spot" was expert opinion layered on top of the data, including the researchers' own, and it lined up with Mike Israetel's MRV (maximum recoverable volume) framework (captions render him as "Mike Israel").

Jeff's own practical heuristic at the time [01:33]: 10 sets/week/body part is a minimum effective dose for the average intermediate. 20 isn't magic, but if you're consistently doing more than 20 sets for a muscle and progress has stalled while recovery feels burdened, cut back. If you're doing 8 sets and it isn't working, volume is probably not too high.

## What Krieger said on the call
Krieger told Jeff he would have drawn the same curve until recently, but data he'd seen was about to "blow the idea of an upper limit out of the water" — even at 20-30 sets you can get significantly more growth than at 10 [02:35]. So the right shape of the curve past 10-20 sets is currently a row of question marks rather than a plateau-then-decline.

Two supporting points from that conversation:
- **No decline has been observed.** Some studies plateau; none show volume causing muscle loss. Any claim about where the ceiling sits is speculation informed by experience, not established research.
- **Volume landmarks are exercise-specific.** A set of heavy Romanian deadlifts imposes far more fatigue and recovery cost than a set of leg curls, so raw set counts across exercises aren't equivalent currency [03:36].

Both Jeff and Krieger flagged the misinterpretation risk explicitly: "no proven ceiling" is not an endorsement to chase 45 sets per body part, especially for beginners.

## Specialization phases
Krieger's suggested use for high volume is a specialization block: push one lagging body part up to roughly **30 sets/week** while holding everything else at maintenance (roughly 10-20 sets), then rotate the emphasis to another body part. Prioritize weak points or whatever you most want to improve. Jeff thought this was most useful for advanced trainees.

## Practical application
- Set your baseline at **10-20 sets per muscle per week**. Beginners can get a great deal from the low end.
- Stalled and recovering fine? **Raise volume first** before changing anything more exotic.
- Running above 20 sets while progress is poor and recovery feels wrecked? **Drop volume.**
- Count sets by exercise cost, not just number — heavy compounds bite into recovery harder than isolation work.
- To specialize: one muscle at ~30 sets, rest at maintenance (10-20), cycle body parts over time.
- Monitor progress and recovery yourself and adjust up or down; the ballpark numbers are a starting point, not a prescription.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The "more is better past 20 sets" claim rests on data Krieger had not yet published at the time of this video (July 2018). Treat it as a heads-up, not a settled finding.
- The upper limit of useful volume is unknown; anyone quoting one is speculating.
- Findings are averages — individual recovery capacity, exercise selection, training age and life stress all shift where your own ceiling sits.
- Higher volume raises injury exposure, which can cost more training time than the extra gains are worth.
