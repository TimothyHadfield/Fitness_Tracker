# Calf Training: Gastroc vs Soleus, Exercise Choice, and How Much Volume

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Scientific Way to Train CALVES (Science Explained)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=21inrjhoFkQ)) · 2018-02-28 · 12 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- Run **at least one standing (gastroc) and one seated (soleus) calf raise variation** — the two heads are trained by different knee positions.
- **Pause at the bottom of every rep.** Bouncing hands the work to the Achilles tendon instead of the muscle.
- Fix technique before adding volume. Only after that, push toward **~70 reps per session, 3x/week** (upper end of Baum et al. 2007).
- **Foot position (toes in/out) barely matters** — the evidence is mixed, so use whatever is comfortable.
- Stalled despite high volume? Take a **1-2 week deload** (full break or 50% volume cut) to resensitise, then rebuild.

## Anatomy [00:00]
"Calves" = the triceps surae: **gastrocnemius** (medial + lateral heads) and **soleus**.
- **Gastroc** originates on the femur, inserts on the heel via the Achilles. Bi-articular: it flexes the knee (like a leg curl) *and* plantar flexes the ankle.
- **Soleus** sits underneath the gastroc but is **larger by muscle volume**. Originates on the tibia/fibula, so it crosses only the ankle — plantar flexion only, no knee action.

Because the soleus is the bigger muscle, Nippard argues it deserves deliberate attention rather than being an afterthought.

## Why calves seem stubborn [01:02]
Two speculative mechanisms, both flagged as unproven:
1. **Androgen receptor density** is highest in the neck and traps and declines moving down the body; per Lyle McDonald, receptor density at the calves is very low, so testosterone exerts less effect.
2. **Fibre type** — current evidence suggests both gastroc and soleus are **type I (slow-twitch) dominant**; the soleus is by some estimates up to **96% slow-twitch**. Type II fibres have greater hypertrophic potential, especially under heavy loads.

His own view: the biggest limiting factor is that people get discouraged and stop training them properly. Example given — pro natural bodybuilder Jeff Alberts' 24-year calf transformation, built on consistency and progression.

## Standing (gastroc-dominant) work [04:07]
- EMG data from Bret Contreras/Bo Heck/Boskey ranked the **donkey calf raise** highest for activation. Nippard notes the usual caveat that EMG amplitude is a proxy, not proof of hypertrophy, and that the inference has drawn criticism.
- No donkey machine? Load it with a partner sitting/plate on the lower back, a Smith bar across the lower back, or a barbell set lengthwise on staggered safety pins so full ROM is preserved.
- **Calf jump**: knees and hips slightly bent, drive explosively up into the pads. Purpose is to change the resistance curve — a **2003 Kyoto University study** found peak calf torque occurs at **lower ankle angles**, i.e. the calves are strongest at the bottom, so explosive bottom-end force carries into the weak top end.
- **Single-leg calf raise**: good for finding and fixing left/right imbalances.

## Foot position [06:09]
- **Riemann et al. 2011** (20 healthy subjects): toes-out (external rotation) increased **medial gastroc** activation.
- Three 2017 studies: one weakly supported more inner-head activity with toes out; **Pereira et al. found no significant difference**; a third also found none.
- Biomechanical reason for the null result: foot rotation comes from **internal/external rotation at the hip**, and the gastroc does not cross the hip. Verdict: **no meaningful difference — pick what's comfortable.**

## Seated (soleus-dominant) work [03:05]
- With the knee bent, the gastroc is already shortened at the knee and is **actively insufficient** at the ankle, shifting work to the soleus. A **2012 study** found that increasing knee flexion from **0° to 45° increased soleus activation**.
- No seated machine? Dumbbells on the knees with plates under the forefoot works, but the machine is better.
- Reps: the size principle (low-threshold slow-twitch fibres recruited first, active longest, most fatigue) gives a theoretical case for **higher reps** on the soleus. But the fibre-type literature is split — some studies show type I fibres growing better on light/high reps, others on heavy/low reps. **Safest bet: use a variety of rep ranges, high and low, rather than one range for everything.**

## Volume and frequency [08:42]
Jeff Alberts' actual routine, relayed by text: standing calf raises, **1-2x/week**, full ROM, **6-8 reps**, perfect technique and progressive overload — repeated for 24 years.

Nippard's ordering: master technique first (pause every rep, mind-muscle connection, try different variations and foot positions). Only then escalate volume to the top of the **Baum et al. 2007** range: **~70 reps per session, 3x/week**, ideally arranged as a daily-undulating setup so rep ranges vary across the week.

## Practical application
- Standing variation (donkey, calf jump, or single-leg) **plus** a seated variation every week.
- Full range of motion with a **pause at the bottom of every rep** to bleed off tendon elastic energy.
- Start conservative — something like Alberts' 6-8 reps, 1-2x/week with hard progression — before assuming you need more.
- If you want the aggressive dose: ~70 reps/session, 3x/week, mixing heavy and high-rep work.
- Toe angle: comfort, not dogma.
- Plateaued at high volume with clean technique? **Deload 1-2 weeks** (zero calf work, or half volume), then resume at lower volume.

## Caveats & unknowns
- No direct research confirms calves are genuinely "stubborn"; the androgen-receptor explanation is called **speculative at best**.
- EMG-to-hypertrophy inference is contested within the research community — treat activation rankings as a first approximation.
- Fibre-type-to-rep-range matching is unresolved; the high-rep soleus case is theoretical, not proven.
- Foot-position findings conflict across four studies (2011 vs three 2017 papers).
