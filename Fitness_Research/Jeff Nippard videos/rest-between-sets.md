# How Long to Rest Between Sets

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How Long Should You REST Between Sets? | Fundamentals Series Ep. 5" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NR94rNsArv0)) · 2018-10-13 · 12 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **Heavy compound exercises: rest 3-5 minutes.** **Isolation exercises: rest 1-2 minutes.**
- The old textbook prescription of ~1 minute for hypertrophy is outdated — longer rest produced more growth *and* more strength.
- Almost never rest under 1 minute. The main exceptions are antagonist supersets when you're short on time, and occasional pump/intensity work.
- Rest periods serve two functions only: enough recovery to maintain performance across sets, and time management.
- You don't need to time your rest precisely — being roughly in the right range is enough.
- Don't shorten rest to burn fat. The calorie difference is trivial; use diet and cardio for fat loss.

## The old model (now questioned)
[03:35] Standard strength-and-conditioning textbook tables still say:
- **Hypertrophy:** ~1 minute
- **Strength:** 3-5 minutes
- **Muscular endurance:** under ~30 seconds

Recent literature has undermined the hypertrophy row of this table.

## The evidence
[04:36]
**Schoenfeld and colleagues, 2016.** Two groups on an 8-week full-body program: **1 minute rest** vs **3 minutes rest** between sets. Results after 8 weeks:
- Greater hypertrophy in the **quads and triceps** in the 3-minute group.
- Greater strength — both **bench press 1RM and squat 1RM** — in the 3-minute group.
- **No difference** in muscular endurance.

A clear win for the longer rest period ([Schoenfeld 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/26605807)).

**Grgic and colleagues, 2017 meta-analysis.** Pooled six studies on rest intervals and found that **resting more than one minute beat resting less than one minute** ([Grgic 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/28641044)). The main proposed rationale: longer rest lets you perform more total volume. Effects also depend on the specific exercise and how hard you're exerting yourself on it.

## Why rest matters: the two functions
[06:07]
1. **Recovery between sets.** Too little rest cuts the work you can do. His example: 3×5 on bench with 300 lb. The first set of five is fine; with only a minute of rest the second set might yield only two or three reps. Since volume ranks high in the training priority list, short rest that shrinks volume is self-defeating.
2. **Time management.** Rest that's too long drags the session out and lets you lose steam — and you start scrolling your phone and dilute the workout's pace.

## Practical recommendations
[07:42]
**Heavy compounds (large muscle mass, high recovery cost): 3-5 minutes.**
- Where you land in that range depends on how damaging the movement is: a **barbell row** might need ~3 minutes, a **heavy squat** closer to 5.
- It also depends on load: a **heavy triple on squats** warrants at least 5 minutes; lighter speed work needs less.

**Isolation exercises (smaller muscles, lower recovery cost): 1-2 minutes.**
- Closer to 1 minute if you want to get in and out quickly; up to 2 minutes if you have time and want full recovery. Don't go beyond 2 — it slows the session pointlessly.

**Don't stopwatch it.** As long as you're roughly in the right range and paying attention, you're fine.

## Exceptions to the 1-minute floor
[09:47]
- **Antagonist (separated) supersets** — e.g. lat pulldown paired with dumbbell fly. While you train the back, the chest is recovering, and vice versa; if each set takes about a minute, each muscle effectively still gets rest. He recommends this only for people genuinely pressed for time.
- **Intensity technique / pump work** — short rest can force more blood into the muscle. He frames this as an advanced technique done for fun, not something fundamentally driving progress.
- **Not for fat loss.** Shorter rest makes you sweat and feel like you're working harder, but the calorie difference between a 15-20 second rest and a 1-2 minute rest is very small. Treat training as the vehicle for muscle gain, and diet plus cardio as the tools for fat loss — structuring a lifting program around fat burning has it backwards.

## Where rest sits in the priority ladder
[00:00-03:05] Rest periods rank below the higher-order variables he has already covered, though they still matter. His recap of the ladder and prior chapters:
- Safety + enjoyment → sustainability → effort (close to failure, not necessarily at failure) → progressive overload + prioritisation → acute variables.
- **Volume:** 10-20 sets per body part per week is the sweet spot.
- **Frequency:** 2× per week per body part beats 1×; 3×+ may help if it lets you do more volume, but at equal volume 2× is the best-supported.
- **Intensity:** 6-15 reps is the practical hypertrophy zone; roughly **75% of volume** should sit there.
- **Exercise selection:** compounds form the core; isolation covers smaller assistance muscles (biceps, calves, hamstrings).

## Practical application
- Program 3-5 minutes between sets of squats, deadlifts, presses and rows — nearer 5 for the heaviest, most damaging work.
- Program 1-2 minutes on curls, extensions, raises and machine accessory work.
- Never habitually rest under a minute; if time is tight, pair antagonist movements instead of just cutting rest.
- Keep an eye on total session length — if the workout is dragging, tighten isolation rest toward 1 minute rather than cutting compound rest.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Optimal rest depends on the exercise and the effort level of the set; individual discretion is expected.
- The 2017 meta-analysis established only that >1 minute beats <1 minute, not an exact optimum.
- He notes some newer literature suggesting more than 20 sets per week may work for some body parts, but calls that speculative.
