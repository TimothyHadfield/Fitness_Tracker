# Five Reasons to Train Glutes (Men and Women)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "5 Reasons Why EVERYONE Should Want Huge Glutes (Men & Women)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BgsGtqJwqRc)) · 2018-09-08 · 14 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- The glutes have four functions: hip extension (primary), hip abduction, hip external rotation, and posterior pelvic tilt.
- Glutes drive the hip-extension half of the squat and deadlift; weak glutes cap your strength on both.
- Strong glutes counteract knee valgus (via abduction and external rotation) and reduce the need to substitute spinal extension for hip extension in the deadlift, which is what causes lower-back rounding.
- Glutes matter more than quads for sprinting and contribute heavily to rotational striking power.
- Glute strengthening both prevents and rehabilitates lower back pain, per a sizeable body of research.

## Reason 1: Aesthetics [02:03]
Glute development reads as overall muscularity. Jeff points to Ronnie Coleman's glute definition as what makes his back and side poses land, and notes that at the NPC Nationals classic physique division, the best-placing competitors also tended to have the best glutes — competitors who looked muscular from the front but "disappeared" turning side-on were the ones lacking glute development, even with big quads.

For recreational lifters the same logic applies in clothing. Side and rear angles are three of the four directions people see you from, so underdeveloped glutes leave most of your visual impression flat.

On attractiveness [04:37]: there is a fair body of research on why men find glutes attractive on women — Jeff cites a Brett Contreras article arguing the popularity of denim and its advertising helped push the glutes past the breasts as the primary sexual characteristic. There is much less research on the reverse, but survey and interview data suggest women do find muscular glutes attractive in men, with a sweet spot somewhere between flat and Ronnie Coleman.

## Reason 2: Performance [05:41]
EMG research shows the squat trains the quads more than the glutes ([Contreras 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/26252837)), but that does not mean the glutes are idle. The squat has two biomechanical demands: knee extension (quads) and hip extension. Of the two primary hip extensors, the hamstrings are near-silent in the squat because they lengthen at the knee while shortening at the hip simultaneously — leaving the glutes to contribute substantially to hip extension.

Because of biomechanical differences, the deadlift involves the glutes more than the squat does. Jeff's observation from dozens of powerlifting meets: he has never seen a high-level lifter with an impressive squat and a flat butt.

The glutes also stabilise the pelvis and, by extension, the core — so they matter in essentially every lower-body movement and in upper-body lifts like the overhead press.

## Reason 3: Injury risk [08:15]
**Knee valgus.** The knees caving in during a squat is hip internal rotation plus hip adduction — precisely the opposite of the glutes' two secondary functions. Powerful glutes performing external rotation and abduction counteract it. This is the logic behind powerlifters squatting with a hip circle around the knees. An alternative explanation is overpowering adductors dragging the knees in, which has some literature support. Caveat: it isn't fully clear what causes valgus in any given case, or how dangerous it is — most experts agree it can contribute to knee pain, but elite lifters who control it well may not be at the same risk as recreational lifters.

**Lower back rounding.** Some *upper* back rounding is probably fine and most good coaches accept it (Jeff rounds his upper back when deadlifting, a function of natural spinal curvature). *Lower* back rounding under heavy load is considered problematic. His mechanism: when the glutes can't carry the hip-extension demand alone, the lifter combines hip extension with spinal extension, recruiting the erectors to finish the lift. Stronger glutes remove that need.

## Reason 4: Athleticism [11:15]
Citing another Brett Contreras article, nearly every athletic movement is heavily glute-influenced. Research on sprinting shows the glutes matter far more than the quads for driving the body forward. MMA fighters and coaches report glutes contributing significantly to striking power, since striking power comes largely from hip rotation.

## Reason 5: Posture and back pain [12:18]
Prolonged sitting is one contributor to postural problems and to what's been called "sleepy glutes" — glutes that don't activate well during exercise or walking. Since the glutes underpin hip extension and an upright torso, strengthening them builds a postural base from the bottom up. Beyond looking more confident, this reduces lower back pain, and the research suggests glute strengthening is rehabilitative for pain that already exists, not just preventive.

## Evolutionary aside [01:03]
From *Sapiens* (Yuval Noah Harari): humans evolved unusually large glutes, and that evolution allowed us to stand and walk upright. Jeff notes more recent literature has questioned the direction of causality — it may be that we developed upright posture first and large glutes followed as a consequence. Either way, big glutes are a distinctly human trait relative to our ape ancestors.

## Practical application
- Train glutes directly, regardless of sex or goal — don't assume squats and deadlifts cover them.
- Include work in all four glute functions, not just hip extension: abduction and external rotation work has a direct injury-risk payoff at the knee.
- If your lower back rounds on heavy deadlifts, treat it as a glute-strength problem rather than only a cueing problem.
- Athletes in sprinting or striking sports should treat glute work as sport-specific, not cosmetic.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This is an off-the-cuff walk-and-talk video: the arguments are mechanistic and observational, with few named studies. Several claims (MMA striking, powerlifter physiques) are anecdote or hearsay, and Jeff presents them as such.
- Knee valgus causation and danger are genuinely contested.
- The evolutionary chicken-and-egg question is unresolved.
