# Bro Splits vs Higher Frequency: What the Frequency Research Actually Supports

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "BRO SPLITS: What Does the Science ACTUALLY Say?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6PgsKMDUExE)) · 2016-05-04 · 15 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **Train each muscle roughly twice per week.** Three times is fine if your split allows it, but there is no evidence 3x beats 2x for hypertrophy.
- Once per week is probably sub-optimal but still produces considerable growth — the classic 8-day-rotation bro split is the version he doesn't defend.
- The frequency research is weaker than its popularity suggests: the strongest positive finding comes from one study using only **three sets per muscle per week**.
- **Volume is the main driver of hypertrophy.** Splits matter only insofar as they let you hit enough weekly volume, recover between sessions, and stay consistent.
- His own fix: keep the big once-a-week volume hit per muscle, then add a small secondary hit (about **4 sets, one taken to failure**) a couple of days later — that already makes it 2–3x/week.

## What actually matters in a split [04:11]
Three criteria, in order:
1. It lets you accumulate enough weekly training volume.
2. It allows sufficient recovery between sessions.
3. Subjective fit — enjoyment, work schedule, time available, and whether it prioritises your weak points.

Claims that one split is *physiologically* superior to another are overblown. People spend too much time worrying about how the week is arranged.

## Enjoyment vs adherence [00:32]
He accepts the criticism that enjoyment and adherence are separate things — a disciplined lifter can adhere to a program they hate (his own example: a phase of 6x20 squats with 1-minute rests, which he completed while dreading it). But adherence has three levels:
1. **Base adherence** — showing up and doing the sessions.
2. **Adherence to effort** — actually pushing hard when it's called for.
3. **Long-term adherence** — sustaining it over 5, 10, 20 years.

Discipline is a limited resource, like attention. Spending it on things that probably don't matter much is a poor allocation. So enjoyment functions as a **tiebreaker between protocols that are physiologically similar** — which, on frequency, most of them are.

## The frequency evidence, examined [05:41]

**Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger meta-analysis (10 studies).** Conclusion: 2x/week promotes superior hypertrophy vs 1x/week; whether **3x beats 2x remains undetermined**; and training a muscle once per week still promoted robust hypertrophy and remains a viable design ([Schoenfeld 2016](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/27102172)). The same paper explicitly acknowledges the split-routine argument — that lower per-muscle frequency permits higher volume per muscle group while maintaining intensity of effort and recovery — and states it remains to be determined whether that is an effective strategy.

The paper also notes that **relatively few trials** showed a preferential effect of any one frequency. Only two significant studies were highlighted:
- **McLester et al.** — 3 days/week vs 1 day/week: 8% vs 1% gain in lean body mass ([McLester](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232186690_Comparison_of_1_Day_and_3_Days_Per_Week_of_Equal-Volume_Resistance_Training_in_Experienced_Subjects)). **But subjects performed only three sets per muscle per week.** Three total sets for shoulders is nothing like real-world programming; unsurprising that spreading such a tiny dose out helped.
- **Schoenfeld et al.** — a difference appeared **only in the biceps**. Thickness changes in the triceps and vastus lateralis were comparable between groups.

The remaining studies found comparable effects across frequencies of 1–3x/week.

**StrengthandConditioningResearch.com review [09:15].**
- *Untrained subjects* (4 studies): higher frequency unlikely to be superior for hypertrophy; one study actually favoured the **lower** frequency.
- *Trained subjects* (3 studies): only one reported a benefit of higher volume-matched frequency — and that one was McLester again. Their own summary table rates research quantity as "few studies" and confidence as **low**.

**Wernbom et al. 2007 [11:18].** Recommends 2–3x/week per muscle ([Wernbom 2007](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/17326698)), but almost none of the underlying studies matched for volume — so the higher-frequency groups were often simply doing double or triple the work. The one volume-equated study showing a frequency effect was, again, McLester.

## His own split adjustment [12:49]
The classic bro split leaves seven full days between hits on a muscle. His version: a large bolus of volume for a muscle, a rest day, a smaller hit of volume, a rest day, then the big bolus again — every 5–6 days, with a top-up in between. That small hit counts, because even **one all-out set to failure** is enough to activate the mechanisms that stimulate muscle protein synthesis. To be safe he uses about **4 sets with one taken to failure** for the top-up.

The single change he made after re-examining the research: add **3–4 sets of wide-grip pull-ups at the start of leg day**, before legs, to carry back frequency over until the next back session. The other muscle groups already got their secondary hit elsewhere in the week.

## Practical application
- Default recommendation: hit each muscle **twice a week**. Go to three only if the split naturally allows it.
- If you prefer a low-frequency, high-volume-per-session style: keep it, but insert a small secondary hit for each muscle mid-week — around 4 sets, one to failure — so nothing waits 7–8 days.
- Prioritise weekly volume and effort over split architecture.
- If you enjoy a lower frequency and it lets you train harder and stay consistent, you are not leaving much on the table.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Whether 3x/week beats 2x/week is genuinely unresolved.
- The frequency data is preliminary, low-confidence, and largely built on volume-unmatched or very low-volume protocols.
- It's untested whether lower-frequency split routines allow enough extra volume per muscle to offset the frequency disadvantage — the meta-analysis authors say so themselves.
- In untrained populations, higher frequency shows no benefit and may even be slightly worse.
- He is openly stating a personal bias toward lower frequency and trying to correct for it; this is an update to an earlier pro-bro-split video, not a neutral review.
