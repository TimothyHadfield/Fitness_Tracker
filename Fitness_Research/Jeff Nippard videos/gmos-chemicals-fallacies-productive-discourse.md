# Appeal to Nature, "Chemicals", and How to Argue About Food Science Properly

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "GMOs, \"Chemicals\", Fallacies and Productive Discourse" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CIKnyxp4eyg)) · 2014-10-19 · 7 min
**Topic:** Myths & Evidence

## Bottom line
- "Avoid chemicals" is a **category error**: everything is made of chemicals, and an intimidating name says nothing about safety.
- The **appeal to nature** — natural is good, synthetic is suspect — fails on its own terms: sharks and cyanide are natural, sucralose and aspartame are synthesised and have extensive safety data.
- But naming a fallacy is **not an argument**. Shouting "naturalistic fallacy" to win without engaging the person's actual concern is intellectual laziness.
- **Informal fallacies are context-dependent**, unlike formal ones. An appeal to authority is fallacious when the authority isn't legitimate, and perfectly sound when they are.
- Mild wariness about a newly modified food is **rational** — it just gets resolved by testing, not by the fact of modification.

## The chemical category error [00:44]
Nippard shows the full chemical constituent list of an apple. Some names are easy to say, some are long and unpronounceable, but **nothing on that list is harmful in virtue of having a fancy name or being classified as a chemical**. Taking everything in the category "chemicals" and importing a negative connotation is a straightforward error.

His worked example: **1,3,7-trimethylxanthine** sounds like something you'd never want in your body. It's **caffeine**. Supplement labels sometimes print the IUPAC-style name to sound sophisticated, and it can backfire when a consumer reads it as scary chemistry.

## The appeal to nature [02:14]
Definition: assuming something is unsafe or negative **because it doesn't occur in nature**, or better **because it does**. Nippard's read on GMO opposition is that the core objection is usually this — the food isn't natural because we modified it ourselves.

Follow the logic through and it breaks:
- **Sharks** and **cyanide** (found in apple seeds) occur in nature and would have to be counted safe.
- **Sucralose** and **aspartame** are synthesised in labs, carry a large body of safety data, and are known to be safe in the relevant amounts — yet get judged bad purely for being synthetic.

He references the Jimmy Kimmel farmers-market segment, where people asked what GMOs were couldn't say, as an illustration of how thin the opposition often is.

## The real target: fallacy-spotting as a substitute for thinking [03:17]
This is the methodological heart of the video. People are increasingly quick to jump on the fallacy bandwagon — labelling a claim "appeal to nature", "naturalistic fallacy", "ad hominem", "straw man" — **and then not engaging with what was actually said**. The label becomes a way to win the exchange rather than a way to understand the position.

Nippard's own concession against his side: **all else being equal, it is reasonable to be somewhat more wary of something synthetically produced than of something that has worked for us for tens of thousands of years.** Eating apples as found in nature is known-safe by long track record. When you start modifying genes, there is nothing irrational about extra caution about that process. What doesn't follow is the conclusion: **once a genetically modified food product has been tested and shown safe, there is no more reason to call it unsafe than there was to assume the original was unsafe.** Wariness is a reason to test, not a verdict.

## Formal vs informal fallacies [04:48]
- **Formal fallacies** are errors of logical structure — always wrong, regardless of content.
- **Informal fallacies** are wrong **depending on context and situation**.

His illustration with the **appeal to authority**:
- "Phil Heath doesn't do If It Fits Your Macros, therefore IIFYM doesn't work / isn't optimal" — here the fallacy charge is roughly right, but to make it stick **you'd still have to show that Phil Heath isn't a legitimate expert on the topic**. The label alone doesn't do the work.
- Your doctor gives you a diagnosis. Replying "that's an appeal to authority" would be absurd — **he genuinely is an authority on that topic**, so speaking from authority isn't fallacious there.

The same context-sensitivity applies to the appeal to nature: people do commit it about GMOs, and it's still legitimate to be wary of the unnatural and sceptical about it.

## Productive discourse instead [06:51]
His closing example: someone says "IIFYM doesn't work because eating donuts all day is bad." The lazy move is "that's a straw man, I never said eat donuts all day." The better move is to **engage**: ask why they think donuts are bad, and look for common ground. The point of the exchange is understanding the underlying topic, not scoring the fallacy.

## Practical application
- When a claim rests on a substance sounding artificial, ask for the **dose and the safety data**, not the name.
- When you're tempted to name a fallacy, first restate the other person's actual concern and answer it. Name the fallacy only if it's still doing explanatory work afterwards.
- For informal fallacies, do the extra step the label skips — e.g. for appeal to authority, actually establish whether the cited person is a legitimate expert in that domain.
- Treat "this is new and synthetic" as a prompt to check testing, not as a conclusion about safety.

## Caveats & unknowns
- His overall verdict is that **GMOs currently on the market have been shown to be safe and there's no reason to avoid them** — with the explicit acknowledgement that you *could* genetically modify something to be toxic. Safety is a property of the specific tested product, not of the modification process.
- No specific studies or safety datasets are cited by name; the video is an argument about reasoning, not an evidence review.
