# Natural vs Enhanced Bodybuilding: John Meadows on Training, Weak Points and Steroids

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Natural vs Enhanced: Becoming the Best Bodybuilder You Can Be ft. John Meadows (MountainDog)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-TGJqJZ3EaA)) · 2018-01-25 · 72 min
**Topic:** PEDs

*Unscripted conversation. John Meadows: 60+ contests, 11 pro shows (placed in 5), CSCS, coach to hundreds of amateur and pro bodybuilders. Roughly half his clients over the years have been natural.*

## Bottom line
- Meadows' "Mountain Dog" template sequences a workout in four phases: **activation → heavy compound → supramaximal pump + one intensity technique → loaded stretch**.
- Compounds go **second or third**, not first — this is what he credits for ending a career of recurring pec strains and for his clients' longevity.
- Only the **last set** of an exercise goes to true failure; earlier sets still count as volume.
- Naturals need **less volume** than enhanced lifters; enhanced lifters recover far faster and can handle more, which is why drug-fuelled programs are poor models for naturals.
- Weak points are an **exercise-execution problem first**, a frequency problem second, and almost never a dose problem. Adding frequency requires cutting volume elsewhere — recovery is finite.
- On drugs: train natural as long as you possibly can, use the lowest effective dose, and monitor blood work **twice a year**.

## The Mountain Dog training structure [08:40]
Meadows organises every "base workout" into phases:

1. **Activation.** A controlled, feel-focused movement — machine press for chest, a strict row or pulldown for back. Not necessarily isolation; the goal is blood flow, nutrient delivery and getting the right muscle firing. **8-10 reps**, working up in weight with the **last set to failure**. Example: warm up, then 200 lb x 8-10, 225 x 8, 250 x 8 (his 8-rep max). Purpose is **50/50 activation and injury prevention** — since adopting it he has never had another pec injury.
2. **Heavy compound.** Favourite for chest is the slight incline barbell bench. Reps drop to about **5-7, generally around 6**, targeting roughly **85% of 1RM** for as many reps as possible. He wants **maximal force applied to the bar** — a sprinter's mentality, driving up as fast as possible — to recruit high-threshold motor units. Form must be perfect; he works people up until they can *just* complete the target reps with clean execution.
3. **"Supramaximal pump" phase.** Get as much cell swelling as possible. The **fourth set here is where the high-intensity technique goes** — a drop set, accentuated eccentrics with manual resistance, ISO holds, or partials. Just **one such set**, not the whole workout.
4. **Loaded stretch finisher.** A movement that elongates the muscle belly hard after it's pumped: flyes for chest, stiff-legged deadlifts for hamstrings, pullovers for lats. He says he's never seen anyone get injured at that point in the session.

**Frequency:** each body part twice a week. The **second session drops the heavy compound phase**, which is how he raises frequency without accumulating joint and connective tissue damage.

Practical note: people feel weaker on the compound at first. Meadows says that's **fatigue, not weakness** — stick with it and strength returns and then exceeds the old numbers. Jeff frames it as one step back to take two forward.

## On failure and overtraining [20:55]
Taking every set to failure is more damage than you need. Only the final set of a movement goes to true failure with good form.

Signs you've gone too far, acutely and chronically:
- Extreme soreness — "can't walk for five days" is overboard. More soreness is **not** more growth; Meadows says he was completely wrong about this when younger.
- Declining performance, peak power and endurance.
- **Bar speed** dropping — his key early warning. If 315 used to fly up and suddenly grinds, something is wrong.
- Loss of pumps, moodiness, disrupted insulin sensitivity.

The fix may be a deload, or something as simple as dialling back the intensity on that one failure set.

## Genetic limits and weak points
Most people claiming a genetic limit have never truly been pushed. Meadows himself was stuck at 210 lb competing for ten years and was convinced he'd capped out — he was wrong. Before accepting a limit, a lifter should have genuinely varied **intensity, volume and frequency** over years.

His weak-point protocol, in order:
1. **Watch them train it.** Is the chest caving in, is tension going to delts and triceps, is the range of motion short? Doing an exercise wrong more often doesn't help.
2. **Then add frequency** — instead of hitting it every five days, go every 2-3 days (48 hours is customary), so it never bottoms out and restarts. Going from 4 to 10 workouts per 30 days can add tissue over a year.
3. **But pay for it.** Recovery is finite. When he raises frequency on a weak body part he **cuts volume on a strong one** (e.g. back). You cannot mindlessly add volume to an existing routine.

**Calf example:** Meadows built 20-inch calves despite what he calls weak calf genetics. His fix was training **out of the stretch** — pause in the stretched position, use progressive overload, build toward the whole stack — rather than the usual fast quarter-reps at the top for sets of 25.

**Biceps:** usually trained too heavy, with lower back or shoulder involvement and insufficient range of motion, so the muscle never lengthens.

Genetics vs work: the best have both. Meadows has narrow clavicles, a wide pelvic girdle, a high pelvic crest and high lats — structural disadvantages he overcame with smart training. But he believes there is a **minimum genetic threshold** for pro status; telling everyone they can be a pro is false hope.

## Natural vs enhanced [30:41]
- Drugs are "a different ballgame" — greatly increased protein synthesis, greatly reduced muscle protein breakdown, far faster recovery.
- **Recovery has its own genetic component:** some naturals recover exceptionally fast, and some enhanced lifters recover poorly. Those are exceptions; the rule is that someone on steroids, GH and insulin recovers roughly ten times faster.
- **This has corrupted bodybuilding methodology.** People on heavy doses grow on bad programs, assume the programs are good, and prescribe them to everyone else.
- **Naturals need less volume**; enhanced lifters tolerate more, and more volume can mean more muscle.
- Meadows: he could never have reached pro status naturally. But you can build an excellent physique naturally — the ceiling is pro-level competition, not a good body.
- He would **never in good conscience tell someone to use drugs**. He trained 8 years natural first (500 lb squat, 365 lb bench), and credits still carrying muscle on next to nothing to that foundation. Advice: turn over every rock — training approaches, nutritional approaches — before crossing that line. Results long-term will be better for it.
- "Just up the dose" is not a weak-point fix: it may grow the whole body but won't selectively fix lagging legs. He is not a fan.

## Harm reduction, dosing and blood work
- Meadows has run **straight HRT off-seasons for ~6 years** and has clients do the same successfully. With focused diet, nutrition and training you can get by on far less than people believe.
- His off-season approach for clients: find the **lowest amount that still produces progress** — often **400 mg test plus maybe 200 mg of something else**, far below what's now called "blasting" — run ~12 weeks, then PCT or drop to a true HRT dose. Ramp up only pre-contest, when protecting muscle in a deficit matters and multiple compounds are needed for the cosmetic look. Benefit: your body responds better to compounds you haven't been hammering year-round. He notes many who "jam it in the off-season, grams and grams" disappear within a couple of years.
- **HRT** = hormone replacement therapy, a low testosterone dose, ~**150-200 mg/week**. **PCT** = post-cycle therapy (hCG, clomid, Nolvadex) to restart natural testosterone, LH and FSH. The more cycles and years, the higher the odds of permanent shutdown — meaning lifelong medication and possible fertility loss. Meadows' last attempt to recover naturally was **2002**; it failed, and he's been on HRT since.
- He criticises gurus starting first-timers on very high test doses plus extras. People can do what they want, but should be educated first.
- **Diminishing returns are real**: past a certain dose you get side effects rather than results. The sweet spot is the most you can take with the fewest side effects and no damage to blood work. Response is highly individual (he could never tolerate trenbolone).
- **Markers he tracks, roughly twice a year:**
  - **Fasting insulin** (not just fasting glucose) — he puts this at the top of the list. GH can create glucose resistance; he knows several people who became diabetic from GH overuse. High insulin is linked to heart disease and much else.
  - **C-reactive protein** — systemic inflammation; should be ~1 or below, and he's seen underground-drug users at 8.
  - **Blood pressure** — chronic elevation causes accumulating kidney damage and scarring that doesn't reverse.
  - **Triglycerides and HDL** — wants triglycerides low, HDL high. He considers total cholesterol fairly meaningless and LDL debatable.
  - **Hemoglobin, hematocrit, red blood cell count** — reliably elevated on gear. Fix: **donate blood twice a year**, which usually keeps them in range.
  - For a TRT candidate he insists on **total and free testosterone** before anything else. He has naturally high free testosterone, a genetic advantage he'd never have known about without regular testing.
- **Health context:** the body isn't meant to carry 300 lb of muscle — high bodyweight for your height strains the heart, and muscle demands far more blood supply than fat. Deaths like Rich Piana's and Dallas McCarver's involved genetic predispositions on top of that. Meadows (turning 46) was scheduled for a coronary calcium score test. There *is* a case where drugs improve health: if total/free testosterone is genuinely low, restoring it to a normal range is healthy, since low testosterone is linked to heart disease and depression.
- He also advises assessing the **person's life**, not just their numbers: spouse, kids, fertility plans, addictive tendencies, and whether their training and diet are even dialled in yet. Bodybuilding, he says, is a selfish sport that has wrecked many relationships. Note too that he kept a corporate job (VP at Chase for 12 years) and treated bodybuilding as a hobby, which he thinks more competitors should do.
- On who uses most: it's often not the biggest guys. In the 90s and 2000s the heaviest abuse was at the **national amateur** level, because pro cards were so scarce. He recounts a 195 lb 212-class competitor whose coach had him on triple Meadows' lifetime maximum, and a lifter taking **18 IU of GH per day**.

## Practical application
- Reorder your sessions: activation (8-10 reps, last set to failure) → heavy compound (~6 reps at ~85% 1RM, explosive intent, perfect form) → pump work with one intensity technique on the final set → loaded stretch.
- Hit each muscle twice weekly, dropping the heavy compound in the second session.
- Take **one set per exercise** to true failure, not all of them.
- Before blaming genetics or adding volume for a weak body part, film and fix execution — full range, stretch emphasis, appropriate load.
- If you raise frequency somewhere, cut volume somewhere else.
- Train calves out of a paused stretch with progressive overload; train biceps lighter with full range.
- Watch bar speed as your primary overtraining warning sign.
- Young and frustrated with slow gains? Eat more.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This is coaching experience and observation, not controlled research. Where research is invoked (Schoenfeld on cell swelling; velocity-based motor unit recruitment) it's mentioned in passing, not cited specifically.
- Any drug discussion here is descriptive of what competitive bodybuilders do; both speakers stress there is no risk-free approach, only lower-risk approaches, and Jeff and Meadows both advise against use.
- Audio glitches and static are noted in the recording; a few passages are hard to follow.
