# Does Foam Rolling Actually Aid Recovery?

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Does Foam Rolling Work? (Better Recovery and Less Soreness?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9fvaZ_U6o3Y)) · 2018-02-20 · 11 min
**Topic:** Recovery

## Bottom line
- Foam rolling is self-massage (a form of manual therapy). The recovery claim is *not* busted: several trials show reduced soreness and modestly better range of motion.
- But it likely offers little that a good dynamic warm-up and cool-down doesn't already provide.
- Unlike prolonged static stretching, it improves ROM without blunting strength — which makes it a safe pre-lift mobility tool.
- Jeff's own practice: no more than 5 minutes per workout, targeted at tight areas, done before training on top of a normal dynamic warm-up.
- Downside risk is essentially zero; the only real cost is time and energy.

## Where the idea came from
Manual therapy traces back to ancient India and China, but the foam roller itself is recent. Feldenkrais-method practitioners used rollers for "body awareness" in the 1980s (Jeff considers that method debunked). Broadway's Jerome Robbins popularised it with ballet dancers through the late 80s/90s. Physical therapist Mike Clark brought it to the lifting world in the late 90s, formalised in his 2001 text *Integrated Training for the New Millennium*. The first foam roller patent came in 2004, from LA physical therapist Stacey Barrows, and the research literature followed.

## What "recovery" even means
A 2010 overtraining paper defines recovery as a multi-level process of re-establishing performance capacity — essentially, how long until your body is back to normal. Researchers usually proxy this with muscle soreness, most often on a 10 cm visual analog scale.

## The soreness evidence
- **A 2017 soccer-player study** used a 1-7 verbal scale ("sore" / "very sore" / "very very sore") and found soreness dropped from 5.6 to 4.8 with rolling. Jeff considers this too subjective to carry much practical weight, though he doesn't dismiss it outright.
- **MacDonald et al., 2014 (Memorial University of Newfoundland):** subjects did 10x10 back squats, then either 20 minutes of foam rolling or nothing. The rolling group reported lower soreness (1-10 scale) at 24, 48 and 72 hours. Authors concluded rolling attenuates soreness.
- **Same group, 2015 replication:** same design but using the more objective *pressure pain threshold* method — a calibrated device applies force and the subject says when pressure becomes pain, so higher force = less soreness. Again, 20 minutes of post-exercise rolling reduced soreness at every time point, and also improved some performance measures including 30 m sprint time.
- **A 2017 sprint-training study** (recovery from high-volume sprints, 0-100 soreness scale) found **no** difference between groups. The authors' explanation: their soreness measurements were always taken *after* a warm-up, whereas the earlier studies measured on arrival at the lab before any activity. The warm-up may have reduced soreness in both groups, wiping out any extra effect of rolling.

Jeff leans toward the earlier studies for lifters, since 10x10 squats resembles high-volume damaging resistance training far more than 40 bouts of 15-30 m sprints.

## Range of motion
- **2015 systematic review (14 studies):** self-myofascial release with a foam roller or roller massager produces short-term increases in joint ROM *without* impairing muscle performance. This is the key advantage over prolonged static stretching, which is known to reduce strength and performance if done pre-training.
- **Vigotsky et al., 2015** (name garbled in captions as "Vygotsky") concluded rolling is unlikely to add much ROM if you're already doing dynamic stretching.

## Practical application
- Spend **no more than ~5 minutes** per session. Target specific tight areas rather than rolling the whole body — mostly to save time, since whole-body rolling has no known downside beyond time and energy.
- Jeff prefers it **before** training, in addition to (not instead of) a 5-10 minute dynamic warm-up that raises body temperature, drives blood flow and takes joints through full ROM.
- Use it to unlock positions for specific lifts: inner thighs and glutes before sumo deadlifts (better hip position); upper back before bench pressing (better arch and setup).
- If you buy the soreness research specifically, note those studies rolled **after** training — so add it to your cool-down too.
- Rolling a sore area before a session can make training feel more comfortable; treat this as self-experimentation.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Soreness ratings are subjective; some scales used in this literature are poorly constructed.
- No good evidence that rolling beats or adds meaningfully to a dynamic warm-up and cool-down — the studies mostly compared rolling to *nothing*.
- More high-quality research is needed before firm conclusions. Verdict: "not busted", but not special either.
