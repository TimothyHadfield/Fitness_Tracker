# Supplements, recovery and physiology

Twenty-eight notes on what actually grows muscle, what you can buy that helps, what you can't, and
what happens to your body when you stop training, get sick, drink, or inject testosterone.
Organised by decision, not by video.

Two framings run through all of it. **Supplements are a rounding error** — Eric Helms' line, quoted
approvingly, is that even the best of them make a very small difference, so be a **late adopter**
and wait a year or two for replication ([worst supplements](worst-supplements-everyone-takes-muscle-growth.md)).
And **the natural/enhanced gap is enormous**: creatine at 5 g/day buys roughly **2 lb of extra
muscle in a year**; a normal bodybuilding steroid dose buys roughly **20 lb**
([steroids are awesome](steroids-awesome.md)).

## How muscle actually grows

**Mechanical tension is the primary driver, and the only one of the three classic candidates that
survives scrutiny** ([build muscle](build-muscle.md)). Schoenfeld's 2010 model proposed tension,
muscle damage and metabolic stress. Two of the three have fallen over.

- **Tension** — force trying to stretch the fibre. Undisputed.
- **Muscle damage** almost certainly does not cause growth. A marathon causes enormous damage and
  nothing good. Damas' work shows the early-program MPS spike goes overwhelmingly into *repairing*
  damage; only once damage subsides does synthesis get redirected toward hypertrophy. The eccentric
  counter-argument confuses correlation with causation — eccentrics let you load more heavily, which
  is tension again.
- **Metabolic stress** fails four tests: short rests, partial ROM, training to failure and
  blood-flow restriction all produce more metabolic stress and equal-or-worse growth.

So: **chase tension, not soreness and not the pump.** Longer rests and full ROM beat short rests and
partials.

**The cascade:** tension, then mechanosensors, mTOR, the nucleus, mRNA, the ribosome, translation,
myofibrillar hypertrophy. Two branches matter. **Leucine also activates mTOR** — roughly **3 g of
leucine**, in about **20 g of high-quality protein** — on a much shorter stimulative window than
training's, so the two complement each other. And the **testosterone branch**, which within the
natural range does relatively little; the dramatic version needs injected high-dose testosterone.

Unknowns the note flags itself: **which molecule does the tension sensing is unsettled** (costameres,
titin, filamin are the candidates, and the review authors call it one of the big remaining questions
in exercise physiology); sarcoplasmic hypertrophy and myonuclear addition are live debates; muscle
damage is poorly understood down to how you'd measure it.

**Fibre type barely matters for rep selection** ([fibre types Q&A](fast-vs-slow-twitch-fibers-dangerous-exercises.md)).
Almost every muscle worth developing is near a **50/50 type I / type II split** — quads roughly
perfect, hamstrings only about **60/40**. And the mechanism doesn't survive testing: type I fibres
respond well to both high and low reps, and so do type II. **The one exception is the soleus**, up
to **90% type I** in some studies, so put some calf work in the **15–25 rep** range. Everything else
gets a mix.

Worth knowing early: **hypertrophy is commonly delayed by one to two months** because neurological
adaptation precedes hypertrophic adaptation. Getting stronger before you get bigger is normal.

### Creatine, properly

From [creatine](creatine.md) unless noted.

- **Plain monohydrate.** HCl and ethyl ester lack comparable evidence and cost more.
- **3–5 g/day, forever, no cycling** — no tolerance develops, and **21 consecutive months** showed
  no adverse health effects.
- **Loading is optional.** 20–25 g/day for a week saturates immediately; 5 g/day gets there in about
  a month. Load only if starting fresh or returning from a long break.
- **Timing barely matters.** The 2013 post-workout finding was small and has **not been replicated**.
- **Effect: 5–15% more maximal strength and power** across a review of ~300 studies, and — more
  usefully — **30% more total reps across five sets** in subjects with *six years* of training. It
  may not build muscle directly; it lets you do more work.
- **Non-responders: roughly 20–30%.** High meat consumers and older trainees are likeliest. Gaining
  weight in the first few weeks with diet and training held constant is a decent signal.
- **Water goes inside the muscle**, not under the skin — fuller and tighter, not bloated. Keep
  taking it while cutting ([fibre types Q&A](fast-vs-slow-twitch-fibers-dangerous-exercises.md)).
- **The caffeine interaction is a non-issue** — the 1996 paper had **nine subjects**; three later
  studies found synergy.
- **Hair loss has never been directly investigated.** The concern traces to one 2009 rugby study
  where 5 g/day for two weeks raised **DHT by 40%**. But weight training raises DHT too, and DHT
  matters for hair loss mainly in men with a **genetic predisposition or family history**. If that's
  you, be more cautious. A reasonable worry, not a proven one.

## Supplements that don't

Be precise about the distinction: **BCAAs, fat burners and ecdysteroids have been tested and failed.
SARMs and turkesterone are largely untested, with a severe manufacturing problem on top.** Different
kinds of no.

**Test boosters.** A 2020 analysis of the five best-selling Amazon products found **three of five
didn't disclose ingredient amounts** at all; half the ingredients present had zero human studies
showing any effect; **saw palmetto and boron each had 2 positive studies against 6 showing nothing**
([worst supplements](worst-supplements-everyone-takes-muscle-growth.md)). The scale argument
settles it: injected testosterone at 600 mg took subjects from 630 to **2,370 ng/dL — a 1,740%
increase**; ashwagandha, the best natural option, managed **96 ng/dL**. The 4.5-star reviews are
placebo plus behaviour change — people who buy pills also start training harder.

**BCAAs.** Leucine triggers MPS, but you need **all nine essential amino acids** to build tissue, so
BCAAs alone is turning the key with no fuel in the tank. The **January 2022 review of 12 studies
found no benefit to performance, strength or muscle mass.** Narrow exceptions for *EAAs*: a
low-quality vegetarian meal, or insurance when training fasted and very lean.

**Beta-alanine works for something else.** A 2012 meta-analysis found it effective only for
**1–4 minute** efforts, nothing under 60 seconds. Useful for endurance, very high-rep and circuit
work; useless where hypertrophy training lives.

**Turkesterone is the cleanest case study in the library**
([turkesterone](turkesterone-explained-s-all-hype-about.md)). There is **no human research on
turkesterone itself** — every promoted study is on animals, insects, or a *different* ecdysteroid.
The best human trial (Campbell 2006, 45 trained men, 8 weeks, DEXA) found **no difference in
fat-free mass, 1RM strength, or any testosterone measure**. The 2019 study the hype rests on has
three fatal problems: the placebo group *lost* muscle, body composition was measured by
single-frequency bioelectrical impedance, and lab analysis found **6 mg of ecdysterone against a
labelled 100 mg**. Trexler's point generalises — a p-value of 0.049 is by definition a ~5% chance
result, and with groups of 12–20, four unusually gifted responders in one group is enough. The
**ISSN's 2018 review explicitly does not recommend ecdysterones.** Independent testing then found
**Greg Doucette's Turk Builder at 0.7 mg against a 5,100 mg claim — 0.15%**, Gorilla Mind's under
1%, and some products with none detectable. (Caveat the note states: the testing lab sells competing
supplements, and ecdysteroid detection chemistry is genuinely contested.)

**SARMs** ([SARMs](sarms-s-all-hype-about.md)). The 2017 JAMA investigation bought 44 products and
found **only 52% contained a single SARM** — one claiming 10 mg contained 0.1–1 mg. No body of
high-quality evidence for muscle building in healthy trainees, not legal to sell as supplements,
banned by WADA. Carpenter's two structural arguments are the keepers: **safety data generated at
therapeutic doses does not transfer to recreational doses**, and an extreme muscle builder that is
also extremely safe is not a plausible package. M1T was sold on the same template.

## Hormones

**Correcting a genuinely low testosterone level clearly helps; moving within the normal range
probably does little** ([increase testosterone naturally](increase-testosterone-naturally.md)).
Bhasin raised subjects from **306 to 570 ng/dL over 20 weeks and got about 3 kg (6.5 lb) of
fat-free mass — with no weight training.** The harmonised normal range for men 19–39 is
**264–916 ng/dL**. Whether 600 to 800 does anything is unknown, and the note says so.

The natural levers, biggest first:

- **Body composition and calories dominate.** A natural bodybuilder's contest-prep case study:
  intake 4,750 to 2,100 kcal/day, body fat 14.8% to 4.5%, testosterone **991 to 227 ng/dL — a 77%
  drop**. Wrestlers in a 500–1,000 kcal/day deficit for just **2–3 weeks** dropped **62%**. Overweight
  men run low too, as do the chronically overfed. Sweet spot: **10–15% body fat**.
- **Dietary fat 20–40% of calories.** Effects are small: a low-fat diet cut testosterone 12% in older
  men; a higher-fat diet raised it 13% in young men.
- **Micronutrients via deficiency** — zinc, magnesium, vitamin D (**up to 3,000 IU/day** if
  sun-deprived).
- **Sleep.** **8 to 5 hours for 5 nights cut testosterone 10.4%**; **33 hours of straight
  deprivation, 27%**. A different study cutting 10 to 4 hours found only a non-significant trend.
- **Volume suppresses it, intensity doesn't.** **10 one-rep-max squats daily for two weeks changed
  nothing**, while large volume increases repeatedly do reduce it.
- **Soy is not a problem** — a 32-study meta-analysis found no effect on bioavailable testosterone.

The note's own conclusion is the important part: **individually these levers are small.** The case is
that stacking several may add up, not that any one is transformative.

### Why acute testosterone spikes are a dead end

The cleanest worked example of a common inference error in the library
([the wood-chopping study](chopping-wood-before-training-boosts-testosterone-48.md)). A viewer asked
about a paper reporting a **48% testosterone rise after an hour of chopping wood** versus about
**20%** from lifting — should you chop wood before leg day? Two independent reasons no.

**The study doesn't transfer.** The subjects were **Tsimane forager-horticulturalists in Bolivia**,
aged **16 to 80**, whose **baseline salivary testosterone was already a third lower** than
age-matched US males; the paper itself notes that non-industrialised populations under energetic and
pathogenic stress run lower across all adult ages.

**And the premise was wrong anyway.** Kraemer's 1990s work — heavier loads and more muscle mass
produce bigger hormone spikes — launched a generation of programming built around chasing them.
West and colleagues at McMaster killed it: testosterone did rise with the high-intensity,
large-muscle-mass protocol, and there was **no difference in muscle protein synthesis between
groups**. Post-exercise hormone increases **cannot be used as proxy markers for hypertrophic
potential**. The spikes return to baseline within about an hour and never leave the physiological
range.

The general lesson: *a hormone moving in the right direction is not evidence that anything
downstream of it moved.* Confirmed from the other end by the bloodwork in
[look enhanced without steroids](look-enhanced-without-steroids.md) — among eight natural lifters
(**415–689 ng/dL**, one at 928), **more testosterone did not correlate with more muscle**.

## Steroids and the natural limit

Nippard's stance: use is a personal and legal decision for an informed adult, and the only ethical
problem is competing in drug-tested natural events while using, or being deceptive about it
([the decision to use steroids](decision-use-steroids.md)).

### How well they work

Bhasin 1996, 10 weeks, 600 mg testosterone/week
([how much muscle without steroids](much-muscle-build-without-steroids.md)):

| Group | Muscle gained |
|---|---|
| Trained natural | **+4.4 lb** |
| Trained + steroids | **+13.4 lb** |
| **Steroids, no training at all** | **+7 lb** |

**600 mg/week with no lifting beat natural training.** The 2001 dose-response follow-up, 20 weeks,
no lifting: the 600 mg group added roughly **20 lb — one pound of muscle per week, without
training**, with individual response ranging **9 lb to 31 lb**.

Dosing context from an anonymous anabolics expert: **100 mg/week** therapeutic replacement;
**150–200** "sports TRT"; **300+** clearly enhanced; **500–600** an entry-level cycle; **1,000
minimum up to 4,000–5,000 mg/week** at pro level. The famous 600 mg studies are moderate-to-low by
competitive standards.

### The costs

From [steroids are awesome](steroids-awesome.md) and
[the decision to use steroids](decision-use-steroids.md):

- **Mortality.** A 2024 study of 1,000+ users over 11 years (average age 27) against 60,000 matched
  non-users: **2.8% vs 1.0%**. The authors called the excess death rate in this young population
  "striking."
- **Heart.** The heart is muscle and grows too — left ventricular thickening, impaired squeeze and
  relax, heart failure risk. Autopsies of 10 young bodybuilders (average age 33): **4 of 10** died of
  acute cardiac dysfunction, average heart weight **517 g** against ~300 g normal and a ~500 g
  exercise-adapted ceiling, past which added mass comes from fibre *number* — an indicator of
  cardiopathy. **HDL drops and LDL spikes within two months** of a cycle.
- **Brain.** MRI on 200+ weightlifters: natural lifters' brain age matched their real age, enhanced
  lifters showed a significantly larger **brain age gap**. Israetel adds that some types of
  intelligence degrade measurably, in current *and* former users.
- **Liver.** Only the oral **17-alpha-alkylated** compounds — adenomas from **6 months to 15 years**
  in, two documented progressions to carcinoma in bodybuilders aged 29 and 39. Injectables don't
  carry this.
- **One 2000 study: 37% gynecomastia** (irreversible without surgery), **43% acne**, **61% negative
  libido changes**. Testes shrink to about **half** size and shut down; more cycles and years means
  higher odds of permanent shutdown, lifelong medication and possible fertility loss.
- **Injury** — connective tissue can't keep pace when the bench climbs 50 lb in six weeks.
- **Women** get all of that plus deepening voice, clitoral enlargement, decreased breast size,
  altered menstruation and baldness — arguably more severe and permanent, on thinner data.

Two caveats the notes insist on. **The research probably under-represents the risk**, because it
isn't ethical to dose subjects at bodybuilding levels, so most evidence is case studies, surveys and
autopsies. And a dissenting review (Hoffman and Ratamess) argues cause-and-effect is not established
and that genetics, diet and other substance use may do much of the work — a conclusion Nippard found
out of sync with its own data, while accepting the takeaway that physician monitoring is critical.

**On "sport TRT" at 150–200 mg/week:** the term has been conflated with a baby cycle. Even when total
testosterone reads normal on paper, **it isn't what you could produce naturally** — it's perpetual
mini-enhancement. There is no safe version, only healthier ones: smallest effective dose, blood work
every few months, heart scans, constant blood pressure monitoring, proper cycling, a coach and a
doctor. Israetel's analogy: you can make a race car safer, but past 200 mph *safer* is the only word
available.

**Age.** Israetel's floor for even discussing supervised use is **25** — steroids can close growth
plates early (some men grow until 22) and brain development runs into the late 20s. Nippard's
version: **at least 10 years of hard, smart natural training first**, which lands most people in
their mid-20s anyway.

### FFMI and the natural limit

**FFMI = lean mass divided by height in metres squared** — the only defensible numeric tool for
judging natural status ([how to tell if someone is on steroids](tell-if-someone-steroids.md)).

- **Realistic natural ceiling: FFMI 22–24, maybe 25** with good genetics and long consistency.
- Kouri 1995, 74 natural and 83 enhanced lifters by hydrostatic weighing: **not one of the 74
  naturals exceeded FFMI 25**, and every lifter at 25+ was on steroids. But 23 lifters sat at 24–25
  and **9 were natural**, and one enhanced subject was smaller than 86% of the naturals.
- **25 is a strong trend, not a physical law.** Five pre-steroid-era Mr. America winners from 1943–49
  broke 25, and a 2017 study put the **average** D1/D2 offensive lineman at **FFMI 25.1**. Seventy-
  four subjects is a small sample; a larger natural cohort should show a tail past 25.
- **Around 29+ is not naturally achievable.** Liver King at 29. Ronnie Coleman at peak: **38.5**.
- At ~12% body fat, FFMI 24 is roughly **5'6" 165 lb, 5'9" 185 lb, 6'0" 200 lb, 6'3" 220 lb**.

**Natural rates of gain**, from the 2020 systematic review in *The Muscle Ladder*: **year 1 10–25 lb,
year 2 5–10 lb, year 3 2.5–7.5 lb** — **17.5–42.5 lb over three years**. Lifetime: **20–40 lb for
men, 12–24 lb for women**, most of it in the first five years, over **5–10 years** of consistent work
([how much muscle without steroids](much-muscle-build-without-steroids.md)).

**Dates matter for reference physiques.** Testosterone was isolated in 1935; the performance
application was realised at the **1952 Olympics**; Ziegler injected John Grimek in **1954**. American
physiques built before the 1950s are therefore safe natural reference points — Steve Reeves in 1947
at 6'1", 215 lb, 10–12% body fat.

And the honest limit: **you can never prove an individual is natural**, only whether a physique is
*possible* naturally. A single blood test can't rule out past use.

### What natural vs enhanced changes about training

The Meadows material is the most practically useful content here
([natural vs enhanced, ft. John Meadows](natural-vs-enhanced-becoming-best-bodybuilder.md)).

- **Naturals need less volume.** Someone on steroids, GH and insulin recovers roughly **ten times
  faster**. **This has corrupted bodybuilding methodology** — people on heavy doses grow on bad
  programs, conclude the programs are good, and prescribe them to everyone else.
- **Weak points are an execution problem first, a frequency problem second, and almost never a dose
  problem.** "Just up the dose" may grow the whole body but won't selectively fix lagging legs.
- **Recovery is finite.** Raise frequency on a weak body part and you cut volume on a strong one.
- **Only the last set of an exercise goes to true failure.** Earlier sets still count as volume.
- **Bar speed is the early overreaching warning.** More soreness is *not* more growth — Meadows says
  he was completely wrong about this when younger.
- On his own use: **find the lowest amount that still produces progress** — often 400 mg test plus
  maybe 200 mg of something else, far below what's now called blasting. People who "jam it in the
  off-season, grams and grams" tend to disappear within a couple of years. Blood work twice a year:
  **fasting insulin** (top of his list), **C-reactive protein** (~1 or below), **blood pressure**,
  **triglycerides and HDL**, and **hemoglobin, hematocrit and red cell count** — for which the fix is
  donating blood twice a year.
- He would **never in good conscience tell someone to use drugs.** He trained 8 years natural first;
  his last attempt to recover natural production was **2002**, it failed, and he's been on HRT since.

### Looking enhanced naturally

Strangers judging eight natural lifters flagged the same five features
([look enhanced without steroids](look-enhanced-without-steroids.md)): **side delts, biceps, upper
pecs, traps, leanness.**

**Leanness is the biggest lever fully in your control** — Nippard gets more steroid accusations at
155 lb and ~10% body fat than he did 30 lb heavier at ~20%. Side delts are the top training
priority: lateral raises **at least 2x/week, 3–5x/week if lagging**. Traps are a genuine statistical
PED signal because **upper traps have higher androgen receptor density** than most muscles; build
them with wider-grip shrugs "up and in", an overhead raise, and direct neck work. Incline pressing
for upper pecs. And much of the perceived gap is photography — switching from diffuse light to a
spotlight turned the same group from reading natural to reading enhanced **in one second**.

## Detraining and muscle memory

**The timeline** ([how fast you lose muscle](fast-lose-muscle-when-stop-working-out.md)):

- **Up to about 2–3 weeks off with normal daily activity: little to no measurable loss.** A study of
  20 subjects with a year-plus of training found **no significant drop at two weeks**. Three weeks is
  Nippard's cutoff, matching Fisher and Steele's review.
- **Two months off: roughly half the muscle you built** — but only half, and everyday activity alone
  preserves the rest. That figure comes from near-novices; the trained response is unclear.
- **Bed rest is the outlier** — significant loss in one week. The gap between bed rest and walking
  around is far larger than the gap between walking and training.
- **Minimal maintenance volume is astonishingly low.** Bickel 2011: cutting volume to **one-ninth**
  of previous levels maintained muscle mass for **32 weeks**. Caveat: it worked in the younger group
  only, so **people 60 and up may need more**.
- **Some short-term "loss" probably isn't muscle** — glycogen and water would explain the fast
  rebound and why you look flatter after a few days off ([training when sick](train-when-sick.md)).

**Diet protects you:** maintenance calories or a slight surplus, and **1.6–2.2 g/kg protein**,
erring high if you aren't training at all.

**Muscle memory: the phenomenon is solid, the mechanism is not**
([muscle memory](muscle-memory-and-detraining.md)). Two models. **Retained myonuclei** — muscle cells
are multinucleated, each nucleus services a fixed territory, growth requires added nuclei donated by
satellite cells, and the 2010 Oslo work found **myonuclei are protected from the apoptosis normally
seen in inactive tissue**, so on resuming you skip the donation step entirely. **That study was in
mice.** Against it, **epigenetics** — Seaborne 2018 (7 weeks on / 7 off / 7 on) found **DNA-level
changes stayed in place through the break**.

The newer note updates the picture: **Snijders 2019 found myonuclei returned to baseline after a
year of detraining in humans**, pushing researchers toward epigenetics as the main driver. Treat the
myonuclear model as the older view. Either way the persistence claim is bounded — **seven weeks**
confirmed, "possibly years" explicitly speculation.

Two consequences worth more than the mechanism. **A 24-week study found periodic training (6 weeks
on, 3 off, repeating) ended at essentially the same place as continuous training** — so a few weeks
off won't derail long-run progress, and if you're injured, rest and heal. And **be sceptical of any
program's results measured right after a layoff**; muscle memory flatters anything.

## Recovery modalities

### Cold — the one with a real cost

**Verdict: busted** ([cryotherapy and ice baths](cryotherapy-work.md)). Cold reliably reduces
**perceived** soreness — a 2015 meta-analysis of 36 articles found a significant DOMS reduction at
24 hours, persisting to 96. Three problems with calling that recovery: the same authors found **no
effect on objective markers** (lactate, creatine kinase, IL-6); **the research is unblindable**, so
placebo is live; and the comparator is usually doing nothing, while newer work finds **cold water
immersion is no better than a 10-minute low-intensity cycling bout**.

**The cost is the headline.** Roberts 2015: 21 trained men, lower body twice weekly for 12 weeks,
10 minutes of cold water immersion after every session versus 10 minutes of easy cycling. **The
cycling group gained more muscle and more strength on every measure tested.** The follow-up
attributed it to a dampened anabolic signal and reduced satellite cell activity.

If you're doing cold anyway, **cold water immersion beats whole-body cryotherapy** in every direct
comparison and costs less. Reasonable use case: when soreness relief now matters more than long-term
adaptation — in-season, back-to-back events. Unexamined variable the note flags: **timing** — cold
hours away from training may not carry the same cost. Related misconception worth keeping: **DOMS is
probably not caused by inflammation at all**, so NSAIDs and ice are being used for a job they were
never suited to.

### Foam rolling — harmless, unremarkable

**Not busted, not special** ([foam rolling](foam-rolling-work.md)). Several trials show reduced
soreness, including one using the more objective **pressure pain threshold** method — 20 minutes of
post-exercise rolling reduced soreness at every time point and improved 30 m sprint time. One
sprint-training study found nothing, plausibly because it measured soreness *after* a warm-up.

The real advantage over prolonged static stretching: **it improves range of motion without impairing
muscle performance**, making it a safe pre-lift mobility tool. But Vigotsky concluded it adds little
if you're already doing dynamic stretching, and the studies mostly compared rolling to *nothing*.
Practical: **no more than about 5 minutes**, targeted at tight areas, before training and on top of
a normal dynamic warm-up. Downside risk is essentially zero.

### Training when sick

Lyle McDonald's **neck check** ([should you train when sick](train-when-sick.md)):

1. **Contagious? Stay out of public gyms.** Full stop.
2. **Symptoms below the neck** (chest congestion, chest cough, wheezing) means **rest**.
3. **Above the neck** (runny nose, headache) is the grey area: **go to the gym, do 10–20 minutes of
   light-to-moderate cardio, then decide.** It filters laziness from genuine illness, and
   **moderate-intensity, moderate-volume cardio can itself improve immune function**, so the trip
   isn't wasted even if you go home.

The backstop: **up to three weeks off is very unlikely to affect long-term muscle mass.** This is an
expert heuristic, not a controlled trial, and the main failure mode is using illness as an excuse.

### On "dangerous" exercises

**There are no contraindicated exercises, only contraindicated people**
([fibre types Q&A](fast-vs-slow-twitch-fibers-dangerous-exercises.md)). The pain-science consensus is
that **instilling fear of an exercise is more likely to cause harm than the exercise itself** — the
nocebo effect. Two genuine caveats: unstable-surface "functional" work adds risk without benefit,
and **ego lifting is the actual danger**, attaching to any exercise. The one case where training
volume produced a genuine medical emergency — rhabdomyolysis — sits with the recovery material in
[programming and volume](SUMMARY-programming.md).

## Alcohol

Two notes, 2016 and 2018, reconcile cleanly into one dose-response picture
([2016](alcohol-influences-muscle.md), [2018](alcohol-impact-fat-loss-muscle-testosterone.md)).

**Alcohol does not break energy balance.** Fit the drinks inside a deficit and you won't gain fat to
any appreciable degree. Alcohol has **no storage form**, is handled like a poison and gets top
oxidation priority — so it isn't itself making you fat. The problem is that while it's in your
system **dietary fat oxidation is severely blunted**, leaving the *other* calories you eat, fat
especially, far more likely to be stored. **Hence the single best tactic: borrow the drink calories
from fat, not carbs**, and treat **a high-fat binge after drinking as the worst case**.

Three reasons it's still hostile to a deficit: **post-drinking binges** with lowered inhibitions;
**no compensation** — people don't eat less later to offset alcohol calories; and it's described in a
2010 review as the **least satiating energy source** there is.

**Testosterone: dose-dependent, and mostly a distraction.**

| Dose | Effect |
|---|---|
| **2–3 standard drinks** | **+17%** in men — transient, not meaningfully anabolic |
| **~120 g (about 8.5 beers)** | **−23%** in one 1990 paper |
| **Acute intoxication (ER patients)** | **−45%** in men; **about 5x higher** in women |

Other studies found no change (Koziris 2000) or a small increase (Vingren 2013). The 2016 note's
verdict — *mixed data, effect sizes too small to matter for physique outcomes* — and the 2018 note's
dose-response curve are the same conclusion at different resolutions. The mechanism is **inhibition
of testicular production specifically**, hence the sex-specificity: **if you don't have testicles,
alcohol's testosterone effect isn't your concern.**

**MPS is where the data is consistent, not mixed.** Heavy post-workout drinking of **8–9 drinks cut
MPS by 37%**, and **still 24%** with optimal protein alongside — **protein partially but not fully
rescues it.** That study used only men; a 2017 study in 10 men and 9 women found alcohol attenuated
**mTOR signalling in men only**. Most of the supporting base is rodent work, all pointing the same
way.

**The biggest real-world cost is probably the next day's training** — dehydration, hangover,
disrupted sleep. Both notes land there. **Doses around 1 g/kg bodyweight also impair recovery
markers** (raised cortisol, slowed reaction times), though a single *moderate* intoxication doesn't
appear to worsen training-induced muscle damage. Practically: count the calories because your
appetite won't, cut dietary fat that day, take protein with or after the alcohol post-workout, know
your personal limit, and **schedule a rest day after a night out** rather than forcing a bad session.

## Individual differences

### Genetics

**50–80% of the variability in muscle size between individuals is genetic**; height is about **80%
heritable**; the metabolic factors behind weight and fat gain about **40%**; **where you store fat,
22–61%** ([understanding genetics](understanding-genetics.md)). So **fat loss is slightly more within
your control** than muscle gain or height. Read heritability correctly: 80% heritable doesn't mean
80% of your height comes from genes — it means 80% of your *deviation from average* does.

The number that recurs across the whole library is **Hubal 2005**: 585 untrained people, the **same**
12-week arm program, MRI measurement, results from **−2% to +59%** change in bicep size. Most gained
about 20%; a few gained nothing; a few gained 40, 50, even 55%. That sample is 0.00007% of the world
population and it still contained outliers, which is why outliers in a population of 8 billion are
guaranteed rather than surprising ([FFMI note](tell-if-someone-steroids.md)). Nippard's analogy: the
range in muscle-growth potential is like the range in height, and nobody accuses an 8'3" man of
taking growth hormone.

**True non-responders probably don't exist, or are rare** — one fixed protocol only identifies who
responds to *that* protocol. Caveat on Hubal: calories and protein weren't controlled. Deeper
markers exist (high responders had roughly **twice the satellite cell count**; **miR-378** expression
fell only in low responders) but Nippard is explicit that turning them into training advice is well
ahead of the literature.

Bouchard overfed **12 twin pairs by 1,000 kcal/day for 100 days**: weight gain ranged **9.5 to
29 lb**, and the most metabolically favoured showed **no increase in visceral fat at all** while the
least favoured showed roughly **200%**. And there is **no optimal macronutrient split** — caloric
restriction is the shared mechanism of every successful diet.

### Sex differences in training

**Minor tweaks to the same program, not different philosophies**
([sex differences](sex-differences-in-training.md)).

- **Women likely reach 50–100% of male hypertrophic potential.** The literature genuinely conflicts —
  one 2001 study found no difference at six months, another found men gaining about twice as much,
  and Lyle McDonald suggests roughly half. That range reflects real disagreement, not hedging.
- **97% of the absolute strength difference is attributable to muscle mass alone.** This is the
  pattern throughout: **most apparent sex differences reduce to muscle mass and body fat, not sex.**
- **Women recover better and fatigue less** — better perfusion clearing metabolites, more type I
  fibre area, estrogen aiding repair — so they tolerate **more sets, more reps, more sessions per
  week, shorter rests**, and since volume tracks growth, arguably *should* do more.
- **Women are relatively better at higher reps, men at explosive work.** Bias tempo accordingly
  without going all-or-nothing.
- **The "bulky" fear is unfounded**; that physique realistically requires steroids or an extremely
  dedicated advanced program.

### Female physiology — the McDonald material

The deepest note in this group ([Lyle McDonald interview](interview-lyle-mcdonald-2.md)). Its
recurring instruction: **individual variability in women is far larger than in men on almost every
variable here**, so track your own pattern rather than trusting a generality.

- **Much of the supposed fat-loss gap is body size, not hormones.** Women average ~10% lighter, ~10%
  less lean mass, ~10% more fat, so lower RMR and fewer calories burned per session. Studies that
  **equalised the calorie burn (~600 kcal/day) found identical fat loss** — but the women had to
  exercise **20–35% longer** to get there. Hedge: that was in *overweight* women, where these
  adaptations largely don't fire.
- **Adaptation is faster.** Leptin can fall **up to 50% within a week** of dieting, before real fat
  is lost, and women's falls **up to about 20% more** than men's. Active thyroid can drop
  significantly **within five days** on aggressive deficits.
- **The cycle.** About 28 days. **Follicular** (weeks 1–2, estrogen dominant): estrogen aids muscle
  remodelling and blunts hunger for about three days before ovulation. **Luteal** (weeks 3–4):
  progesterone stimulates **thigh fat storage**, stimulates hunger and cravings, raises body
  temperature about a degree, and **blocks the androgen receptor**.
- **Never compare consecutive weeks — compare week 1 to week 1** across cycles, anchored on
  menstruation onset. **It can take a month to know whether a diet has actually stalled.**
- **Cycle-phase periodisation has some support.** One study clustered 14 workouts a month —
  10 follicular + 4 luteal, the reverse, or evenly spread — and the **follicular-focused group made
  better strength and muscle gains than both others**. Rests on "five or six studies"; effect size
  isn't settled.
- **PMS week makes a natural deload** (coordination collapses, ligament laxity and injury risk rise);
  early follicular is usually the strongest week.
- **Recovery is an intensity effect.** At **80% 1RM and above the sex difference disappears**; at
  **65%** a woman might do 30 reps to a man's 15. Same researcher: 20 sets of maximal singles showed
  **no** recovery difference; 10x10 at 70% showed women recovering **faster**.
- **Losing the menstrual cycle is a health event, not a cosmetic one.** Amenorrhoea costs bone
  density and **may be irreversible**. He is explicit this isn't hyperbole, and notes women who lose
  their cycle from psychological stress alone.
- **Refeeds and diet breaks more often, not fewer.** The brain takes about four days to register the
  leptin drop, so one day every 3rd–4th day, or two days every 5–7, beats dieting a fortnight then
  eating. **Diet break of two weeks at maintenance every 8–10 weeks**, more often as you lean out.
  His supporting case: military recruits at ~4% body fat with **testosterone at castrate levels**,
  where **one week at maintenance calories reversed many of the hormonal adaptations with the
  training load unchanged.**
- **Birth control largely erases the cyclic picture**, and he won't generalise across six application
  types and eight synthetic progestins.

The one place Nippard pushes back and wins a concession: **water retention alone doesn't explain
stalls** — women who lose 3–4 lb, stall, get cut from 1,500 to 1,300 kcal and stay stalled would
still be drifting down if it were only water. McDonald concedes; both mechanisms operate.

### Kids and teens

**Weight training does not stunt growth**
([does weight training stunt growth](weight-training-stunt-growth.md)). The myth traces to a **1964**
Japanese observation that child manual labourers were short — correlational, and confounded by
**undernourishment**, which matters far more for height than lifting. Premature growth-plate closure
from loading has only been demonstrated **where an actual bone fracture occurred**, and those come
mainly from contact sports.

The **2014 international consensus statement** (243 references, endorsed by the American Academy of
Pediatrics, the NSCA and others) says the fears are unsupported, and goes further: mechanical stress
on developing growth plates **may be beneficial for bone formation**, with high loads playing a
critical role in bone mass acquisition during and before puberty. Conditions, non-negotiable:
**proper technique instruction, sensible progression, adult supervision.**

### Do muscles make you more attractive?

A survey of about 50,000 of Nippard's audience plus the published literature
([do muscles make you more attractive](muscles-make-more-attractive.md)). All correlational,
self-reported, from a self-selected fitness audience.

- **Muscle and strength both track with more partners** and with a roughly **20% higher likelihood of
  being in a relationship**. Bench press was the strongest single correlate, but squat and deadlift
  showed the same trend — **it's being strong that counts, not the lift**. Women showed the same
  pattern.
- **Body fat had zero relationship to partner count** — a flat line from shredded to overweight. But
  the highest body fat groups held the most people rating romantic satisfaction at the worst level.
- **The mismatch.** Women overwhelmingly preferred **level 3 of 5** muscularity (**41%**); only
  **10%** chose level 5, and more preferred level 2 than level 5. Men mostly wanted level 4, with
  **33% wanting level 5** — so a third of men chase a physique a tenth of women rate as most
  attractive, in an audience already biased toward muscle.
- **The ceiling.** A meta-analysis of **170,000+ subjects** tested six masculine traits (height,
  voice pitch, facial masculinity, testosterone, 2D:4D, strength/muscularity). **Strength and
  muscularity won** — but **all six combined explained only about 5% of the variance** in mating
  outcomes.
- **Kindness and intelligence outrank physical attractiveness** for women of all orientations. In the
  jerk-versus-nice-guy study only **7% of women picked the jerk**, and making him more handsome still
  left him losing **85% of the time**. "Nice guys finish last" is about neediness, not niceness.

## What's genuinely weak here

- **The mechanosensor step is unresolved**, as are sarcoplasmic hypertrophy and myonuclear addition;
  muscle damage is poorly understood down to how you'd measure it.
- **Citrulline versus nitrate** is a live disagreement inside this library.
- **Vitamin D's muscle-gain evidence is weak** — the recommendation reasons from deficiency
  prevalence and trial-design limitations, not gains data. **Sleep supplements** have very low
  responder rates; amber blue-blocking glasses 30–60 min before bed are the cheaper first move.
- **Whether moving testosterone within the normal range does anything is unknown**, and the notes say
  so rather than guessing.
- **Steroid research systematically under-represents risk** — bodybuilding doses can't be ethically
  administered, so most evidence is case studies, surveys and autopsies, and causation is
  under-established even where the pattern is consistent.
- **The FFMI 25 ceiling rests on 74 natural subjects** and is breached by both pre-steroid-era and
  college-football data.
- **The muscle memory mechanism is contested** — myonuclear retention shown in mice and contradicted
  in humans; epigenetic persistence confirmed for seven weeks and speculated beyond.
- **The Roberts cold-water result is one study on 21 men, lower body only**, and cold-therapy
  soreness research is fundamentally unblindable. **Foam rolling has mostly been compared to
  nothing**, not to a competent warm-up.
- **Alcohol's MPS base is largely rodent work** with one good human study; the sex-difference finding
  came from 10 men and 9 women.
- **Female hypertrophic potential (50–100% of male) reflects real conflict in the literature**, and
  cycle-phase periodisation rests on a handful of studies.
- **The attractiveness data is a self-selected survey**, and steroid use at the top end is
  speculation, not measurement.
