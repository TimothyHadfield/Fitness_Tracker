# Back Squat: Full Technique Guide (Setup, Execution, Error Fixes)

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Get A Huge Squat With Perfect Technique (Fix Mistakes)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bEv6CCg2BC8)) · 2019-01-15 · 10 min
**Topic:** Technique

## Bottom line
- Squat as a heavy, low-to-moderate rep lift: at least ~70% 1RM, 5-10 reps for hypertrophy, 3-6 reps for strength. Above ~10 reps your cardiovascular system usually fatigues before your legs, degrading form, activation and strength gains.
- Weight stays centred over mid-foot the whole way; bar path is a straight vertical line. Do not cue "weight on the heels."
- Break at hips and knees together (high bar), push knees forward and slightly out to track over the toes, one-second descent, no pause in the hole, explode up.
- Depth: parallel is the minimum for hypertrophy/general strength; hip crease below knee for regulation depth. Most people can reach this with a high-bar position and an appropriate stance.
- Prefer honestly lightening the bar for a few months to fix form over losing months to an injury.

## Muscles worked [00:08]
The squat is simultaneous hip extension and knee extension.
- **Hip extension:** glutes primarily, with help from adductor magnus. Hamstrings can extend the hip but contribute little here — the knee is bent, so their length barely changes through the range of motion.
- **Knee extension:** quadriceps.
- **Isometric lumbar extension:** spinal erectors.
- **Abs are not highly active** despite the common belief. EMG shows low abdominal activity, which makes sense — the abs do spinal flexion and anti-extension, and neither happens in a squat.

Because it spans multiple joints and large muscle masses, the squat is highly conducive to progressive overload.

## Setup [02:11]
- Check plates are matched on both sides and the bar is centred in the rack.
- Set rack height so the bar clears the J-hooks on the walkout; roughly armpit height suits most bar positions.
- **Bar position:** high bar (on the traps) vs low bar (on the rear delts) is over-exaggerated in practice. Research suggests high bar is somewhat more quad dominant, low bar somewhat more hip dominant. Nippard finds high bar protects the lower back more and feels more comfortable for most people. Too high = it pushes your head forward; too low = it feels like it will slide down your back.
- **Grip:** hands as close to the shoulders as comfortable. A closer grip recruits the upper back better, giving the bar a more stable base. Hands only stabilise — keep the bar mashed between palms and upper back rather than actively gripping it as in a bench press.
- **Unrack:** both feet under the bar, push the hips forward to lift it out. Do not squat it up with the knees — that adds needless quad fatigue before the set and can contribute to knee pain.
- **Walkout:** three steps back — one small step with the non-dominant leg, one medium step with the other, one mini step to even the feet.
- **Stance:** at least wider than shoulder width, toes flared 15-30 degrees.

## Execution — brace, descend, lift [04:15]
**Brace.** Even pressure through heel and big toe, weight centred over the mid-foot. Avoid the "weight on your heels" cue — it throws balance off and makes the hips shoot up early. Head slightly extended, eyes gazing straight ahead. Elbows pointing slightly back, not directly under the bar (that leads to elbow pain). Take a large breath deep into the diaphragm and push the gut out against the belt to build a rigid column down the spine.

**Descend.** With high bar, break at hips and knees at the same time. Push the knees forward and slightly out so they track over the toes while sitting the hips straight down. Press through heel, big toe and pinky toe with even distribution. From the side, the bar travels straight down over the middle of the foot. Control the descent, actively driving the upper back into the bar and not letting the chest fall forward. Roughly a one-second negative — going much slower drains you too early.

**Lift.** At the bottom ("in the hole"), don't pause unless paused squats are the deliberate drill — use the stretch reflex and reverse immediately on hitting depth. Stay upright, but Nippard dislikes the "chest up" cue because it makes some lifters lean too far back; he prefers driving the back up into the bar with the head slightly up, which pulls the spine into a good lifting position. Explode as if jumping through the ceiling. Don't chase a squeeze or a burn — this lift is about moving heavy weight. He holds his breath to about two-thirds of the way up, then exhales, and re-braces at the top for the next rep.

## Common errors and fixes [07:18]
- **Knee valgus (knees caving in), on the way down or up.** Usually poor hip external rotation. Fix: adequate toe flare; actively drive the knees out toward (not far beyond) the toes; a mini band or hip circle around the knees to cue the pattern and strengthen the hip abductors; a heeled squat shoe can help, since foot pronation / losing the arch is a known contributor to valgus.
- **Too much weight plus not enough depth** — these usually arrive together. The squat starts with the eccentric, so it's easy to cheat by overloading and only going halfway down. The deadlift is harder to cheat because an unliftable bar simply stays on the floor.
- **Butt wink / lower back rounding.** If rounding is excessive, cut depth slightly or adjust stance width (wider or narrower). Some butt wink is basically inevitable for many lifters and is not a big concern if mild and painless. Foam rolling and dynamic stretching beforehand acutely increase joint range of motion and may reduce it.
- He also notes he has a separate myth-bust video on why letting the knees travel past the toes is fine, and why the "keep knees behind the toes" cue can be harmful to the lower back.

## Practical application
Set the bar at armpit height, high-bar, hands close, hips-forward unrack, three-step walkout, feet slightly wider than shoulders with 15-30 degrees of flare. Brace hard against the belt, break hips and knees together, one second down over mid-foot, hit at least parallel, and reverse instantly and explosively. Load 70%+ of 1RM in the 3-6 range for strength or 5-10 for size. If technique is poor, deload for a few months and rebuild.

## Substitutes if you cannot squat
Injury is really the only reason not to squat. In order of preference: machine or hack squat (or Smith machine squat if more comfortable), then lunges (great, but without the same overload potential), and as a last resort leg press plus a lower-back extension, which together capture a decent share of what the squat trains.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Rep-range guidance has exceptions; the "over 10 reps" limitation is a general rule, not absolute.
- The high-bar/low-bar difference is real but small, and the quad/hip split is a tendency rather than a hard division; comfort and lower-back tolerance should drive the choice.
- Achievable depth depends on individual skeleton and mobility, so stance width has to be individualised.
- Mild butt wink is left explicitly unresolved — treat it as a concern only when severe or symptomatic.
