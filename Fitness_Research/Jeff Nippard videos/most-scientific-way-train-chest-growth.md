# Chest Training for Growth: Fibre Direction, Bench Press and Incline Angle

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "The Most Scientific Way to Train CHEST for Growth (9 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HtV1FIPyTEw)) · 2016-12-28 · 12 min
**Topic:** Exercise Selection

## Bottom line
- The flat bench press is well supported by the literature as a mass builder for the pecs — pec size correlates tightly with bench 1RM, and a program with bench as the *only* exercise still grew the pecs.
- Add **incline pressing at roughly 45°**, the angle that produced the highest EMG activity in the cited 2010 paper, to hit the upper (clavicular) head.
- For flies, use an **internally rotated grip** (thumbs toward each other, palms down) — the Bayesian cable fly — because internal rotation puts the pec at a better length to contract forcefully.
- Load heavy early in the session for mechanical tension; save metabolic-stress finishers (long-duration stretch holds) for the end.
- **Train chest twice per week** — the best available meta-analysis supports 2× over 1×; whether 3+ is better is still open.

## Anatomy and why it dictates training [01:30]
Two heads: an **upper/clavicular head** attaching along the clavicle, and a **lower/sternal head** attaching along the sternum. Fibre direction is the key:
- Upper fibres run **downward (descending)**
- Mid fibres run **straight across (transverse)**
- Lower fibres run **upward**

When the sternal head contracts it pulls origin and insertion together, bringing the upper arm across the body — **transverse shoulder adduction** (transverse = horizontal plane; adduction = toward the midline). Because the upper fibres run descendingly, they also assist **shoulder flexion** — arms straight up, as in a front raise. That's the whole basis of incline work.

## The flat bench press [02:31]
Bodybuilders often criticise the flat bench as a bodybuilding movement, but the literature largely supports it:
- A 2014 study (name garbled — sounds like "Akagi et al", *uncertain*) concluded **pec size was tightly correlated with 1RM bench press strength**. People with a big bench have big pecs.
- Many EMG studies show it activates the pecs well.
- A study (name garbled — *uncertain*) showed a program containing **bench press as the only exercise** was very effective at growing the pecs.
- A **powerlifting arch** positions the upper back in a sort of decline posture, which is likely a good way to get very high overall pec activation, especially at relatively high intensity.

## Incline pressing for the upper chest [04:35]
A well-developed upper chest creates the "pec shelf" look. The incline bench closes the shoulder adduction angle and creates a larger **shoulder flexion moment** than the flat bench — which is exactly what the descending upper fibres do, hence more upper pec work.

**Angle:** experiment, but start at **roughly 45°**, the angle that elicited the highest EMG activity in Trebs et al.'s 2010 paper (*name uncertain from captions*). A **close grip** is mentioned as another way to bias the upper chest.

## Flies and the length-tension relationship [05:06]
Every muscle has a resting length at which it contracts most forcefully — the **length-tension relationship**. Stretch it too far past resting length and it loses force; shorten it too far and it also weakens.

**External rotation lengthens the pec** slightly, putting it in a less optimal position to contract. **Internal rotation** puts it in a stronger position. Hence the **Bayesian cable fly**: thumbs facing one another, palms facing down. The upright bench support in the seated version also restricts cheating and allows scapular retraction, which is a safer shoulder position.

## Mechanisms and session structure [05:37]
Brad Schoenfeld's landmark 2010 review outlined three mechanisms of hypertrophy: **mechanical tension, muscle damage, and metabolic stress**.

- **Tension is likely the most important**, so it gets attention early in the workout via a heavy, overloading stimulus that progresses week to week or month to month.
- **Metabolic stress** can be added by lengthening rep duration in the **stretch position**, which restricts venous return from the muscle back to the heart. His example: **flat dumbbell isometric holds at the end of the session**. This allows metabolite buildup that can signal for hypertrophy, and it offers a novel progression route — adding time under tension at a fixed load.
- Because long sets generate significant fatigue and impede subsequent exercises, save these techniques for the **end of a session as a finisher**.

## Frequency and volume [06:39]
The most recent and comprehensive meta-analysis on frequency concluded **2× per week beats 1× per week** for maximising growth. Whether 3 or more is better is still very much up for debate, and the answer for the pecs comes down to how much weekly volume you need to fit in. Nippard's position: **for 99% of people, twice per week with sufficient weekly volume and training intensity is enough to optimise growth.** No specific weekly set numbers are given in this video.

## Practical application
- Open the chest session with heavy flat bench press, powerlifting arch, progressing load over weeks and months.
- Add incline pressing at ~45° for the clavicular head; a close grip is another upper-chest bias.
- Do flies with an internally rotated grip (Bayesian cable fly) rather than externally rotated.
- Finish with a metabolic-stress technique — e.g. flat dumbbell isometric holds in the stretched position — progressing by adding time under tension at fixed load.
- Chest twice per week.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Several researcher names were badly garbled by the auto-captions (the 2014 correlation study, the bench-only training study, and the 2010 incline-angle study) and are flagged as uncertain above.
- The pec size / bench 1RM finding is a **correlation**, not evidence that benching caused the size.
- Optimal incline angle is presented as individual — 45° is a starting point, not a settled figure.
- Whether 3+ sessions per week beats 2 is explicitly stated as unresolved.
- Weekly set volume is not prescribed here; it is deferred to his program.
