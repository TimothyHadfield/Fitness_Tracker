# 100 Days of Low-Volume Training: What Halving Your Sets Actually Does

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "I Cut The Number Of Sets I Do In Half" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DzjWEn2BS_k)) · 2025-10-13 · 20 min
**Topic:** Volume & Intensity

## Bottom line
- He ran ~6.5 sets per muscle per week for 100 days while cutting, with 1–2 sets per exercise taken to failure, and lost 5.5 lb fat against only 1.8 lb lean mass — gaining strength throughout.
- On a cut, go low volume and high intensity. Recovery capacity is reduced in a deficit, and low volumes maintain muscle fine as long as every set is hard.
- On a bulk, keep volume low for most muscles and blast ONE muscle with 10–30 sets per week, rotating which muscle every few months.
- When volume is low, take nearly every set to failure — a Sept 2025 study found the failure group outgrew the 2-RIR group on a one-set-per-exercise program.
- Volume research is real but has practical limits: studies blast one or two muscles, run at maintenance calories, and last only 6–12 weeks.

## What he actually did
Split: Upper / Lower / Rest / Upper / Lower / Arms & Delts / Rest, repeated for 14 weeks. One to two sets per exercise, almost every set to failure.

Weekly volume by muscle: shoulders 10, back 9, glutes 9, quads 8, most muscles 6, some 4 — averaging about 6.5 sets per muscle per week. Chest, for example: 2 sets incline press + 2 sets pec deck on Monday, 2 sets machine chest press on Thursday = 6 sets/week.

A sample leg day: 2 sets squats, 2 sets leg curls, 1 set lunges, 2 sets leg extensions, 1 set abduction, 2 sets calves — about 45–60 minutes.

For context, the standard science-based guideline is 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week, not counting warmups.

## The volume literature
[02:50] **Krieger 2010** — meta-analysis of 8 studies comparing 1 set vs 2–3 sets per exercise. 2–3 sets was about 40% more effective than 1 set.

**Krieger and colleagues, 2016** — meta-analysis switching the unit to sets per muscle per week; found a clear dose-response, more volume meant more gains.

**Pelland and colleagues (2024)** — the largest volume meta-analysis to date, 35 studies. Clear dose-response holding from 4 sets per muscle per week all the way up to 43 sets per week.

**Nippard's four practical criticisms of that body of work:**
1. These studies loaded high volume onto one or two muscles (usually biceps/triceps or quads) while everything else was trained normally or not at all. High volume for the *whole body* is a different proposition, and the authors themselves flag likely recovery problems.
2. Almost all were run at roughly maintenance calories. Nobody knows if the findings hold in a deficit.
3. They are short — typically 6–12 weeks. Whether higher volume keeps pulling ahead over a year or two, or whether low volume catches up as you approach your natural limit, is unanswered.
4. Returns diminish. More volume does not give proportionally more growth.

He explicitly declines to use the "it's just edema, not real muscle" criticism — he thinks the effect is too large across too many studies, and the Pelland authors address it in the paper.

## Evidence for low volume
**Bickel and colleagues** — 70 subjects trained legs 3x/week for 16 weeks: 3 sets squats + 3 sets leg press + 3 sets leg extension each session, 27 sets/week for quads. Half then dropped to **one set of each exercise once per week — 3 sets total per week**. They maintained 100% of size and strength for 16 weeks, then another 16 weeks: 32 weeks (224 days) of maintenance on 3 sets a week. Some subjects kept gaining.

**Herman and colleagues, September 2025** — 42 resistance-trained subjects on two full-body workouts per week, one set per exercise, ~30 minutes per session. Split into a failure group and a 2-RIR group. After 8 weeks both made significant gains, but **the failure group grew better overall**. Two takeaways: experienced lifters can gain on low volume, and on low volume you should train to failure.

**Robinson and colleagues, 2023 meta-analysis** — the closer you train to failure, the more muscle you gain. Nippard's conclusion: if any variable deserves the crown, it's training hard, not volume.

The logic that ties it together: with only a small number of sets you can afford to push nearly every one to failure. On a high-volume plan the accumulated fatigue and injury risk would outweigh the marginal benefit.

## Results after 100 days
- DEXA day 1 to day 100: **−5.5 lb fat mass, −1.8 lb lean mass** (lean mass includes water lost during the cut).
- DEXA day 30 to day 100 (70 days): **+0.5 lb lean mass, −2.3 lb fat mass** — the lean gain may be within measurement error but was encouraging.
- Strength: matched his Smith machine bench press max and added 15 lb to his leg extension max under a strict fasted testing protocol, despite being 7 lb lighter and very advanced.
- Hit new PRs during the lowest-calorie final week: 5 reps → 6 reps → 7 reps with five plates on the pendulum squat across three weeks, plus a 4-rep PR increase on the machine chest press.
- By week 3 he looked harder and fuller with more vascularity, mostly attributable to getting leaner.

## The focus effect
He reports that on high-volume workouts his attention taper mid-session — more phone time, longer rest, socialising. Knowing you only have one or two sets to make it count forces intensity on every single set. He describes not missing intensity on a single set across the whole 100 days, and going from dreading workouts to looking forward to them.

## Practical application
- **Cutting:** run low volume (roughly 4–10 sets per muscle per week), 1–2 sets per exercise, nearly all to failure. High volume still works but is not ideal when recovery is compromised.
- **Bulking:** keep most muscles at low volume, pick one muscle and give it 10–30 sets per week, rotate every few months. He plans to do this himself on his next lean bulk.
- **Maintaining:** the Bickel data means you can hold gains on remarkably little — as few as 3 hard sets a week for a muscle group — during busy periods.
- Track lifts and strength; maintained or rising strength while losing weight is the best practical proxy for retained muscle.

## Caveats & unknowns
- This is an n=1 experiment on an advanced lifter in a deficit; he says explicitly not to base your training on one person's experience.
- The volume meta-analyses genuinely do show more volume equals more growth on average — he is not claiming the research is wrong, only that its context (single muscles, maintenance calories, short duration) limits the application.
- Whether low volume matches high volume over a year or more is still unknown.
- He publicly reversed his previous position that training volume should not change when you start cutting.
