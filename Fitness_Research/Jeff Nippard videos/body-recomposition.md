# Body Recomposition: Building Muscle and Losing Fat at the Same Time

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "How To Build Muscle And Lose Fat At The Same Time: Step By Step Explained (Body Recomposition)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M4K0s792wAU)) · 2019-11-02 · 10 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Recomposition is possible for **nearly everyone**, not a rare edge case. Fat tissue and muscle tissue are separate systems; you do not need a surplus to build muscle.
- **Training is the driving force**, not diet. No amount of protein builds muscle without a progressive training stimulus.
- Pick a **primary goal** based on current body fat, then set calories around maintenance: **+5 to 25%** if the priority is muscle, **-10 to 20%** if the priority is fat loss.
- Protein on a sliding scale: **1.2 g/lb of lean body mass if you carry more fat, up to 1.6 g/lb LBM if you are lean.**
- **Sleep is the most underrated variable.** One hour less per night, five nights a week, flipped weight loss from 83% fat to 85% lean mass.

## Why recomp works at all [00:00]

The "you must bulk then cut" doctrine comes from old bodybuilding lore about needing a massive surplus. A **2013 study from Garthe and colleagues** overfed subjects by **600 extra calories per day** and found they did **not** gain significantly more muscle but gained **over three times the fat mass** — nearly all the extra bulk arrived as fat.

Jeff is not anti-bulking (he ran a big bulk himself). If you want maximum muscle and strength as fast as possible, a larger surplus is the way — but the faster gain comes bundled with more fat, and many people do not want that trade. It is not an inevitability, because fat and muscle are separate systems.

## Who can recomp [01:33]

1. **New lifters** — the most primed for growth you will ever be; calories can be pulled from stored fat to fuel muscle building on basic progressive training and a reasonable diet.
2. **Overweight individuals** — huge energy reserves in body fat mean you can be in a deficit and still have plenty of fuel for building.
3. **Detrained individuals** — people who were previously jacked and stopped for injury or life; the **muscle memory effect** makes rapid rebuilding, and therefore recomp, common.
4. **Anabolic steroid users** — rapid muscle building lets the body tap fat stores.
5. **The sub-optimised trainee** — Jeff's added fifth category: anyone who counts as intermediate or advanced by training age but has not truly been putting in the effort in training or nutrition. You can be years into the gym and still functionally a newbie if you have not trained hard or smart.

Walk into any commercial gym and most people fall into one of these.

**Chris Barakat's lab observations:** he sees recomposition every single semester, in every training study they run, including in well-trained subjects — once details like nutrient timing and macronutrient optimisation get real attention. One subject, a basketball player who could squat 1.75x bodyweight, **gained 20 lb of lean mass in 10 weeks while losing about a pound of fat.**

The only group for whom recomp is impractical: **truly advanced trainees with already-optimised training and nutrition, near their genetic ceiling** — a tiny sliver of the population.

## The five steps [04:06]

**Step 1 — Recognise training as the engine.** You could eat a perfect diet and 9,000 g of protein a day and still build little muscle without progressive stimulus. The car analogy: training is the engine, nutrition is the fuel, sleep and stress management are the oil changes and tyre rotations. Better fuel improves performance, but without an engine the car does not move. Focus on **progressive overload, effort, and proper technique**, then fit volume to your advancement level — **10 to 20 sets per body part per week** is a good ballpark for most people and most muscles.

**Step 2 — Decide a primary goal.** You want both, but one must lead.
- Currently leaner — **8-12% body fat (male) or 18-22% (female)** — lead with **building muscle**.
- Not so lean — **15-20%+ (male) or 25-30%+ (female)** — lead with **losing fat**.

**Step 3 — Set calories around maintenance.**
- Muscle-first: **slight surplus, +5 to 25% over maintenance.** The leaner, less advanced and more genetically gifted you are, the bigger a surplus you can get away with.
- Fat-loss-first: **slight deficit, -10 to 20% off maintenance.** The more fat you have to lose, the bigger the deficit.

**Step 4 — Set macros, protein first.** Protein is the most important macro for recomp. Sliding scale based on **lean body mass**: the leaner you are, the closer to **1.6 g per lb of LBM**; the more body fat, the closer to **1.2 g per lb of LBM**. Jeff's worked example: 165 lb at ~10% body fat, so quite lean, picks **1.5** → about **222.8 g/day**, rounded to **225 g**. He notes this is higher than strictly required to build muscle, but higher intake is both safe and advisable for recomp. Then set **fat at a minimum of 20% of calories** and fill the remainder with **carbs**.

**Step 5 — Optimise the details.** Beginners can recomp on steps 1-4 alone. The more advanced you get, the harder recomp becomes and the more the details matter.

## Sleep [07:39]

The single most underrated factor for transforming a physique. **Wang and colleagues, 2018:** 36 subjects in a caloric deficit split into two groups; one slept **one hour less on five nights per week** (allowed an extra hour on weekends to catch up). After **8 weeks**, total weight loss was the same in both groups — but the **well-slept group lost 83% of their weight as fat**, while the **sleep-restricted group lost 85% of their weight as lean mass.**

Jeff's caveat: these subjects were not weight training, so lifting would likely have flattened the difference somewhat — but not nearly enough to erase an effect that large.

## Supplements [08:40]

There is no supplement that causes recomposition. A few help.

**Tier one:**
- **Protein powder** — makes hitting the daily total easier, usable at any time of day. Especially important for vegan lifters; he recommends "vegan whey," a **rice and pea blend** offering a full amino acid spectrum and high leucine. A **casein source before bed** may have merit.
- **Creatine — 3 to 5 g of monohydrate per day.** By far the most studied supplement on the market, cheap, minimal side effect risk. An obvious choice.
- **Caffeine** — increases strength, prolongs time to fatigue, increases acute fat oxidation. Unlike creatine it is subject to tolerance, so reserve it for your heaviest or most demanding sessions, or cycle off periodically to resensitise.

Peri-workout nutrition (nutrient timing around the session) is another optimisation lever, though he points elsewhere for the detail.

## Practical application
1. Get training right first: progressive overload, real effort, decent technique, 10-20 sets per muscle per week.
2. Judge your body fat and pick the lead goal — muscle if lean, fat loss if not.
3. Find maintenance calories, then add 5-25% or subtract 10-20%.
4. Protein at 1.2-1.6 g per lb of lean body mass depending on leanness; fat at ≥20% of calories; carbs fill the rest.
5. Protect sleep — it decides whether the weight you lose is fat or muscle.
6. Creatine 3-5 g daily, protein powder as needed, caffeine strategically.

## Caveats & unknowns
- Recomp gets progressively harder as you advance; near-genetic-ceiling lifters with optimised training and nutrition are the exception where it is not practical.
- Fastest possible muscle and strength gain still favours a larger surplus — recomp trades speed for leanness.
- The sleep study's subjects were not resistance training, so the fat-vs-lean split would likely be less extreme in lifters.
- The lean-body-mass protein model requires knowing your body fat percentage, which is itself an estimate.
