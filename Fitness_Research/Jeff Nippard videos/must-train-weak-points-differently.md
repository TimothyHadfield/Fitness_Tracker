# Four Strategies For Bringing Up A Lagging Body Part

**Source:** Jeff Nippard, "Why You Must Train Weak Points Differently (Men and Women)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N0KctY21JOw)) · 2019-05-07 · 17 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Weak points get **20–25 total weekly sets** (compounds included), spread across **3–6 sessions per week**.
- Raise frequency rather than piling sets into one session — extra sets in a single workout stop paying off past a point.
- Small muscles tolerate up to 5–6 sessions per week; large muscles cap at 3–4.
- Never pre-exhaust a weak point before a compound. Light *pre-activation* is fine and useful; fatiguing it is counterproductive.
- Place weak-point work early in the session, use isolation exercises to add frequency, and reserve intensity techniques for the end.

## 1. Increase training frequency
Higher frequency lets you accumulate more weekly volume without "wasted sets" — beyond a certain per-session volume, additional sets in the same workout add nothing. Twenty sets for glutes on one day makes far less sense than splitting the same 20 across two to four workouts. The point of training is to elevate muscle protein synthesis and amino acid sensitivity, and hammering a muscle harder in one sitting doesn't push those further once you're past the useful threshold.

There is a ceiling in the other direction too: too much frequency for one body part interferes with recovery elsewhere and builds systemic fatigue.

**General rule — the smaller the muscle, the more frequency you can get away with:**
- Small muscles (side delts, biceps, triceps, abs, calves): **up to 5–6× per week**, provided you aren't obliterating them each session. They recover fast.
- Large muscles (quads, glutes, chest, lats, traps): **cap at 3–4 sessions per week.** More muscle mass and often multiple joints means a larger recovery burden. You *can* go higher, but with diminishing returns plus more injury and overtraining risk.

**Volume target for a weak point: 20–25 total weekly sets including compound work, spread over 3–6 sessions** depending on the body part and how advanced you are. Some people can tolerate more.

**Exercise selection when raising frequency.** Using shoulders as the example: do a heavy compound like an overhead press or dumbbell press **no more than 2–3× per week**, then fill the remaining frequency with isolation work such as lateral raises. Compounds by definition hit multiple muscles, which makes them less useful for prioritising one, and they carry a bigger recovery cost. Keep them in, but be careful with how often.

## 2. Exercise order
Training a body part early in a session means you're stronger for it than if it's last — that much research and experience agree on. But putting the weak point *first* isn't automatically right: if it's a small muscle, fatiguing it early can wreck the rest of the workout. Lagging biceps trained first will be fresher, but then they're pre-fatigued for all your back work, capping the loads you can use there.

**Pre-exhaustion is a bro-science error.** Research on pre-exhaustion shows that fatiguing a target muscle before a compound makes it *less* active in that compound, not more — burning out the quads with leg extensions before leg press just causes other muscles to take over. Counterproductive.

**Pre-activation is different and does work.** You're not fatiguing the muscle, just warming it up and establishing a mind-muscle connection. A few banded lateral walks or light glute bridges before squats will get the glutes more involved. High-rep kickbacks to burnout before squats will do the opposite — the quads and hamstrings take over.

**Jeff's rule:**
- Prioritise the compound exercise first in the session.
- Optionally precede it with a few *light* pre-activation isolation sets for the weak point.
- Place the exercises that target the weak point earlier overall — e.g. if back is the weak point, do pulling before pushing on upper days.
- Use isolation work at the end for extra emphasis, and add more isolation volume on other days per strategy 1.

## 3. Mind-muscle connection
On isolation work aimed at the weak point, deliberately focus on feeling the muscle squeeze and stretch through each rep. Practical tricks: perform the set with your eyes closed to focus internally, or verbally mark each rep — "squeeze" on the concentric, "stretch" on the eccentric, out loud or in your head. Anything that forces intentional focus on the target muscle increases its activation.

## 4. Specialised intensity techniques
Drop sets, static holds, accentuated eccentrics and similar all have a place. The science-based objection is that they generate fatigue that can reduce overall volume — Jeff's counter is that as long as they're applied **to isolation exercises, sparingly, and at or near the end of a session**, that isn't much of a concern. If a body part has stalled on conventional training, these are a reasonable escalation to ensure the muscle is maximally stimulated.

The failure mode is gym bros supersetting and drop-setting everything, which does compromise your ability to train the muscle at higher frequency — and frequency matters more. But skipping these methods entirely leaves gains on the table.

Example shown: partner-applied manual resistance on the eccentric only during dumbbell curls, performed at the end of an upper-body session — a much harder stimulus than three sets of 15 standard reps done on autopilot.

## Practical application
For any lagging muscle:
1. Set weekly volume at 20–25 sets, compounds counted.
2. Split it over 3–6 sessions — toward 5–6 for small muscles, 3–4 for large ones.
3. Keep heavy compounds for that area to 2–3× per week; fill the rest with isolation work.
4. Optional light pre-activation before the main compound. Never pre-exhaust.
5. Order the session so weak-point work happens early.
6. Deliberate mind-muscle focus on all isolation sets.
7. Add drop sets / eccentrics sparingly at session end when progress stalls.
8. Be patient — weak points take time, and consistency plus progression is what actually moves them.

## Caveats & unknowns
- The volume and frequency numbers are Jeff's practical rules of thumb, not direct study outputs; individual tolerance varies with advancement.
- No specific studies are named in this segment — the pre-exhaustion and frequency claims are described generically.
- Intensity techniques remain contested within the evidence-based community precisely because of the fatigue-vs-volume tradeoff.
