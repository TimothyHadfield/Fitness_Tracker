# What to believe — the library reconciled

Five sources, roughly 10,000 papers, and they do not always agree. This file is the arbiter.

It is **not** another summary. Each source folder already has one, and each of those is written
from inside that source's point of view. This file does the thing none of them can: where two
sources disagree, it says which is right, why they differ, and how confident that verdict deserves
to be. Where the honest answer is that nobody knows, it says that too, and says what would settle
it.

## How to use this

Read the section covering your question. Each one states the disagreement, gives a verdict, and
attaches a confidence level. Then follow the links into the individual notes, which carry the
sample sizes, durations and effect sizes that the verdicts rest on.

Three things worth knowing before you start.

**Disagreement between these sources is usually not about evidence.** Far more often it is two
people answering slightly different questions — per-session against per-week volume, rep-equated
against set-equated effort — and then being read as if they had contradicted each other. Several
of the library's apparent fights dissolve on inspection. Those are marked as framing disputes
rather than adjudicated, because pretending to settle them would be false precision.

**Where there is a real disagreement, evidence vintage usually explains it.** The literature moved
between 2019 and 2025 on volume, on lengthened partials, and on rep-range floors. An older source
is not wrong for having been current. But a reader acting today needs the current answer, and
that is what the verdicts give.

**The library cites three retracted papers, and two of them were load-bearing.** See
[RETRACTION-AUDIT.md](RETRACTION-AUDIT.md). Where a verdict below turns on that, it says so.

## How to read this literature

Four sources, four characteristic ways of being wrong. Knowing a source's failure mode is worth
more than knowing any claim it makes: failure modes are stable, claims are not.

### Each source is wrong in its own particular way

**The ISSN's failure is the gap between its body text and its numbered list.** Across all 27
documents the numbered position is repeatedly firmer than the review above it. The
[2023 energy drinks stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/energy-drinks-2023.md) asserts improved
repeat-sprint performance while its own sections call that literature "largely null" and cite a
paper titled *"…null or negative effect on sprint performance."* The
[omega-3 stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/omega-3.md) does it four separate times, and
[meal frequency](ISSN%20position%20stands/meal-frequency.md) recommends for athletes it
concedes have never been studied. The rule is mechanical: **when the body says equivocal and the
position says improves, trust the body.**

Its second problem is quieter, because it is not a declarable conflict — self-citation. The
[EAA stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/essential-amino-acids.md) is substantively a review of
its own authors' laboratory output, and the one study matching free amino acids to a protein's
exact composition — which the intact protein won — is absent from its 127 references. The
[probiotics](ISSN%20position%20stands/probiotics.md) recovery recommendation rests on two
studies by the stand's own lead author, n=15 and n=29, never replicated. Check whether a
document's authors wrote the studies it stands on.

**House of Hypertrophy's failure is mechanism doing work that outcome data should do** — usually
flagged by him, which is why it is diagnosable. His stretch-mediated chest section reaches a
hypertrophy conclusion from a *strength* study with no growth measurement; the
case for isolation work in
[are isolation exercises useless](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/isolation-exercises-useless-muscle-hypertrophy-19-studies.md)
runs almost entirely on joint mechanics with one small trial testing it directly; and his "isolate
a muscle to raise its compound involvement" principle has no growth data at all.

The second problem is structural: **twenty-six videos have no retrievable reference list**, and
they are disproportionately his longest and most confident. The
[87-study mechanisms video](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/most-important-muscle-building-stimulus-87-studies.md)
is the channel's intellectual centre and cites on screen only, so all eighteen studies behind its
mechanical-tension case are unverifiable; the biceps, triceps and lats guides are the same. Those
claims may be sound; they are not checkable.

**Nippard's failure is the audit trail rather than the reasoning.** Three videos carry the wrong
reference list entirely — sexual-health papers on
[training when sick](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/train-when-sick.md), creatine papers on
[muscle imbalances](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/fix-muscle-imbalances-asymmetries-4-tips.md); a
Farina 2010 citation in
[the cable kickback note](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/build-bigger-glutes-perfect-training-technique.md)
is about decoding neural drive and does not support the claim beside it; one study appears with
three different effect sizes across three notes. His older exercise content leans on EMG, which he
has since disowned as a basis for ranking
([the EMG note](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/our-best-work-yet.md)), and in
[Science-Based Lifting Is Over](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/lifting-over.md) he says *no study has
ever directly measured back muscle hypertrophy* — his back rankings were indirect evidence plus
personal experience. He retracts in public, which is the mitigation.

**Menno's failure is contrarianism as a stance.** He is the sharpest methodologist here and the
weakest citer, and the two combine badly: an absolute claim with no source behind it. "Virtually
every instance full-body wins" is contradicted by a study in his own reference list; the 70% of
1RM connective-tissue threshold his whole high-rep injury argument rests on is uncited. The
[electrolyte video](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/i-read-every-electrolyte-study-industry-lying.md)
opens with "the industry is lying" on an observational figure, then concedes the ceiling it is
measured against is probably too low; the
[warm-up video](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/warming-bs-like-cooling-down.md) claims "zero hard
data" for injury prevention, then prescribes a warm-up set regardless.

### The retraction problem

The Barbalho volume papers — twin trials reporting that 5 weekly sets beat 10, 15 and 20 — were
**retracted for implausible data**, and they were load-bearing: one of the three legs of the
volume dispute this library records.
[House of Hypertrophy](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md)
reports it and removes the papers from an argument they would have supported; Menno names them
too. The Nippard material predates the retraction and cites the result straight; a correction is
now applied inline to
[his programming summary](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/SUMMARY-programming.md).

Generalise it. **The older a source, the more likely it rests on work since withdrawn** — a video
is frozen at its upload date while the literature is not. So before acting on a striking,
oft-repeated single result, check the journal page or Retraction Watch; and prefer claims carried
by several independent groups, since a retraction then takes one leg off an argument rather than
all of it. And treat a source's silence as uninformative: Nippard did not know.

### The measurement problems all five share

Most disagreements in this library dissolve into one of the following.

**Proxies standing in for growth.** EMG is the worst offender: it measures excitation rather than
activation, reads higher under heavier loads that grow no more muscle, is blind to passive
tension, and is unreliable even at its own job — constant stimulation at three joint angles gives
three amplitudes
([the EMG teardown](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/ryan-humiston-has-made-mistake-emg.md)).
Acute muscle protein synthesis is barely better: measured over six hours it did not correlate with
hypertrophy across a 12-week programme. Whole tier lists rest on those two measures.

**Untrained subjects, generalised.** Most of the striking results ran on beginners — 34 untrained
subjects for
[squats versus hip thrusts](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/squats-vs-hip-thrusts-glute-growth.md),
27 untrained men for the mind-muscle result, 15 for a widely-cited volume null. Beginners grow on
almost anything, which compresses differences between conditions.

**Incomplete programs, generalised.** Much of the volume literature trained one or two muscle
groups. That is not a program, and its recovery demands are not a program's. The 20 g per-meal
protein ceiling likewise came from trials training one muscle with one or two exercises.

**Short duration.** Nippard declined to raise his volume recommendation on a 35-study
meta-analysis finding gains out to 42 sets, precisely because most of those trials ran 6–10 weeks.
Nobody has tested high volume for a whole body, in a deficit, past 12 weeks.

**Site-specific measurement read as whole-muscle growth.** The trained-subject lengthened-partials
null measured only 45–55% of humerus length — where the effect is smallest, a caveat House of
Hypertrophy raises himself
([the note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/finally-here-new-study-muscle-growth-epic.md));
the isolation-exercise nulls measured one mid-muscle site, which cannot detect regional
differences at all.

**Studies powered to find nothing, reported as "no difference."** Menno calls it the beta fallacy;
his cleanest cases are a trial whose power analysis was run on pre-post change rather than the
between-group difference
([note](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/50-more-gym-time-nothing-2-new.md)) and one claiming 82%
power from a *post-hoc* analysis landing exactly on its own sample size of 22
([note](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/3-epic-new-studies-build-more-muscle.md)). The scale is
easy to miss: **about 80% of protein supplementation studies find no significant gain**, purely
for being too small and too short — on those alone the field would have abandoned protein
([Trommelen interview](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/muscle-protein-synthesis.md)). Failure to reject
the null is not equivalence.

### What actually deserves high confidence

Short, because honesty makes it short. Each rests on measured hypertrophy or a hard outcome and
survives independent replication.

- **Rep range barely matters between roughly 6 and 35 reps** when sets are taken close to failure
  — and the dissenting low-rep studies have an identified confound: they gave the low-rep group
  more sets and longer rest
  ([note](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/rep-ranges-and-training-intensity.md)).
- **More volume produces more growth across the range anyone actually trains**, roughly 4 to 20+
  weekly sets per muscle. Where it stops is unsettled; that it rises is not
  ([note](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/junk-volume.md)).
- **Frequency does almost nothing once weekly sets are equated** — a negative claim, but a
  well-powered meta-analytic one
  ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/often-should-train-muscle-new-study-epic.md)).
- **Acute post-workout testosterone and growth hormone do not predict growth.** Four independent
  lines converge, and a positive control makes the null informative: raising testosterone
  pharmacologically *does* work, additively
  ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/spike-testosterone-growth-hormone-naturally-via-lifting.md)).
- **Protein intake plateaus near 1.6 g/kg/day** — meta-analyses, nitrogen balance and IAAO
  converge, and the ISSN's independent 1.4–2.0 g/kg overlaps it
  ([HoH](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/protein-myth-much-really-need.md),
  [ISSN](ISSN%20position%20stands/protein-2017.md)). Measured on fat-free mass, not hypertrophy.
- **Total daily intake dominates timing.** The 30-minute window is retired by the
  [2017 ISSN timing stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/nutrient-timing-2017.md), and the pre-sleep
  protein effect disappears once total intake is matched
  ([teardown](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/protein-before-bed-really-work.md)).
- **Creatine monohydrate at 3–5 g/day works** — two ISSN stands eighteen years apart agree
  ([2017](ISSN%20position%20stands/creatine-2017.md)) — but measured directly it is
  **0.10–0.16 cm of thickness, effect size 0.11**, which the pooling authors called trivial
  ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/science-behind-creatine-much-more-muscle-strength.md)).

Note what is absent: exercise selection, technique cues, tempo, rest intervals, every tier list.
And the shared caveat — almost none of it rests on trained lifters studied beyond twelve weeks.

### Funding and conflicts, documented

**The Nippard-funded null.** One of the 2024 trained-subject studies finding no advantage for
lengthened partials over full range of motion was funded by Jeff Nippard — another source in this
library — and House of Hypertrophy discloses it in
[Most important studies of 2024](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/most-important-muscle-building-studies-2024.md).
It cut against Nippard's own prior position — the least worrying direction for a bias to run, but
on the record either way.

**The ISSN.** In **15 of 27 documents a declared tie bears directly on that document's own
conclusions** — not industry ties in general, ties in the category the stand assesses.
[Creatine 2017](ISSN%20position%20stands/creatine-2017.md) was funded by a supplement-industry
trade association; [HMB 2025](ISSN%20position%20stands/hmb-2025.md) has a lead author employed
by a company holding HMB patents; [beta-alanine](ISSN%20position%20stands/beta-alanine.md) a
lead author funded by a manufacturer; [probiotics](ISSN%20position%20stands/probiotics.md) six
authors employed by or founding probiotic businesses. The
[2023 energy drinks stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/energy-drinks-2023.md) carries a statement
reading "No potential conflict of interest was reported" directly above a section declaring share
ownership in an energy drink company.

Counter-examples matter as much:
[sodium bicarbonate](ISSN%20position%20stands/sodium-bicarbonate.md) declares nothing and
overreaches least of any stand; the
[ketogenic stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/ketogenic-diets.md) argues against the diet though
its authors hold ketosis patents. Disclosure is not disqualification, just a place to look harder.
Nippard, separately, notes the studies supporting spot reduction tend to be supplement-company
sponsored ([note](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/lose-bodyfat-from-specific-bodyparts.md)).

## How much to train, and how hard

Three sources argue about this constantly; the fourth is silent, the ISSN corpus being nutrition and
never prescribing a set count. Most of the fight is about which question is being answered.

### The volume ceiling: three claims, and only one real disagreement

Nippard's position is **10–20 hard sets per muscle per week** with a **6–8 set per session** cap
([junk volume](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/junk-volume.md)); by 2025 he had run himself at **~6.5
weekly sets** for 100 days on a cut and reversed publicly on whether volume should hold in a deficit
([100 days of low volume](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/cut-number-sets-half.md)). House of Hypertrophy
reports the Pelland meta-regression — 35 studies, 1,000+ subjects — finding a square-root curve with
**no plateau and no inverted U out to 43 weekly sets**, later sets worth about a third of the first
([more sets, more growth](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md))
— then recommends **10–20** anyway. Menno reads *the same preprint*
([67 studies](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/maximize-muscle-according-67-studies.md)) and also lands
on **10–20**, minimum effective dose **4**.

So on the recommendation there is **no disagreement at all**. The dispute is about what the 43-set
figure means and who may override it.

Here Menno is the most careful of the three, not the most permissive. He treats 43 as **the point
where the data ran out**, says an inverted U is "most likely true" despite the analysis finding none,
and gives his reason: the pooled samples are college-aged subjects without jobs, families or
disrupted sleep. Elsewhere he is the most aggressive
([volume and recovery](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/volume-recovery-brutal-truth.md)): five days of
squats to 40% velocity loss failed to induce overreaching — strength *rose* while perceived recovery
fell 11% — so the binding constraint he names is psychological, logistical and articular, not
neuromuscular. Both positions are his; "doubting a ceiling exists" flattens him.

House of Hypertrophy's override is the best-argued objection here, and it is not Menno's: **many
pooled studies used incomplete programs**, training one or two muscle groups, so systemic fatigue
does not resemble a real week. It lands hardest on the headline result — Enes 2024 (31 trained men,
22 → 42 or 52 weekly quad sets, high volume winning) trained **quads only**
([2.2× more muscle](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/2-2x-more-muscle-new-study-surprising.md)),
and the pool averages ~10 weeks.

**Verdict, high confidence:** the dose-response is real and monotonic within the studied range, and
nothing has demonstrated a weekly ceiling. House of Hypertrophy's calibration is the right one —
*confident moderate beats low, not confident very high beats moderate*. **Moderate confidence**
that an inverted U exists somewhere: asserted on sample-composition grounds, never shown. What would
resolve it: a whole-body trial past twelve weeks, in a deficit, at 30+ fractional sets. One counting
caveat nobody states loudly enough — the meta-regression's best-fitting scheme was **fractional**
(indirect sets count 0.5), so "10–20" off that curve is more work than 10–20 counted traditionally.

### The Barbalho retraction, and what went with it

The "less is more" leg of this dispute was largely two Barbalho papers — trained men and untrained
women, every muscle trained **once weekly** at 5/10/15/20 sets, concluding 5–10 sufficed and 15–20
were worse. Both are **retracted** for improbable data (Vigotsky; Stronger by Science), and House of
Hypertrophy says so while *building the argument they would have supported*
([training splits](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md)).

Exactly what depended on them:

- **Nippard's entire 2019 per-session-ceiling argument.** [Is workout volume killing your
  gains?](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/workout-volume-killing-gains.md) exists to explain the Barbalho
  result; the ~5-sets-per-session ceiling is the reconciling hypothesis, otherwise sourced to rodent
  MPS data he flags as an assumption. Remove Barbalho and the *explanandum* goes, not just the
  evidence.
- **Three further Nippard claims cited to Barbalho directly**: the 5–10 sets/session figure in
  [high-frequency full body](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/high-frequency-full-body-training.md), the
  per-session threshold in [5 training
  mistakes](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/5-training-mistakes-everyone-makes-when-they.md), and the
  "counter-study" — "an actual reversal, not a plateau" — in [bro science vs real
  science](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/bro-science-vs-real-science.md).

These videos predate the retraction. What survives: three long-rest
plateau trials (8–10 weeks, n=35–49), two German Volume Training trials in which
[neither group showed significant ultrasound
hypertrophy](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/german-volume-training-actually-build-muscle-fast.md)
— detection failure, not a ranking — and Fisher and Steele's 24-week women's trial. **Verdict, high
confidence: the "5 beats 20" position has no usable primary evidence**, and any claim here sourced to
Barbalho should be read as withdrawn. The other objection — that high-volume gains are swelling — is
also dead, [Alvarez](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/many-sets-muscle-growth.md) and
[Menno](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/more-volume-more-muscle-just-swelling.md) both finding it
resolved by 24 h with no volume effect, though on n=13 with no effect sizes.

### Per session versus per week — the distinction that does the work

A different question, and the two answers are compatible: a per-session cap plus an unbounded weekly
curve means spread the sets, not stop adding them.

- **Nippard:** ~6 sets/session, from **Krieger's unpublished, paywalled meta-analysis of nine
  studies**; he flags the wide variance and calls the 10–12 figure for back/glutes/quads coaching
  speculation. Helms dissents — [no per-session minimum and probably no meaningful
  maximum](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/training-full-body-5x-per-week-smart.md) — and Nippard concedes
  on camera that the argument may be invalid.
- **House of Hypertrophy:** the per-session companion analysis finds the **same rising shape, no
  plateau past 20 sets**, holding across short and long rest — which made him
  [retire his own rest-interval hypothesis](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md).
- **Menno:** reports the **same analysis**, calls its 11-set figure a **plateau**, then declines to
  use it in favour of Krieger's unpublished 6
  ([max volume per workout](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/max-volume-should-per-workout.md)).

**Much of this is a framing dispute over one number.** Both describe the same statistical criterion —
11 sets is where added sets stop having a >50% probability of exceeding the smallest detectable
effect — and one calls it "where the uncertainty starts" on a still-rising curve while the other
calls it "the plateau." Menno concedes it means "low likelihood of further gains", not zero.
**Verdict, moderate confidence:** no per-session wall has been demonstrated, only steep diminishing
returns, and 6 is a *prior* rather than a finding — a nine-study analysis nobody outside a paywall
can read, against a 35-study regression. Redistributing past ~10 sets in a session costs nothing if
the ceiling is fictional. What would resolve it: that analysis rerun **controlled for weekly
volume**, which Menno correctly notes it was not.

### Failure: a settled question wearing a disagreement's clothes

Everyone agrees failure is not required; the fight is over how far short you may stop.

House of Hypertrophy: **3–1 RIR matches failure**, the null holding at **6–19 weekly sets**, the best
test an 18-subject within-subject trial in lifters averaging **7.7 years** with **verified RIR
accuracy** ([how hard should you
workout](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/hard-should-workout-new-study-epic.md),
[four longitudinal
studies](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/training-failure-must-hypertrophy.md)); beyond 3 RIR is
[unresolved, not permitted](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/leaving-more-3-reps-reserve-vs-training.md).
Nippard: 1–3 RIR, last set to failure. Menno goes furthest — [4–6 RIR can match 0
RIR](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/close-failure-should-train.md) — rejecting the effective-reps
model on recruitment, fibre-activation and EMG grounds.

But read his condition: *if total reps are equated*. He states the two routes himself — fewer sets to
failure, or more sets stopped short — which is not a contradiction of House of Hypertrophy but the
same finding in another currency: Lasevicius needed **2–3 extra sets** for 5 RIR to match failure.
**Verdict, high confidence: there is no physiological disagreement here.** House of Hypertrophy
counts hard sets, Menno counts reps, and the exchange rate is what looks like a dispute. The one live
question — whether a *set-equated* prescription can sit past 3 RIR — has the answer no.

**How much is really about judging RIR?** Less than the framing suggests: all three say the same
thing. Nippard's Barbosa-Netto data has **47% of lifters getting 16–20+ reps** on their
self-selected "10-rep" weight ([not training hard
enough](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/not-training-hard-enough.md)); Halperin's pooled 12 studies give a
**1-rep average error** with individuals out by **10–13**
([RIR accuracy](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/become-laser-accurate-leaving-reps-reserve.md));
and Menno — supposedly the anti-RPE voice — opens [three tests for proximity to
failure](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/3-tips-estimate-proximity-failure.md) with "most people don't
train nearly as close to failure as they think." The reconciliation is House of Hypertrophy's:
people judge proximity adequately **when trying to** and badly when merely training, which makes
occasional failure sets **calibration rather than stimulus** and means an unaudited "3 RIR" silently
becomes junk volume. Nippard's anchoring set and Menno's velocity-loss test are the same fix. **High
confidence**, one residual: in-set estimates at heavier loads are markedly more accurate, so anyone
training by RIR belongs on the heavier half of the load range.

### Frequency: nobody is still claiming it, once volume is equated

Nippard holds **2×/week beats 1×** on Schoenfeld 2016 while criticising his own side's evidence: the
strongest pro-frequency study used three weekly sets per muscle, and the **novelty confound** sits
inside every trial ([bro splits](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/bro-splits-vs-higher-frequency.md)).
House of Hypertrophy reports Pelland finding **no consistent or large effect** — 1× → 2× compatible
with nothing, nothing beyond 2×, the "wins only at recoverable volumes" defence failing in both
bins — and says he is **"genuinely surprised"**
([frequency](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/often-should-train-muscle-new-study-epic.md)).
Menno reads the same analysis as a **91% probability** of a small positive effect, most of it running
through the volume frequency permits. The MPS mechanism is gone either way —
[the argument runs on *mixed* protein synthesis, which does not track myofibrillar
MPS](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/high-frequency-training-more-frequent-muscle-protein.md).

**Verdict, high confidence: nobody credible still claims frequency is an independent growth lever.**
It is a container for volume; prefer 2×/week because it keeps sessions off the steep end of the
diminishing-returns curve, not because the muscle needs restimulating.

### One number to correct outright

Nippard's minimalist table gives **~64%** of maximum gains at 1–4 weekly sets and **~84%** at 5–9
([train like a minimalist](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/train-like-minimalist.md)) — the
**uncorrected** figures from a meta-analysis published with a set-counting error. Its own author's
fix puts under five weekly sets at **53%**
([minimum dose](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/build-muscle-while-barely-training-using-science.md)).
**Verdict: use 53%, low confidence** — the correcting video has no reference list, but a
known-erroneous number cannot outrank its authors' correction.

## Load, reps, and range of motion

Four sources, effectively three: the ISSN position stands say nothing about rep ranges, range of
motion or tempo. Where the other three conflict it is almost always because one is quoting a study
the others have taken apart.

### The equivalent rep band: the library should say 6–35

The library carries three numbers: Nippard's **3 to 35**
([rep ranges](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/rep-ranges-and-training-intensity.md)), Henselmans' **4 to
30, probably higher** ([optimal rep range](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/optimal-rep-range-muscle-growth-isn-t.md)),
House of Hypertrophy's **6 to 35**
([40+ studies](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/many-reps-muscle-growth-40-studies.md)) — whose
own summary says 5–35 in one section and 6–35 in another, so the inconsistency is not only across
sources.

They differ over one confound. The low end is defended by six studies comparing 2–5 reps against
moderate or high reps; five found parity, and **four of those five gave the low-rep group more sets,
longer rest, or both**, almost always because researchers equated *volume load*
([reps below 5](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/reps-below-5-maximize-muscle-growth.md)):
Mangine gave 3 minutes' rest against 1; Schoenfeld 2014 gave **7 sets of 3 against 3 sets of 10** at
3 minutes against 90 seconds; Campos and Chestnut & Docherty did the same in smaller measure. Two
clean comparisons survive and contradict — Weiss 2000 (44 untrained men, 4 sets, 6 minutes' rest)
found 3–5 reps as good as 13–15 and 23–25; Schoenfeld 2016 (19 trained men, 3 sets, 2 minutes' rest)
found 2–4 reps grew *less* vastus lateralis and elbow flexor than 8–12.

This is the sharpest argument in the domain, and decisive in a way that is easy to miss: the
confounds run *toward* the low-rep condition. Handed twice the sets and double the rest, ≤5 reps
should have beaten higher reps if it were equally potent per set. It only tied. And the volume-load
equating that produced the confound is itself unjustified — 6–35 reps grow similarly at matched
*sets* despite wildly different volume loads, so equating it introduced a confound rather than
removing one.

The verdict is uncomfortable for Nippard specifically. **His "3 to 35" rests directly on Schoenfeld
2014** — the 7-sets-of-3 study — and he reports the 7-versus-3 set count in the same note, treating
it as an argument about practicality rather than as grounds to discount the equivalence claim. The
floor of his band is downstream of the confound.

The positive evidence comes from Vann et al. — 15 trained men, within-subject, where the leg doing
**5 reps at 82.5→95% 1RM near failure averaged no gain at all** while the leg doing 10 reps at a
fixed 60% with roughly 10 reps in reserve grew, despite the heavy leg doing *more* weekly sets
(18 vs 15) and training *closer* to failure
([5 reps](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/5-reps-maximize-hypertrophy.md)). That is what moved
House of Hypertrophy's floor off 5, and he has not lowered it since, declining to in 2025 on a
bioRxiv preprint (n=14, 3–5 vs 20–25 reps to failure, 9 weeks, similar growth) on small-sample
grounds ([3–5 reps](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/3-5-reps-truly-maximize-growth.md)).

**Verdict: the library should say 6–35 reps, moderate confidence.** Sub-6 sets clearly build muscle;
what is unproven is that they do so as efficiently per set. The correction applies wherever 5–35,
5–30 or 3–35 appears, including
[Nippard's top-level summary](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/rep-ranges-and-training-intensity.md) and
House of Hypertrophy's own volume section. **1–2 rep work builds essentially nothing**
([maxing out](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/maxing-out-effective-building-muscle-one-rep.md));
35 is a soft ceiling — 20% of 1RM (60+ reps) was clearly inferior in the cleanest comparison, yet a
curl trial averaging 100 reps on set one matched 80%
([how light is too light](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/light-too-light-maximizing-muscle-growth.md)).
Nippard's practical zone of **6–15 reps for ~75% of volume** survives untouched; it was always a
fatigue-and-injury argument, not an efficacy claim.

### Lengthened partials: the trained-subject evidence, and the funding

Nippard's 2023 position was the strongest in the library: long-length partials beat short-length in
**7 of 8** studies and full ROM in **4 of 5**, with Dr Milo Wolf estimating a **5–10%** edge
([long-length partials](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/world-s-smartest-muscle-building-technique.md)).
House of Hypertrophy built the same case earlier, from Pedrosa onward
([full ROM is not optimal](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/full-range-motion-not-optimal-building-muscle.md)).

2024 broke it. Nippard's own within-subject trial — trained lifters averaging **4.9 years**,
8 weeks, every set to failure — came back **6.6% vs 5.9%, not significant**
([his own study](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/everyone-thinks-m-wrong-about-training.md)); a second
2024 trial across 15 sites with just under **300 subjects** found the same null. House of
Hypertrophy covers both and stops recommending partials generally
([2024 round-up](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/most-important-muscle-building-studies-2024.md),
[beyond failure](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/training-beyond-failure-new-study-epic.md)).
Henselmans notes a 2025 reanalysis of the founding Pedrosa data summing all sites: at the
whole-muscle level partials do **not** significantly beat full ROM — only *shortened* partials
clearly underperform
([three 2025 studies](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/3-awesome-new-studies-build-muscle-2025.md)).

**On the funding.** House of Hypertrophy discloses that the null within-subject study was **funded
by Jeff Nippard**, who is also an author. Record it, then read it correctly: the funding ran
*against* the funder's public position — Nippard had spent a year advocating partials, the study he
paid for failed to support them, and results were withheld from him until the statistics were
final. Bias predicts the opposite result, and the null replicates in the ~300-subject trial he had
nothing to do with. The real limitation is not money: **both trained-subject nulls measured only
mid-muscle regions (45–55%)**, precisely where any effect is smallest.

That caveat is now partly tested: a 2025 preacher-curl trial in 13 trained lifters found a dead heat
at 50% and a modest partial advantage at 70%, Bayes factor **4.87** — moderate evidence, not a win
([faster muscle growth](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/faster-muscle-growth-new-study-epic.md)).
Of six studies, all three in untrained subjects favour partials; all three in trained show parity.

**Verdict, moderate-to-high confidence: for trained lifters, lengthened partials are at least as
good as full ROM and not better.** Nothing has found them worse, so Henselmans' point stands — these
are nulls, not reversals
([on null findings](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/2-things-i-t-stand-about-science.md)). Full ROM
stays the default, and Henselmans' non-hypertrophy reason is the best anyone gives: **a half rep has
no defined endpoint, so you cannot track progressive overload on it**
([full ROM is not dead](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/full-rom-dead-stretch-mediated-hypertrophy-lengthened.md)).
Reserve partials for exercises that load the stretch poorly — leg extensions, calf raises — where
all three converge. Keep separate and do not weaken: **choosing exercises that reach longer muscle
lengths still wins** (seated over lying leg curls, overhead over neutral triceps extensions). That
is a different variable and the nulls do not touch it.

A library defect worth fixing: the same study is written up as **30 subjects** in the Nippard note
and **25** in the House of Hypertrophy note — almost certainly recruited versus analysed, but it
should be checked against the preprint.

### Effective reps: Henselmans against the field, and half right

Henselmans rejects the effective-reps model — that only the last ~5 reps of a set count — on three
grounds: full motor-unit recruitment arrives nearer **80% of 1RM (~8 RIR)** than 85%; fibre-type
activation climbs gradually rather than switching on; and the EMG curves *taper* toward failure, the
opposite shape to a linear count of five
([how close to failure](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/close-failure-should-train.md)). His best
point is that the 2024 Robinson meta-regression cited as vindication found a **log-linear** curve
and explicitly rejected the linear model — so it contradicts what it was said to confirm. Vieira
2021 and Grgic 2021 both find the failure advantage **disappears in rep-equated studies**.

**Verdict: right about the model, overreaching about the practice.** The discrete five-rep cut-off
is not supported — high confidence, and the library should stop implying it. But "drop RIR and RPE
for hypertrophy" does not follow. His equivalence is conditional on equating *total reps*, which
means doing meaningfully more sets, and an RIR estimate is what tells you whether you are. The
trained-subject null is bounded at **3–1 RIR**; beyond that the evidence is four studies pointing
three ways, and Lasevicius needed 2–3 extra sets for 5 RIR to match failure. Set against Nippard's
finding that **47% of lifters** taking their usual "10-rep" weight to failure get 16–20+ reps
([junk volume](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/junk-volume.md)), telling people to stop estimating
proximity is the recommendation here most likely to cost a reader growth.

### Tempo and time under tension: closer than they look

The apparent disagreement is an artefact of dates. All three hold that **0.5–8 seconds per rep is
equivalent** when sets reach failure, from the same Schoenfeld meta-analysis
([Nippard](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/lifting-tempo-fast-vs-slow-reps.md),
[House of Hypertrophy](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/time-under-tension-overrated-10-studies.md)).
House of Hypertrophy supplies the reason the pro-slow studies were wrong — Usui and the two Watanabe
trials **fixed load and rep count**, so the slow group was necessarily closer to failure; they
measure effort, not tempo — and kills the **30–60 second rule** with Lacerda's fast sets, which ran
25–38 seconds and grew the same. His 2025 meta-analysis (14 studies, 278 subjects) puts the
eccentric effect at **SMD 0.06**, credible interval spanning zero
([best rep speed](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/best-rep-speed-size.md)).

The only real divergence was Nippard's 2023 **2–4 second eccentric**, which Henselmans attacked:
slowing a given load adds time under tension, not tension; eccentric overload needs ~20–50% more
*load* before growth differences appear; and you forfeit the stretch reflex
([rep tempo](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/best-repetition-tempo-everyone-got-wrong.md)).
**Nippard retracted slow negatives in December 2024**
([retrospective](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/lifting-over.md)). The disagreement no longer exists.

**Verdict, high confidence: control the eccentric, do not deliberately slow it.** The one surviving
constraint is at the extreme — super-slow (10s up / 4s down) underperformed in Schuenke, a single
6-week untrained trial. Adopt House of Hypertrophy's framing library-wide: **time under tension is
not mechanical tension**, which is why the 30–60 second rule and the slow-eccentric prescription
fail for the same reason.

### What would settle the open questions

Two. Whether 3–5 reps matches 6+ per set needs a trained-subject trial equating **sets and rest**
with an adequate sample; the whole current case is one n=14 preprint and Weiss 2000 in untrained men
against Schoenfeld 2016 in 19 trained men. Whether lengthened partials help **distally** needs
trained-subject trials measuring the 70%+ regions; one n=13 study is all there is.

## Choosing exercises, and how much of the advice is evidence

### The shared weakness: the rankings are not measured growth

Before any disagreement between sources, there is a defect all three video sources share.
**Almost every exercise ranking in this library is built on EMG, moment-arm modelling, resistance
profiles or coaching feel — not on measured hypertrophy.** Jeff Nippard's 2016–2019 per-muscle
videos run on activation data outright; his 2024–25 tier lists replace that with biomechanical
reasoning and personal experience, which he states in every caveats section. House of Hypertrophy's
tier lists do the same. Menno Henselmans reaches his verdicts almost entirely by strength-curve
reasoning, and his own note on the three "overrated" exercises concedes that only two of the cited
studies test anything directly.

House of Hypertrophy produced the library's hardest argument against this method, and it is worth
restating because it is decisive: the **triceps long head shows high EMG in the bench press and
barely grows from it** — exactly the inference EMG is used to make
([EMG cannot rank exercises](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/ryan-humiston-has-made-mistake-emg.md),
[the biarticular note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/maximize-muscle-growth-new-study-exercise-choice.md)).
He then softened the position — arguing EMG is informative for *subtle* comparisons like grip widths
and bench angles — and built the
[chest guide](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/grow-chest-most-detailed-guide-internet-72.md),
[side delt](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/bigger-side-delts-ranking-best-exercises-using.md),
[rear delt](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/build-rear-delts-28-studies.md) and
[forearm](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/build-forearms-faster-most-detailed-guide-web.md)
rankings on activation data anyway. **The earlier position is the better-reasoned one**, and his own
failed side-delt prediction (below) is the reason.

**What is actually backed by measured growth.** The list is short, and every entry is a study that
measured muscle size, not activation:

| Claim | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Overhead triceps extensions beat pushdowns | Maeo 2023: 21 untrained, within-subject, 12 wk, ROM matched — **1.4× whole triceps, 1.5× long head** ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/best-triceps-hypertrophy-exercise.md)) |
| Benching alone under-develops the triceps long head | Brandão 2020: 43 untrained men, 10 wk, **MRI by head** — bench grew the lateral head, skull crushers the long ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/bench-press-enough-triceps-hypertrophy.md)) |
| Seated leg curls beat lying | Maeo 2021, with the short head of biceps femoris as internal control — **~50% more** |
| Leg press and leg extension grow different quad heads | Burke 2024: 28 trained, within-subject — RF favours the extension, VL the press ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/maximize-muscle-growth-new-study-exercise-choice.md)) |
| A leaning-back leg extension beats an upright one | 22 untrained men, within-subject — **1.4–2.7× rectus femoris**, VL equal ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/bigger-quads-new-study-epic.md)) |
| Squatting alone leaves the rectus femoris behind | Fonseca, 49 untrained, 12 wk ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/squat-enough-leg-growth.md)); replicated in 63 untrained women ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/unilateral-vs-bilateral-training-muscle-growth.md)) |
| Straight-leg beats bent-leg calf raises for gastrocnemius | Burke 2024 plus Kinoshita 2023, two independent designs ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/bigger-calves-ultimate-guide-32-studies.md)) |
| Curls grow the elbow flexors more than rows | Mannarino, 10 untrained, within-subject — curl arm ~11%, row arm ~5% ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/compound-exercises-enough-biceps.md)) |
| The neck needs direct work | Conley 1997: 22 men, 12 wk — compound-only group **zero** neck CSA change, +13% with nine weekly sets ([note](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/neck-and-trap-training.md)) |
| Free weights ≈ machines near failure | Schwanbeck 2020 (n=36) and Hernández-Belmonte 2023 (36 trained men) ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/3-exercises-don-t-build-more-muscle.md)). Hypertrophy is equal; **strength transfer is specific to the mode trained** |
| Squats ≈ hip thrusts for glute growth at all three regions | 34 untrained subjects ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/squats-vs-hip-thrusts-glute-growth.md)); Plotkin 2023, cited by Menno |
| Deeper squats grow more quad; 90° leaves glutes and adductors short | Bloomquist 2013 (MRI), Kubo 2019 — though in Kubo the depth advantage was **adductor and glute**, with quadriceps statistically indistinguishable (4.9 ± 2.6% vs 4.6 ± 3.1%) |
| Randomly switching exercises does nothing | Baz-Valle: 19 trained men, 8 wk, 80-exercise randomiser vs fixed list — **null**, raw numbers favouring fixed ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/switch-exercises-every-session-more-muscle-hypertrophy.md)) |
| Push-ups match the bench press for growth | Kikuchi 2017: **18 men with ≥1 year of training**, 3 sets to failure twice weekly, bench at 40% 1RM vs kneeling push-ups — ~3 mm thickness at pecs and triceps in both, no significant difference; Kotarsky 2018 replicates at 4 weeks ([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/best-chest-exercises-muscle-strength.md)) |
| Cable and barbell preacher curls grow the biceps equally | Nunes 2020: opposite resistance profiles, hypertrophy equal; the cable was stronger only when tested at long muscle lengths ([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/best-chest-biceps-workout.md)) |
| Bench angle does not change pec hypertrophy | Chaves 2020: 8 wk, untrained men, flat / incline / both — no meaningful difference. **The weakest entry here**: House of Hypertrophy dismantles the same study on method, and the three sources read it three different ways ([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md)) |

That is **sixteen claims** out of everything these five sources assert about exercise choice.

**Everything else in this domain is unmeasured.** That includes: the entire back and lat ranking in
both video sources — House of Hypertrophy's
[36-study lats guide](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/build-lats-ultimate-guide-36-studies.md)
states twice that it contains *zero* direct measurements of lat growth and has no retrievable
reference list; every side delt, rear delt and forearm ranking; every chest region-targeting claim
(grip width, reverse grip, incline angle, decline, dips, pullovers); the Smith machine; the trap bar
versus conventional deadlift, which is
[biomechanical modelling throughout](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/trap-bar-deadlift-vs-conventional-deadlift-depth.md);
and the internal ordering of every one of Nippard's
[tier lists](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/back-exercises-ranked.md). The ISSN corpus contributes
nothing here at all — it is a nutrition body and has no position on exercise selection.

### The one principle that survives: biarticular conflict and muscle length

House of Hypertrophy treats this as the only usable predictor, and unusually, **all three sources
converge on it from different directions**. The rule has two halves: a two-joint muscle grows poorly
when one of its actions opposes what the compound requires at the other joint, and it grows better
when the non-working joint is positioned to hold it long.

Nippard applies it without naming it — the rectus femoris on squats, the triceps long head on
presses, the short head of biceps femoris that pure hip extension never trains
([hamstrings](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/episode-8-hamstrings.md)). Menno independently runs the
same audit on the squat and reaches the same conclusions about gastrocnemius, hamstrings and rectus
femoris ([note](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/squats-don-t-train-think-they-train.md)).

**Verdict: accept it, with confidence — but only for the version that has been tested.** It is the
best-evidenced idea in the domain because six of the growth studies above are instances of it, three
of them within-subject. It is *not* a licence to promote every length-based ranking. House of
Hypertrophy's own three-way split is the correct refinement
([lateral raises](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/have-talk-about-lateral-raises.md)):
**biarticular positioning holds up; range of motion is now equivocal in trained lifters; and
"where in the range the exercise is hardest" — the resistance-profile logic driving most of
Nippard's tier list — has two studies behind it and both are null.** Menno's one dissent, that
squats do build the vastus medialis and VMO-targeting is a myth, is unresolved but low-stakes.

### Menno on rows

He calls rows overrated: the resistance curve inverts the strength curve, the weak shortened
position caps the load for every muscle involved, and pulldowns, pull-ups and lat isolation are the
better lat stimulus ([note](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/3-most-overrated-exercises-build-muscle-superior.md)).
Nippard crowns the **chest-supported row the single best back exercise overall**, with five more
rows or row-adjacent movements in S tier, and states flatly that everyone should have a row
([back ranked](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/back-exercises-ranked.md),
[width vs thickness](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/back-width-vs-thickness.md)).

**Verdict: unresolved, and neither side has the evidence to settle it. Low confidence in both.**
No trial has compared rows to pulldowns for lat growth. Nippard's case is stability plus stretch
plus Lehman's *activation* data; Menno's is a strength-curve argument with one supporting study
(Mannarino) that measures the *biceps*, which both sources already agree on. House of Hypertrophy's
lats guide, the most thoroughly researched treatment in the library, contains no growth data either
and reaches Nippard's structural conclusion — classify pulls by joint action, and close-grip
vertical and horizontal pulls should develop the lats comparably. Practically: run both, because
nobody can tell you which wins.

### Menno on warm-ups

A preprint had trained lifters do four sets to failure at 10RM after zero, one or two warm-up sets;
total reps were near-identical. He concludes warm-ups are overrated and that there is **"zero hard
data"** that warming up reduces injury
([note](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/warming-bs-like-cooling-down.md)). Nippard prescribes a
three-phase protocol: 5–10 minutes of cardio, 3–4 minutes of dynamic work, then a ramping pyramid
([note](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/warm-up-and-mobility.md)).

**Verdict: Menno is right on the narrow claim, Nippard on the practice, and the gap is smaller than
either implies. Moderate confidence.** Menno exempts maximal-force work, which is exactly Nippard's
phase 3 — and Nippard's own cited Fradkin review is five studies. What Menno actually strips out is
the mobility ritual, and here he has the better evidence: Warneke 2024 found dynamic stretching and
foam rolling no better than any other warm-up, and both sources agree static stretching before the
trained muscle is a performance cost. Menno still recommends a warm-up set per exercise, so the
practical distance is a few minutes of foam rolling.

### Where two sources rank the same exercise differently

**Incline versus flat bench — the sharpest conflict.** Nippard makes incline the default on Chaves
2020, "confirmed with longitudinal data"
([chest 2022](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/best-worst-chest-exercises-build-muscle.md),
[chest ranked](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/chest-exercises-ranked.md)). House of Hypertrophy
dismantles that same study: pec thickness rose **54–62% across eight training sessions** — about
4.6% per workout against a literature averaging 33 sessions — and **only 30 of 47 completers appear
in the hypertrophy data**, unexplained. Menno takes a third position: an incline does not train the
upper pec more, it trains the lower pec less. **Verdict: House of Hypertrophy wins the study
critique decisively; Chaves cannot carry the claim.** Incline work survives on a different argument
— the upper pec starts at ~15 cm² against 25–27 cm², so equal percentage growth buys half the
absolute size ([the arithmetic note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/effective-flat-bench-press-upper-middle-lower.md)).
Do incline; the usual reason for it is wrong. Moderate confidence.

**Cable versus dumbbell lateral raise.** Nippard's 2024 list: cable S tier, dumbbell B, entirely on
resistance profile ([shoulders](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/shoulder-exercises-ranked.md)). House of
Hypertrophy predicted the same and then ran the trial — 24 trained lifters averaging 7.1 years, one
arm each, 8 weeks — and found **similar growth at both regions**. **Verdict: the null wins;
Nippard concedes it by 2025 ([note](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/7-things-helped-shoulders-grow.md)).
High confidence that the choice does not matter, moderate only because total growth sat near
measurement error.** This is the cleanest demonstration in the library that resistance-profile
reasoning does not predict growth.

**Best chest exercise.** Nippard's machine chest press (S tier, 2024) against the one direct
comparison, which found machine chest press ≈ flat barbell bench
([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/bench-press-enough-chest-growth-24-studies.md)). The
ranking is a preference, not a finding — and Nippard's own 2026 list drops the machine out of the
top 25 entirely.

**Deadlifts.** The one place Menno's contrarianism lands on consensus: he replaces them with RDLs,
Nippard ranks them C tier as a back builder and "probably not optimal" for size
([only 25](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/only-25-exercises-need.md)). Agreement, on no growth data
either way.

## Nutrition and supplements

This is the domain where the ISSN position stands should dominate, and largely do. It is also where
they need the hardest reading, for two documented reasons: their numbered position statements are
repeatedly firmer than the review paragraphs directly above them, and a declared in-category
commercial tie appears in 15 of 27 documents
([corpus summary](ISSN%20position%20stands/SUMMARY.md)). Where a creator contradicts a stand
below, the creator is usually reading a newer paper — not correcting it from outside.

### Creatine: a real effect, and two inflated headlines

The [2017 ISSN stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/creatine-2017.md) calls creatine "the most
effective ergogenic nutritional supplement currently available." House of Hypertrophy's
[creatine note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/science-behind-creatine-much-more-muscle-strength.md)
reports the analysis that puts a number on the hypertrophy half: pooling ten trials that measured
muscle directly by ultrasound or MRI, creatine added **0.10–0.16 cm of thickness, effect size 0.11**,
which those authors called trivial. He re-scores that 0.11 against hypertrophy-specific thresholds
taken from a Stronger by Science blog post, benchmarks it against a literature average, and gets
"about one-third faster muscle growth."

**Why they differ is not what it looks like.** The obvious story — industry-funded society versus
independent creator — is wrong here, and checking the authorship shows why. That direct-measurement
meta-analysis is Burke et al., *Nutrients* 2023 ([PMID 37432300](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432300/)),
and two of its authors, **Candow and Forbes, include the seventh author of the ISSN stand itself**.
The field moved; the stand did not get rebutted from outside it. The real gap is the measurement:
the ISSN's enthusiasm rests on **lean mass** (1–1.4 kg over 7–14 weeks), and creatine is an osmolyte
that demonstrably raises intracellular water. Lean mass and muscle thickness are measuring different
things, and both source's headlines pick the flattering one.

**Verdict.** Creatine's performance effect is secure — 5–15% more maximal strength and roughly 30%
more total reps across five sets ([Nippard](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/creatine.md)) — and its
hypertrophy effect is small and partly fluid. Nippard's own framing, **2–3 lb of lean mass**, is the
honest one ([supplements summary](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/SUMMARY-supplements-and-physiology.md)).
Take 3–5 g/day of monohydrate; skip loading unless you want the effect this week. **Do not quote
"one-third faster"** — it is a relabelling against a non-peer-reviewed threshold set, with no new
measurement behind it, and House of Hypertrophy does not say so in the video. *Confidence: high on
performance, moderate on ~1–2 lb of actual contractile tissue a year.*

**On disclosures.** Self-citation is modest in bulk — 21 of 269 references carry an author's name —
but concentrated where the stand overreaches: "athletes who take creatine experience a lower
incidence of injuries" rests on Greenwood 2003a/b and Kreider 2003, all non-randomised
college-football observation from this author group. The manuscript was prepared at the request of,
and funded by, the Council for Responsible Nutrition, a supplement trade association. That does not
touch the saturation data, which is decades old and independently replicated. It does bear on the
passages that read as regulation rather than physiology: that under-18 label advisories are "likely
unnecessary", and the closing warning that sport bodies restricting creatine may face "legal
liability" over head injury — advocacy resting on rodent infarct volumes.

### Protein: the total, and the deficit

Four of the five sources converge on the same daily number by different routes, which is the
strongest signal in this domain. The fifth, Barbell Medicine, gives **four different figures across
nineteen articles** — 1.3–3.1, 1.6, 1.4–1.6, and 1.6 g/kg of *target* body weight — and
cross-references none of them; only the 1.6 is traced correctly to Morton. Their spread is a fact
about publishing, not about protein, and it does not move the band. See
[what the fifth source changed](#what-the-fifth-source-changed) for the detail. The [ISSN 2017 stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/protein-2017.md) gives
**1.4–2.0 g/kg/day as a floor**; Nippard gives 1.6–2.2 bulking; Menno gives **1.8 g/kg** flat, the
1.6 plateau from the Morton meta-analysis he co-authored plus a standard deviation of insurance
([note](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/much-protein-really-need.md)); House of Hypertrophy strips
the fitted line off the 2018 dataset and finds that **above 1.25 g/kg protein explains 1% of the
variance** in fat-free-mass change ([note](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/protein-myth-much-really-need.md)).
**Verdict: 1.6–1.8 g/kg. Everything above 2.0 is insurance, not effect.** *Confidence: high.*

**Protein in a deficit is where Menno breaks, and he is right on the narrow claim.** The library
records 1.8–2.7 g/kg for cutting; he holds there is no evidence for raising protein in a deficit at
all. Check what the higher figure rests on: House of Hypertrophy points out that Mettler and
Longland, the two trials always cited, used **40% deficits against 1.0–1.2 g/kg control arms**, and
the ISSN's anchor is Pasiakos 2013, where 1.6 and 2.4 g/kg both beat **0.8**. Every one of those is
adequate-versus-inadequate. **Nobody has run 1.6 against 2.4 in a deficit.** So the upper half of the
cutting range has no trial behind it, and saying "eat more protein when cutting" is an
asymmetric-risk judgement — cheap, probably harmless, possibly useless — not an evidence claim.
*Verdict: 1.6–1.8 g/kg suffices in a moderate deficit; confidence moderate-high. At extreme deficits
or stage leanness, unstudied — take 2.0–2.2 as insurance and know that is what you are doing.*

### Per-meal dose and pre-sleep protein

The 20–30 g per-meal ceiling is dead and all four sources agree; absorption was never the limit,
since humans absorb virtually all of even 300 g
([Nippard](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/much-protein-absorb-one-meal.md)). The ISSN got to 20–40 g in
2017, ahead of the popular account. But its **3-hourly distribution** advice rests on a single
12-hour acute study using only 80 g of protein, which the stand itself calls "quite low" and its own
recommendation "pragmatic." The only outcome data
[House of Hypertrophy](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/protein-limit-meal-they-were-wrong.md)
finds shows three servings slightly beating two. *Verdict: 3–5 feedings, stop optimising. Confidence:
high on the dead ceiling, low on distribution mattering beyond a few percent.*

**Pre-sleep protein is the cleanest adjudication in this domain, and it is settled by the ISSN stand
against its own position.** House of Hypertrophy's
[teardown](House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/protein-before-bed-really-work.md) shows the 2021
Reis review did not control total daily protein — its key trial left groups at **1.9 versus 1.3
g/kg/day**. Nothing there separates "protein before bed" from "more protein." The ISSN stand *flags
that exact confound in its own body text*, then issues the 30–40 g casein recommendation anyway, and
spends more words on a null trial's non-significant trend than on its null result. Nippard includes
the counter-evidence honestly — two trials at ~2 g/kg total found no difference — and still
recommends 40 g. **Verdict: pre-sleep protein is a way of hitting your daily total, not a lever of
its own.** If your overnight gap is 10+ hours and your total is under 1.6, a pre-bed feed helps
because of the total. At 2 g/kg it does nothing measurable. *Confidence: high on the confound;
moderate on "no effect" — the three matched trials are underpowered, which is absence of evidence.*

### Caffeine: the dose is settled, the cycling is not

Menno is filed as departing from consensus here, and on **dose he barely does**. The
[2021 stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/caffeine-2021.md) — the best-executed document in the corpus,
which walks back three of its own 2010 claims — already states that **1–3 mg/kg is ergogenic** and
argues for the low end because side effects scale linearly while performance does not. Menno's
~1 mg/kg is that logic one notch further, supported by blinded trials finding 1, 3 and 6 mg/kg
equivalent ([note](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/7-new-studies-must-know-about-caffeine.md)).

The genuine disagreement is **tolerance**. The ISSN reviews a three-way contradictory literature —
tolerance in 28 days, none in 20, habitual intake irrelevant at 6 mg/kg — and declines to recommend
cycling. Its best contribution is the withdrawal-reversal confound: short abstinence means the
caffeine trial is partly relief from withdrawal, which *looks* ergogenic. **That cuts against
Menno's 4–9 day washout as much as for it.** *Verdict: unresolved, and both sides
outrun the evidence — Nippard's "insurmountable" and Menno's "largely negates it" alike. Nobody has
run the study that would settle it: abstain, retest, confirm the response is restored. Confidence:
high that the low dose is right and that dosing early in the day is right (insomnia in 6 of 13 at
18:00 versus 1 of 13 in the morning); low on cycling either way.* One disclosure matters: the stand's
lead author consults for a genetic testing company, and the pivotal CYP1A2 trial is her own. Treat
the genotyping recommendation as a hypothesis.

### Where the stands are firmer than their own reviews

Four cases a reader would act on:

- **[HMB 2025](ISSN%20position%20stands/hmb-2025.md)** asserts there are *no* negative effects on
  glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, above a body describing rodent impairment, reduced
  human insulin secretion, and a closing "further research is needed." Its lead author is employed by
  MTI Biotech, the second is its consultant, both hold HMB patents, and the pivotal kinetics,
  toxicity and genotoxicity papers are the authors' own. Treat HMB as the stand's own summary does —
  a clinical muscle-preservation agent, not a bodybuilding supplement.
- **[Essential amino acids](ISSN%20position%20stands/essential-amino-acids.md)** claims EAAs
  stimulate MPS *more than* an isonitrogenous protein isolate. The one published study matching free
  amino acids to a protein's exact composition — which the intact protein won — is absent from its
  127 references, and the [2018 ISSN review](ISSN%20position%20stands/issn-review-update-2018.md)
  says the opposite. Two authors hold EAA patents; the document is substantively a review of its own
  lab. Keep the mechanistic case *against* BCAAs, which every source here agrees on.
- **[Beta-alanine](ISSN%20position%20stands/beta-alanine.md)** is right drug, wrong job for
  lifters — 1–4 minute efforts, nothing under 60 seconds. The
  [2007 creatine stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/creatine-2007.md) claimed beta-alanine synergy;
  the 2015 stand, on the same two studies, found none. Stout and Hoffman authored both.
- **[Omega-3](ISSN%20position%20stands/omega-3.md)** converts "equivocal" into "may decrease
  soreness" four separate times, on a stand with four in-category ties. Defensible on general-health
  grounds; not a gains supplement.

The counterexamples are the reading rule. [Sodium bicarbonate](ISSN%20position%20stands/sodium-bicarbonate.md)
declares nothing and overreaches least; the [ketogenic stand](ISSN%20position%20stands/ketogenic-diets.md)
argues *against* a commercially popular diet whose ketosis patents its own authors hold. **The
further a stand's conclusion sits from its authors' commercial interest, the more closely its
numbered positions track its own review.** Where they diverge, trust the review.

## Injury, pain and the clinical questions

Every other section of this file works the same way: two or more sources make incompatible claims,
and the job is to say which is right. **This section cannot do that, because there is only one
source.** Sixty of Barbell Medicine's 155 notes are clinical — injury, rehab, pain science, lab
interpretation, cardiovascular risk, pregnancy, ageing — and the other four sources are a nutrition
body and three hypertrophy channels
([why the source is here](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/SUMMARY.md)). Nothing in the library
contradicts this material, and that is not evidence it is right. It is the absence of a test.

So the verdicts below answer three weaker questions instead: does the claim match the paper it
cites, does the source contradict itself, and is this evidence or a clinician's practice pattern?
Barbell Medicine make that last one easier than most — several articles raise it themselves — but
the two kinds of writing arrive in the same voice.

### The asymptomatic-imaging data: the strongest thing in the source

The one place in the clinical material where the evidence carries the conclusion, and the numbers
are worth having exactly. Knee osteoarthritis on MRI in asymptomatic uninjured adults over 40:
**up to 43%** (Culvenor 2019, SR+MA,
[note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/osteoarthritis-symptoms-causes-treatment.md)). Meniscus tear
in **230 knees** of asymptomatic adults, mean age 44, at 3.0 T: **30%** (Horga 2020). Meniscal
change in asymptomatic collegiate basketball players: **50% preseason rising to 62% postseason**
(Pappas 2016); about **one third** of ~300 asymptomatic athletes (Beals 2016)
([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/second-opinion-meniscus-injury.md)). SI joint degeneration
in pain-free adults: **65.1%, of which 30.5% substantial** (Eno 2015,
[note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/si-joint-movement-cause-hip-low-back.md)). Spondylolysis:
**3–7% of asymptomatic adolescents, 11.5% of adults**, with Beutler's 45-year follow-up and
Andrade's systematic review finding no association with back pain
([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md)).

Two of those do more than count. **Barreto 2019 scanned both shoulders of 123 people with pain in
one**, controlling for age, occupation and training history within the same body — the best single
design in the section
([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/barbell-medicine-guide-shoulder-pain.md)). **Pappas is
longitudinal**: the same athletes accumulated findings across a season without developing symptoms.
A dissociation, not a snapshot.

**Verdict, high confidence — for the weak claim only.** A structural finding is weak evidence that
it caused your pain. The strong version, that findings never matter, is not what these studies
show, and Barreto's own result cuts against it: full-thickness supraspinatus tears and glenohumeral
OA ran **about 10 percentage points more common on the painful side**. The shoulder guide builds
**6,710 words on that one study, with three inline sources in total**, and never engages its own
10-point gap. The sciatica guide reaches the identical conclusion across 15 verified references.

### Nearly all the rehab dosing is recommendation, not evidence

The prescriptions are specific and almost entirely uncited. Heavy slow resistance at **3-0-3 tempo,
RPE 8, 2–3× weekly, held 12–16 weeks** — a 4,701-word guide carrying **four citations**
([tendinopathy](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/barbell-medicine-guide-tendinopathy.md)). Pain rules:
**≤3–4/10** during heavy slow resistance; **≤4/10 during and 24 hours after, two consecutive
breaches → cut volume** ([elbow](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/lateral-elbow-pain-lifters.md));
symptoms rising no more than **2 points out of 10** (meniscus); pain-out-of-10 plus RPE summing to
**≤10** (spondylolysis). Every one of those numbers is presented without citation.

They are unusually candid about this. [Hip pain part II](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/training-hip-pain-part-ii.md)
concedes there is **no research on heavy slow resistance for hip tendinopathy at all**; the
[rehab mistakes article](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/biggest-mistakes-re-making-lifting-rehab.md)
runs **3,639 words with zero citations** and says there is nothing special about its sample
programmes.

**Verdict: follow the direction, ignore the precision. Moderate confidence on direction, none on
dose.** Load management rather than rest is mainstream physiotherapy, but the direct evidence across
all 24 rehab articles is **two papers**: Kerkhoffs 2002 (Cochrane, immobilisation versus functional
treatment in acute lateral ankle ligament injury) and Maetz 2023 (SR+MA of RCTs, loading versus
passive modalities in **midportion Achilles tendinopathy only**). Everywhere else — hip, spine,
shoulder, knee, elbow — the rest-is-worse claim is uncited inference from those two plus detraining
physiology. Treat 3-0-3 and 4/10 the way you treat "8–12 reps": a place to start, not a finding.

### The absolute statements their own citations do not carry

The recurring error is stating **absence of demonstrated benefit as demonstrated absence** — "no
evidence that any modality speeds up the healing process"
([ankle](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/manage-ankle-sprain.md)), "no physiological justification"
for passive modalities, "none of these have demonstrated a benefit beyond placebo" — all uncited or
over-read. [Core stability](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/core-stability-matter.md) rests
"absolutely no scientific evidence" on a single narrative critical analysis (Wirth 2017), which is
also the only source for the same claim in the sciatica articles.
[Running and knee OA](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/running-knee-osteoarthritis.md) calls
recreational running "protective" while its own source found **13.3% OA prevalence in competitive
runners against 3.5% in recreational** — a dose signal the headline erases. And "all treatments for
pain are mediated by placebo effects" is asserted twice, once as a section heading, with **no
citation at all** ([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/placebo-nocebo-effects-pain.md)).

The pattern is consistent enough to be a reading rule: **a well-evidenced negative finding about
measurement gets restated as a positive claim about reality.** Clinicians cannot reliably detect SI
motion — true, kappa −0.06 — becomes the SI joint is not a pain source, which the article's own
Laslett material contradicts. Manual muscle testing cannot detect gluteal weakness — true — becomes
gluteal weakness is not plausible
([hip pain I](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/training-hip-pain-part-i.md)). Discount the absolute
version and the measurement finding survives; it was the better claim anyway.

### The lab thresholds — the most actionable content in the library, and its defect

Nothing else in this library tells a reader whether to see a doctor, so this is where being wrong
costs most.

**Creatinine.** The physiology is established: creatinine is a muscle metabolite used as a kidney
test, so a rise means filtration fell or muscle mass rose and CKD-EPI cannot tell which.
**1.4–1.5 mg/dL against a 0.7–1.2 reference** is the realistic muscularity case; above
**1.5 mg/dL (133 µmol/L)** always investigate; **cystatin C** is unaffected by muscle mass and
settles it; a prior value beats a threshold, so **0.8 → 1.4 warrants evaluation even if muscle was
gained in between** ([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/abnormal-labs-exercise-part-i-kidney-function.md)).
The 1.5 line and the compare-to-a-prior sequence are conventional judgement, untested as a pathway.
This is the cleanest article in the clinical batch and it sells nothing.

**Liver enzymes, and the documented defect.** The timeline is the useful part — transaminases keep
rising for **48 hours**, peak at **4–7 days**, reach baseline only at **10–12 days** — and albumin
and INR are unaffected by exercise, so a normal INR effectively excludes acute liver failure. But
**the article gives three different escalation thresholds: >5× the upper limit of normal in the body
text, >10× in the FAQ, <3× in the patient scripts**
([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/elevated-liver-enzymes-after-exercise-means.md)). Its key
takeaway also claims **100% of resistance trainees show elevation** on the strength of Pettersson
2008 — **n=15, previously untrained, one session, no control** — and at least five of its reference
entries print an author and title that do not match the paper their own link resolves to.

**Verdict: use >5× ULN, the most conservative of the three, and never quote the FAQ number** — a
reader taking >10× could sit on a 7× elevation. *Confidence: high that the mechanism is real and
commonly missed, since a physician survey found **56%** did not list exercise-induced muscle damage
in their differential; low on any specific threshold, because the article contradicts itself and no
trial has tested rest-then-retest against usual care.* The same discipline applies to CK: **>5× ULN
is sensitive but not specific**, athletes routinely exceed it, and the classic rhabdomyolysis triad
appears in **fewer than 10% of cases**
([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/rhabdomyolysis-causes-treatments-return-sport.md)).

### Where the other sources reach these topics

Not Nippard, as you might expect, but **Menno** — the only other source that goes here seriously,
arriving from outside medicine and landing in the same place. His
[herniated-disc note](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/herniating-disc-3-taught-me-5-injury.md) runs on
the same papers Barbell Medicine use — Zhong 2017 and Benson 2010 on spontaneous resorption,
Lewis & O'Sullivan 2018 on reframing musculoskeletal care — and adds two they do not carry: Dubé
2024, finding no clinically meaningful difference between one physiotherapy session and many, and
Buzasi 2021, finding booster sessions moved only pain catastrophising. **Independent convergence on
a contested position, from a source with no clinical training and every incentive to be contrarian,
is the closest thing to corroboration this section has.** He overshoots in the same direction too —
"MRI scans are contraindicated" overstates "not routinely indicated" — and his
[injury-management video](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/7-essential-injury-management-tips-lifters.md)
rests on one narrative review plus an uncited **70% 1RM** connective-tissue threshold.

**On posture he beats Barbell Medicine on their own ground.** His
[four posture myths](Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/4-myths-about-improve-posture.md) cites Swain 2020,
a systematic review *of* systematic reviews finding no established causal link between spinal
posture and low back pain. Barbell Medicine assert the equivalent — "a specific squat form is not
correlated with injury or pain" — **with no citation, in an article citing eleven papers elsewhere**
([squat guide](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/squat-correctly-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md)).
The only place in this section where the qualified source is the worse-sourced one.

Nippard touches the edges and does well where he has a study — his
[knees-over-toes note](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/knees-go-over-toes.md) kills the rule with Fry 2003's
torque numbers (unrestricted 150 Nm knee / 28 Nm hip; restricted 117 / 302) — then attaches uncited
coaching numbers, **≤60 spinal-flexion reps per workout, 48 hours between sessions**
([crunches](Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/footnote-crunches-dangerous.md)), exactly as Barbell Medicine
attach uncited rehab doses. Two sources, one failure mode. House of Hypertrophy and the ISSN corpus
contribute nothing here.

**What would resolve any of this: a second qualified source.** Until one is ingested, the status of
every clinical verdict above is *unaudited* — internally consistent, mostly well cited where it can
be checked, never once tested against a rival reading.

## What the fifth source changed

Barbell Medicine arrived after the verdicts above were written. A fifth independent reading is the
best test this layer gets, so it is worth saying exactly what it moved and what it did not. The
short answer: **it moved almost nothing, and that is the point.** Two new verdicts, three
strengthened, one number in a table, and a long list of confirmations.

The full working record is in `sources/cross/parts/bm-revisions.md`.

### What it confirmed

**The rep band, volume, frequency and tempo verdicts are untouched.** They do not contest them.

**The exercise table grows from thirteen claims to sixteen** — push-ups matching the bench press
(Kikuchi 2017, and unusually a *trained* sample with direct thickness measurement), cable and
barbell preacher curls growing the biceps equally, and bench angle not changing pec hypertrophy.
Three of their studies confirm rows that were already there. Kikuchi deserves particular notice:
trained subjects, direct measurement, a clean null. It belongs higher in the confidence ranking
than several entries that preceded it.

**The high-protein ceiling verdict is strengthened.** Their top tier — 2.3–3.1 g/kg for lean
dieters — rests on Helms 2014 alone, which is *the same confound the existing verdict already
names*: trials comparing higher protein against inadequate protein, never 1.6 against 2.4. A second
source has now built a high-protein recommendation on that literature without noticing.

**Range of motion is the clearest corroboration in the library.** Their 2022 article concluded that
long-length partials "may produce similar results compared to full ROM" and advised against using
them for much of training — **two years before the trained-subject nulls**, and for the right
reason. Independent convergence from a source with no stake in that debate is real evidence, and it
lifts the table row to moderate-high. The same fact that makes Nippard's 2023 position look worst
makes this one look best.

### What it added

**Overtraining now has a verdict, and Barbell Medicine win it.** Grandou 2020 pooled 22 overload
studies: 10 reported zero performance decline and no reliable biomarker emerged. The failed attempts
carry the argument — three athletes squatting a true 1RM daily for 30 consecutive days all
*improved*; nine supervised weeks at ~90 weekly sets to failure produced nobody meeting criteria.
Only Fry 1994 showed a real drop, recovering in 2–8 weeks, short of what the syndrome requires.
Nippard cites that same paper as proof overtraining is real. Both also cite Saw 2016, which found
self-report beat hormones, resting heart rate and HRV — after which Nippard recommends HRV as "the
more objective marker."

**HRV gets its own verdict: do not use it to skip sessions.** Both head-to-head resistance-training
trials are null, and in Chen 2011 1RM returned to baseline at ~30 hours while HRV took ~60 — so HRV
would have pulled an athlete from a session they were ready for. Under 5% of the parameters
wearables report have been validated.

**Rest periods get a verdict, and nobody wins outright.** Barbell Medicine have the better single
trial (Schoenfeld 2016, trained men, 8 weeks, 3 minutes beating 1) and the worse literature review —
their hypertrophy case rests on six mostly-untrained studies and never engages the 2024 network
meta-analysis in which every band's credible intervals overlap. This is also the clearest case in
the library of a **recommendation stated before its own evidence**: the numbers appear in the
article's opening and survive a review that never tests them.

### What it complicated

**Daily protein: the number holds, the "convergence" rhetoric does not.** Four of five sources agree
on 1.6–1.8 g/kg. The fifth gives four figures across nineteen articles — 1.3–3.1, 1.6, 1.4–1.6, and
1.6 g/kg of *target* body weight — none citing the others. Only the 1.6 is traced correctly to
Morton 2018. And the convergence was always thinner than it looked: **Morton 2018 is doing the work
in every source that gets it right**, which is one meta-analysis wearing four hats.

**Effort: a dissent worth recording, not a revision.** They prescribe RPE 6–8 on compounds even for
hypertrophy and RPE 8–10 on isolation — below the library's 1–3 RIR floor, and a split nobody else
uses. No citation supports it in any of the three articles that state it, and their own publisher
contradicts it twice. The verdict stands. But the underlying logic is coherent as *programming
preference* rather than efficacy: compounds are expensive per unit of per-muscle stimulus, isolation
cheap. On Menno's terms — equate total reps by adding sets — RPE 6–8 on compounds is defensible.
Barbell Medicine never require that.

**RIR accuracy: right answer, wrong citation.** Their dedicated 4,831-word RPE article carries
exactly one reference — Helms 2017, *Self-Rated* Accuracy — for the claim that trainees become
accurate within weeks. Self-rating measures confidence. Their own separate article cites Halperin
correctly (2–5 rep under-prediction) and adds Malleron 2025, which found **no significant
trained-versus-untrained difference in RIR accuracy**. That is new to the library and cuts against
the intuition that experience fixes this — strengthening the existing conclusion that calibration
has to be deliberate and periodic rather than accumulated. The two articles are never reconciled.

**Creatine: a third inflated headline.** They cite Burke 2023 — the right meta-analysis — and report
it as "a couple of pounds or so in a year," converting a regional thickness result (0.10–0.16 cm,
pooled effect size 0.11, which the authors called possibly minimal) into a whole-body mass unit the
paper never measured. They report neither the effect size nor that verdict. They are still the least
enthusiastic of the three secondary sources, and they sell creatine. The ranking is now four-way:
**ISSN > House of Hypertrophy > Barbell Medicine > Burke's own reading.** Do not quote "one-third
faster", and do not quote "a couple of pounds a year" either. What survives: 3–5 g/day of
monohydrate, performance effect secure, roughly 1–2 lb of actual contractile tissue a year.

### One study, three readings

Chaves 2020 is now read three incompatible ways by three sources. Nippard cites it as confirming
incline pressing "with longitudinal data". Barbell Medicine cite it as a null showing bench angle
does not matter. House of Hypertrophy dismantles it on method — pec thickness rising 54–62% across
eight training sessions, and only 30 of 47 completers appearing in the hypertrophy data.

The verdict on incline work is unchanged and the reason is arithmetic rather than physiology: the
upper pec starts at ~15 cm² against 25–27 cm², so equal percentage growth looks like less. But
**Nippard's incline default rests on a study its other two readers treat as showing nothing**, and
one of them shows why it cannot be trusted in either direction. Filed with the Wolf sample-size
discrepancy in the defects list below: a study rendered differently across notes is a library
problem, not a literature problem.

## Defects this reconciliation found in the library itself

Reading four sources against each other surfaces errors that reading any one of them does not.
Recorded here rather than quietly corrected, so the correction is auditable.

- **The same study is written up with different sample sizes in different notes.** The Wolf
  lengthened-partials trial appears as 30 subjects in one note and 25 in another. One of them is
  wrong; both cannot be right, and the difference is large enough to matter for a within-subject
  design.
- **The rep band was inconsistent inside a single summary.** The House of Hypertrophy summary
  gave 6–35 in one section and 5–35 in another. Fixed; the settled figure is 6–35.
- **A minimalist-training figure was superseded by its own authors.** The 64% / 84% numbers held
  elsewhere in the library were corrected to 53% by the paper's authors. The cross-source section
  uses the corrected figure.
- **One study is read three incompatible ways.** Chaves 2020 is cited by Nippard as confirming
  incline pressing, by Barbell Medicine as a null, and dismantled on method by House of Hypertrophy.
- **The same paper is dated two different years.** Maeo's overhead triceps extension study appears
  as 2022 in one folder and 2023 in another — online 2022, issue 2023. One is enough.
- **Nippard's "3 to 35 reps" rests on a study he himself describes as mismatched.** The
  Schoenfeld 2014 comparison gave the low-rep group seven sets of three against three sets of ten.
  He reports the mismatch and does not draw the conclusion that it invalidates the equivalence.

## What this layer does not cover

**Anything with a second qualified opinion on the clinical questions.** Barbell Medicine filled the
injury and rehabilitation gap, but it filled it alone — there is nothing in this library to test it
against, so the clinical section adjudicates it against its own citations instead. That is weaker
than adjudication and the section says so.

**Anything about individuals.** Every verdict here is about group averages from trials averaging
around ten weeks. Several sources note real individual variation in response to volume, rep range
and frequency, and none of them can tell you which kind of responder you are. A verdict of "no
difference on average" is not a verdict that there is no difference for you.

**Long-term training.** Almost nothing in this library runs beyond six months. The questions
people actually care about — what works across years, what a decade of training does — have
essentially no controlled evidence, and no amount of reconciling short trials produces it.
