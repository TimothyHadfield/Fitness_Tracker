# Volume Load Is the Wrong Volume Metric — Count Hard Sets Instead

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Volume Load Does NOT Drive Muscle Hypertrophy (Science Overview)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QhQs4o0v1UU)) · 2021-09-20 · 10 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- **Volume load = sets × reps × load.** Three sets of 10 with 100 kg is a volume load of 3,000 kg. It is widely treated — including by researchers in published papers — as the thing that drives hypertrophy.
- His claim: volume load is **unrelated** to hypertrophy, and the appearance of a relationship comes from studies where volume load happened to co-vary with something that does matter.
- Four lines of evidence break the link: **low vs high load** (30% 1RM produces more volume load than 80% with equal growth), **volume load accumulated over time** (high-rep groups add far more volume load with no growth advantage), **failure vs non-failure** (failure gives more volume load with equal growth), and **volume-load-equated failure vs non-failure** (equal volume load, very unequal growth).
- The metric that does track hypertrophy is the **number of sets**, where a set means **5–35 reps taken within about 3 reps of failure**.

## The case that looks like it supports volume load
He opens with the two studies that would convince you if they were all that existed.

**Schoenfeld et al. (2014)** had 17 men with 1+ years of training do either **7 sets of 3** or **3 sets of 10** to failure per exercise, the unequal set counts existing specifically to equate volume load. After 8 weeks total volume load per exercise was similar between groups, and biceps growth was similar. Matched volume load, matched growth.

**Schoenfeld et al. (2016, rest intervals)** had 13 men with 6+ months of training do 3 sets to failure at an 8–12RM load, 3× per week for 8 weeks, resting either **3 minutes** or **1 minute**. Longer rest allows more reps to failure, so the long-rest group accumulated **greater volume load on every exercise**. It also produced greater growth in elbow flexors, triceps brachii, anterior quadriceps and vastus lateralis. More volume load, more growth.

Taken alone, that is a persuasive pair.

## The four findings that break it
**1. Load.** With everything else equal, reps to or near failure at **30% 1RM** build as much muscle as at **80% 1RM** — the position established in his rep-range work and supported by Lopez's network meta-analysis. But 30% 1RM means roughly 30 reps versus roughly 8–12, so the volume load at 30% is far higher. **Lim et al.** demonstrates it directly: 21 previously untrained men trained leg press and leg extension for 3 sets, 3× per week for 10 weeks, at either 30% or 80% 1RM to failure. The **30% group accumulated greater average volume load on both exercises**, yet slow-twitch and fast-twitch vastus lateralis fibre growth was similar. Higher volume load, no extra growth.

**2. Volume load accumulated over time.** **Schoenfeld et al. (2016, Asian J Sports Med)** followed men with 1.5–9 years of training doing 3 sets per exercise, 3× per week for 8 weeks, at either **25–35 reps** or **8–12 reps** to failure, both progressing load weekly. By week 8 the high-rep group had increased their total volume load by roughly **31,000 kg** relative to week 1; the moderate-rep group by roughly **10,700 kg** — a threefold difference in progression rate. Since both rep ranges are known to produce similar growth, your **rate of volume load increase does not track hypertrophy** either.

**3. Failure vs non-failure.** Training to failure produces more reps and therefore more volume load than stopping a couple short, yet the meta-analytic evidence (**Grgic et al.**) shows 1–3 reps from failure is about as effective as failure. **Santanielo et al.** shows it within-subject: men averaging 5 years of training did unilateral leg press and leg extension at 75% 1RM, twice per week for 10 weeks, one leg to failure and the other roughly 1–3 reps short. The failure condition accumulated **greater total volume load**; vastus lateralis growth was **similar**.

**4. The decisive one — equate volume load and growth still diverges.** **Lasevicius et al.** had untrained men do unilateral leg extension at 30% 1RM twice per week for 8 weeks. One leg did **3 sets to failure**. The other did roughly **7 sets stopping about 14 reps short of failure**, arranged so total volume load matched. Volume load ended up similar between conditions; **quadriceps growth was much greater in the failure leg.** Same volume load, radically different outcome. If volume load were the driver, this result is impossible.

## What replaces it, and how to use it
Sets — but not any sets. His working definition is a bout of **5 to 35 reps taken within 3 reps of failure**. A set of 30 reps at 6 RIR does not enter the tally. That filter is what makes "count your hard sets" a usable prescription rather than a tautology.

So: stop tracking tonnage as a proxy for stimulus — rising tonnage from higher reps at lighter loads means nothing for growth. Count **hard sets per muscle per week** instead, and treat load progression as confirmation that you are adapting rather than as the stimulus metric itself.

**Flagged:** the volume-load-over-time argument (finding 2) has a hole. He reports the two groups' volume load progression but not that study's own growth outcome — he substitutes "as we know from other data, both groups would likely have experienced similar hypertrophy." That is an assumption imported from elsewhere doing the work of a result. The other three lines, especially Lasevicius, carry the conclusion without it. Note also that Lim, Lasevicius and Santanielo are individually small; the argument's strength is convergence across designs, not any single study.
