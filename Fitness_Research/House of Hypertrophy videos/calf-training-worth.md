# Calves Grow About as Well as Everything Else

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Is Calf Training Worth It?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IstKq7yDpik)) · 2025-03-10 · 8 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- The "calves are hard to grow" claim rests largely on a 2004 muscle protein synthesis study — and MPS does not reliably predict long-term growth.
- Pooling calf hypertrophy across five research groups worldwide, calves typically grow **6–20%**, which is what we see for muscles generally.
- Where calf growth looks lower, it is usually the **soleus** dragging the average down — gastrocnemius growth repeatedly lands close to what the same groups report for quads, hamstrings, biceps or pecs.
- Two case studies in highly trained calf-trained individuals still showed meaningful growth, including 7–23% thickness increases in an 18-year bodybuilder.
- Verdict: calves respond robustly and are worth training. Whether soleus specifically grows less than gastrocnemius is genuinely unsettled.

## The evidence people cite against calf training
A 2004 paper found that after nine sets of calf raises, soleus muscle protein synthesis increases were substantially lower than what is commonly reported for the quadriceps. The authors offered two explanations: the calves are effectively chronically trained by standing and walking, leaving less headroom; and they — the soleus especially — are heavily slow-twitch.

He rejects both as sufficient grounds:

- **MPS does not always predict growth.** This is the load-bearing objection and it is correct.
- **Slow-twitch fibres are not growth-proof.** There are multiple instances of slow-twitch fibres growing more than 10% in 6–12 weeks, including in trained individuals.
- **Gastrocnemius isn't soleus.** The gastrocnemius has a much more even slow/fast split.

## What the growth data actually show
He walks through five research groups. A methodological point he makes first is worth keeping: **absolute** change (mm of thickness) and **relative** change (%) can diverge when starting sizes differ, so both matter.

- **Japan:** calves increased roughly **2–6%**, which the researchers noted was lower than their own triceps and hamstring findings. But splitting by region shows it was the **soleus** that lagged; lateral and medial gastrocnemius growth was not far off their hamstring head data in either absolute or relative terms.
- **USA:** trained subjects training quads and calves in the same study — gastrocnemius absolute and relative changes close to the quad gains.
- **Brazil:** soleus grew well here, with values similar to what the same group saw for biceps and brachialis in a comparable study.
- **Norway:** medial gastrocnemius growth similar to their quadriceps findings in a study with the *same subjects*.
- **Germany:** medial and lateral gastrocnemius similar to their pectoralis major findings in comparable subjects.

Folding in studies he doesn't itemise, he puts typical calf growth at **6–20%** — indistinguishable from the general hypertrophy literature.

## Two case studies in trained calves
1. A lifter with 10+ years of experience, accustomed to calf training: 3 sets of straight-leg calf raises, 3×/week, 6 weeks. One leg trained full ROM, the other alternated lengthened and shortened partials each session. The alternating leg **didn't really grow**; the full-ROM leg gained **4.14% medial gastrocnemius CSA** — notable given the timeframe and training age. He flags that measurement error was not reported, so the increase could be partly noise.
2. A competitive bodybuilder with 18 years of experience: 4 sets of straight-leg calf raises 5×/week **plus one hour of intense calf stretching six times per week**, for 12 weeks. Thickness increases across calf regions ranged **7% to 23%**, with variability and fluctuation in the measures — he still reads it as genuine growth.

## Soleus vs gastrocnemius
Three studies point to lower soleus growth. One found soleus growing numerically more in absolute terms and similarly in relative terms, and four others found similar or better soleus growth. His verdict is "mixed," which is the honest one.

## Flag
Nearly every comparison here is **between studies** — calf data from one paper set against quad or pec data from a different paper by the same group. He notes the need for direct within-study comparisons, but the whole argument still rests on cross-study inference, and the two case studies are n=1 each.
