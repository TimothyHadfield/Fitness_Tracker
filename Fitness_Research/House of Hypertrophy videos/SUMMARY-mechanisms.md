# Mechanisms

Twenty-six notes on the physiology of hypertrophy — what the stimulus actually is, what happens
inside the fibre, and why most of the mechanisms people train for turn out not to be mechanisms at
all. This is the channel's intellectual centre and its most-cited material, and it is also where the
citation problem is worst: **seven of these twenty-six notes rest on videos with no retrievable
reference list**, including the 40-minute, 87-study video that carries the whole tension argument.
Where that is true it is said in place, because the reader cannot check those claims.

---

## The current best model, in one paragraph

**Mechanical tension is the stimulus. Everything else is a byproduct or a bystander.** A muscle fibre
generates force actively (myosin pulling actin) and has force imposed on it passively (titin and the
extracellular matrix under stretch); mechanosensors in and around the fibre convert that force into a
signalling cascade that ends in more contractile protein. To grow a muscle you want as many fibres as
possible recruited, producing high individual forces, for a decent duration, all inside one time
window. That definition is what makes a light set to failure and a heavy set roughly equivalent, and
what rules out both a 1RM (duration too short) and a 50-rep set (central fatigue blocks fast-twitch
recruitment). The other two members of the classic triad — metabolic stress and muscle damage — have
been tested repeatedly by raising them deliberately, and growth does not follow. The honest residual,
which he keeps and which this summary keeps, is that damage and metabolites may contribute up to some
low threshold that every training style already crosses, in which case chasing more of them would
look exactly like it does: useless.

---

## The reading rule for this whole domain

Two distinctions do most of the work, and they are the reason this file keeps repeating itself.

**Signalling is not size.** Almost every positive mechanism finding here is a measurement of a
protein complex activating over hours, or of muscle protein synthesis over a day. Almost every
negative finding — the ones that killed the pump, lactate, damage and hormone spikes — is a
measurement of muscle size over eight to sixteen weeks. That asymmetry is not an accident of the
literature; it is why the negative claims in this domain are better evidenced than the positive one.
[Damas 2016 is the cleanest illustration](biggest-muscle-building-myth-ever-microtears.md): the
myofibrillar protein synthesis spike after a first session did **not** correlate with eventual
hypertrophy, and only began to correlate weeks later once damage had subsided. An acute anabolic
signal can be pointed at repair rather than growth. Treat every "X raises mTOR / MPS" claim as
unproven until someone measures a limb.

**Rodents are not lifters.** The four studies that carry the entire positive case for tension are
mechanistic preparations, and at least one of them is explicitly a rat study —
[the stretching note names Rindom 2019 as a rat mTORC1 dose-response experiment](stretching-builds-muscle-similar-lifting-weights.md),
which is almost certainly the same "2019 Danish study" that
[the 87-studies note describes without naming a species](most-important-muscle-building-stimulus-87-studies.md).
Hyperplasia is quail, rat, mouse and crab. Myofibril thickness and splitting is chicken and mouse.
Lactate's pro-growth mechanism is cell culture plus one rat study that fed caffeine alongside the
lactate. Where a claim below rests on animal or in vitro work, it says so.

**And check whether there is a list at all.** Of these twenty-six, the ones citing on screen only —
so unverifiable — are the
[87-studies mechanisms video](most-important-muscle-building-stimulus-87-studies.md),
[hyperplasia](gain-more-muscle-fibers-training.md),
[sarcoplasmic hypertrophy](science-sarcoplasmic-hypertrophy.md),
[the 33-study fibre tally](sarcoplasmic-vs-myofibrillar-hypertrophy-packing-33-studies.md),
[myofibrillar packing](stronger-bigger-muscles-myofibrillar-packing-hypertrophy.md),
[the 2024 myths video](90-still-believe-builds-more-muscle.md) and
[the calf video](calf-training-worth.md). Those are disproportionately his longest and most confident
videos. The claims may be sound; they are not checkable.

## Mechanical tension

### The case is an elimination argument, and he throws out his own side's evidence first

[The 87-studies video](most-important-muscle-building-stimulus-87-studies.md) sets an evidential bar
before naming a winner: a stimulus qualifies if there is a plausible mechanism, if protocols raising
it produce more growth, and if it can be isolated from the other candidates. He then applies the
third criterion to the classic animal literature usually quoted **for** tension — gastrocnemius
tenotomy overloading the soleus, limbs immobilised stretched versus shortened, weights hung from
chicken wings, spring-loaded bars stretching chicken muscle — and discards all of it, because the
electrical signalling, metabolite accumulation and damage that accompany tension are confounded with
it. Discarding the evidence he grew up on before touching anyone else's is the single most credible
move in this domain.

What replaces it is four newer mechanistic studies. A 2019 Danish group blocked active force
production while leaving electrical input intact and found the key growth-signalling complex did not
activate; the same group equated peak tension between eccentric contractions and pure passive stretch
and found **equal** activation, implying total tension registers rather than its active or passive
origin. A 2008 US study raised active force at constant electrical input and saw signalling rise. A
2001 Canadian study found peak tension linearly related to activation of a hypertrophy-signalling
protein. **All four measure signalling over hours, not size over months, and none of the four is
verifiable — this video has no reference list.** The human-level backing is that protocols
*presumed* to equate or elevate tension produce equal or greater growth, and "presumed" is doing real
work, because tension cannot be measured inside a training human. Mechanism is measured in cells,
outcome is measured in people, and nothing measures both at once.

### Tension is not load, and it is not time under tension

Two corrections follow, and they are the practically useful part.

**Load is a forcing function, not the stimulus.** Taking a light set to failure produces high overall
mechanical tension, because Henneman's size principle means fast-twitch fibres get recruited late
under light loads and immediately under heavy ones — so the final reps before failure look similar
either way. **This is why proximity to failure matters more than load**, and why the whole 5–35 rep
band works.

**Time under tension is not mechanical tension**, and
[the 2024 myths video](90-still-believe-builds-more-muscle.md) makes this his cleanest correction. A
long slow set spends most of its duration far from failure. What matters is time under *high*
tension, which is reached in the last reps of a hard set regardless of tempo. Practical version:
**there is no set-duration target to hit and no reason to prolong a set; get to or near failure
anywhere in roughly 6–35 reps.** That video is also unreferenced — it names no authors for any of its
six studies.

### Two experiments that stretch the definition past external load

[Counts's no-load curls](biceps-curls-no-weight-build-muscle-optimally.md) — 13 untrained subjects, 6
weeks, one arm doing 4×20 maximal voluntary squeezes with **no weight at all** and 30 s rest, the
other 4×8–12 dumbbell curls at 70% 1RM with 90 s rest — produced similar biceps thickness gains at
50%, 60% and 70% of upper-arm length. He clears the obvious objection with Hubal's 585-subject
unilateral data (trained arm +18.9% CSA, untrained arm +0.2%), so cross-education cannot explain it.
The honest reading is not "throw away your dumbbells": subjects reported the no-load sets as *harder
and more uncomfortable*, which is precisely the problem external load solves.

[Warneke 2023](stretching-builds-muscle-similar-lifting-weights.md) — 69 subjects with some lifting
experience, 6 weeks — found **one hour of static calf stretching every day, held at 7–8/10 on a pain
scale with a device**, matched 5×10–12 calf presses three times weekly for gastrocnemius thickness
and straight-leg isometric strength, with non-significant between-group differences. Passive tension
is still tension. The caveats are large and he supplies them: the strength test was straight-leg
isometric, which suited the stretch group's training; "non-significant" across roughly two dozen per
arm is not equivalence; calves are undertrained even in trained lifters; and prior human work with
stretches held up to two minutes found nothing (Nunes 2020 review), while the animal work showing
+141% CSA (Alway 1989) used stretches held up to 24 hours. He pitches the use case as **rehab for
people who cannot train**, not as training advice.

[The mind-muscle connection note](mind-muscle-connection-help-build-more-muscle.md) sits alongside
these: Schoenfeld's 27 untrained men, internal focus ("squeeze the muscle") versus external ("get the
weight up"), 4×8–12 to failure on barbell curl and leg extension, produced **significantly more elbow
flexor thickness with an internal focus and no difference at all for the quads**. Calatayud's EMG
work suggests the cue only takes at 20–60% 1RM and does nothing at 80%. Practical payoff: **use an
internal focus on single-joint upper-body work at moderate loads; don't use it on lifts you want to
get strong at**, since it needs lighter loads to work.

### Practical payoff of the tension model

Modest, and he says so himself in [the 2025 review video](much-muscle-gain-causes-growth.md), in what
is the most self-aware paragraph on the channel: **people hypothesise that some style maximises
tension, rebuild their programme around it, and ignore outcome studies showing that variable doesn't
matter. Prefer growth-outcome research over speculation about tension.** The residue that survives
that rule is small: train close to failure, in a broad rep range, and stop optimising tempo. The one
genuinely speculative extension he offers — that long-length training stacks high active *and*
passive force, possibly read by different mechanosensors, with calcium stiffening titin so passive
force is greater when active tension is present — he labels "100% not a confirmed definitive fact",
and it has no outcome test attached.

## Metabolic stress: dead as a driver, alive as a threshold

### Lactate and the burn

[The lactate video](huge-muscle-building-myth-22-studies.md) clears three background errors first —
humans make **lactate, not lactic acid**; lactate does not cause fatigue and is a usable fuel for
muscle and brain; and lactate alone does not cause the burn (Pollak's infusion experiment produced
pain only when lactate, ATP and hydrogen ions were infused **together**, never individually).

The pro-lactate hypertrophy evidence is **in vitro, animal or hormonal, with no human growth
outcomes**: a 2022 review of lactate activating satellite cells in a dish and in rodents, and one rat
treadmill study that fed lactate *and caffeine* and so cannot separate them. The remaining pillar is
the acute testosterone rise, which is a dead end for reasons covered below.

The disconfirmations need no mechanism, which is why they are persuasive. **Infusing lactate into
humans during leg extension training raised blood and muscle lactate without raising anabolic
signalling above saline** (Liegnell 2020) — and he volunteers the design flaw that favours him, since
the infusion went into the arm rather than the working leg. **400 m running produces muscle lactate
comparable to five sets of four lower-body exercises**, and repeated sprint cycling matches three
sets of curls to failure; neither builds meaningful muscle. One minute of rest raises lactate more
than five minutes, yet myofibrillar protein synthesis was largely greater after the five. Cuffing
between sets of leg extensions raised lactate and produced **the same** quadriceps growth at eight
weeks. And cuffing for 3–5 minutes *after* sets, to trap metabolites, added nothing in two studies
and **reduced** growth in women (Dankel 2016). Lactate also does not sit still — it is shuttled out
as fuel — so measuring it in the muscle is measuring a flow, not a pool.

### The pump

[The pump video](pump-best-indicator-hypertrophy-true-false.md) separates measured from inferred with
unusual care. The apparently supportive literature — drop sets beating straight sets (Varović, Fink),
30 s rest beating 3 min on arms (Fink), constant-tension partial skull crushers beating full ROM
(Goto) — comes from studies where **the pump was never measured**, and he flags this each time. The
only direct test is Hirono: acute quadriceps swelling after session one correlated with six-week
hypertrophy at Spearman's rho of just **0.4–0.6**.

Against it: 30% 1RM gives a far bigger acute CSA increase than 80% (Jenkins) with similar long-term
growth; BFR at 20–40% 1RM gives an enormous pump and matches 70–90% loading (Lixandrão's
meta-analysis); 2.5+ minutes rest beats 90 seconds on compounds despite a smaller pump; and for
constant tension it is three studies against one (Pinto, two Tanimoto studies) once you look past
Goto. [The 2024 myths video](90-still-believe-builds-more-muscle.md) adds an FST-7-style trial in
trained lifters where **arm growth was minimal and non-significant, with some subjects slightly
losing size**.

**Practical payoff: the pump is a byproduct, not a target — but it is a legitimate regional
recruitment cue.** If changing your pull-up technique to hold scapular retraction produces a bigger
upper-back pump, that is reasonable evidence the change recruited the upper back. It also aids
enjoyment and adherence, and most pump-chasing techniques don't cost you anything, so chasing it is
harmless rather than wrong.

### Blood flow restriction, the hard case

BFR is the strongest argument metabolic stress has, and he handles it properly. He **accepts** that
light-load BFR matches heavy training for hypertrophy, then rejects the inference, because the belief
that it must work through metabolites rests on equating tension with load. Light loads to failure
generate high tension; the metabolites are plausibly a byproduct of the effort needed to get there.
The awkward counter-evidence is three studies finding BFR produces **preferential slow-twitch
growth**, which tension should not predict — and his rebuttal is that all three used frequencies
(seven consecutive days; five days a week; untrained subjects to failure three times weekly) that
would leave central fatigue present, suppressing fast-twitch recruitment rather than enhancing
slow-twitch growth. A 2021 Australian study in well-trained subjects, BFR three times weekly for nine
weeks, found no preferential slow-twitch growth. **This is a hypothesis, not a test** — nobody has
manipulated fatigue and watched the fibre-type effect disappear.

### Where he pushes back on his own conclusion

[The 2025 review video](much-muscle-gain-causes-growth.md) is the least tidy and probably the most
honest treatment. Separating metabolic stress from tension in humans is extremely difficult; there
are **over 4,200 metabolites in human serum** and most are unexplored; a low threshold that every
training style already exceeds cannot be ruled out; extreme levels have never been tested in humans
(the in vitro work used continuous high lactate exposure for two to six hours, sometimes days); and
**capillarisation** is a plausible indirect route by which high-metabolic-stress styles might help
long-term. **Practical payoff: none. There is nothing to add to a programme and nothing to remove
from one. The burn is a sensation, not a target.**

## Muscle damage: the mechanism that got demoted twice

### Microtears

[The microtears video](biggest-muscle-building-myth-ever-microtears.md) dismantles the
repair-it-bigger story using **Schoenfeld's own 2012 review** as the strongest statement of the case,
answering its four proposed pathways: inflammation (conflicting evidence, and the relevant events
occur without damage); satellite cells donating myonuclei (training that causes no damage still
activates satellite cells, and so does aerobic training — damage-driven activation may serve repair
of fibre and connective tissue rather than adding myonuclei); IGF-1 potentiation (inconsistent, and
acute hormone spikes don't predict growth anyway); and post-damage swelling activating osmoreceptors
(same contrary evidence as the pump). Note what that means for satellite cells: **this library's only
sustained treatment of them is as a rebutted step in the damage argument**, not as a mechanism in
their own right.

The outcome data is the strongest part. A **2011 US study of 14 untrained men** gave one group a
three-week ramp-in before an eight-week eccentric cycle-ergometer programme and sent the other in
cold; the pre-trained group had little damage or soreness throughout, the naive group had much more,
and **quadriceps hypertrophy was comparable**. **Damas 2016** found the MPS spike correlating with
growth only after damage attenuated. **Foley 1999** — six men, five sets of ten eccentric curls at
110% of concentric 1RM — found muscle volume finishing roughly **10% below baseline** and staying
smaller for weeks, plausibly from destruction of fibre subpopulations. Excessive damage is
net-negative. And the indirect checks all point the same way: higher reps, short rest and
eccentric-only work all raise damage without raising growth, and short rest lowers it.

He is also careful about the antioxidant argument — vitamin C and E sometimes reduce both damage and
long-term growth, which is read by some as proof damage matters. His alternative is that antioxidants
may blunt **capillary adaptations** supporting long-term growth, and that not all studies find either
reduction. That is the clearest passage in the domain separating "consistent with" from "evidence
for."

### The repeated bout effect

[The RBE note](most-unappreciated-muscle-building-principle.md) closes the case from the other side
and is the best evidence in this domain that damage is not load-bearing. A 2020 Greek study put
untrained men through deliberately brutal work — maximal eccentric-only knee extensions on an
isokinetic dynamometer, **15 reps × 5 sets, once weekly for 10 weeks** — with pain-free ROM, DOMS and
concentric/eccentric/isometric strength measured at 1, 2, 3 and 5 days after every single session,
plus creatine kinase and C-reactive protein. **By weeks 8–10 there was effectively no soreness,
eccentric strength barely dropped, and CK and CRP had fallen continuously.** Damage vanished; growth
continued. He adds two honest details: recovery did not improve monotonically (isometric recovery was
worse in week 8 than week 7), and **roughly 2–4 of the subjects still showed notable fatigue in week
10** — individual variation in recovery is real.

**Practical payoff, and it is the only real one damage produces: your recovery capacity is a
trainable adaptation, not a fixed ceiling.** Exercises, volumes and frequencies that feel
unrecoverable in month one may be fine by month three, which is an argument for introducing
stretch-position work, higher frequencies and higher volumes gradually rather than concluding early
that you cannot tolerate them. He is explicit that this does **not** mean the lost soreness was
driving growth. It is also worth naming what this rests on: one study, in untrained men, on a
protocol about as far from a gym session as it is possible to get, measuring damage markers and not
hypertrophy. The four training applications are extrapolations and he phrases them as such.

### Where the notes disagree with each other

This is the one place his position visibly moved and then moved back.
[The 2022 microtears video](biggest-muscle-building-myth-ever-microtears.md) treats the question as
settled. [The 87-studies video from the same year](most-important-muscle-building-stimulus-87-studies.md)
explicitly refuses to rule out a low-threshold contribution.
[The 2024 myths video](90-still-believe-builds-more-muscle.md) says damage "may play some role, or
none". [The 2025 review video](much-muscle-gain-causes-growth.md) notes the review it covers doesn't
address damage at all and calls the evidence merely "unpromising". And Schoenfeld, whose reviews
supply the demolished case in both directions, has stayed more agnostic in interviews than any of
these videos. **Treat "tension is the sole driver" as this channel's reading, not a consensus
position.**

## Hormones: four notes, one verdict, four different questions

The hormone material is the best-organised part of the domain because he keeps four questions apart
that the fitness industry routinely collapses into one.

**Acute spikes after a session.**
[The spike video](spike-testosterone-growth-hormone-naturally-via-lifting.md) gives six correlation
studies: two positive (Mangine, 33 trained men, testosterone spikes predicting quad growth; McCall,
11 experienced men, GH spikes correlating with biceps *fibre* CSA but **not** whole-biceps CSA, with
IGF-1 barely moving despite GH supposedly acting through it) and four null (West & Phillips n=56,
Fink n=20, Mitchell n=23, Morton n=49). Four of six null, and the two positives disagree about which
hormone matters. The interventional designs are the arm trials: **Ronnestad** (n=11, leg work
*before* the curls) found greater elbow-flexor CSA in the high-hormone arm at two distal sites only;
**West** (n=12, leg work *after*) found no difference anywhere. He builds an exercise-order
explanation from Spiering's acute androgen-receptor data and then dismantles it himself — receptor
content down-regulates after a spiking session (Ratamess), and three exercise-order studies (Simao,
Spineti twice) found arm growth similar regardless of order, if anything favouring isolation first.
Two independent lines finish it: **free-weight squats and benches spike free testosterone more than
Smith-machine versions and grow the same** (Schwanbeck, 8 weeks), and **short rest spikes GH more and
grows less**. That last one is the cleanest disconfirmation in the domain — the variable that
maximises the spike is the variable that builds less muscle.
[The 2025 review video](much-muscle-gain-causes-growth.md) adds Wilkinson 2006, where fibre CSA rose
with **no** post-workout systemic hormone change at all, and supplies the unglamorous explanation:
training-induced spikes sit inside normal daily variation, are orders of magnitude below exogenous
dosing, and resolve within 90 minutes.

**Resting levels over a training block.**
[The resting-testosterone note](lifting-weights-not-increase-testosterone.md) pools two
meta-analyses (Potter, nine RCTs in untrained men 18–75; Hayes & Elliott, 17 trials in men 60+) and
six individual trials in trained men, and finds **no chronic rise in total, free or bioavailable
testosterone**. The most striking case is three Danish trials in men aged 60–78 with **bioavailable
testosterone averaging around 129 ng/dL** — the population where an effect should be easiest to
find — where the placebo-gel resistance-training arm saw nothing. Two dissenters exist and he
presents both: Izquierdo's non-failure group (6×3 at a 6RM load) raised resting total testosterone
where the matched failure group did not, and Kraemer found resting *free* testosterone rising in
young men only, unreplicated by Petrella's comparable design. He also draws the boundary properly:
nearly all this evidence used full-body splits, loads below 80% 1RM, and 12 weeks or less.

**Between people.**
[The between-person note](myth-testosterone-muscle-hypertrophy.md) covers McCall (n=11), Morton (49
trained men, backward-elimination regression across a hormone panel, plus **intramuscular free
testosterone** compared between high and low responders) and Mobley (67 untrained men sorted into
low/moderate/high responders). **Resting testosterone does not separate high from low responders, and
neither does intramuscular free testosterone** — the mechanism's best hiding place, checked and
empty. Every responder tier contained the full spread; one high responder had low testosterone, one
low responder had high.

**Within a person.**
[The raising-testosterone note](science-increasing-testosterone-muscle-strength.md) is the one that
says yes, and says it precisely: raising your own testosterone works, but **additively, not
synergistically**. Bhasin 1996 (NEJM), four groups: testosterone-only +6.7% quadriceps CSA,
weights-only +5.4%, sum 12.1%, combined 13.7%. Casaburi (100 mg/wk) gave +1.07 / +0.49 / +1.41 kg leg
lean mass; Chasland (cream) gave +0.7 / +0.7 / +1.4 kg. Large changes do a great deal alone —
hypogonadal men on 100 mg/wk for 10 weeks **with no training** gained 5 kg fat-free mass, +12%
triceps CSA, +8% quadriceps CSA, +22% bench and +45% squat. Small changes do something small:
Finkelstein's goserelin-suppression dose-response design, the cleanest available, puts a rise to
~805 ng/dL at **2–4%** for thigh muscle area and leg press. **Practical payoff: natural levers on
testosterone — body fat, sleep, deficiencies, stress — are real but should not be expected to change
your physique**, since the whole natural range is worth a few percent. He also reserves two ways
supraphysiological levels might be genuinely synergistic long-term — better work capacity and
recovery, and far greater **myonuclear addition** raising the ceiling on fibre size — and labels both
as theory with no outcome data. That is this library's only substantive mention of myonuclei; **there
is no coverage of muscle memory or myonuclear permanence anywhere in these 26 notes.**

**Androgen receptors, the successor claim.**
[The AR note](hidden-key-building-muscle-legit.md) is a model of how to handle a fashionable
mechanism. Morton (49 trained men, 12 weeks) found high responders had greater AR content with linear
relationships to lean mass and both fibre types; **Mobley (67 untrained men) found AR content
identical across responder tiers**, and almost nobody citing the first study mentions the second.
Ahtiainen (21 weeks) and Mitchell (16 weeks) found the *change* in AR content correlating with
growth — a different association again. All human data here is associational and between-person; the
only causal evidence is animal work showing that **blocking** ARs reduces training-induced
hypertrophy, which supports a role for receptors, not for increasing them. And there is no
established way to raise AR content: the L-carnitine L-tartrate claim rests on **one 10-subject,
21-day study** (Kraemer 2006), and he draws the right analogy — D-aspartic acid's first human study
found a 42% testosterone rise and **six subsequent studies failed to replicate it**, one finding a
decrease. The intermittent-fasting citation circulating online measured testosterone, not AR content;
the caffeine one **was conducted on rats**. His own verdict: "there isn't a proactive cool takeaway
from this video." **Practical payoff of the entire hormone block: nothing to do. Don't design
sessions around spikes, don't test your testosterone hoping it explains your results, and don't buy
anything sold as an AR upregulator.**

## What kind of growth: the inside of the fibre

### The taxonomy

[The definitional video](science-muscle-growth-actually.md) names five routes to a larger muscle:
**myofibrillar hypertrophy** (myofibrils and sarcoplasm grow at the same rate), **sarcoplasmic
hypertrophy** (sarcoplasm outpaces), **myofibrillar packing** (myofibrils outpace), **hyperplasia**
(more fibres), and **increased muscle length**. The first three are one continuum defined only by a
ratio — sarcoplasmic hypertrophy does **not** mean the myofibrils shrank. He flags a subtlety most
explanations skip: packing need not increase fibre size at all. He also flags that hyperplasia in
humans is "far from clear" and that achievable length increase may be limited, so the three fibre-CSA
routes are what most likely drive real growth. Nothing in that video is connected to an outcome
measure, which is appropriate for a definitional piece and worth remembering when it gets quoted.

### The tally, and a genuine reversal

[The 33-study tally](sarcoplasmic-vs-myofibrillar-hypertrophy-packing-33-studies.md) counts **15
studies finding myofibrillar hypertrophy, 11 finding packing, 7 finding sarcoplasmic hypertrophy**,
split across direct electron-microscopy assessment (15), specific tension (12) and cross-sectional
comparison (6). The striking cell is a zero: **no specific-tension study has ever found the decrease
sarcoplasmic hypertrophy predicts.** Load separates nothing, because almost every study used moderate
loads and moderate loads produced all three outcomes. The only candidate differentiator is
**volume**, and it rests on a slight majority of five studies, one of which (Penman 1969) ran no
statistics and reported no growth measurement, with one cross-sectional finding pointing the other
way.

[The dedicated sarcoplasmic video](science-sarcoplasmic-hypertrophy.md) works through six candidate
drivers: sarcoplasmic reticulum and t-tubules have **never been measured** after resistance training;
mitochondria are probably ruled out (relative space doesn't rise and may fall); glycogen is
contradictory and, if it is the driver, makes this form of growth transient and diet-dependent; and
**creatine phosphate and sarcoplasmic proteins look most promising, each on a single study**. It
answers the "it's just swelling" objection credibly (a five-month triceps study measured 4–6 days
post-session; sarcoplasmic protein increases aren't a swelling phenomenon; the pattern appears in
long-trained bodybuilders). But it never asks whether a lifter should *want* proportionally less
contractile tissue, and it frames 21+ weekly sets as a lever for producing it — a threshold applied
retrospectively to five studies, not one any study tested.

A year later [the dense-vs-puffy video](low-reps-dense-muscles-high-reps-puffy.md) deflates the whole
construct, and this is a real reversal of emphasis. The folk case gets taken apart: Meijer 2015 found
bodybuilder fibre specific tension 62% below power athletes and 41% below untrained, but **nine of
the twelve bodybuilders admitted steroid use** and it is a single-time-point comparison that cannot
establish causation; Ikegawa's elite comparison has the same problem; Burd 2010's greater
sarcoplasmic protein synthesis at 30% 1RM **reverses direction at 4 hours** and is an acute measure
regardless. Then the evidence against: **every long-term study directly finding myofibrillar
hypertrophy trained at roughly 7–12 reps** — the exact range the "puffy" story blames. The 1RM gap
between 8–12 and 25–35 reps (Schoenfeld 2015) is not evidence of a different growth type, because a
1RM is a skill and technique differs by load (Król 2017, Larsen 2022); the better test is isometric
strength, and a 2017 meta-analysis found **similar isometric gains between 8–12 and 20–35 reps**. And
the magnitude point that ends the argument: in MacDougall's data **triceps fibre CSA rose 23% while
myofibrillar relative space fell 2%**. A 2% relative shift is not a visibly puffier muscle. **The
later note is right and should override the earlier one.**

[The muscle-confusion study](muscle-confusion-produces-sarcoplasmic-hypertrophy.md) is the one new
data point since. Fox et al., **20 trained men, within-subject**, one leg on a constant programme
(4×9–12 to failure on unilateral leg press and leg extension) and the other randomly varying reps,
sets, contraction type or rest each session, twice weekly for 8 weeks. **Whole-muscle and fibre CSA
rose equally — muscle confusion adds nothing and subtracts nothing.** Inside the fibre, both legs
started at ~70% myofibrillar space; the constant leg held at ~70%, the varied leg fell to ~61%. He
then argues against his own finding twice: the paper **contradicts itself** (phalloidin staining
agrees with the actin assay but myosin heavy chain fell in *both* legs, which would make both
conditions sarcoplasmic), and the varied leg — which included eccentric-only sets at 110% of normal
load — may simply have been **swollen** at measurement three days post-session, having never
developed a full repeated bout effect. Swelling was not measured, so the question is unresolvable.
The generalisable point he draws is the valuable one: **two protocols producing identical
whole-muscle growth may not be producing the same tissue, and virtually the entire hypertrophy
literature is blind to this.**

### How a myofibril actually grows

[The myofibril video](stronger-bigger-muscles-myofibrillar-packing-hypertrophy.md) is blunt that
**there appear to be no human studies examining the myofibril level at all**, and says so twice.
Myofibrils grow longer (sarcomeres added in series), thicker (sarcomeres in parallel) or more
numerous (splitting). Thickness and number rest entirely on two bodies of animal work: chicken
wing-stretch overload giving +55% fibre CSA, +36% myofibril diameter and **45% of myofibrils showing
splitting** (Ashmore and Summers), and mouse data linking myofibril diameter and number to fibre CSA
with a critical-size splitting hypothesis (Goldspink). **There is no evidence that entirely new
myofibrils are manufactured.** None of it is verifiable — no reference list.

Length is the one route that transfers, via fascicle length as the human proxy. It responds to fuller
or long-length-biased range of motion (two "McMahon" studies: 0–90° beat 0–50°, and a long-length
40–90° partial beat a short-length 0–50° partial on knee extensions) and responds more strongly to
**eccentric-overload training** (Reeves and several others). Two studies suggest the window may close
after **5–8 weeks in untrained people** (Baroni at week 8, Blazevich at week 5), though nobody has
tested a trained lifter new to eccentric overload. **Practical payoff — the only one this whole
section produces: do some work through a full range or biased to long lengths, use eccentric-overload
methods if you have the equipment, and front-load them rather than assuming you can add them any
time.**

## Hyperplasia

[The fibre-number video](gain-more-muscle-fibers-training.md) is the cleanest example in the library
of animal data being strong and human data being unobtainable. **In animals, an ~11.6% fibre-number
increase under weight-training protocols, ~21% under stretch, ~5.4% under synergist ablation** — and
the weight-training subset was five studies, *all five* positive, including both that used direct
counting. He deliberately down-weights the stretch and ablation protocols despite their bigger
numbers, because only the weight-training rat work (15 sets of 15 reps at 65–75% 1RM in a custom rig)
is remotely analogous.

In humans there is no answer and probably cannot be one. Direct counting requires removing the whole
muscle, and **a human biceps is estimated at 198,000–419,000 fibres**. Cross-sectional counting has
not been done either, and it has a specific failure mode: an increase in pennation angle or fibre
length brings more fibres through the plane of a slice without any new fibres existing, and both
change with training — so the method is biased toward false positives. Every human study therefore
measures something else and infers backwards. Four cross-sectional studies found trained subjects
with bigger whole muscles and same-sized fibres (implying more fibres), which he kills by noting a
biopsy samples one site and unsampled fibres elsewhere could be larger; a fifth found **fibre
splitting in powerlifters' vastus lateralis and trapezius, absent in controls**. Against: two studies
found bigger muscles *and* bigger fibres, and the only longitudinal attempt (8 trained men, 12 weeks)
found estimated fibre number unchanged — though removing two outliers left six subjects showing an
increase, which he reports and then guts himself. Two hypotheses for why a fibre would split at all
are offered as speculation: abnormal regeneration after damage, and a fibre-size threshold set by
oxygen diffusion distance to the innermost mitochondria (supported in mice, rats and crabs). Steroids
may make hyperplasia more likely and this cannot currently be established either way. **Practical
payoff: none whatsoever. No training variable here is shown to control it, and he lays out three
defensible reader positions and endorses none.**

## Fibre types, and whether some muscles are special

Fibre type appears in this domain three times and never as a programming variable. Henneman's size
principle underwrites the load-equivalence argument
([87 studies](most-important-muscle-building-stimulus-87-studies.md)); the BFR slow-twitch findings
are the one place fibre-type-specific growth is claimed, on an untested rebuttal; and
[the calf note](calf-training-worth.md) uses it to answer the "calves are genetic" claim. That case
rests largely on a 2004 muscle protein synthesis study, **and MPS does not reliably predict long-term
growth** — the same discipline applied here as everywhere else. He adds that slow-twitch fibres are
not growth-proof (multiple instances of >10% growth in 6–12 weeks, including in trained people) and
that the gastrocnemius has a far more even fibre split than the soleus. Pooling across five research
groups by country, **calves typically grow 6–20%, indistinguishable from muscles generally**, with
gastrocnemius growth repeatedly landing close to what the same groups report for quads, hamstrings,
biceps or pecs; two case studies in very experienced lifters still showed growth (4.14% medial
gastrocnemius CSA in a 10-year lifter over 6 weeks; 7–23% thickness in an 18-year bodybuilder).
**Practical payoff: train calves normally and expect normal returns.** Two large caveats: nearly
every comparison is **between studies** rather than within one, and this video has no reference list
at all — no study is named aloud, only countries.

## How much, for how long, and why people differ

[The 16-studies video](muscle-growth-slows-down-fast-16-studies.md) takes on Counts et al.'s 2017
claim that most lower-body growth potential is reached in three months. Four studies do show early
plateaus — quads levelling at 6–8 weeks (Abe, DeFreitas, Baroni), vastus lateralis growing well from
week 0–3 and barely from 3–6 (Brook) — and **progressive overload was applied in all of them**, so
that is not the explanation. Two point the other way: a 1970 study found no biceps plateau across 100
days, and Narici found quadriceps growth rising across six months. His reinterpretation is that
untrained people grow well for three months *while ignoring everything outside the gym*: none of the
plateau studies gave nutritional instruction, and all held every training variable except load
constant, usually with one exercise per muscle. **That reinterpretation is untested.** One of his
four remedies is worth carrying: Scarpelli's within-subject design, where one leg did **20% more
weekly sets than the subject's own habit** and the other a prescribed 22 sets, favoured the +20% leg —
and for 8 of 16 subjects the prescribed number was the *bigger* jump. **Bigger volume jumps were not
better than modest ones.**

[The 2025 review video](much-muscle-gain-causes-growth.md) puts numbers on the ceiling: roughly
**5 kg (low responder), 12 kg (average), 21 kg (high responder)** of lifetime natural fat-free mass
gain for men, with women gaining at a similar *relative* rate but about **70% of the absolute**
amount. Short-term, the average male gains ~1.5 kg fat-free mass in ~10 weeks; a high responder ~3
kg, a low responder ~0.4 kg. His own caveats: this merges a short-term meta-analysis with an
FFMI-derived ceiling from natural bodybuilders (22.7–25); **fat-free mass is not muscle mass**; the
curve stops at five years; real trajectories are lumpy; and nobody should be labelled a *true* low
responder, since they may respond better to different variables or to something outside training.

Individual variation is real and mostly unexplained. Hubal's **585 subjects** trained identically for
12 weeks: many gained close to 20% biceps CSA, some over 50%, and **some lost about 5%**
([AR note](hidden-key-building-muscle-legit.md)). Surviving candidate explanations are thin —
androgen receptor content is contested, resting hormones are ruled out, **capillary density** is
raised with the interesting implication that cardio might support long-term hypertrophy, and ribosome
biogenesis capacity is mentioned once as a reason not everyone benefits from more volume
([raising testosterone](science-increasing-testosterone-muscle-strength.md)). Recovery capacity
varies visibly too, in the RBE individual data.

## Three findings that are mechanism only

[Cross-education](cross-education-effect-training-one-limb-increases.md) is a real effect with almost
no use for a healthy lifter. Manca's meta-analysis of 31 studies (3–12 weeks) found **+11.9% strength
in the untrained limb**, against his own hand-tally of ~+27.6% in the trained limb — so the untrained
side gets roughly 40% of the strength gain. Lower body transfers more than upper (+16.4% vs +9.4%)
and eccentric-only transfers most (+17.7%). But **size barely transfers**: of 11 studies, 4 found
growth and 7 did not, three of those nulls failed to grow the *trained* limb either, and three of the
four positives reported non-significant trends. Both proposed size mechanisms fail on his own
evidence: systemic hormones (single-joint work barely spikes anything, and spikes don't predict
growth) and mirror activity (the untrained muscle activates at 3–6% of the trained one, comparable to
daily tasks that grow nothing). **The one place it pays off is immobilisation:** across five
casted-arm studies, trainers gained **4.6% strength and held size flat (+0.2%)** in the immobilised
arm while controls **lost 13.3% strength and 3.8% size**. A second, quieter payoff: it validates
within-subject unilateral designs for **hypertrophy** outcomes while making them questionable for
**strength** outcomes — and those designs are everywhere in this literature.

[The EMG-redistribution note](change-muscle-activation-any-compound-exercise-maybe.md) is the
domain's best methods lesson. Stronska trained 27 men (average bench 105.2 kg) with isolation work
for whichever bench-press muscle showed the lowest relative EMG — **36 weekly sets** for the target,
three times a week for six weeks — and each group's target muscle rose in relative EMG while the
others fell. He then spends four of eleven minutes explaining why the measurement cannot carry that
claim: surface EMG approximates **neuromuscular excitation**, not activation, and does so imperfectly
since changing muscle length or joint angle changes the recording at constant excitation (Vigotsky
2017); and normalising to a maximum isometric contraction makes **between-muscle comparisons
meaningless**, because the denominator is an arbitrary property of the reference contraction — which
is exactly how the study assigned its groups. What survives is a within-subject, same-exercise,
same-electrode pre/post comparison consistent across three groups. **That is suggestive of changed
recruitment and is not evidence of changed growth**, and surface EMG amplitude is not a validated
predictor of hypertrophy (Vigotsky 2022, a paper in his own description). Subjects also *stopped*
training everything else, so the effect could be de-training the competitors rather than training the
target.

The [mind-muscle](mind-muscle-connection-help-build-more-muscle.md) and
[no-load](biceps-curls-no-weight-build-muscle-optimally.md) results, covered above, are the third
case: both real, both n=13–27, both untrained, both single-joint.

## Weak points

**The positive case for tension is weaker than the negative cases against everything else, and it is
unverifiable.** Four signalling studies, at least one of them a rat experiment, cited on screen in a
video with no reference list, plus an inference from protocols "presumed" to equate tension. The
demolitions of the pump, lactate, damage and hormones rest on measured hypertrophy over training
blocks. Hold "metabolic stress and damage are not drivers" much more firmly than "tension is *the*
driver."

**Mechanism claims get quoted as training findings.** Every practical-sounding sentence in the
sarcoplasmic, myofibril, hyperplasia and titin-stiffening material is either animal ultrastructure or
an inference borrowed from a volume tally of five studies. **High volume "produces" sarcoplasmic
hypertrophy** is the worst offender: three of five studies, classified after the fact, against a 21+
weekly set threshold no study tested, with one cross-sectional finding pointing the other way — and
the video never asks whether a lifter should want proportionally less contractile tissue. His own
later note answers it: the shift is about 2% of relative space, which he doubts is visible.

**Acute proxies keep re-entering through the back door.** He is rigorous about MPS not predicting
growth when demolishing damage and calf pessimism, and about EMG not predicting growth when
demolishing Stronska — then returns to that same Stronska study seven months later in his
exercise-selection material with the normalisation critique dropped, defending it on exactly the
grounds he dismantled ([noted in the EMG file itself](change-muscle-activation-any-compound-exercise-maybe.md)).

**"Non-significant difference" gets read as equivalence** in several places: the stretching study
(~two dozen per arm, six weeks, one muscle), the cross-education size tally where three of four
"positive" studies were non-significant trends, and the muscle-confusion null. The confusion null is
the best-designed of the three — within-subject, 20 trained men — and is entitled to its claim; the
other two are not.

**Two conflicts are left standing.** Short rest wins for isolation work in
[the pump note](pump-best-indicator-hypertrophy-true-false.md) and loses for compounds in
[the myths note](90-still-believe-builds-more-muscle.md); he calls the isolation literature "not
crystal clear" and leaves it. And the damage position hardens and softens across four videos without
any of them acknowledging the others.

**Three things a reader may come here for and will not find.** *Satellite cells* appear only as a
rebutted step in the damage argument, as a speculative route to fibre splitting in
[the hyperplasia note](gain-more-muscle-fibers-training.md), and as an in vitro lactate response —
there is no standalone treatment. *Myonuclei* appear only as a two-sentence speculation about why
supraphysiological testosterone might raise the fibre-size ceiling. **There is no coverage of muscle
memory or myonuclear permanence anywhere in these 26 notes**, so nothing here should be used to
answer a question about detraining and retraining.

**Finally, the citation problem tracks the stakes exactly.** The unreferenced videos are the 87-study
mechanisms centrepiece, hyperplasia, sarcoplasmic hypertrophy, the 33-study tally, myofibrillar
packing, the 2024 myths video and the calf video — the taxonomy, the mechanism and the tallies —
while the hormone and metabolite demolitions all carry full lists. The part of this domain that is
checkable is the part that says no.
