# The Hypertrophy Rep Range Is Roughly 5 to 35, Not 6 to 12

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "What Is the Best Rep Range to Build Muscle? (Research-Backed)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rW3qkooaqV8)) · 2020-05-04 · 6 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- The strength-hypertrophy-endurance continuum holds at its ends but **fails in the middle**. There is no evidence that growth is best achieved exclusively at 6–12 reps.
- [Schoenfeld's 2017 meta-analysis](https://elementssystem.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Schoenfeld-17-altas-bajas.pdf) found low and high loads produced **similar hypertrophy provided sets were taken to failure**. The included studies span **30–85% 1RM**, roughly **5 to 35 reps**.
- The mechanism he gives is motor unit recruitment: heavy loads recruit high-threshold units immediately, light loads recruit them as fatigue accumulates near failure. Both routes end at high recruitment, so both produce mechanical tension.
- **Below ~5 reps:** some evidence that sets as low as 3 reps grow muscle well, but three of those studies gave the low-rep group more sets, so it is not clean. Heavy low-rep work is also harder on joints and connective tissue.
- **Above ~35 reps:** [Lasevicius 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29564973/) found 40, 60 and 80% 1RM produced similar elbow-flexor and vastus lateralis growth, while **20% 1RM produced roughly half** — 20% 1RM usually means 60+ reps to failure.

## Why the range is so wide
Motor units come in three broad classes — slow units (small motor neurons, slow-twitch fibres, low force, fatigue resistant), fast fatigue-resistant units (fast oxidative fibres, faster and stronger), and fast fatigable units (fast glycolytic fibres, fastest and strongest, fatiguing quickly). Recruitment follows the size principle: low force demands recruit slow units first, higher demands progressively add the faster ones.

High recruitment matters because it is a main component of mechanical tension, which he treats as the best-characterised driver of hypertrophy — mechanosensors in and around the fibre detect force and start the signalling cascade that ends in growth ([Wackerhage 2019](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00685.2018)).

The intuitive conclusion is that you should therefore lift heavy. His correction is that light loads get to the same place by a different path: force requirements start low, but as reps approach failure, faster units are progressively recruited to sustain force output. Two studies support comparable recruitment at task failure across loads ([Morton 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31294822/), [Muddle 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29673119/)).

## The two edges of the range
**The low end.** He notes evidence that reps as low as 3 can build muscle optimally, then immediately undercuts it: three of those studies used more sets in the low-rep condition, so load and volume are confounded. Combined with the higher joint and connective-tissue stress of heavy multi-set work, his practical line is to **stay above 5 reps if hypertrophy is the sole goal**.

**The high end.** The only fair comparison is Lasevicius, which ran 20/40/60/80% 1RM on biceps curl and leg press. Growth at 40–80% was similar; 20% was significantly worse, about half. He is careful that the actual threshold may sit lower than 60 reps, and 35 is a safety margin rather than a measured cutoff — with the practical aside that few people would voluntarily do 35+ rep sets anyway.

**Flagged:** this is a 2020 video and the argument leans hard on recruitment as the mediator. Recruitment at failure is an acute surrogate, not a growth outcome; the hypertrophy claim rests on the meta-analysis, and the motor-unit material is explanation rather than evidence. He is also explicit that the low-rep-end evidence "isn't entirely clear", which is the right level of confidence.
