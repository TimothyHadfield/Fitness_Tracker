# The Interference Effect Has Shrunk: Cardio and Lifting Combine Fine

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "You CAN Combine Cardio & Weights (and Should)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bjSWKtKlEh4)) · 2023-01-23 · 12 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Updated meta-analyses find **combining cardio with resistance training does not compromise hypertrophy or strength**, including in a subgroup analysis restricted to trained individuals. The 2012 meta-analysis that did find interference has been superseded by more and better data.
- The context that produced this result matters and he states it: cardio **2–4×/week for 20–45 min**, resistance training **2–4×/week with 3–4 exercises per muscle group for a few sets each**. Extreme volumes on both sides almost certainly still interfere.
- **Power is the exception.** Both the 2012 meta-analysis and one of the 2022 ones found power adaptations *are* compromised. Sprinters, jumpers and Olympic lifters should take this seriously; hypertrophy trainees need not.
- **Same-session vs separate sessions is the one place he gives a real recommendation.** A 2021 meta-analysis restricted to trained individuals found strength gains lowered when cardio was done in the same session, not when it was separate. If you must combine, lift first.
- Mode does not matter: subgroup analyses on running and cycling did not change the results. HIIT vs continuous does not matter for whole-muscle growth either — and he explicitly refuses to use the one fibre-level result that says otherwise, for reasons he explains.

## The health case comes first
A 2018 cohort study found higher VO2 max associated with lower mortality risk. Resistance training carries its own benefits, including preserving fast-twitch fibre contractile function, which aerobic training may not do as well. A 2019 meta-analysis found resistance training alone associated with a **21% lower all-cause mortality risk**, and cardio plus resistance training with a **40% lower risk** — associations from observational data, which he does not caveat as clearly as he caveats the training data.

## Where "cardio kills gains" came from
Hickson, 1980. A strength group trained the lower body five times weekly; an endurance group did cardio six days weekly; a combined group did both. After 10 weeks the combined group had smaller squat strength gains. He points out the obvious: eleven sessions a week is extreme, and the fact that the combined group's strength *declined* midway through hints at overtraining rather than a clean interference mechanism.

Wilson's 2012 meta-analysis pooled everything available at the time and did find lower strength and hypertrophy with concurrent training, using more typical frequencies than Hickson's. At face value that confirmed the effect. Two things have changed since.

## What changed
**The mechanism did not survive.** The proposed pathway — cardio triggering intracellular signalling that directly inhibits the anabolic response to lifting — is supported in animal studies but not in humans, where the pathways turn out to be complex and not mutually exclusive.

**The outcome data reversed.** A recent meta-analysis of the updated literature found concurrent training did not produce lower strength or hypertrophy than resistance training alone, and a subgroup analysis restricted to trained individuals did not change that. A second 2022 meta-analysis likewise found whole-muscle growth unaffected.

He caveats this three ways. First, the volumes in these studies were moderate — prepping for a bodybuilding show while also training for a marathon almost certainly does involve interference, and Hickson's protocol is the evidence for that. Second, the "trained" subjects in the subgroup analysis were mostly not *highly* trained, and a 2017 review hypothesised interference becomes more pronounced at high training status. Third, power is genuinely compromised.

## HIIT vs continuous, and a data-quality argument worth keeping
Both modalities appear across the updated meta-analyses, and neither compromised whole-muscle hypertrophy. But one meta-analysis found that at the level of **fibre-type-specific growth**, HIIT plus resistance training did compromise slow- and fast-twitch fibre growth while continuous training did not — from the same analysis that found whole-muscle size unaffected by either.

He treats this as a reason to distrust the fibre measurement rather than a reason to avoid HIIT, and the argument is the most useful thing in the video. Fibre growth requires biopsy; a biopsy samples very few fibres and may not represent the muscle; and repeat biopsies from the same person show substantial variation in fibre size, which makes pre-post comparisons unreliable. Since the two measures disagree within a single analysis, he goes with the whole-muscle outcome. He still allows a hedge: HIIT is arguably more fatiguing, so at higher overall volumes continuous work may be easier to recover from.

## Practical arrangement
- **Separate sessions if possible.** The 2021 meta-analysis isolating trained individuals found same-session cardio lowered strength gains; separate-session cardio did not. Hypertrophy was not assessed in that analysis, and he says he *presumes* the same holds — flagged as a presumption.
- **If same session, lift first.** Doing cardio first raises the chance that fatigue degrades the effort and therefore the stimulus of the lifting.
- **Running or cycling both work.** Running produces more muscle damage than cycling, which could matter at high aerobic volumes, but the repeated bout effect reduces damage over time, and at moderate volumes the meta-analyses show no penalty.
- Sleep, nutrition and stress reduce whatever interference exists.

**Flagged:** the mortality figures are observational and the 21% vs 40% contrast is presented without the confounding caveat that the training data get. Everywhere else he is careful — bounding the conclusion by the actual training volumes in the studies, distinguishing "not highly trained" from "trained", and refusing to let a fibre-biopsy result overturn whole-muscle outcomes without first explaining why the measurement is weak.
