# Adding Load vs Adding Reps: What One Re-Analysis Can and Cannot Tell You

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Is Adding More Load or Repetitions Better for Hypertrophy and Strength?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0MenoivXo_U)) · 2022-02-21 · 18 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- The only data directly comparing the two commonest progression methods is a **pooled re-analysis by Nóbrega and colleagues** of two of their own earlier studies — **not a randomised trial**. It found similar 1RM gains but greater quad growth for load progression.
- The hypertrophy difference is fragile: **n = 14 vs n = 10**, previously untrained men, different frequencies (6 vs 9 weekly sets), different durations (12 vs 8 weeks), one exercise, one measurement site.
- The authors' proposed mechanism — that **volume load progression** drives growth — is contradicted by at least three other datasets where volume load progression differed markedly and hypertrophy did not.
- There is also **no mechanistic reason** to expect load progression to win: anywhere from ~30–80% 1RM, sets taken to or near failure produce comparable growth, so both progression styles keep you in the effective zone.
- Practical read: **either method works**. Pick whichever lets you keep training hard and keep records. Do not restructure a programme around this study.

## The study he is dissecting
[00:30] Nóbrega et al. did not run a new experiment. They combined data from two of their previous, deliberately similar leg-extension studies.

- **Rep-overload group** (from study 1): **14 previously untrained men**, 3 sets to failure per session, 2 min rest, **2×/week for 12 weeks** = 24 sessions. Load was set at **80% 1RM in session one and never adjusted** — so with every set going to failure, progression happened by adding reps. One exception: 1RM was re-tested once at week 6 to reset them to a true 80%, then held fixed again for the remaining six weeks.
- **Load-overload group** (from study 2): **10 previously untrained men**, same 3-sets-to-failure structure, **3×/week for 8 weeks** = 24 sessions. Load was adjusted every set and session to keep each set landing at **9–12 reps to failure** — so progression happened by adding weight.

Measures: leg extension 1RM, vastus lateralis cross-sectional area at ~50% of femur length, total accumulated volume load, and volume load *progression* across the 24 sessions.

## What it found
[03:32] **Total volume load** was numerically higher for the load group but not statistically different. **Volume load progression** across sessions *was* statistically greater for the load group (ANCOVA-adjusted for baseline).

[04:03] **Strength:** no statistical difference. Rep group **+31.4%** / **+14 kg**; load group **+38.3%** / **+15.3 kg**.

[04:34] **Hypertrophy:** vastus lateralis CSA increases were statistically **greater for the load group**, in both relative and absolute terms.

[05:04] Correlating the pooled subjects, **total volume load did not predict** 1RM or CSA gains — but **volume load progression did**. The authors used this to argue that the load group's steeper volume load climb explains its extra growth.

## Why the hypertrophy result should not be trusted yet
He spends more than half the video attacking the result, which is the right instinct.

[06:05] **It is not a randomised controlled comparison.** Two independent studies were merged after the fact, so subjects were never randomised between the conditions.

[06:35] **Both groups trained sub-optimally for strength**, which makes the "no strength difference" finding hard to generalise. Both used loads permitting 8+ reps, whereas Schoenfeld and colleagues found that maintaining **2–4 reps to failure** on squat and bench produced larger 1RM gains than maintaining 8–12. The interesting untested question is what happens if both progression styles start from a 2–4RM load.

[07:37] **Frequency and duration were not matched.** The rep group did 6 weekly leg-extension sets over 12 weeks; the load group did 9 weekly sets over 8 weeks. His reasoning: at ≤10 weekly sets, frequency itself appears not to matter for strength or size, but **9 weekly sets probably beats 6**, so the load group likely gained faster per week — while the rep group had four extra weeks. Because gains decelerate over time rather than accruing linearly, these two errors do not cleanly cancel. His honest conclusion is that the effect is **impossible to disentangle**, not that it is harmless.

[09:39] **Small samples make responder-lottery explanations plausible.** Hubal and colleagues trained 585 people (243 men, 342 women) on preacher and concentration curls for 12 weeks; most gained 10–30% biceps CSA, but individuals landed far above and far below that band. With 10 and 14 subjects, it is entirely possible the load group simply contained better responders.

[11:11] **The measurement was narrow.** Vastus lateralis CSA at one site (~50% femur length) tells you nothing about other VL regions or other quad heads — and leg extensions are known to grow **rectus femoris** heavily, which was not measured. Only one exercise, only untrained subjects.

## The counter-evidence on volume load progression
[11:42] This is the strongest part of the video. The authors' explanatory mechanism — more volume load progression means more growth — has been tested elsewhere and repeatedly fails:

- **Schoenfeld et al. (low vs high load, 8 weeks):** trained men (1.5–9 years' experience), 3 sets per exercise, 3×/week. One group did 25–35 reps to failure, the other 8–12, both adjusting load to stay in range. **Volume load progression was greater in the 25–35 rep group**, yet growth was comparable — as expected, since 30–80% 1RM taken near failure produces similar hypertrophy.
- **Brigatto et al.:** trained men (2–8 years), identical weekly sets, exercises and rest intervals, delivered in one session vs two per week, 8 weeks. **Volume load progression was greater in the twice-weekly group**; muscle thickness gains were similar.
- **Souza Junior et al. (two studies, not in his reference list):** progressing by shortening rest intervals vs by adding load. Volume load progression was larger for the load condition; arm and thigh CSA changes were similar.

[16:48] He then runs a nice reductio: if shortening rest is as good as adding load, and adding load beats adding reps, then **shortening rest would beat adding reps** — a conclusion nobody would accept. That absurdity is a reasonable argument that one of the premises is wrong.

## Where the reasoning leans on mechanism
[12:12] His central rebuttal — "there is currently no rationale as to why load progression would beat rep progression" — is a **mechanistic argument used to discount an outcome measurement**. Mechanical tension theory says that as long as sets go to or near failure within roughly 30–80% 1RM, both progression styles should stimulate growth about equally. That is a good reason for scepticism, but absence of a known mechanism is not evidence that the observed difference is false. He is careful enough to frame it as "counter evidence" rather than refutation, and to state plainly that further research is required.

[17:48] He closes by noting a Menno Henselmans Instagram post on the same analysis, under which **Brad Schoenfeld said his team has a study on this topic due for publication** — the direct randomised test this question needs.
