# Individual Responses to Rep Range: The Average Hides Real Differences

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Best Rep Range for Muscle Growth Isn't What You Think" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6AFNeodq7tM)) · 2023-10-02 · 12 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- The settled finding — roughly **6 to 35 reps** builds similar muscle when sets are taken to or near failure — is an **average**. This crossover study is the first to show what sits underneath it.
- 24 subjects each trained **both** an 8–12 and a 27–31 rep range for 12 weeks apiece. Group means were similar, but individuals diverged sharply: one lost ~5% thigh lean tissue on 8–12 reps and gained just over 10% on 27–31; another gained ~13% on 8–12 and only ~3% on 27–31.
- Practical read: how well you grow in one rep range does not predict how well you grow in another. "High responder" and "low responder" are properties of a person **and** a training variable, not of a person alone.
- He **disagrees with the study authors' own conclusion** that rotating rep ranges delayed a plateau, and shows why the average is misleading. This is the strongest part of the video.
- Recommendation is modest: if you are growing well on your current rep range, stay. If you are not, try another — or simply include several so part of your training hits whichever suits you.

## The study
24 previously untrained women, **average age 58, BMI 27**. Leg press, leg extension, leg curl and calf raise, three sets each, three times a week, for **24 weeks**.

- Group 1: weeks 1–12 to voluntary failure in an 8–12 rep range; weeks 13–24 to voluntary failure in a **27–31** rep range.
- Group 2: the reverse.

Load was increased throughout to keep subjects failing inside their assigned range, and he shows the progression graphs to make the point that overload actually happened. Thigh lean soft tissue mass was the outcome. (He notes the odd 27–31 range with no explanation for why the researchers chose it.)

Because every subject trained both ranges, the design can identify whether a given person grew meaningfully more with one. On average, growth was comparable — consistent with the rest of the load literature. Individually, it was not.

## The limitations he raises himself
**Order effect.** Growth rate slows over a long study, so the range trained in weeks 13–24 is disadvantaged. His rebuttal is the right one and it is empirical: **50% of subjects saw their largest gain in the rep range they trained in the final 12 weeks**. Order cannot explain the spread.

**"Voluntary failure" is not momentary failure.** Getting genuinely close to failure is harder at 27–31 reps, so some subjects may have under-shot there. He flags this as speculation, and notes it cannot account for the subjects who grew *more* on high reps.

**Demographics.** Untrained women averaging 58 years old is not the audience. His defence: there is no strong evidence that rep-range responses differ by demographic, the group averages match the broader literature drawn from many populations, individual differences have also been observed in younger trained men, the subjects tolerated multiple lower-body sets to voluntary failure, and the total gains landed where you would expect. That is a reasonable defence, honestly made.

**Flag:** the limitation he does not raise is the one that most undercuts individual-response analyses. Separating true individual response from measurement error and regression to the mean requires either repeated exposures to the same condition or a non-training control arm, and this design has neither. Thigh lean soft tissue mass is also a fairly crude proxy for muscle. Some of the spread he treats as real inter-individual variation may be noise.

## Why the "high responder" framing needs a qualifier
It is common to run one programme on a group and sort people into high and low responders. This data says the label is conditional on the variables used. He also points back to the Neves frequency study covered previously on the channel, where trained subjects doing nine weekly quad sets split some grew more when the sets were spread over three days while others gained the same either way.

He is careful not to overcorrect: average results still matter, still refute myths, and still make a sound starting point. Not every variable will show this much individual variation.

## Fibre type is the obvious explanation, and it does not hold
The tempting story is that slow-twitch-dominant people grow better on high reps and fast-twitch-dominant people on low reps. Against it: the soleus is slow-twitch dominant in most people, yet earlier work found soleus hypertrophy comparable between high and low loads. No mechanism is currently established.

## The disagreement with the authors
The authors argued that switching rep ranges halfway prevented the usual post-12-week slowdown, since both groups grew at a similar rate in both halves.

He rejects this using a worked two-subject example. Subject A gains 0.8 kg in weeks 1–12 on 8–12 reps, then only 0.1 kg on 27–31. Subject B gains 0.1 kg then 0.8 kg. Averaged, growth looks steady across both halves — but nothing was delayed. Different people simply grew at different times. He says this is what most of the dataset looks like.

The implication reverses the authors' advice: for someone already growing well in a given rep range, **switching may cost them**. Rotating ranges is insurance for the uncertain, not a plateau-breaking mechanism.
