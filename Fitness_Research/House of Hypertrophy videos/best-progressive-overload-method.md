# Adding Load or Adding Reps: The First RCT Says It Barely Matters

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The BEST Progressive Overload Method (New Research)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BU5cxNJJjic)) · 2022-10-17 · 7 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The first **randomised controlled trial** comparing load progression against rep progression found **similar hypertrophy** across quads, gastrocnemius and soleus, and near-identical Smith machine squat strength gains (2 kg edge to the load group).
- This supersedes an earlier Brazilian comparison that favoured load progression — that one **wasn't randomised**; it pooled two separate studies with different training frequencies and durations.
- For hypertrophy, the method of progression is largely irrelevant so long as you keep sets in the effective rep window and near failure.
- For **1RM strength**, he does not accept the null: the trial used a non-specific test and rep ranges too high to be informative, so load progression remains the safer bet.

## Why the earlier study didn't settle it
A Brazilian paper released earlier in 2022 found leg extension strength gains equal between load and rep progression but greater vastus lateralis growth with load progression. Dhim had already covered it and rejected the design: it **combined the results of two separate studies**, one that progressed load and one that progressed reps, which left training frequency and duration differing between the groups. He had said at the time that better-controlled work was needed and that a researcher had one in progress. This video is that study arriving — a good illustration of the channel tracking a question across releases rather than treating each new paper as final.

## The trial
Trained individuals were randomised to a **load group** or a **rep group**, both training four lower-body exercises. The load group aimed to add weight across sessions while staying in the **8–12 rep range**. The rep group held the first session's loads constant and added reps across sessions. Food diaries — which he notes are not always reliable — showed similar fat, carbohydrate, protein and calorie intake between groups before and after.

Strength was tested by **Smith machine squat 1RM**; growth by thickness of the mid quad, lateral quad, medial and lateral gastrocnemius, and soleus. Virtually all growth measures were similar between groups. Mid-quad growth slightly favoured the **rep** group. Squat 1RM slightly favoured the load group, by 2 kg.

## The mid-quad anomaly
The researchers speculated that the rep group's higher reps on the squat later in the study enhanced rectus femoris recruitment through accumulated fatigue in the other vasti, whereas heavier low-rep squats might inhibit rectus femoris involvement at the hip. **He says outright he isn't convinced** and that verifying it would need direct research on how rectus femoris recruitment differs by rep range. His alternative — chance, or the rep group happening to contain better rectus femoris responders — is offered as equally plausible given every other muscle came out even.

The overall result fits the broader literature: roughly **6–35 reps per set are similarly effective** when taken to or near failure, so any route that keeps you in that window should work.

## Why he won't call strength a tie
Two problems with the strength finding. First, the test was a **Smith machine squat that neither group trained** — they used a free-weight squat, so the test may have been too non-specific to reveal a difference. Second, both groups worked at **eight or more reps**, while 1RM gains favour loads permitting five or fewer. His conclusion: load and rep progression are probably equivalent for hypertrophy, but for maximal strength, progress load and train heavier than either group did.
