# Inter-Set Stretching Fails Again — And an FST-7 Programme Fails With It

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Can Stretching Between Sets TRANSFORM Muscle & Strength?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mCEaJXVG6A4)) · 2023-03-13 · 11 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- A fourth study — 22 trained subjects, 8 weeks, arms — again finds **no meaningful hypertrophy or strength benefit** to stretching between sets. His verdict: **not a validated method**.
- His stated position has now moved twice: positive (Sept 2020, on Evangelista alone) → "slight majority against" (Oct 2021) → **"we can't say it's validated"** (Mar 2023). Three of five published studies find nothing; none finds harm.
- The stretching group lifted **lower loads** across sets and accumulated less total volume — the predictable consequence of static stretching depressing force — yet **1RM gains were equal**. Cost and benefit both appear to be near zero.
- The study doubles as an accidental test of **FST-7**, and the FST-7-style programme produced **poor hypertrophy for many subjects, including regional size losses**.
- His one live hypothesis: **transitioning immediately into a loaded stretch straight off the last eccentric** may be a different stimulus from walking over to a static stretch. Evidence for it is one small soleus finding and one unpublished conference abstract.

## The study

22 trained individuals, 8 weeks, two biceps-focused and two triceps-focused sessions per week. The volume was extreme: **28 weekly isolation sets each for biceps and triceps**, rising to **46 weekly sets each** once compounds are counted. Rest was **45 seconds** between sets and 2 minutes between exercises, per FST-7 convention. All sets went to concentric failure in the 10-12 rep range, with load adjusted each set to stay in range. Diet was comparable between groups.

The stretching group stretched the trained muscle for the **full 45-second rest interval** on the first and last exercise of each session, at a self-reported **70-90% pain/discomfort level** — a much more aggressive protocol than the pain-free 30 s in the original Evangelista trial.

**Results.** Total training volume was lower in the stretching group. Since sets and reps matched, that means they used lighter loads — consistent with static stretching reducing force production. Despite that, **1RM gains on biceps curl and triceps skull crusher were comparable**. Hypertrophy was measured at mid-biceps, lower brachialis, and upper and lower long-head triceps: **no difference anywhere except lower long-head triceps**, which favoured stretching.

He does not take the win. The researchers themselves urged caution on that one region, and he goes further by reading the **individual data points plotted behind the group means**: most subjects saw similar gains, with a few outliers on either side that plausibly drove the average. **Reading individual data rather than group means is the single most rigorous move in this video** and it is the reason his conclusion is "no effect" rather than "a regional effect."

## The FST-7 subplot

The protocol replicated FST-7, the method of a well-known bodybuilding coach whose name the auto-captions garble (rendered "Harney rambot" — do not rely on that spelling). FST-7's premise is that seven sets with short rests maximise the pump, which stretches the fascia from the inside and thereby aids growth. He notes two things about the replication: FST-7 normally puts the seven-set block only at the **end** of a session, whereas this study used it at start and end, so it is not identical.

The outcome is nonetheless striking. Hypertrophy was **lackluster for many subjects** — most lost size at one region, causing the group averages there to be negative, and several lost size at other regions too. Subjects were doing on average **9-11 more weekly isolation sets per muscle than they had before the study**, and he suggests, following the authors, that the abrupt volume jump caused enough damage to divert protein synthesis toward repair rather than growth.

He also uses this as indirect evidence against the pump: these subjects would have achieved an enormous pump, there is **no evidence at all** that the pump stretches fascia, and there is minimal evidence the pump directly drives hypertrophy. He is explicit that this is an indirect inference, and he notes some individuals in the study did grow well — individual response, not a blanket condemnation.

## The rest of the literature

Five studies now. The first (untrained subjects) found a benefit; one from 2022 found a **slight benefit in one muscle**; the other three, including this one, found none. One study found a benefit for **isometric but not leg press strength** — a single result he declines to build on. Since the majority fails, the method is not validated.

His caveat is real rather than face-saving: the studies **differ in stretch duration, pain level, and how the stretch is entered**, so a specific protocol could still work even though the general method has not.

## The one remaining hypothesis

The 2022 study that found a soleus benefit had subjects transition **immediately into a weighted stretch after the final rep**, rather than finishing the set and walking over to stretch. His mechanistic argument: during the lowering phase, the passive elements producing stretching force are in a **stiffer state** than in a cold passive stretch, so entering the stretch immediately may exploit that stiffness.

He supports this with a Silva et al. study reporting greater gastrocnemius growth from post-set calf stretching — and then **excludes it from his own summary table because it was only presented at a conference and never published.** (This is the same unpublished calf study that circulates elsewhere in the library as a PowerPoint slide.) He then points out the conflict he could have ignored: the published soleus study found the benefit **only in the soleus, small in size, with no gastrocnemius benefit** — directly contradicting the unpublished Silva result.

**Where the argument leans on mechanism:** the stiff-passive-elements case is a plausible story with one small positive outcome and one unpublished abstract behind it. He hedges it correctly, but it is the only part of the video not grounded in growth data.
