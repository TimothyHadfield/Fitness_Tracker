# Failure vs 1–2 Reps in Reserve: The Best-Designed Study So Far

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How HARD Should You Workout? - This NEW Study is Epic" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xcu2725W8ro)) · 2024-03-04 · 14 min
**Topic:** Effort & failure

## Bottom line
- A 2024 within-subject study in **18 trained lifters (average 7.7 years' experience, half of them competitive powerlifters or bodybuilders)** found **similar quadriceps growth** between training to failure and stopping at 1–2 reps in reserve, over 8 weeks of unilateral leg press and leg extension twice weekly.
- Five design features make it strong: the most experienced sample to date, verified RIR accuracy (subjects were off by **less than one rep**), a **within-subject unilateral design** so genetics/nutrition/lifestyle cannot confound, **individualised set counts** based on each subject's pre-study volume, and app-guided nutrition targeting **1% monthly weight gain**.
- Volumes ran **10–17 weekly quad sets**; other well-designed failure literature sits at **6–19 sets per muscle**. The null result is specific to that band.
- The main caveat he raises himself: at **very low volumes (~2 weekly sets)**, one study found failure produced better muscle mass gains — though with imprecise body-composition measures.
- Fatigue is the real difference, not growth. Recovery is faster and soreness lower when not training to failure. He treats that as a reason to choose, not a reason to avoid failure.

## The study design, feature by feature
[01:00] Eighteen trained subjects, 7.7 years' average experience. One leg did **all sets to momentary muscular failure**; the other did **leg press at 2 RIR and leg extension at 1 RIR**. Twice per week, eight weeks. He notes the researchers published supervision footage of both a failure set and a 2-RIR set, which is a check most studies do not provide.

[02:10] RIR accuracy was tested before the study: subjects were **off by less than one repetition** on both a 1-RIR and a 3-RIR test. That is the piece most failure research lacks, and it is why the non-failure condition here can be trusted.

[03:11] The within-subject design puts the same person in both conditions, removing between-subject confounds and effectively giving 18 subjects per condition instead of nine. Dominant legs were split evenly between conditions and the leg trained first alternated every session.

[03:42] Set numbers were **individualised**: each subject's habitual weekly quad set count was recorded and maintained, then increased by 20% halfway through. Someone doing 12 weekly sets did 12, then ~14. This avoids the common problem of assigning everyone an identical volume that is far above or below what they are used to. Rest was **4 minutes** between leg press sets and **2 minutes** between leg extension sets, with loads raised as needed to stay at the target proximity to failure within the prescribed rep ranges.

## Results
[05:12] **Overall quadriceps growth** (averaged across rectus femoris and vastus lateralis) was **similar** between conditions. He places this alongside three other well-designed studies with the same finding (Nobrega, Lacerda, Santanielo) and several meta-analyses reporting similar growth with and without failure.

He addresses the contrary evidence directly: a 2023 meta-regression appeared to favour training to failure. His reading — and, he says, the authors' own stated caveats — is that it shows **getting close to failure is required**, not that reaching failure is superior. Numbers in the well-designed literature imply non-failure groups were mostly stopping **three or fewer reps short**, with the majority at 1–2 RIR.

### The regional finding
[06:46] Split by muscle, **vastus lateralis** growth slightly favoured the failure leg and **rectus femoris** slightly favoured the non-failure leg. The researchers' speculation: leg press was always performed first, and there is evidence leg press better grows the VL while leg extension better grows the RF. Failure on the leg press maximised the VL stimulus but left fatigue that blunted the leg extension stimulus — an interaction between failure and exercise order.

[07:48] He then presents the evidence against his own attractive explanation. The Santanielo study used the same exercise order, failure versus ~1–2 RIR, and measured VL only. If the logic held, the failure leg should have grown slightly more. It did not — gains were non-significantly better for the **non-failure** leg. His conclusion is that the small regional differences remain unexplained. This is a good example of the channel's habit of killing its own neat story.

## Fatigue
[08:50] In-session indirect neuromuscular fatigue measures partly suggested failure training improved leg press fatigue resistance as the weeks went by. Post-session recovery was not measured. He supplies a separate study: trained subjects doing **six sets of barbell bench press to failure** recovered more slowly and were sorer than at 1 and 3 RIR (this paper is not in his reference list, so treat it as unverified). His framing is balanced — fatigue is real, is a reason to avoid failure if you need to limit it, and has not been shown to harm growth in these studies. His added claim that habituation to failure produces adaptations reducing fatigue and soreness is asserted from earlier videos, not evidenced here.

## Three qualifications on the takeaway
[11:51] **Volume range.** The result holds at roughly 6–19 weekly sets per muscle. At very low volumes it may not: one study using about **two weekly sets per muscle** found better muscle mass increases with failure than with stopping just short (Giessing et al.), though he notes the muscle mass measures were imprecise. If you train very low volume, failure is both more likely to matter and more manageable.

[12:53] **Mixing is allowed.** Nothing prevents combining failure and non-failure work in one program, and no study has tested the combination. One practical benefit: regular exposure to failure keeps your RIR judgement calibrated.

[13:24] **Exercise type.** Nearly all the highest-quality failure research trains the quads — a muscle group that is hard to take to genuine failure. Isolation work for smaller muscles is easier and less demanding to fail on, and the evidence there is genuinely split: one barbell curl study favoured failure over ~3–4 RIR, another found 1–2 RIR similar to failure. His practical suggestion is to push most upper-body isolation work to failure if you are willing and able — a recommendation that outruns two conflicting studies, and he says as much.
