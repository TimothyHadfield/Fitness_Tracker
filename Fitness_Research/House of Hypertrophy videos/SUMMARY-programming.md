# Programming

Twenty-five notes on everything that sits between "which exercise" and "how many reps": how long to
stand around between sets, how to add work over weeks, whether to organise those weeks into blocks,
which intensity techniques buy time and which buy growth, when to stop training, and whether cardio
takes any of it back. They span **2020 to 2026**, and this is the domain where he changed his mind
most — on inter-set stretching he reversed twice in thirty months, and on rest intervals he spent
four years loosening a number rather than tightening it. Where two notes disagree, the newer one is
named in place.

Three of these twenty-five carry **no retrievable reference list at all** — he cites on screen only
and the description carries nothing. They are flagged where they appear, because you cannot check
them.

---

## The organising idea: rest is the sleeper variable

Read these notes in isolation and rest intervals look like a minor comfort question. Read them
together and rest turns out to be the hidden term in half the other arguments in this domain,
because **rest length and set count are substitutes for each other**.

The demonstration is Longo's third arm, reported in the
[2020 deep dive](short-vs-long-rest-intervals-muscle-hypertrophy.md) and again in the
[2022 guide](optimal-rest-time-muscle-strength-12-studies.md): **3 sets at 3 minutes** beat **3 sets
at 1 minute** for quad growth, but **4–5 sets at 1 minute** (averaging **4.5**) matched the
3-minute condition — in roughly the same total session time. Ahtiainen 2005 is the same result at
the other end of the range: **2 minutes matched 5 minutes** when the 2-minute group performed on
average **one extra set per exercise**. Shorter rest lowers the yield per set; it does not forbid
growth, and you can buy the difference back with sets.

That cuts both ways, and it is why he is so suspicious of trials that equate the wrong quantity.
Villanueva 2015 — **22 untrained men, mean age 68**, 8 weeks — found **1 minute beat 4 minutes** for
lean body mass, and it is the one result that would overturn the whole picture. He disqualifies it
in one move: **volume load was equated between groups**, so the 1-minute group necessarily trained
closer to failure on its later sets. It is a proximity-to-failure finding wearing a rest-interval
costume. He runs the same argument in the
[2024 meta-analysis note](rest-periods-new-study-changes-things.md), where that study is one of the
inputs.

The consequence for reading anything else in this library: **a set is not a fixed unit of stimulus,
and its size depends on the rest that preceded it.** Two volume trials prescribing "10 sets" are not
running the same experiment if one rests 60 seconds and the other rests three minutes. His volume
work makes exactly this connection — see
[How Many Sets Per Week: Six Trials That Disagree, and the Rest Interval That May Explain Why](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md)
and [How Many Weekly Sets Per Muscle? Rest Length Changes the Answer](many-sets-per-muscle-examine-science.md)
in the volume domain — and it is one of the more valuable ideas anywhere in this source.

He also kills the mechanism that produced the old prescription. The short-rest rule descends from a
**1987** survey finding bodybuilders resting **10–90 seconds** against powerlifters at **120–420
seconds**, plus **1990** data showing shorter rest produced larger acute growth-hormone spikes.
Acute anabolic hormone elevations do not predict hypertrophy, so the chain breaks at the hormone
step. The cleanest refutation is on the defenders' own terms: McKendry 2016 found **1-minute rest
produced higher acute testosterone but lower myofibrillar protein synthesis than 5 minutes**.

## How long to rest between sets

Four notes across five years. The direction never changes; the precision keeps loosening.

**The 2020 deep dive** ([40 min, 35 refs](short-vs-long-rest-intervals-muscle-hypertrophy.md)) sets
the frame with five compound-exercise training studies, sample sizes and training status stated for
each: Longo (**28 untrained**, leg press, 10 wks), Schoenfeld 2016 (**13 men, at least 6 months
experience**, 7 exercises, 8 wks), Buresh 2009 (**12 men**, 10 wks), Fink 2017 (**14 untrained**,
bench and squat at 40% 1RM, 8 wks) and Villanueva. Four of the five favour the long rest —
**2.5–3 minutes over 1 minute or less**. Verdict on compounds: with sets and effort matched,
**2.5–3 minutes wins**.

The isolation picture is the opposite of a consensus, and he refuses to give a number. Four studies,
four verdicts: Buresh favours 2.5 minutes but is confounded by compounds; Fink (**22 subjects**,
8 wks) found arm CSA favoured **20RM with 30 seconds rest** over **8RM with 3 minutes**; Ozaki 2018
(**9 untrained men**, unilateral curls) found **3 min, 90 s and a five-load drop set all
equivalent**; Fink 2018 (**16 men**, pushdowns only) found **drop sets beat 3 sets with 90 s rest**.
His answer is "rest as long as is comfortable and practical," which given that spread is the correct
answer rather than a dodge.

**The 2022 guide** ([12 studies](optimal-rest-time-muscle-strength-12-studies.md)) supplies the
better organising axis and it is the single most useful reframe in this section: **rest should track
how much muscle mass the exercise recruits, not whether you are training for size or strength.**
Large-muscle-mass work — compounds plus lower-body isolation — wants **2.5–3 minutes**;
small-muscle-mass upper-body isolation tolerates short rest and *possibly* prefers it. For strength:
**3 minutes beats 1.5 minutes and 30 seconds**, and **3 and 5 minutes are statistically
equivalent** (de Salles 2010), with leg press numerically favouring 5 and him explicitly refusing to
read a non-significant single measure as a finding. Practical addition: if 2.5–3 minutes is
unaffordable, **superset unrelated muscle groups** — you shorten the session while still resting
long between sets of the same exercise.

**The 2024 note** ([the first meta-analysis on rest and hypertrophy](rest-periods-new-study-changes-things.md))
is where he softens rather than hardens. Longer rest (**over 60 s**) beats shorter (**60 s or
less**) for arm and thigh growth. Split four ways, the **intermediate** band shows the largest point
effect and the **widest credible interval**, every band overlaps heavily, and the network analysis
shows uncertainty about any benefit beyond intermediate durations. The paper floats **~90 seconds**
as possibly enough; he says outright not to treat that as a conclusion, and points out the inputs:
it coded the progressive-rest-reduction studies as a flat **80 s**, included the confounded
Villanueva trial, and included a 2007 study on untrained women comparing **20 s to 80 s**.

**Where this lands, and the gap that matters more than the number.** Current position: **rest as
long as you feel you need — longer on compounds, shorter on isolation.** The important admission is
that **no study compares 2.5–3 minutes to 2 minutes**. Every compound study in his evidence base
compares a long rest to **1 minute or less**, so nothing here speaks to 3 versus 5 minutes, and the
practical difference between most real prescriptions lives in exactly that untested gap.

**Who can rest shorter.** Recovery between sets tracks **your absolute strength on the lift**, not
your sex or age directly. Ratamess 2002, part one: **22 men and women with 1+ year experience**,
bench at 75% 1RM with 1, 2 or 3 minutes — women sustained more reps at every interval, but adjusting
for bench strength erased the difference (mean 1RM **36 kg** vs **112 kg**). Part two tested it
directly: **22 men split by bench 1RM (81 kg vs 141 kg)**, and the weaker group sustained reps
better at every interval. He then presents the counter-evidence himself — Fulco 2009 matched men and
women for adductor pollicis strength and the women still recovered better (**71% vs 65%** of
baseline at one minute). Practical reads, both explicitly labelled extrapolations from acute data:
**1–2 minutes on compounds for women**, and **1–2 minutes for older lifters** (from Theou 2008 and
Bottaro 2010, both single-session torque studies).

**No working mechanism.** He raises central fatigue — the field's leading explanation for his own
conclusion — and then dismantles it. The muscle-mass premise looks backwards (isometric elbow
flexion produced *more* central fatigue than knee extension, which produced more than cycling), and
the timing does not work: central fatigue after 2-minute maximal contractions clears in **30 seconds
to 1 minute**, so for typical hypertrophy sets it should clear in **30 seconds or less**. He also
rules out volume load, arithmetically: 3 sets to failure at **60% 1RM** accumulate roughly
**7,020 kg** against **4,320 kg** for 3 sets at **80%**, while growing the same muscle. Volume load
is not tracking the stimulus, so it cannot be the mediator. Hard sets near failure are.

## How to progress

Two trials, converging, and a reframe that outlasts both.

The [2022 RCT](best-progressive-overload-method.md) — the first randomised comparison — put trained
lifters on four lower-body exercises, one group adding load inside **8–12 reps**, the other holding
session-one loads and adding reps. Growth was similar across mid quad, lateral quad, both
gastrocnemius heads and soleus (mid-quad slightly favouring the **rep** group); Smith machine squat
1RM favoured load by **2 kg**. It supersedes an earlier Brazilian comparison favouring load, which
was not randomised and pooled two separate studies with different frequencies and durations.

The [2024 within-subject trial](best-progressive-overload-way.md) is the better design: **39
previously untrained people, unilateral leg extension, 4 sets, 2–3×/week for 10 weeks**. One leg
adjusted load to keep failing inside **9–12 reps**; the other held the session-one load and drifted
from an average of **9 reps to 15**. Vastus lateralis growth and strength gains were similar.
Because each subject was in both conditions, genetics, diet and lifestyle cannot confound it.

**The reframe is the durable part.** Progressive overload is **not training harder than last time —
it is what keeps each session as hard as usual.** Adaptation means you can now lift more weight for
the same reps *or* more reps at the same weight; doing either restores the same proximity to
failure. Successful overload is therefore **evidence that adaptation happened, not its cause**.

Practical boundaries. Since roughly **6–35 reps** grow similarly near failure, rep progression has a
ceiling — past about **35 reps**, add load. On **small-muscle exercises** where load jumps are
unavailable without microplates, adding a rep is the realistic route, and as training age rises rep
additions become far more common than load additions. Within-session variants also work: one study
had all subjects curl to failure from a **10RM** load, one group holding it across sets, one
dropping **5%** on the final two sets, one dropping **10%** — biceps growth similar in all three,
with the 10% group rating sessions **slightly easier** despite still training to failure.

**For maximal strength he declines the null**, and rightly. Both trials tested at eight-plus reps,
and the 2022 trial's strength test was a **Smith machine squat neither group trained**. Sets of
**2–4 reps** beat **8–12** for bench and squat 1RM, and holding a low rep range requires load
progression by definition.

**One claim to treat as framing, not finding.** He argues that adding sets or sessions "is not
progressive overload" because it adds work rather than maintaining hard work per set. That is a
definitional move, and his own cited set-progression study — trained lifters escalating to **42–52
weekly quad sets** — found more growth from adding sets. Take the empirical result (load and rep
progression are interchangeable for hypertrophy) and leave the sets argument as vocabulary.

**Shrinking rest is a third progression channel**, and a legitimate one.
[Two Souza-Junior trials](decrease-rest-between-sets-overtime-more-hypertrophy.md) (**about 21–22
subjects total, ~11 per group**, trained men aged 20–22, 6 sessions/week, 8 weeks) held one group at
**2 minutes** while the other tapered from **2 minutes to 30 seconds**. No significant difference in
squat or bench 1RM or in thigh and arm CSA. The volume-load data is the actual finding: the constant
group overloaded by **adding weight**, the tapering group by **removing rest** with load flat — and
in study two, bench load **dropped sharply in weeks 3–4** as rest shrank. Same outcomes either way.
Caveats he supplies and then partly ignores: both studies come from the same research group, both
ran only 8 weeks, nobody has tested holding **30 seconds** for months afterwards, and **~11 per
group** is underpowered enough to hide a real difference — which undercuts the equivalence claim as
hard as it undercuts the percentages he refuses to read.

## Does periodisation matter

For size, no. The [periodisation note](periodization-size-vs-strength-science-says.md) rests on the
**2022 Danish meta-analysis (Moesgaard/Mosegaard et al.)**, which found **no significant hypertrophy
difference between periodised and non-periodised training** in trained and untrained subjects, and
**no difference between linear and undulating** either.

For strength there is an effect and a confound. Periodisation produced greater 1RM gains — but the
periodised arms typically spent more time at lower reps and heavier loads, which independently
improves 1RM (in one study the non-periodised group trained only at 8–10 reps while the periodised
group included sets of 4–5). The study that would settle it — periodised versus
non-periodised-but-heavy — has not been run. **What survives the confound: undulating beat linear
for 1RM in trained subjects only**, since both models used heavy low-rep work equally often.

He hedges the hypertrophy null rather than banking it, for three reasons worth keeping. Many
periodised arms dropped to **4 reps or fewer**, which is not the best growth range. Most studies ran
**12 weeks or less**; the longest, a **9-month** Kraemer trial, found greater fat-free mass in the
undulating group (indirect measure, untrained women). And a 12-week study in older women found
muscle gains favouring a **15/10/5** pyramid over **12/10/8**. The strongest argument for varying is
not average effects but **individual differences**: some people grow the thigh better at **8–12
reps** and others at **27–31**, and it may differ between muscles within one person, so varying is a
hedge against not knowing which you are. His position: vary if you want to, and the evidence is not
strong enough to say you are missing out if you don't. The reason to vary is **not** fibre-type
matching — he says the research does not support that.

**Does a strength block first make the hypertrophy block work better?**
[The 2021 note](building-strength-first-enhances-muscle-growth-later.md) covers Carvalho: **26 men
averaging 4.5 years' experience**, squatting at least 1.5× bodyweight, pair-matched on VL thickness.
One group did **4 sets of 8–12 to failure with 1 min rest for 8 weeks**; the other did **3 weeks of
4 sets of 1–3 to failure with 3 min rest** then five weeks identical to the first. At week 3 VL
thickness **favoured the pure hypertrophy group**; by week 8 the strength-first group had grown
more. He still declines to recommend it, and rejects the authors' mechanism — they credited heavier
loads meaning more mechanical tension, and he points out that recruitment and force scale with
**effort, not absolute load**, so two groups both taking 8–12 to failure are at the same relative
stimulus. His alternatives (carried-over neural adaptations, resensitisation to a habitual rep
range) are labelled speculation, and he notes he has **no strong evidence** for the second. Three
indirect lines run the other way: linear beat reverse-linear for fat-free mass in trained women,
alternating rep ranges does not beat a fixed range, and linear and undulating models are equivalent
for growth. **This video's description carries no reference list**, so every study in it —
Carvalho, Prestes, the Grgic meta-analysis — is unverified.

## Does exercise order matter

Thinly covered, and what exists points to "not much, with one exception".

**Pre-exhaustion** — isolation immediately before a compound for the same muscle — is the direct
test. [The 2025 trial](training-style-intense-but-work.md): **41 trained individuals**, twice weekly
for **8 weeks**, four leg exercises, everything to failure in **8–12 reps**. The pre-exhaustion
group paired **leg curl into RDL** and **leg extension into Smith machine squat**. Traditional
training produced **slightly greater** thigh growth and body composition change, with real
statistical uncertainty; strength and endurance were similar, which he flags as surprising in both
directions. Pre-exhaustion cost **higher perceived exertion** and **lower volume load** — harder
sessions, lighter weights, no bonus — and bought about **36% shorter sessions**. Intermittent lower
back pain was reported, mostly on RDLs, but only **22% of subjects had trained RDLs beforehand**, so
unaccustomed exposure is the likelier explanation than the movement. **The video's only source is an
unpublished preprint.**

**Cardio before or after lifting** is the real exception, covered below: if the two are in the same
session, **lift first**. And the [time-of-day note](morning-vs-evening-training-muscle-hypertrophy.md)
supplies a useful methodological warning about order findings — the Küüsmaa trial found leg press
1RM gains similar whether lifting came before or after cardio, while a meta-analysis pooling nine
other studies found lifting first is better. One study's null routinely gets overturned when the
literature catches up.

## Advanced and time-saving techniques, one by one

The distinction to hold throughout: **an effect on performance within a session is not an effect on
hypertrophy over a training block.** Most of these techniques trade one for the other.

**Antagonist supersets** ([11 studies](increase-performance-while-saving-time-11-studies.md)).
Pairing opposing muscles back to back — row/bench, leg curl/leg extension, curl/extension —
reliably *raises* acute rep performance, and the rest interval between the paired exercises is what
decides it. **Minimal rest raises reps; 2+ minutes does not.** Maia 2014 found going straight into
leg extensions after curls beat rest intervals of 30 s, 1, 3 and 5 minutes. Robbins 2010 is the
informative counter-case: **2 minutes** between the paired exercises produced no volume-load
advantage — but still finished quicker. The long-term evidence is nearly absent and he does not hide
it. Robbins 2009 (**15 men, 1+ year, 8 weeks**) found 1RM gains not different, with session duration
**~20 min traditional versus ~10 min superset** — but used the 2-minute pairing that does not raise
performance anyway, and never measured hypertrophy. Fink 2022 (**23 untrained**, 8 weeks,
resistance-band curls and extensions) found CSA and isometric strength no different and
**numerically favouring the traditional group**; his explanation — that bands let subjects reach
**30–60 reps** where every positive study used **10RM or heavier** — is labelled speculation resting
on the unproven co-activation mechanism. Honest summary: **a time-saver that probably costs nothing
and might help.** Stretching the antagonist during rest gives a similar acute bump (three
near-identical Brazilian studies on chest stretching before seated rows) with **no long-term
research at all**.

**Drop sets** have the best data of the group. The
[within-subject trial](they-trained-one-side-drop-sets-vs.md) ran **29 trained lifters averaging
7.6 years**, one arm each way, preacher curls and kneeling pulldowns, 2–3×/week for **10 weeks**.
Normal arm: **4 sets of 8–12 to failure** on curls, **2 sets** on pulldowns. Drop arm: **2 drop
series** on curls, **1** on pulldowns, load cut **8% whenever concentric velocity fell 20%** below
the segment's fastest rep — roughly **1–3 reps between drops**, about four drops, **16–24 total reps
per series**. Elbow flexor thickness **numerically favoured normal sets** but sat inside the
pre-declared smallest effect of interest, in sessions **10 minutes shorter — 60% less time**. Two
meta-analyses, each pooling five studies on conventional equipment, agree, with time savings of
**30–70%**. Strength is the exception: he argues normal sets keep an edge, because some pooled
normal-set conditions used lighter peak loads and because within-subject designs are confounded for
strength by the **cross-education effect** (no such transfer exists for hypertrophy, so the design
is fine for growth). Practical position: drop sets on a **few** movements, ideally isolation or
machine work, or as a final load-reduction set. Caveats he raises himself: training was only
**semi-supervised**, subjects reported discomfort, and the study was **funded by Tonal Systems**,
whose device was used and which subjects kept.

**The rest-interval tension is worth carrying.** A single set with multiple drops is effectively
**zero rest**, and it does not lose to three normal sets. He gives two candidate explanations
without committing: the long-rest evidence comes mostly from **large-muscle-mass exercises** while
the drop-set studies used arm isolation, and some drop-set conditions accumulate **more hard reps**
(Ozaki's drop arm is arguably five sets against three) — though that does not explain Fink 2018,
where both did three. His hedge if you want one: use the Ozaki style, where the drop protocol
effectively gives you more sets than you would otherwise do.

**The 3/7 method** ([one study](3-7-method-build-more-muscle.md)). At **70% 1RM** (roughly a 12RM),
perform **3, 4, 5, 6 then 7 reps with 15 seconds between mini-sets**. Stragier 2019 — **43
previously untrained men, twice weekly for 8 weeks**, a machine biceps exercise, the sequence run
**twice per session with 2.5 min between bouts** — found greater biceps long-head thickness and
greater 1RM than **8 sets of 6 at 70% with 2.5 min rest**. This note contains his best piece of
self-argument: his own first objection was that 8×6 at 70% is a strawman control, and he defeats it
with arithmetic — at 70% most people manage about **12 reps**, so set one sits at roughly **6 RIR**,
and assuming one rep closer per set, **5 of the 8 sets land within 3 reps of failure**, which is not
meaningfully suboptimal. He rejects the authors' metabolic-stress mechanism and offers none of his
own: he does not know why it works. The clear benefit is time — his own back-of-envelope estimate,
not reported session durations — **~5–6 minutes per session versus ~19–20**. The strength result
proves less than it looks, since the comparison was never against genuinely heavy training.

**Rest-pause**, the nearest relative, has only two studies and neither settles anything. Enes 2021
(**19 men, 2+ years**, 8 weeks) found lateral thigh thickness similar — but the rest-pause group
**did not go to failure** and did only one extra rep bout per set. Prestes 2019 (**18 subjects**, 6
weeks) found quad thickness greater for rest-pause, with a serious confound he catches and most
summaries miss: **the normal group applied no progressive overload**, so their proximity to failure
drifted further away as they got stronger while the always-to-failure group progressed
automatically.

**Inter-set stretching** is his cleanest documented reversal, told across three notes.
**September 2020** ([cautiously positive](stretching-between-sets-help-build-more-muscle.md)):
Evangelista, **29 untrained men, 8 weeks**, a **30-second pain-free stretch inside 90 seconds of
rest**, 4×8–12 to failure — **only vastus lateralis reached statistical significance**; elbow
flexors, triceps and rectus femoris trended without reaching it. Two boundary conditions were
already visible: painful pre-training stretching produced *less* VL growth (Junior 2017), and **60
seconds** of quad stretching cost more force than **30** (Siatras 2008).
**October 2021** ([two new studies](stretch-between-sets-more-hypertrophy.md)): Nakamura (**16
untrained men**, within-subject, flywheel squat, 5 weeks) found **nothing at any of twelve
regions**; Wadhi (**26 trained men, ~3 years**, loaded cable chest stretch at ~15% of working load,
8 weeks) found nothing at either pec region. He states plainly that this is a self-correction.
**March 2023** ([the fourth study](stretching-between-sets-transform-muscle-strength.md)): **22
trained subjects, 8 weeks**, an FST-7-style arm programme at **28 weekly isolation sets** per muscle
(**46** counting compounds), **45 seconds** rest, stretches held for the **full 45 seconds at
70–90% discomfort**. Nothing anywhere except lower long-head triceps — and he refuses the win after
reading the **individual data points behind the group means** and finding a few outliers plausibly
driving it. That move is the most rigorous thing in the video.

**Reconciled: three of five published studies find nothing, one finds a small single-region effect,
the original finds one significant site of four, and none finds harm.** Strength is never harmed —
in the 2023 study the stretching group used **lighter loads** across sets and still matched 1RM
gains, so cost and benefit both look like zero. The studies are too heterogeneous to pool (trained
versus untrained, loaded versus unloaded, 30 s versus 60 s versus 45 s, flywheel versus
conventional), so his verdict is **not validated**, not refuted. The one live hypothesis is that
**transitioning straight into a loaded stretch off the last eccentric**, while the passive elements
are still stiff, may be a different stimulus from walking over to stretch — supported by one small
soleus finding and one unpublished conference abstract that he then **excludes from his own summary
table** for being unpublished. The same 2023 note also delivers an accidental verdict on **FST-7**:
growth was lacklustre for many subjects with **regional size losses**, on an abrupt jump of **9–11
more weekly isolation sets per muscle** than subjects were used to.

**Flexing between sets** resolves more cleanly
([note](flexing-between-sets-enhance-hypertrophy.md)). Schoenfeld 2020: **27 men, 1+ year, 8 weeks,
3×/week**, six exercises, **3 sets to failure at 8–12RM with 2 min rest**, the isometric group
holding a maximal contraction for the **first 30 seconds of every rest**. No benefit at elbow
flexors, triceps or lateral thigh; **mid-thigh slightly favoured the holds** — and the explanation
is exercise selection, not flexing. Squats and leg presses train the **rectus femoris** poorly
(Kubo 2019 found squats at two depths failed to grow it at all), so a seated maximal quad hold is a
low-grade leg extension filling the one gap in the programme. The honest reading is not "flexing
builds muscle" but "**an isometric leg extension between squat sets partly substitutes for a leg
extension**". Note also that the positive interval depended on removing two outliers post hoc, which
he reports openly.

## Deloads, breaks and time off

[The 2022 breaks note](secret-long-term-muscle-strength-gains-maybe.md) covers Ogasawara: **14
previously untrained men, 24 weeks**, one group training continuously, one running **6 weeks on / 3
weeks off**, both doing **3 sets of 10 bench at 75% 1RM three times a week with 2–3 minutes rest**
during training weeks. Pec growth, triceps growth and bench 1RM matched at week 24 despite the break
group doing **25% less total training**. The shape of the curves is the interesting part: the
continuous group ran at **~15% pec growth in the first six weeks and only ~5% in the last six**,
while the break group ran at **~15% first** and **~15–20% last** — their gains never decelerated.
The candidate mechanism is **desensitisation of the anabolic signalling pathway**: p70S6K
phosphorylation falls after roughly **7 sessions** in humans, and a **10-day break restores it**
(12 days in the companion rat study). That leads him to a **10-day break recommendation that no
trial has ever tested**, and he says so.

The direct test arrived in 2023: [39 trained individuals, 9 weeks, week 5 completely
off](train-less-more-muscle-new-training-breaks.md). Every lower-body growth measure — rectus
femoris and VL at three sites, both gastrocnemius heads, soleus — matched the continuous group, on
**40 fewer lower-body sets, about 11% less work**. Strength was **slightly worse** (isometric leg
extension and Smith machine squat 1RM). It does not support the resensitisation hypothesis, though
nine weeks may be too short to test it. A detail rarely reported: the break group **felt lethargic
after the break rather than refreshed**.

**Practical position: take breaks or deloads when your body or mind wants one, not on a calendar.**
And the honest gap — **deloads are not tested at all.** Neither study reduced volume; both stopped
completely. He is sceptical that a deload resensitises anything, on the grounds that reduced volume
still stimulates the pathway, and labels that pure speculation.

**Detraining and muscle memory** are handled only as much as the breaks question requires, so treat
the timelines as indicative rather than settled. Several studies show strength and size losses
inside **3–4 weeks**; one found **fibre cross-sectional area not significantly reduced after 8
days**, which is what makes **10 days** the interesting number. Losses during Ogasawara's break
blocks **rebounded quickly** on return, and humans regain lost size and strength far faster than
they built it. He also interrogates his own outcome measure rather than assuming it: some of what is
lost may be **glycogen and its associated water** — though doubling relative quadriceps glycogen
raised vastus CSA by only **3.5%**, and in a training study glycogen scaled with fibre size rather
than driving the measurement, so he concludes it contributes little.

## How hard is it to overtrain

[The 2025 note](overtraining-easier-think.md) is his most disciplined video in this domain, and the
title is a question he answers *no*. A systematic review of **22 studies that deliberately tried to
induce overreaching or overtraining found 10 produced no sustained performance decrease at all**.

The definitions do the work: **functional overreaching** costs days to a few weeks then improves;
**non-functional overreaching** costs weeks with no improvement after; **overtraining** costs
months. Everything short of the last two is ordinary fatigue.

What failed to cause it: **18 trained individuals** doing a two-week intensification of over **40
weekly quad sets** — hack squats and leg presses six days a week plus front and back squats on the
seventh, all **3 sets of 6RM** — finished with **leg press strength improved four days after the
final session**. What did cause it: **11 trained men performing 10 sets of one-rep maxes six days a
week for two weeks — 60 1RM attempts weekly** — losing strength and needing **2–8 weeks** to return
to normal loads, with two medically diagnosed with knee overuse syndrome. The instructive detail is
what did *not* change there: **testosterone was unchanged and maximum reps at 70% 1RM were not
reduced** even as maximal strength fell. That is why the field defines overtraining by **recovery
time**, not by markers or symptoms.

**The real risk is too much too soon**, and it is a different mechanism. **Six untrained men** doing
**5 sets of 10 eccentric-only curls at 110% of 1RM** had elbow flexor size fall **below baseline for
numerous weeks**. Case reports after **two sets of 25 maximal eccentric curls** record strength loss
lasting **43–47 days** and one clinical **rhabdomyolysis** diagnosis — non-functional overreaching
reached in a **single session**. He bounds it properly (six subjects; a **3%** incidence rate; many
similar protocols producing nothing) and concludes the **repeated bout effect** would likely have
prevented all of it with gradual ramping.

What gradual ramping looks like: two companion 12-week studies, trained men and trained women, squat
/ leg press / leg extension twice weekly at **2 RIR except the last set of each exercise**. Men went
from a **22-set** control to **42 weekly quad sets** (average 32) and **52** (average 37); women
from **22** to **26** (average 21) and **38** (average 28). **Strength gains were best in the
highest-set groups**, size tended to follow, and there were **no injury-related dropouts**. His
caveats are the right ones: only quads were escalated, hamstrings held at **8 weekly sets**, other
muscles untracked — evidence for **specialisation phases**, not for high volume everywhere. The soft
spot he does not raise: a review's failure to induce overtraining partly reflects **ethical limits
on what researchers may impose**.

## Fatigue: what it is, whether you can measure it, and whether it matters

[The CNS fatigue note](most-misunderstood-muscle-building-area.md) fixes a term that is almost
always used wrongly. **CNS fatigue is reduced electrical drive from brain and spinal cord to the
muscle**, measured by **voluntary activation** or inferred from strength loss in an untrained
contralateral limb. It is not "feeling tired", and it is not **peripheral fatigue** — changes inside
the muscle that also lower force. Most of the corrections in the video come from conflating the two.

Three beliefs it overturns. **Heavier loads do not produce more CNS fatigue — lighter ones do**:
40% loads produced more than 80%, and the clinching design trained one leg at each a week apart,
finding slower recovery after the 40% session **and strength loss in the opposite leg** that the 80%
session did not cause. The driver looks like **contraction duration**, not load. **The deadlift is
probably not uniquely fatiguing** — two of three studies fail to support the belief, including the
best-designed (12 trained men, deadlift/squat/bench, **4 sets to failure at 80% 1RM**, comparable
swelling, soreness, damage and performance loss); the third found the deadlift recovered slowest but
"far from substantially" so. **Training to failure produces more overall fatigue, but the only study
measuring CNS fatigue specifically found it similar** to stopping short — the extra fatigue appears
peripheral and damage-related, and he notes this contradicted his own assumption.

Then the move that matters: **does it matter?** Lower reps minimise neuromuscular fatigue per unit
of stimulus, but if high-rep fatigue were counterproductive it would show up as less growth, and it
does not — **8–12 versus 25–35 reps to failure**, three sets per exercise three times a week for 8
weeks, produced similar elbow flexor, triceps and quad growth. Three situations where he would still
avoid failure: **concurrent cardio or sprint work**, **older individuals**, and **poor lifestyle
recovery — high stress, poor sleep, or dieting**.

**Detection: hand grip strength is not a valid indicator** — at least two studies find no
correlation, which is a straightforward debunk of a common gym-app metric. Vertical jump is his
speculative preference while he simultaneously states **no study has properly tested it**. Recovery
problems announce themselves through **performance stagnation, regression and subjective feeling**;
tracking beyond that is optional.

**Recovery capacity is trainable**, and this is the most useful finding here. In a Greek study,
untrained subjects doing **5 sets of 15 maximal eccentric quad contractions once weekly for 10
weeks** went from damage and strength loss lasting **at least five days** in week 1 to **virtually
full recovery within 1–2 days by week 10**. The ceiling case: **13 trained men** trained a programme
**four days running** at ~9.5 RPE with **four hard sets each for chest and quads per session**, and
bench and squat performance held constant — recovery inside **24 hours**. Two individual-difference
signals he flags for corroboration: **two to four subjects** in the Greek study still fatigued
notably at week 10, and in the four-day study the **strongest individuals (bench 10RM at 1.25×
bodyweight) failed to replicate day-one performance**, hinting at a strength threshold beyond which
recovery gets harder.

## Stress, sleep and recovery

**Sleep is barely covered in this domain** — it appears only as one item in the list of lifestyle
factors that would make him avoid failure training, and as one of three things (with nutrition and
stress) said to reduce whatever cardio interference exists. Anyone needing a sleep position should
not look for one here.

**Psychological stress** gets a note to itself
([Minimize This to Max Muscle and Strength](minimize-max-muscle-strength.md)), and it is the weakest
sourced in the domain: **the video cites on screen only, the description carries no reference list,
and the narration gives no author names — every study in it is unverified.** What it claims: in an
acute study, **210 students screened, 31 analysed (18 low-stress, 13 high-stress)**, a hard
leg-press session (ramping to a 10RM, then **six sets to volitional failure**) left low-stress
subjects recovering maximal isometric force **within 48 hours** and high-stress subjects only just
recovering **within 96 hours** — with baseline measures, workloads and heart-rate responses similar
during the session, which is what makes it convincing rather than suggestive. Over **12 weeks**,
**81 men and 54 women** in three four-week mesocycles: **bench and squat 1RM gains greater in the
low-stress group**; limb circumference favoured them numerically but not statistically, and he flags
both that the study may be underpowered and that **circumference is a crude proxy**. His defensible
conclusion is that stress interferes with **strength**; the hypertrophy case is unresolved. The
programming takeaway is the good one: in periods of **unpredictable, uncontrollable** stress,
deliberately reduce load or take a break, since muscle memory makes lost ground cheap to regain. The
stress-reduction half — meditation, breath work, nature, walking — is general wellbeing evidence
with **no hypertrophy or strength outcome anywhere in it**.

**Time of day** is a non-factor ([note](morning-vs-evening-training-muscle-hypertrophy.md)). The
three studies cited for evening superiority are: one **never peer-reviewed or published** (a 2005
conference presentation on bodybuilders, existing only as a blog write-up), one whose difference was
**not statistically significant** (Sedliak: quad volume **3.5% evening versus 2.7% morning**, with
**9 and 7 subjects**), and one genuine result — Küüsmaa, **42 untrained men, 24 weeks**, where the
four groups were similar at 12 weeks and the two evening groups had grown more by 24. Grgic's **2019
meta-analysis** found **no significant hypertrophy difference**, and no strength difference once
tests were averaged across times of day — the evening strength advantage is an **untrained**
phenomenon that disappears once you train consistently at a given time. **Train when your schedule
allows.** He under-claims here rather than over-claims, discounting both the non-significant result
favouring evenings and the significant one, for different and defensible reasons.

## Cardio and concurrent training

Three notes, in descending order of how well they are evidenced.

**The interference effect has shrunk** ([note](combine-cardio-weights-should.md)). Updated
meta-analyses find **combining cardio with lifting does not compromise hypertrophy or strength**,
including a subgroup analysis restricted to trained individuals, superseding Wilson's 2012 pooling.
The proposed molecular mechanism — cardio triggering signalling that inhibits the anabolic response
— is supported in animals but not humans. He bounds the conclusion by the protocols that produced
it, which is the important part: **cardio 2–4×/week for 20–45 minutes, lifting 2–4×/week with 3–4
exercises per muscle for a few sets each**. Extreme volumes on both sides almost certainly still
interfere, and Hickson's 1980 origin study — five lower-body strength sessions plus six cardio
sessions weekly, **eleven sessions a week** — is the evidence for that. Three caveats survive:
extreme volumes, "trained" not being "highly trained", and **power being genuinely compromised** in
both the 2012 and one 2022 analysis. **The one real recommendation: separate sessions.** A 2021
meta-analysis in trained individuals found same-session cardio lowered strength gains where
separate-session cardio did not; hypertrophy was not assessed and he flags that he is *presuming*
the same holds. **If same session, lift first.** Mode does not matter — running and cycling
subgroups change nothing. One meta-analysis found HIIT compromised fibre-type-specific growth while
continuous did not, from the same analysis finding whole-muscle size unaffected by either, and he
treats that as a reason to **distrust the biopsy measurement** rather than avoid HIIT: biopsies
sample few fibres, repeat biopsies from one person vary substantially, and where two measures
disagree inside one analysis he goes with the whole-muscle outcome.

**Lifting cannot replace cardio** ([note](lifting-replace-cardio-true-false.md)). Lifting does
produce real cardiovascular adaptations — increased **capillary and mitochondrial density**, and
eccentric cardiac hypertrophy is not uncommon in bodybuilders — so the "lifting isn't cardio at all"
position is wrong. But in **16 well-trained powerlifters and strongmen**, 8 weeks of HIIT lifting
(**seven sets at RPE 8–9 using 60% 1RM**, squat or deadlift, sets lasting **16–30 seconds**) raised
VO2max **less** than duration-matched HIIT cycling, with leg extension strength gains similar in
both. His explanation is that lifting **does not involve constant effort** — easier eccentrics,
pauses between reps. The biggest VO2max responders to lifting are simply those with the **worst
baseline VO2max**, so a trained lifter should expect little. **Circuit training** is the exception
worth knowing: a 2017 Spanish meta-analysis found notable VO2max increases, and Alcaraz 2011 (**26
trained men, 8 weeks**, **35 seconds** between exercises in a round) found bench and half-squat
strength and lean body mass gains **similar** to traditional training. The genuinely useful
inversion closes the note: **capillary and mitochondrial adaptations are local to the muscles
trained**, so running does nothing for your upper body, and deliberately using resistance work to
build local cardiovascular adaptations where your cardio does not reach is a sound idea. The
mortality figures he leans on in both cardio notes — **21% lower all-cause mortality risk** for
lifting alone against **40%** for lifting plus cardio — are observational associations, and he
caveats them less carefully than he caveats the training data.

**Cardio may help you grow** ([note](cardio-increase-muscle-growth-here.md)) is the weakest of the
three and he says so. Thomas 2022: **14 untrained individuals**, one leg cycling **3× weekly for 6
weeks** while the other did nothing, then **both legs training bilaterally for 10 weeks**. Slow- and
fast-twitch fibre growth **tended to be superior in the pre-conditioned leg**; cycling had
significantly raised **capillary density**, those increases correlated with later hypertrophy, and
high-responding legs had more capillaries than low. The chain — cardio to capillaries to hypertrophy
— therefore rests on **one 14-person untrained single-leg study** plus correlational and
older-adult data (Snijders 2017, Moro 2019, both finding lower capillary density accompanying lower
fibre hypertrophy), and the fibre measurements come from the biopsies he elsewhere calls unreliable.
The options he offers: run cardio **concurrently**, run a **cardio block before a lifting block**,
or **alternate** concurrent and lifting-only phases. He volunteers every limitation; the title does
not.

## Weak points

**Three of the twenty-five notes summarise videos with no retrievable reference list**, and they are
not trivial ones. [Psychological stress](minimize-max-muscle-strength.md) cites on screen only with
no author names at all, so its headline numbers — 96 hours versus 48, the 12-week 1RM result — are
entirely uncheckable. [The strength-block video](building-strength-first-enhances-muscle-growth-later.md)
names Carvalho, Prestes and Grgic in the description text with no links. [The 2024 rest
meta-analysis note](rest-periods-new-study-changes-things.md) has no description list either and
relies on a **creator-maintained web page** that could change or disappear. Add three more resting
on **unpublished preprints**: [pre-exhaustion](training-style-intense-but-work.md) (one source, a
preprint), [drop sets](they-trained-one-side-drop-sets-vs.md), and
[the week-off trial](train-less-more-muscle-new-training-breaks.md).

**Numbers presented for populations the evidence does not cover.** The **1–2 minutes for women** and
**1–2 minutes for older lifters** figures in the
[2020 rest guide](short-vs-long-rest-intervals-muscle-hypertrophy.md) come entirely from
single-session rep-sustainment and torque studies; the only long-term rest study in older adults is
the one he disqualifies. He labels them extrapolations, but numbers travel further than their
caveats.

**Mechanism-forward conclusions with thin outcome data.** The
[cardio-capillary chain](cardio-increase-muscle-growth-here.md); the
[10-day break](secret-long-term-muscle-strength-gains-maybe.md) that no trial has tested; the
[vertical jump](most-misunderstood-muscle-building-area.md) he recommends while stating no study has
assessed it; and the [short-rest adaptation hypothesis](rest-periods-new-study-changes-things.md)
built on three indirect strands, one of which confounds rest with load, rep range and occlusion.

**Underpowered nulls read as equivalence.** The
[tapering-rest studies](decrease-rest-between-sets-overtime-more-hypertrophy.md) are two null
results from the same research group at **~11 per group**; he applies the small-sample argument to
the percentages favouring the tapering group but not to his own headline claim of equivalence. The
[drop-set trial's](they-trained-one-side-drop-sets-vs.md) "practically equivalent" framing rests on
the authors' pre-declared smallest effect while the raw direction favoured normal sets, under
semi-supervision and industry funding.

**One definitional claim doing empirical work.** That adding sets or sessions
["is not progressive overload"](best-progressive-overload-way.md) is vocabulary, not a finding, and
his own cited set-progression study cuts against it. The
[first progression note](best-progressive-overload-method.md) also defers two technical objections
to a pinned YouTube comment that is not retrievable.

**Citation errors inherited from his descriptions.** The same **Ahtiainen 2005** study is linked as
PMID 15507691 in [the 2022 rest guide](optimal-rest-time-muscle-strength-12-studies.md) and as
PMID 16095405 in [the 2020 deep dive](short-vs-long-rest-intervals-muscle-hypertrophy.md) and
[the tapering-rest note](decrease-rest-between-sets-overtime-more-hypertrophy.md). Several PMIDs in
the 2020 rest note do not match their stated years (Ratamess "2002" resolving to a 2012-era PMID,
Fulco "2009" to a 1999-era PMID, Zghal "2005" to a 2015-era PMID). The 2022 Danish periodisation
meta-analysis appears as **"Mosegaard"** in
[one note](periodization-size-vs-strength-science-says.md) and **"Moesgaard"** in
[another](secret-long-term-muscle-strength-gains-maybe.md), and the same MDPI signalling link is
attributed to **Jacko** in the [2022 breaks note](secret-long-term-muscle-strength-gains-maybe.md)
and to **Ogasawara** in [the 2023 one](train-less-more-muscle-new-training-breaks.md).

**What is genuinely untested, and should be said rather than filled in.** No study compares
**2.5–3 minutes to 2 minutes** of rest. No study tests a **deload** — every break study stopped
completely. No study tests the **10-day break** he recommends. No study tests **holding a 30-second
rest** for months after tapering into it. No long-term study exists on **antagonist stretching** at
all, and the long-term hypertrophy evidence for **antagonist supersets** is two studies, both
limited, neither showing an advantage.
