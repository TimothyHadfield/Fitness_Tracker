# Exercise Selection When Recruitment Is Individual: Three Principles That Aren't on the Usual List

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "UNDERRATED Exercise Selection Principles for Muscle Growth" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E61a27ODSq0)) · 2022-08-15 · 11 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **Neuromechanical matching** — the nervous system preferentially recruits whichever muscle or region has the best leverage for the movement — is mostly shared across people, but **not always**. Where leverage differs between individuals, so does which muscle an exercise actually trains.
- Consequence: another lifter's report that an exercise "built their X" does **not** transfer. Track your own training and run your own small experiments.
- Autoregulated exercise selection beat a fixed programme for lean mass in trained men over 9 weeks — the only outcome study in the video.
- On compounds, **your technique biases the load toward your already-stronger muscles**, which can silently work against your goal.
- A block of isolation work for one muscle may increase that muscle's involvement in a compound lift — on EMG only, and from one study.

## Where recruitment is shared, and where it isn't

The shared case is his own example: the middle deltoid has the greatest moment arm for shoulder abduction, a finding consistent across the moment-arm literature ([Hik & Ackland 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30411350/)), so lateral raises preferentially grow the middle delt in virtually everyone.

The unshared case is the video's actual subject. [Boyer et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33678131/) recorded surface EMG from semitendinosus, semimembranosus and biceps femoris while trained subjects performed **Nordic curls and stiff-legged deadlifts**. The highest-activation head was not the same person to person: on the Nordic curl some showed biceps femoris highest, others semitendinosus or semimembranosus, and the stiff-leg deadlift split similarly. [Stronska et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32460639/) found the same on the barbell bench press — some subjects showed the chest highest, others the triceps, others the anterior deltoid. [Hug et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31589090/) reports individual activation signatures during walking and cycling.

He raises the EMG objection himself, cites [Vigotsky et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35006527/) for it, and then gives two specific reasons for keeping these particular datasets: Boyer retested in a second session and got similar recordings, and Stronska normalised against maximal isometric bench contractions at 90° of elbow flexion. That second defence sits awkwardly with his own [teardown of Stronska's normalisation](change-muscle-activation-any-compound-exercise-maybe.md) seven months earlier, where he argued at length that comparing percent-of-MVIC values *between* muscles is not meaningful.

## The principles

**1. Run n=1 experiments.** If recruitment patterns differ, so does the right exercise list. He recommends logging training and testing selections against your own progress, and notes anatomy and preference vary too.

The supporting outcome study is [Rauch et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29016481/): **17 men, average 5.6 years of training**, assigned to a fixed programme or an autoregulated group free to pick exercises per muscle group each session from a supplied list, with all other variables matched. After **9 weeks, lean body mass gains were greater in the autoregulated group**. His inference — trained lifters know which exercises suit them — is plausible but not tested by the design; autonomy, novelty and effort are all live alternatives.

**2. Watch your compound technique.** [Li & Zhang 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20415155/) found people with more back strength relative to knee strength used a back-dominant lifting strategy, and vice versa — technique drifts toward whatever is already strong. Applied to the bench press: if you press up and *then* back, much of the lifting phase happens far from the shoulder line, which favours the anterior deltoid. Pressing back off the chest and up only as needed shortens that moment arm and favours the chest, and matches the bar path elite bench pressers use. He notes this is easier to execute on slow, hard reps; on easy reps just aim back. Filming yourself is the practical tool.

**3. Recruitment may be trainable.** In Stronska, subjects were grouped by whichever of chest, triceps or front delt showed the lowest relative activation, then did **three isolation exercises per session, four sets of 10–15 to failure, three times a week for six weeks** for that muscle, plus three sets at a 4RM bench weekly to hold strength. All three groups raised the trained muscle's recorded activation on the bench; the chest and triceps groups also showed decreases elsewhere. A separate US study found gluteus maximus involvement in bilateral and unilateral squats rose after **one week of daily glute band training**. His suggested use: to make squats hit the glutes harder, run a block of glute isolation while trimming other work.

## Against the library's exercise-selection criteria

This is a different framework, not a competing one. The library's account — the three criteria that dominate from 2024 onward ([exercise selection summary](../Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/SUMMARY-exercise-selection.md)) — scores an *exercise*: tension in the stretched position (failing it is an automatic F), whether it feels good, and whether it progresses simply, with resistance profile the most common reason something gets demoted. Every one of those is a property of the movement, assumed to hold for everyone.

Dhim's three principles are properties of the *lifter*. Only one touches the library's list, and obliquely: "feels good" is a preference criterion, and Rauch is the closest thing either framework has to outcome evidence that preference-led selection wins. What is conspicuous is what he never mentions — **resistance profile and stretched-position tension do not appear in this video at all**. It predates the library's 2024 shift by eighteen months, and reads as the pre-stretch consensus: pick by recruitment, not by where in the range the tension sits.

**Overreach flag:** every recruitment claim here rests on surface EMG, which he elsewhere argues cannot predict hypertrophy; the "isolate a muscle to increase its compound involvement" principle has no growth data behind it at all, only changed EMG amplitudes in one six-week study.
