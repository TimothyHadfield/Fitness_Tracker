# Training Splits and Frequency: What 20 Studies Actually Show

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Comparing Training Splits for Muscle Growth (20 STUDIES)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sc-QdAadde0)) · 2022-05-16 · 35 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- **Below roughly 12 weekly sets per muscle, split architecture is irrelevant.** Schoenfeld's 2019 meta-analysis of 13 volume-equated studies found no frequency effect — and 11 of those 13 used ≤12 weekly sets per muscle. Bro splits, full body, upper/lower and PPL are all viable at that volume.
- **Above 12 weekly sets, the five available studies conflict completely.** Two found no difference, one favoured full body over bro splits, one favoured bro splits over full body, and one favoured upper/lower over full body. He does not pretend to resolve this.
- **He identifies and discards the Barbalho volume studies as retracted** on data-integrity grounds — the two studies most often used to argue that 5 sets beats 20. This is the single most important thing in the video for anyone tracking the volume-ceiling debate.
- The **rest-interval confound is his own contribution**: four of the five high-volume studies used **1–1.5 min rest**, which makes each set less effective and so raises the ceiling on productive sets per session. With 2.5–3 min rest, the per-session ceiling may be **6–10 sets**, in which case low-frequency splits would look worse than these studies suggest. He labels this **speculation** and immediately supplies the counterexample that breaks it.
- **The real-world argument he does endorse**: frequency's practical value is that it lets you *add* weekly sets, and in trained lifters a **modest ~20% increase over habitual volume** accelerates growth — while **larger jumps of 30–120% do not**.

## The 12-set line, and what sits either side of it

Everything in this video hangs on one distinction he makes early and never lets go of: the research equates weekly sets per muscle between conditions, deliberately, so that frequency can be isolated. That is good science and it also means the studies answer a narrower question than the one lifters ask.

Below the line, the evidence is clean. Schoenfeld's **2016** meta-analysis of 7 studies found twice weekly beat once weekly, and that result is still circulating as proof that bro splits are inferior. He points out it is six years old and superseded: the **2019** update, 13 studies, found **no frequency effect at all** ([Schoenfeld 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/)). The crucial qualifier — which he supplies and most summaries don't — is that **11 of the 13 studies used 12 or fewer weekly sets per muscle**, so the accurate conclusion is conditional on volume, not universal.

Three studies from that pool illustrate it:
- **23 trained men** (mean bench 1RM 103 kg, squat 1RM 132 kg), bro split at **1x/week** versus full body at **5x/week**, both at **10 weekly sets** per muscle (15 for arms if compound sets are counted). After 8 weeks, **similar lean mass gains** ([Gomes 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29489727/)).
- Untrained subjects doing leg extension only: **6 sets on one day** versus **2 sets across three days**. After 11 weeks, **similar quadriceps thickness** ([Ochi 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30013480/)).
- **18 trained men** (bench 77 kg, squat 103 kg), muscles trained **1x** versus **2x weekly**, 4–12 weekly sets (16 biceps and 24 triceps sets if compounds count). After 6 weeks, **no statistical difference** in vastus medialis or elbow flexor thickness ([Yue 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29216446/)).

He is consistent about reporting **both** the direct set count and the count including compound-exercise contributions, which repeatedly shows that "12 weekly sets" for arms in these designs is often 24 or more. That detail matters and almost nobody reports it.

## The retracted evidence

The theoretical case for higher frequency at higher volumes is a **per-session ceiling on productive sets**. The two studies most often cited for it are Barbalho's, identically designed in trained men and in untrained women: all groups trained each muscle **once weekly** at **5, 10, 15 or 20 weekly sets** — so weekly sets equalled per-session sets. **5–10 sets were sufficient, and 15–20 produced slightly worse growth.**

He then states plainly that **both studies have since been retracted**, probably because the data were not legitimate, and that many studies from this group contain "atypical and improbable data" ([Barbalho 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31188644/), [Barbalho 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30779716/); analysis by [Vigotsky et al.](https://osf.io/preprints/sportrxiv/sg3wm/) and [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/barbalho/)). He removes them from his own argument even though they support the conclusion he is building toward. This is the clearest example of the channel's core virtue, and it directly undercuts the "less is more" position that these two studies underwrite elsewhere.

## Above 12 sets: five studies, four different answers

He reports all five, in full, with 1RMs and set counts:

| Study | Subjects | Comparison | Weekly sets | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Evangelista 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8372753/) | Untrained men (bench 60 kg, squat 87 kg) | Bro split 2x/wk vs full body 4x/wk | 16/muscle (triceps 40–48, biceps 32 with compounds) | **No difference** in quads, triceps, biceps at 8 weeks |
| [Brigatto 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29528962/) | Trained men (bench 94 kg) | Split 1x/wk vs 2x/wk | 16/muscle (arms 24 with compounds) | **No difference** in triceps, biceps, VL, anterior quads at 8 weeks |
| [Zaroni 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31260419/) | Trained men (bench 99 kg, squat 109 kg) | Bro split 1x/wk vs full body 5x/wk | 15/muscle (arms 30 with compounds) | **Full body superior** for triceps, biceps, VL at 8 weeks |
| [Bartolomei 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32168178/) | Trained men (bench 96 kg, squat 108 kg) | Bro split 1x/wk vs full body 3–4x/wk | 15 chest, 20 quads, ~25 indirect traps | Chest similar; **bro split superior** for VL and traps at 10 weeks |
| [Lasevicius 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6724585/) | Trained men (bench 79 kg, squat 161 kg) | Upper/lower 2x/wk vs full body 3x/wk | 36 quads, 12 arms (24 with compounds) | **Upper/lower superior** for RF, VL, biceps, triceps at 10 weeks |

His verdict is "utterly confusing," which is the honest one. Two of the five are null, and the three positive results point in three different directions — including one that directly contradicts another with near-identical subject profiles (Zaroni and Bartolomei both compared bro splits to full body in trained men benching around 95–100 kg, and reached opposite conclusions on vastus lateralis).

Note the volume in Bartolomei's bro-split group: **20 quadriceps sets in a single session**, which still beat full body. And note Lasevicius: **36 weekly quadriceps sets**, with the *lower*-frequency arrangement (upper/lower, 2x) beating the higher (full body, 3x). Neither result fits the per-session-ceiling story.

## Two explanations he offers, and how much weight each bears

**Individual response.** A within-subject study assigned each of **19 untrained men** one leg to a high-frequency condition (unilateral leg extension, 3 sets of 9–12 to failure, **5x/week**) and the other to low frequency (**2–3x/week**). Over 8 weeks: **6 grew more on the high-frequency leg, 7 grew more on the low-frequency leg, 6 were the same** ([Damas 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30289872/)). He flags the design flaw himself — **volume was not equated** (15 weekly sets versus 6–9), so this is not a clean frequency comparison. He still thinks the underlying point is likely true, and adds a genuinely useful hypothesis: individual differences may **only start to matter above 12 weekly sets**, which would explain why the low-volume literature is consistent and the high-volume literature is not.

**Rest intervals.** Four of the five high-volume studies used **1–1.5 minutes** between sets; only Bartolomei used 2 minutes. Short rest makes each set less effective — resting **2.5–3 minutes** produces more hypertrophy than 1.5 minutes or less, at least in compound exercises — and [Longo et al.](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/abstract/9000/volume_load_rather_than_resting_interval.94362.aspx) shows the trade explicitly: 3 sets with 3 min rest beat 3 sets with 1 min rest, but **4–5 sets with 1 min rest matched 3 sets with 3 min rest**. His inference: if short rest lowers the value of each set, the ceiling on productive sets per session is **higher** with short rest — so these studies may have let their low-frequency groups stay under the ceiling. Run the same studies with 2.5–3 min rest and low frequencies might look worse.

He then tests his own speculation against three sources on where a per-session ceiling might sit:
- **Rat gastrocnemius**, electrically induced contractions, 3 min rest: protein synthesis rose up to **10 sets** per session and was lower at 20 ([Ogasawara 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28729395/)). Limitations he states: rats, and protein synthesis rather than growth.
- **Humans**, 8 versus 12 quadriceps sets in one session with 2 min rest: **little difference** in myofibrillar protein synthesis ([Damas 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31268828/)) — suggesting little benefit past ~8 sets per session with longer rest. Same protein-synthesis limitation.
- **Krieger's analysis**: with 2+ min rest, growth plateaus beyond **6–8 sets per muscle per session** ([Weightology](https://weightology.net/the-members-area/evidence-based-guides/set-volume-for-muscle-size-the-ultimate-evidence-based-bible/)). He raises two confounds against it himself: untrained subjects cluster on the low-set end of the curve and grow more readily, biasing the left side; and the high-set end comes from studies with high *weekly* volumes, so the downturn could be under-recovery rather than a per-session limit.

Combining them he lands on a tentative **6–10 sets per muscle per session** with long rest — then immediately gives the counterexample: Bartolomei used 2 minutes' rest and **20 quadriceps sets in one session**, and the bro split still won. One study, but it is the right one to raise, and he raises it against himself.

## The question the research doesn't answer

The volume-equated design isolates frequency, but no lifter equates volume. He addresses this directly and treats it as a fair objection: in practice, **higher frequency is a tool for fitting more weekly sets in** — a lifter on two full-body sessions who wants more quadriceps work adds a third.

Why do that at all? Two studies in trained lifters:
- Men with **≥3 years of training** performed **12, 18 or 24 weekly quadriceps sets** with no statistical difference in anterior thigh growth — but when sorted into responder groups, the **high responders were those who had raised their weekly sets most relative to their pre-study volume** ([Aube 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32058362/)).
- Trained men trained unilateral leg extension and leg press twice weekly for 8 weeks; **one leg got 20% more weekly sets than they had been doing**, the other got a prescribed **22 weekly sets**. **Vastus lateralis growth favoured the +20% leg.** And for **8 of the 16 subjects**, the prescribed 22 sets was itself a **30–120% increase** over their habitual volume — yet the modest +20% leg still won ([Scarpelli 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32108724/)).

That second result is the most practically loaded finding in the video, and it points somewhere neither side of the usual volume argument does: what predicts growth is not an absolute weekly set number but a **modest increase relative to your own current volume**, with large jumps performing no better and possibly worse.

## Practical position
- At **≤12 weekly sets per muscle**, pick whatever split you'll adhere to. Bro splits are not inferior at this volume.
- Above 12, no architecture is demonstrably better; individual response may dominate, so treat the choice as personal and test it.
- If you use short rest (1–1.5 min), you likely need more sets per session for the same effect; with 2.5–3 min rest, a **6–10 set per muscle per session** working ceiling is a reasonable if unproven default, which argues for spreading high weekly volumes across days.
- Use frequency as a **container** for added volume, and add in **~20% increments** over what you're already doing rather than jumping to a target number.

## Caveats & flags
- **The rest-interval mechanism is speculation stacked on protein-synthesis proxies** — rats, acute MPS measurements, and a paywalled analysis with confounds he names. He says so, but it is the video's central original argument and it rests on no growth outcome.
- The individual-response study **did not equate volume**, so it cannot support a frequency conclusion; he notes this and then largely proceeds as if it did.
- The bodybuilder survey he opens with — **235 competitive bodybuilders, 85.5% on split routines, 53.6% using PEDs**, which he dates to 2022 — does not match the description's only survey reference, [Hackett 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22990567/), a smaller and earlier study. Treat the survey numbers as unconfirmed.
- Trial durations are **6–11 weeks** throughout, which is short for detecting split-level differences that would compound over years.
