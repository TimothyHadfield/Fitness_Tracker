# Chest Exercises: The Evidence Says Almost All Of Them Work

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The BEST Chest Exercises for Building Muscle (33 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qsDleTVaKhk)) · 2023-12-13 · 17 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- The flat barbell bench is not overrated. Direct hypertrophy studies show it grows **all three pec regions well** — one Japanese study recorded a **43% increase in mid-pec thickness** in untrained men.
- Machine presses, push-ups, Smith machine and dumbbells all appear **comparable** to the barbell bench for pec growth. The one direct hypertrophy comparison available — machine chest press vs flat barbell bench — found similar growth. The rest is EMG plus inference.
- The usual arguments for a "best" chest exercise all fail on inspection: heavier loads are not required for hypertrophy, easier progressive overload does not imply better hypertrophy, and the machine-stability-equals-recruitment claim rests on a study that strapped subjects down on a leg extension.
- His two speculative picks — **cambered bar bench press** and **deficit push-ups** — are chosen purely for deeper stretch, and he is explicit that **no chest data supports this** and that two studies elsewhere found more stretch did not mean more growth.
- **Dips are unresolved.** No published EMG or hypertrophy study compares dips to other chest exercises; he declines to conclude anything.

## The flat barbell bench is fine
Multiple papers show the pectoralis major grows very well from flat barbell benching, at or slightly above expected rates of hypertrophy. He cites a Japanese study in previously untrained men that measured mid-pec thickness rising throughout to a **43% increase**.

Two studies address whether it neglects regions. A 2022 Australian study on trained lifters over 8 weeks compared normal sets (4×5 at 85% 1RM) against a cluster condition using the same variables with 30 seconds between each rep. The **normal group grew all three pec regions substantially**; the cluster group did not, which he attributes — reasonably — to the inter-rep rest leaving them far from failure. A second Japanese study in untrained men ran 24 weeks of flat benching, one group continuous and one taking a **three-week break every six weeks**; both saw substantial and similar growth across all three regions. He flags the break finding as remarkable in passing and points elsewhere for it.

The take-home he draws is narrow and correct: flat benching grows all pec regions well. He explicitly defers the obvious follow-up — whether dedicated upper- and lower-chest work is therefore needed — to later videos rather than answering it here.

## The EMG caveat, stated up front
Before comparing exercises he spends a full minute on why EMG is weak evidence, which is worth recording because most of this video's comparisons rest on it. EMG records electrical activity, not activation, and there are steps between the two; noise and technical error corrupt the measurement itself; and there are studies **failing to find any association between EMG and actual hypertrophy**, though some indirect papers do find one. He adds a useful qualifier later: EMG is probably more reliable when comparing **similar** exercises to one another, which is why he leans on it slightly harder for Smith machine vs barbell than elsewhere.

He also names a specific blind spot that matters here: **EMG does not measure the passive forces a muscle generates when stretched**, and emerging evidence suggests those contribute to hypertrophy. That is the mechanism behind the whole final third of the video.

## Exercise by exercise

**Machine chest press.** One EMG paper favours the barbell; two find similar activity, one of them showing two machine variants matching flat benching across all three pec regions, front delt and triceps. Decisively, a recent study **directly compared pec hypertrophy** between machine chest press and flat barbell bench and found it quite similar. This is the strongest comparison in the video because it is an actual growth outcome.

**Push-ups.** A Japanese paper compared high-rep push-ups to high-rep bench pressing and found similar chest and triceps growth — but he immediately marks the high reps as a drawback rather than letting it pass. Weighted push-ups are the better test: a well-constructed load-equated EMG analysis found comparable pec, triceps and even front delt activity versus flat benching. A separate study progressed push-ups through harder variations in the 4–8 rep range against bench pressing at 6–8 reps; chest growth **leaned toward the push-up group but nothing reached significance and the study was only four weeks**. His stated conclusion is restrained: this shows the barbell bench is not undeniably superior, and push-ups are viable *contingent on* being able to progress them long-term into the 6–35 rep range.

**Smith machine.** Five EMG studies, majority finding similar pec, triceps and front delt activity to barbell. **No hypertrophy data at all.** He labels his position a "tentative hypothesis" rather than a conclusion.

**Dumbbells.** Three studies find slightly *lower* triceps activity than barbell, and he offers a clean mechanical explanation: barbell benching produces lateral forces alongside vertical ones and the triceps are heavily involved in those, whereas dumbbells cannot produce lateral force without the dumbbells falling sideways. For pecs and front delts, four studies mostly find similar activity. The stretch argument would favour dumbbells — but he lists three complications: large dumbbells physically block a deeper stretch; most lifters do not execute for maximum stretch anyway; and extra stretch beyond an already solid stretch may simply not add growth. Verdict: **largely similar**, not superior.

**Dips.** No published EMG or hypertrophy comparison to other exercises exists. One paper compares EMG between dip variants (ring and normal dips producing decent chest, front delt and triceps levels). He states firmly that nothing can be concluded and defers to a future lower-chest video.

## Why "more stretch" is not yet a chest recommendation
He presents his own counter-evidence rather than skipping it. Two studies question whether extra stretch adds growth:
- A Japanese paper comparing 90° vs 140° knee flexion squats found glute and adductor growth better with the deeper range, but **quad growth similar**.
- Another Japanese paper compared partial skull crushers to a full range of motion that achieved a better stretch, and **triceps growth was better for the partial**. He identifies this as an outlier — the only study to date finding a better stretch produced worse hypertrophy — but reports it anyway.

He also notes there is **no chest data at all** on this question, and dismisses the "convergence at the top" argument for dumbbells on the grounds that tension is low there and the contracted position is not particularly stimulatory.

His picks — the **cambered bar bench press** and **deficit push-ups** — are therefore offered as hypothesis. The reasoning is that they let you attain a deep stretch *consistently and repeatably* in a way dumbbells do not, and his stated confidence is "I don't anticipate these being inferior", which is about as weak a claim as one can make while still making one. He separately floats **stretched-position partials** on the bench (the Ronnie Coleman bottom-range style), citing three studies where a partial ROM at long muscle lengths built more muscle than full ROM, and notes an Arnold anecdote about Sergio Oliva — which he explicitly labels as not proof.

## Why everything works, and where the video overreaches
His explanation for the uniform findings is coherent: heavier loads are not a prerequisite for hypertrophy, proximity to failure is what matters, and this is why higher reps with lighter loads match lower reps with heavier ones. Ease of progressive overload does not imply superiority — very partial squats progress beautifully and build little. And the stability-improves-recruitment claim comes from a study that manipulated stability by **strapping the leg and torso down on a leg extension**, whose generalisability he calls questionable. His stated principle — hypertrophy outcomes outrank indirect mechanistic speculation — is the right one and he applies it consistently.

The overreach is in the title. A video promising the "best" chest exercises ends up naming two exercises with **zero direct evidence behind them**, chosen on a mechanism (stretch) that his own cited studies show does not reliably transfer. He labels this hypothetical repeatedly and honestly, but the framing still promises more than the evidence delivers. Several exercise verdicts here — Smith machine, dumbbells, deficit push-ups — are EMG and inference rather than growth data.
