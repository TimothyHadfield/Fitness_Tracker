# The 2024 Round-Up: Failure, Range of Motion and Set Volume

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Most IMPORTANT Muscle Building Studies from 2024" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z5O01gmNW3s)) · 2024-12-23 · 13 min
**Topic:** Evidence & method

## Bottom line
- **Failure vs 1–2 RIR** may depend on volume: at 10–17 weekly sets they look equivalent; at **one set per exercise** the raw numbers lean toward failure, though not statistically conclusively.
- **Lengthened partials are not superior to full range of motion in trained lifters.** Two 2024 studies — one with just under 300 subjects, one within-subject — both found no difference. The earlier pro-partials results were in untrained people on a narrow set of exercises.
- **More sets keep adding muscle** in the largest analysis to date, but strength plateaus far earlier. That mismatch is not evidence the extra "growth" is swelling; strength is simply a poor proxy for hypertrophy.
- His practical set recommendation: get training quality right first, then **roughly 12–20 weekly sets per muscle** for most people.
- Two speculative physiology findings: untrained muscles may **shrink** to support trained ones (worse in an energy deficit), and trained lifters may have **more fibres**, not just bigger ones.

## Training to failure
[00:41] Refalo et al. is the best-designed failure study to date, on trained subjects with the highest average experience in this literature. Within-subject: one leg trained unilateral leg extensions and leg presses **to momentary failure**, the other stopped **1 rep short on the extension and 2 short on the press**. Pre-study testing confirmed subjects judged proximity to failure fairly accurately. **Quad gains were similar**, matching other well-designed studies.

[01:34] Volume matters for interpreting that. Subjects kept pre-study quad volume with a 20% bump midway, landing at **10–17 weekly quad sets**. The hypothesis is that failure matters more when sets are few.

[01:59] A brand-new study tested exactly that: **42 trained individuals**, twice weekly, **one set per exercise**, one group to failure and one aiming for 2 RIR. Estimation accuracy was reasonable — better on bench than squat, and improving over time. **Differences were not statistically conclusive**, but the raw numbers leaned toward failure on most measures, the biceps excepted. An earlier one-set-per-exercise study (Giessing et al.) also tended to favour failure.

His conclusion is hedged: **not case closed**, but failure may buy something at low volumes while being largely unnecessary at higher set counts. This is a directional read of a null result — he says so explicitly, which is the right handling.

## Range of motion
[03:10] The old "full range is superior" position came from comparisons against **shortened** partials. Recent data favouring **lengthened** partials came from untrained subjects training calf raises, leg extensions or a hip extension exercise.

[03:59] The new evidence changes that. A randomised trial across **15 locations** with **just under 300 subjects** (≥6 months' experience) ran a two-day-per-week programme for **12 weeks**, one set to failure per exercise. One condition used full range throughout; the other used lengthened partials on selected exercises. **Arm and thigh muscle area gains were essentially identical.** He lists the limitations without prompting: area was **estimated from circumferences and skinfolds**, subjects were not highly trained, partials were not applied to every exercise, and total volume was low.

[04:52] A second 2024 study — **funded by Jeff Nippard**, and he says so — took **25 individuals averaging 4.9 years of training**, with one side of the body doing full range on everything and the other lengthened partials on everything. **Elbow flexor and triceps growth were similar.**

[05:11] Two hypotheses survive. First, lengthened partials might only help on exercises hardest at short muscle lengths (calf raises, rows, lateral raises, leg extensions) — but the trained-subject study *did* include leg extensions and found nothing. Second, the benefit may be confined to **distal regions**, and both trained-subject studies measured only around the muscle's middle.

Current take: **range of motion is not the most important variable**; full range and lengthened partials are both viable, mixing them is fine.

## How many sets
[07:15] The largest analysis to date suggests higher set numbers build more muscle, while **strength gains max out at much lower set numbers**. A popular inference from that gap is that the extra growth is swelling, not tissue. He rejects it: there is not enough evidence that swelling confounds these results, and post-training swelling **decreases notably** as a programme continues.

[07:47] Crediting a Greg Nuckols article, the better explanation is that **strength is a bad instrument for inferring hypertrophy** — an energy deficit, for instance, blunts lean mass gains without necessarily blunting strength gains.

[08:19] He still argues against chasing very high volumes: returns diminish sharply, **credible intervals widen beyond ~25 weekly sets** because few studies go there, and an individual study published that month found no extra growth from higher sets. Recommendation: fix training quality and exercise selection first, then **as much volume as you can practically handle — likely 12–20 weekly sets per muscle**.

## Physiology worth watching
[09:44] **Muscle competition.** A 2024 study found some *untrained* muscles decreased in size, seemingly to support growth of trained ones, and more so when calorie and protein intake were lower. If it holds, specialisation blocks become more attractive. He calls the research "in its infancy" — the diverted-resources story is interpretation, not a measured mechanism.

[10:22] **Hyperplasia.** A 2024 paper used an **indirect equation** to estimate fibre number in trained versus matched untrained people. Trained muscles were **70% larger**, from both larger fibres and a greater estimated fibre count. Cross-sectional with estimated counts, so it cannot show lifting causes new fibres.
