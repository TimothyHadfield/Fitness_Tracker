# Barbells, Dumbbells, Machines, Bodyweight and Bands: No Modality Is Inherently Better

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The BEST Training Mode for Muscle Growth (Most Get WRONG)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luDHXXqqbgk)) · 2023-01-08 · 28 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **No training modality is inherently superior for hypertrophy.** Recruitment follows the *movement*, not the equipment; any modality that lets you take a set to or near failure in the 6–35 rep range with a decent stretch produces comparable mechanical tension.
- **The anabolic hormone argument is dead.** Free weights do produce larger acute testosterone and growth hormone spikes ([Shaner et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24276305/); [Schwanbeck et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32358310/)), but a substantial body of work fails to connect those spikes to long-term growth ([West & Phillips](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22105707/)), and free weight training including squats largely does not raise resting testosterone chronically.
- **Variable resistance doesn't help.** Four studies — two Finnish 2013 trials, a Polish preacher curl trial, and a 1995 Canadian preacher curl trial — all found growth equivalent to standard resistance.
- **Eccentric overload doesn't help either.** Of **11 studies**, only 4 favoured eccentric overload; the rest found similar hypertrophy.
- **The direct comparisons agree.** A 2020 Belgian trial (free weight vs machine vs combined, untrained, circumference) and a 2020 Canadian trial (free weight vs machine, *trained* men and women, ultrasound) both found comparable growth; two push-up-vs-bench-press trials found the same.
- **Bands are the one modality he is sceptical of** — not from hypertrophy data (there is none) but from three mechanical problems: it is easy to let the band snap back and lose the eccentric entirely, the resistance curve peaks where the muscle is short, and progressive overload is impractical long-term. Bodyweight has the same overload problem for the lower body specifically.

## The argument in one line

He builds the case from a stimulus-first premise rather than from the outcome studies, then checks it against them.

**Mechanical tension is the primary hypertrophy stimulus** — muscle damage and metabolic stress lack strong evidence as powerful drivers. Tension is force produced by muscle fibres, detected by mechanosensors within and around the fibre, converted into a growth signalling cascade.

Getting high tension takes two steps. First, **recruit the muscle**. Recruitment follows **neuromechanical matching** — the nervous system recruits muscles according to their leverage for the movement being performed. The biceps have leverage for elbow flexion, so elbow flexion recruits the biceps. Elbow flexion can be produced by a barbell, a dumbbell, a machine, bodyweight or a band. **The movement determines recruitment; the equipment does not.**

Second, **expose the recruited fibres to high force for a decent duration**, which means training to or near failure in roughly the **6–35 rep range**. Low reps get recruitment and force high immediately; high reps start low, but as you approach failure recruitment and force climb until the last few reps are comparable to a low-rep set (consistent with [Morton et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31294822/) on fibre activation at task failure). Below 6 reps, the duration of that high-tension exposure is probably too short. Above ~35 reps, central fatigue may start to limit your ability to recruit fibres at all.

A third factor, added separately: **passive forces**. Stretched fibres and the surrounding extracellular matrix generate force that mechanosensors also detect, which is his candidate explanation for why exercises loading a muscle at long lengths consistently out-grow those that don't. And all five modalities can theoretically achieve stretch — barbell squats, Bulgarian split squats, hack squats, pistol squats and banded squats all stretch the quads.

Conclusion of the setup: **no modality has an intrinsic advantage on either recruitment or stretch.** Everything after this is testing that claim against specific counter-arguments.

## Counter-argument 1: free weights spike more hormones

True, and irrelevant.

Free weight sessions do produce larger acute rises in anabolic hormones. A **2020 Canadian study** comparing a free weight session to a machine session found greater temporary elevations in free testosterone ([Schwanbeck et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32358310/)); a **2013 US study** comparing barbell squats to leg press found greater temporary rises in testosterone and growth hormone ([Shaner et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24276305/)).

The link from spike to growth is what fails. A substantial number of studies find that greater temporary elevations in anabolic hormones do not produce more long-term hypertrophy ([West & Phillips](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22105707/)). His alternative reading, offered as a possibility rather than a fact: the acute rise may reflect **energy usage** rather than a growth signal.

The chronic version — "squats raise your baseline testosterone" — fails too: many studies find free weight training, squats included, largely does not chronically raise resting testosterone.

## Counter-argument 2: machines can vary the resistance

Variable resistance means the machine's cam alters the load through the range so the muscle is challenged more evenly — the barbell curl is hardest at the midpoint and easy at both ends; a variable-resistance machine flattens that. He notes the historical irony that Nautilus popularised this from the 1970s, and that Arthur Jones, who built Nautilus and believed variable resistance was superior, was also an early promoter of one-set-to-failure HIT.

Four studies, all null:

- A **2013 Finnish** study in trained men, leg press + leg extension + leg curl: vastus lateralis gains comparable ([Walker et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23825004/)).
- A **second 2013 Finnish** study in previously untrained older men: same result ([Walker et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23636698/)).
- A **2019 Polish** preacher curl study: arm circumference gains no different ([Staniszewski et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7075227/)).
- A **1995 Canadian** preacher curl study: biceps gains similar ([O'Hagan et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7476067/)).

His explanation fits the framework: whether or not resistance is varied, taking the set near failure ensures high tension regardless.

He adds a disclaimer that variable resistance could theoretically be produced without machines, but every study on it used machines — so the finding is about the machines as tested.

## Counter-argument 3: machines can overload the eccentric

Muscles are substantially stronger lengthening than shortening, so flywheels and similar devices increase the load on the lowering and return to normal for the lifting.

**11 studies** compare eccentric overload to normal training. **Only 4 suggest more hypertrophy; the rest find it similar.** He lists all eleven in the description, which makes this the most checkable claim in the video.

His mechanistic reconciliation, credited to **Chris Beardsley**: eccentric overload may generate disproportionately more fatigue, which impairs the active force muscle fibres can produce. If active force drops while eccentric force rises, total mechanical tension may not actually be higher — which would explain the null results. He presents this as a candidate explanation, not a demonstrated one.

Same disclaimer as above: eccentric overload can be done without machines (weight releasers), but the studies used flywheels and machines.

## The direct comparisons

Having argued from mechanism, he checks the outcome data.

**Free weights vs machines.** A **2020 Belgian** study put previously untrained individuals on a free-weight-only, machine-only, or combined program; chest, thigh and upper arm circumference increases were comparable across all three ([Aerenhouts & D'Hondt](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/21/7848)). He raises the criticisms himself — untrained subjects, and circumference is a crude measure. A **2020 Canadian** study answers both: trained men and women, ultrasound, free-weight-only vs machine-only programs, similar biceps and quadriceps growth ([Schwanbeck et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32358310/)).

**Bodyweight vs free weights.** A **2017 Japanese** study in trained men compared high-rep bench press to high-rep push-up training; chest and triceps hypertrophy were similar ([Kikuchi & Nakazato](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1728869X17301028)). He rejects the "but it was high reps" objection on the grounds that high reps are no less effective than low reps for hypertrophy. A **2018 US** study in men with some training experience compared low-rep bench press to low-rep push-up training, the latter using harder push-up variations to reach low reps ([Kotarsky et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29466268/)). Neither group reached statistical significance for chest gains but both increased numerically, and the percentages actually favoured push-ups — which he immediately downplays as likely random variation in a **4-week** study. The usable conclusion is the weaker one: barbells are not inherently superior to bodyweight.

**Bands.** No hypertrophy comparison exists at all. He says so directly.

## Could dumbbells specifically be better?

The strongest version of the pro-dumbbell case: dumbbells allow the most freedom of movement, so they might let you carry out a movement the muscle has leverage for more completely. A dumbbell bench press permits more horizontal flexion than the barbell, machine or bodyweight versions, and the chest has excellent leverage for horizontal flexion. Some (not all) EMG analyses find higher chest activation with dumbbell pressing. Dumbbells may also allow a greater stretch.

He rejects all three limbs.

**On EMG.** It does not measure muscle activation; it estimates neuromuscular excitation. Critically, **changing muscle length alters the EMG recording even when actual excitation is held constant** — a structural confound in exactly the comparisons people use EMG for ([Vigotsky et al.](https://andrewvigotsky.com/static/studies/Vigotsky_2022_Sports_Med_sEMG_hypertrophy.pdf)). He gives the cleanest available counterexample: EMG records higher quadriceps activity from a leg extension held isometrically at a **short** muscle length, yet studies measuring actual growth find the **long**-length isometric produces notably more quadriceps hypertrophy. The measure and the outcome point opposite directions.

**On the extra horizontal flexion.** The extra range happens at the top, where tension is low and the muscle is shortened, and there is data indicating extra range in a shortened position does not add hypertrophy.

**On the extra stretch.** Stretch matters, but *more* stretch does not automatically mean more growth. A **2019 Japanese** study found similar quadriceps hypertrophy between a 90° and a 140° knee flexion squat, despite the deeper version producing a greater quad stretch ([Kubo et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31230110/)). There may be a **threshold** of passive tension beyond which more adds nothing.

Verdict: not convinced, and he wants direct research.

## The nuances he does accept

Modalities aren't interchangeable in every respect, and the differences are mostly about *which* muscles get involved rather than how much growth is available.

- **Extreme instability is bad** — if stability rather than muscular fatigue is what limits the set, the stimulus suffers. But typical exercises across all modalities are reasonably stable.
- **Barbell vs dumbbell bench press.** The dumbbell version is less stable, which may increase biceps recruitment through antagonist co-activation, limiting triceps force. The barbell version also involves lateral forces (you push outward against the bar) which probably enhance triceps recruitment; you cannot apply meaningful lateral force to dumbbells without throwing them sideways. Net: **the dumbbell version likely involves less triceps**, useful or not depending on your goal. He notes the exception that neutral-grip dumbbell pressing does appear to train the triceps well, citing research finding notable triceps hypertrophy from it. (The bench press EMG evidence here is [Saeterbakken et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21225489/), [Welsch et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15903389/) and a T-Nation summary, all in his list.)
- **Push-ups vs bench pressing.** Push-ups need more core stabilisation, so more abdominal recruitment, and allow scapular protraction, so more serratus anterior.
- **Barbell squats vs Bulgarian split squats or hack squats.** All produce hip and knee extension; only the barbell squat requires loading and stabilising the spine, so more spinal erector recruitment.

Each of these he frames as "ideal or unideal depending on your goals" rather than better or worse.

## Where modalities are genuinely complementary

The most practically useful section. Because recruitment follows movement, and **some movements can only be produced by certain equipment**, a single-modality program has real holes:

- **A vertical pull is impossible with a barbell or dumbbell.** Bodyweight, machines or bands are required. A vertical pull is probably a good idea for back growth, so barbell- or dumbbell-only programs are suboptimal.
- **Isolated knee extension** — likely needed to properly train the rectus femoris — cannot be done effectively with a barbell or dumbbell.
- **Isolated knee flexion** is likewise a machine or bodyweight job, and probably wanted for full hamstring development.

Modalities are not an either/or.

## Where bands and bodyweight actually fall short

Not on stimulus — on **progressive overload** and **resistance profile**.

Progressive overload works as a cycle: a challenging session drives muscle and strength gains, which enable you to add load or reps, which keeps sessions challenging. Recent research indicates progressing via **load or reps is largely equally effective** ([Plotkin et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/)), so adding reps with bands or bodyweight is fine — until you run past ~35 reps per set, at which point adding load becomes necessary. Harder bodyweight variations effectively add load, particularly for the upper body; **progressing bodyweight lower-body work long-term is much harder**, and he thinks bodyweight alone is more than likely not optimal for long-term lower body hypertrophy. Heavier bands exist but the practicality of that path is questionable.

Bands have two further problems:

1. **It's easy to let the band snap back on the lowering**, leaving almost no eccentric loading. He is careful to separate this from eccentric overload — maximal eccentric loading is not additive (see the 11 studies above), but *some* eccentric loading is needed.
2. **The resistance curve peaks where the muscle is short.** He does not claim maximum tension at long lengths is required, but you probably want *decent* tension there, and some banded exercises supply almost none.

He labels all of this as speculation, because there is no band-vs-anything hypertrophy trial. The band evidence base is mixed EMG — some showing similar recordings to free weights, some showing higher with free weights ([Aboodarda et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27681867/)) — and he has already argued EMG cannot settle this. Bands do build muscle: a **2020 Japanese** study found banded curls and extensions produced measurable biceps and triceps hypertrophy ([Fink et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42978-020-00092-z)).

## Individual factors

Closing section, all preference-level: comfort (Bulgarian split squats vs back squats produce the same knee and hip extension); lower joint loading with machines and bands, which may suit some circumstances; restricted machine paths that may not fit your anatomy; and **significant limb imbalances**, where a bilateral exercise lets the stronger side do disproportionate work and dumbbells offer a fix.

## Overreach flags

Two places where the conclusion runs slightly ahead of the evidence, both of which he labels:

- The **band scepticism** is entirely mechanistic. There is no hypertrophy trial comparing bands to anything, and he says so — but the practical takeaway ("I'm sceptical resistance bands are truly on par") is stated more firmly than a mechanism-only argument supports.
- The **stability and biomechanics** section (dumbbells recruit less triceps, push-ups recruit more serratus) is inference from force analysis and EMG, not from measured regional growth.
