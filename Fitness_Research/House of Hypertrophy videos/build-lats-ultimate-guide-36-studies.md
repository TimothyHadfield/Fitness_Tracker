# Building the Lats: A 36-Study Guide With No Lat Hypertrophy Study In It

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How to Build Your LATS: The ULTIMATE Guide (36 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=veoIr2CUEAY)) · 2023-09-04 · 25 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **The 36 studies contain no direct measurement of lat growth.** He says so twice, unprompted: on barbell rows, "the only way to solve this is if we had research directly comparing lat hypertrophy between barbell rows and other rows — since this currently does not exist"; on isolation work, "unfortunately there isn't any directly related research." Every recommendation is built from **moment-arm analyses, isometric sEMG, and extrapolation from quad, glute, triceps and biceps studies.** This independently corroborates the admission already in this library that no study has directly measured back hypertrophy.
- His organising principle is **joint action, not exercise name**. Close-grip vertical *and* horizontal pulls are the same movement for the lats — **shoulder extension** — and he expects them to develop the lats comparably. Wide-grip vertical pulls are **shoulder adduction**. Wide-grip horizontal pulls are **shoulder horizontal extension**, where the lats have poor leverage.
- **Regional bias:** shoulder extension (close grips, both planes) favours the **upper lat**; shoulder adduction (wide-grip vertical pulls) the **middle and lower lat**; **medium-grip vertical pulls** plausibly hit all regions reasonably well.
- **Minimum viable programme:** one shoulder-extension compound plus one shoulder-adduction compound — or medium-grip vertical pulls alone. Isolation work is optional unless you are maximising or your compound selection is skewed.
- **Wide-grip rows are the one thing he advises against for lats** — they load rear delts, infraspinatus and supraspinatus, and the lats have "a lot lower leverage" through that range.
- **Pullovers ranked: machine > cable > free weight**, on resistance-profile reasoning. But he refuses to write off free-weight pullovers, arguing stretch under limited leverage may still be a real stimulus — backed with glute data, not lat data.

## What the 36 studies actually are

This is the most important thing to record about this video, and it is not a criticism of him — he is the one who states it. The video has no reference list; sources appear on screen only. From the narration, the evidence falls into four buckets:

1. **Anatomical and moment-arm analyses** — papers dividing the lats into regions (2013 Canadian, 1998 Australian, 2007 Australian) and mapping leverage across shoulder angles in extension, adduction and horizontal extension (a "classic" US analysis, 1997 and 2008 Australian analyses).
2. **Surface EMG**, mostly isometric — the 2007 six-region paper, a 2002 US pulldown grip comparison, a 2009 Canadian inverted row vs barbell row comparison, a 2005 Japanese seated row vs barbell row comparison, a 2022 Spanish pullover vs pulldown comparison, and unnamed sEMG work on pullovers.
3. **Hypertrophy trials on entirely different muscles** — lengthened-partial leg extensions, calf presses and hip extensions; partial vs full-range skull crushers; incline vs preacher curls; deep vs 90° squats and squats vs hip thrusts for the glutes; a free-weight vs machine programme comparison; and the push-up vs bench press comparison.
4. **One anecdote** — a Reddit user reporting chest soreness from very wide pull-ups, which he clearly labels as such.

So the honest reading of the "36 studies" framing is: **36 studies bearing on lat training, zero measuring lat size.** He is transparent about it, but the title is not, and anyone citing this video as evidence for a lat exercise ranking is citing a chain of inferences, not an outcome.

One thing makes the inference chain more defensible: he notes that **isometric contractions likely improve sEMG reliability**, and says so at both points where he leans on isometric data. He has repeatedly criticised sEMG elsewhere and does not quietly drop that criticism when the data suit him.

## Lat regions are real

Regional hypertrophy is not a myth, and the 2007 six-region paper reported that the nervous system can activate some lat regions more than others depending on the movement. His supporting example is an actual hypertrophy finding, which is why he uses it: barbell squats and hip thrusts both preferentially grow the **lower** gluteus maximus. It establishes the phenomenon, though not its magnitude in the lats.

## Vertical pulls: what the grip changes

**Close and neutral grips (close-grip pull-ups and pulldowns, chin-ups, underhand pulldowns)** are predominantly **shoulder extension**, where the **teres major and lats have the largest moment arms** and rear delts and teres minor have less. Since the exercise is hardest near the contracted position, the upper lat has greater leverage at those low shoulder angles, and the 2007 paper's isometric sEMG at ~0° found the upper region most active, he concludes close-grip vertical pulls **bias the upper lat** — not exclusively; the other regions still work.

**Underhand vs neutral:** the biceps are more involved with an underhand grip, which raises the question of whether the lats do less. The only data he found is the 2002 US analysis, which reported **comparable lat activity** between neutral close-grip and underhand pulldowns. He treats that as preliminary — one study, absence of contrary data.

**Wide grips** shift the movement toward **shoulder adduction**. Here the rear delts' leverage "rapidly drops off", while teres major, all three lat regions and the mid and lower pectoralis major retain good leverage. Within the lats, the **middle and lower regions have slightly more leverage than the upper**, and the 2007 paper's isometric adduction contractions showed greater lower-region activity. So wide grips **bias the middle and lower lat**.

He then handles an inconvenient detail rather than ignoring it: that same graph gives the mid and lower **pecs** good leverage in adduction, which conflicts with nobody recommending pull-ups as a chest exercise. His resolution is that near-pure adduction requires a very wide grip with elbows flared right out, which almost nobody uses; typical medium grips blend adduction and extension, diluting pec contribution. **Medium-grip vertical pulls may therefore cover all lat regions reasonably well** — his most practically useful conclusion, arrived at from the geometry rather than from any study.

## Rows: the same movement, a different range

Close-grip rows (close-grip barbell rows, seated rows, inverted rows, one-arm rows with the elbow tucked) also produce **shoulder extension**, are also hardest near the contracted position, and therefore also **bias the upper lat**. Three things differ from vertical pulls:

**Range of motion.** Close-grip vertical pulls move the shoulder roughly 170°→0°; rows roughly 75°→0°, sometimes into hyperextension. The intuitive conclusion is that vertical pulls win. He argues not, citing three studies where **partials at long muscle lengths beat full range of motion** — leg extensions, calf presses, and a then-unpublished hip extension study. He is careful: "it's unclear how far this logic extends", and he does not recommend training pulls as lengthened partials. The point is only that more range is not automatically better.

**Lat length.** Vertical pulls stretch the lats more — but he argues the exercise **is not challenging at the most stretched position**, and preliminary data suggest length only pays off where it coincides with high tension. Supporting cases: a Japanese study where **partial skull crushers grew the triceps more** than full-range ones, which stretch the triceps where the exercise is easy; and a study where **preacher curls beat incline curls** despite incline curls achieving the greater stretch.

**Shoulder hyperextension and scapular retraction.** Rows involve more of both, which heavily recruits the upper back. He is "skeptical this negatively interferes with the lat stimulus", since muscle activity is not mutually exclusive — reasonable, but an assertion.

Net: **close-grip vertical and horizontal pulls may develop the lats comparably.**

**Wide-grip rows are the exception.** These are shoulder horizontal extension, where the 1997 Australian analysis puts the best leverage in the **rear delts, infraspinatus and supraspinatus**, with the lats much lower. If lats are the goal, use close grips with the elbow near the body.

## Are barbell rows inferior?

Two sEMG studies say maybe: a 2009 Canadian study found **greater lat activity with inverted rows than barbell rows**, with greater spinal erector activity in barbell rows; a 2005 Japanese study found **slightly greater lat activity with seated rows**, with greater trap activity in barbell rows.

His response is the best passage in the video. He offers the pro-argument (a barbell row's torso-stabilisation demand diverts effort), then the counter-argument (the findings may simply reflect sEMG's known limitations, and since a barbell row should still be limited by shoulder extension strength, lat involvement ought to be similar), then concludes he **does not know**, that direct lat hypertrophy comparisons do not exist, and that barbell rows are fine if you want them. The only concrete reason to avoid them is spinal erector fatigue if you already train that area heavily — a programming reason, not a hypertrophy one.

## Does the equipment matter?

Largely no. He cites a trial comparing a **free-weight to a machine programme** with comparable hypertrophy across measured muscles, and the push-up vs bench press comparison as a second instance. Finer differences exist — pull-ups may involve the forearms more, one-arm rows add rotation — but nothing that is a lat-specific advantage. **Pullovers are the exception**, and that is where his ranking comes from.

## Do you need isolation work?

His answer: **probably not, and there is no research on it.** With at least one shoulder-extension compound and one shoulder-adduction compound, he thinks the probability of excellent long-term lat development is "extremely high"; medium-grip vertical pulls alone may suffice.

His argument for isolation is honest about being an extrapolation: a bench press study found **individuals differ in relative recruitment** of chest, triceps and shoulders, so someone might get relatively poor lat stimulus from a compound movement that works well for someone else. Hence: add isolation if your lat development disappoints after months or years; if you are maximising; or if your compound selection is skewed (his example: only wide-grip rows).

## Which pullover, and why

Two studies of maximal isometric shoulder extension at different elevation angles both found **lat activity highest at low shoulder elevation and progressively lower as the arm rises overhead**, consistent with the lats losing leverage overhead. Everything below follows from that.

- **Free-weight pullovers** are hardest **overhead**, exactly where lat activity is lowest, and at those angles the mid and lower pec regions have decent leverage — a few sEMG studies do report greater pec than lat activity during them. This is the basis of the internet claim that they are not a lat exercise.
- He **declines to accept that conclusion**, and the counter-argument is the most interesting reasoning in the video: lat leverage overhead is **limited, not zero**, and limited activity plus a good stretch may still be a real stimulus. His evidence is the back squat — hardest at the bottom, where the glute max is stretched but has poor leverage and low sEMG activity, yet a Japanese study found **deeper squats grew the glute max more** than 90° squats. Again: real hypertrophy data, from a different muscle.
- **Cable pullovers** reach a comparable stretch but keep tension through a **greater active range**, including the low elevation angles where lat activity is highest. The 2022 Spanish study found greater lat activity in a cable pulldown than a barbell pullover — not an identical movement, but a similar resistance profile.
- **Machine pullovers** rank first, **assuming the machine maintains high resistance throughout** — tension at the lengthened position (with a possible lower-lat bias) *and* at the lower angles where the lats are most active.
- **High rows** with the arm slightly across the body may be the best isolation-adjacent option: a good stretch (the lats wrap the rib cage, so crossing the body lengthens them) at a **lower arm elevation** where leverage is better. Whether this beats a machine pullover he states plainly is **unknown**.

Note the framing throughout: "we may speculate", "this is hypothetical", "currently unknown". The ranking is a resistance-profile argument, not a result.

## What this changes for the library

- The library's **"no study has ever directly measured back hypertrophy"** entry is confirmed from a second, independent source — by someone with strong incentive to claim otherwise in a video titled "36 studies".
- The library's pick of a **close-grip cable row or neutral pulldown** sits comfortably inside his framework, but that framework says it is **half a programme**: both are shoulder extension, biasing the upper lat. On his reasoning it should be paired with a **wide-grip vertical pull** for the middle and lower regions, or swapped for a **medium-grip vertical pull**.
- **Wide-grip rows** should be recorded as the one common back exercise he actively argues against for lats.
- Any lat exercise ranking here — his or ours — should carry the note that it rests on moment arms and isometric sEMG, and would be overturned by a single decent direct trial.

## Where the conclusion outruns the evidence

The regional-bias claims are the weakest link. They chain a moment-arm model to isometric sEMG at a joint angle that in one case (20° abduction, for the wide-grip adduction claim) **is not reached in the actual exercise** — he acknowledges this and assumes the bias persists anyway — and then to regional growth nobody has measured. He hedges nearly all of it correctly, but the video's structure delivers a confident-sounding exercise hierarchy from evidence that cannot support a hierarchy at all.
