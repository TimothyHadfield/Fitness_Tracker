# You Don't Have to Wait 48 Hours: Two Trials on 24-Hour Rest

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How Long Should You Wait to Train a Muscle Again?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xyELvDINO7M)) · 2023-04-24 · 10 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- Two trials have directly compared training the same programme with **24 hours** between sessions versus **48–72 hours**. Neither found a penalty for the 24-hour condition in strength or size.
- The "muscles need 48–72 hours to recover" rule is not something the training literature actually supports as a **requirement**. High-frequency training — up to 5 sessions per muscle per week — has repeatedly come back fine.
- The explanation Dhim favours is the **repeated bout effect**: the 24-hour group was probably training somewhat fatigued in the early weeks, and probably recovering fully within 24 hours by the later weeks.
- **Connective tissue is the honest gap.** How much resistance training damages tendons, how long repair takes, and how training variables change that is not clear from the literature, so he refuses to give a confident answer on 24-hour training and tendon risk.
- None of this makes short rest intervals mandatory. Training a muscle 48–72 hours or more apart remains perfectly fine.

## The two direct trials
[00:30] **Yang and colleagues (2018, Singapore).** 30 active men assigned to a 24-hour or a 48–72-hour group, both doing the same session three times a week for **12 weeks**, with nutritional patterns similar between groups. **10RM strength across a range of exercises increased similarly**, and lean mass increased comparably. No sign that 48–72 hours of rest is essential.

[01:32] **Carvalho and Santos (Portugal).** 21 **trained** men, again 24-hour versus 48–72-hour, on the same three-day-per-week programme for **7 weeks**. Neither group showed measurable fat-free mass increases at all — Dhim attributes this to fat-free mass being an imprecise hypertrophy measure and suspects a more sensitive method would have found growth. Bench press and leg press 1RM gains were fairly similar, with the numbers slightly favouring the 24-hour group; the difference was not statistically significant, and he immediately offers the mundane explanation that the 24-hour group may simply have had slightly better average responders.

That is characteristic of the channel: he does not bank the non-significant difference that suits his argument.

## The frequency literature backs it up
[03:05] Quite a few studies find training a muscle **five or more days per week** works fine. He highlights Zaroni and colleagues (2019) in trained men: all subjects trained the **same exercises for the same total weekly sets**, but a bro-split group hit each muscle once or twice weekly while a full-body group hit each muscle five times weekly, for 10 weeks. Back squat, bench press and row strength gains were **not statistically different**, though squat percentages leaned toward full body — and triceps, elbow flexor and vastus lateralis hypertrophy **tended to be superior** for the full-body group.

Note what this means for the 48-hour rule specifically: a five-day frequency implies well under 48 hours between hits, and it did not blunt anything.

## Fatigue, and the repeated bout effect
[04:39] His framing here is worth keeping. It is logical to want to be fully recovered every session, but training under *some* degree of fatigue may be fine, provided the stimulus isn't killed and recovery isn't made worse. He notes data showing that training a muscle when it isn't fully recovered does not necessarily worsen recovery. He is careful to say **some** degree: training under extreme fatigue, where the muscle can barely contract, probably isn't worthwhile.

[06:11] The mechanism he leans on is the repeated bout effect — the collection of adaptations that shorten recovery duration with repeated exposure to the same training. His claim is that the 24-hour groups were likely fatigued only in the early weeks and fully recovering within 24 hours later on. That's a plausible inference, but it was **not measured in either trial**, and he presents it as a belief rather than a result.

[06:41] He also flags that all of this is averages, and some individuals may not fit. His practical test is sensible: if you train a muscle more often than every 48 hours and you consistently struggle to recover with lacklustre gains, that will become apparent.

## The connective tissue caveat
[06:41] Tendon injuries tend to arise from gradual accumulation of micro-damage — a mismatch between damage and repair. But it is **not clear** how much resistance training damages tendons and ligaments, how long repair takes, or how training variables alter that. So he will not say whether 24-hour training raises the risk of accumulating connective tissue damage.

He offers three partial mitigations rather than an answer: most high-frequency programmes use **lower set numbers per session**, so per-session connective tissue strain is lower; different tendons and ligaments likely have different damage resistance, and different people have more or less resilient tissue; and deloads may be what actually dissipates accumulated connective tissue fatigue regardless of how you train. He notes there doesn't appear to be cellular research settling it.

## Practical takeaway
[08:12] The claim is a permission, not a prescription: you *can* build a successful programme in which a muscle is trained 24 hours apart, and training 48–72 hours or longer apart is equally fine. Recovery duration will be shaped by your training variables and by non-training factors either way.

**Overreach flag:** the repeated-bout explanation for why 24-hour training works is inferred, not measured — neither trial tracked recovery status across weeks, so the mechanism is a story that fits the result rather than evidence for it.
