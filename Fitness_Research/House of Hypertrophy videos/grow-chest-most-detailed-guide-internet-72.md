# Chest Training: Which Press, Whether You Need Incline, and Whether Flies Earn a Slot

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Grow Your Chest: The Most DETAILED Guide on the Internet (72 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wa8vJg-h_a8)) · 2024-02-26 · 49 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **Every horizontal press is roughly interchangeable for the pecs.** Barbell, dumbbell, Smith, machine, push-up — all fundamentally load shoulder horizontal flexion, all show similar pec growth or activity, and strength transfers between them in both directions. Pick on preference, not on a claimed superiority.
- **Dedicated upper-pec work is justified, but not for the reason usually given.** Flat benching grows the upper pec at a *similar percentage* to the middle pec, but from a smaller starting size — so in absolute centimetres the upper pec gains less. The case for incline is arithmetic, not activation.
- **Dedicated lower-pec work is probably not needed.** In the same two studies, the lower pec grew comparably to the middle pec in both percentage and absolute terms from flat pressing alone.
- **Isolation flies are not required.** Removing the triceps from the movement does not raise pec activation — two analyses found fly and bench pec activity comparable. Flies earn a slot for programme-design reasons (chest volume without triceps volume), not because they stimulate the pecs harder.
- **Tension near the shortened position appears to add little.** Three studies found lengthened partials beat full ROM; this drives his repeated dismissal of "squeeze at the top" arguments for converging machines, cables and the Lee's fend press.
- He ends by dismantling a study that *supports* the popular incline-only position, on the grounds that its growth numbers are physiologically absurd. That is the most valuable ninety seconds of the video.

## Decision 1: which horizontal press?

[04:13] A study of flat barbell benching three times a week for six months grew the middle pec **37%**. He immediately refuses the obvious inference. Machine pressing has produced similar pec growth and activity to barbell; dumbbells evoke similar pec activity to barbell; push-ups have produced similar pec growth to barbell benching. Strength carries over in both directions between machine and barbell, and between Smith and barbell, and push-up performance correlates fairly strongly with bench performance.

His reasoning is mechanical rather than statistical, and it is the right kind: all these variations load shoulder horizontal flexion with the pecs stretched at the bottom, so taken to or near failure they all expose the pecs to high tension. The differences that remain are second-order. He gives five of them as tie-breakers rather than rankings:

1. **Push-ups** additionally engage rectus abdominis (plank position) and serratus anterior (scapular protraction can happen). Progressive overload is harder, but not impossible — reps between roughly 6 and 35 are similarly effective, and one paper found adding reps at a fixed load produced similar growth to adding load in a fixed rep range. Weighted push-ups extend the runway further.
2. **Dumbbells train the triceps slightly less.** You cannot apply lateral force into dumbbells without dropping them; every other variation generates lateral force whether you notice or not. EMG confirms reduced triceps activity. Whether that is a feature or a bug depends on your programme.
3. **Failure is safer on some variations than others.** [08:16] A velocity-loss study on trained lifters ran four Smith-machine bench groups — one rep per set, or reps until velocity dropped **15%, 30% or 50%**. Pec growth was best in the 50% velocity-loss group, which by extrapolation is roughly **70–80% of the maximum possible reps**. No true-failure condition was included, but since 1–3 reps from failure matches failure elsewhere, the practical instruction stands. On a free-weight barbell that means a spotter or safeties; without them, other variations are the safer bet.
4. **Recovery perception differs slightly.** A fatigue study compared Smith, dumbbell and barbell benching in trained men. Chest fatigue, swelling and soreness were comparable across all three over 96 hours (itself indirect evidence of a similar pec stimulus); triceps fatigue was lower with dumbbells; but *perceived* recovery was worse after the barbell. He calls this not very important — fatigue is not inherently bad and adaptation blunts it.
5. **Stretch depth.** Dumbbells permit a deeper pec stretch than a barbell, and EMG cannot see stretch-mediated force, so this is a real possibility EMG comparisons would miss. The cambered bar and deficit push-up have the same logic. **Floor presses go the other way** — they cap how far the pecs lengthen and are therefore likely suboptimal for growth, though fine for strength or if pain limits bottom-range work.

The stretch case rests on three lines: [12:21] three studies where a **lengthened partial beat a full range of motion**; isometric training at long muscle lengths beating short; and the exercise-comparison finding that seated leg curls stretch the hamstrings more than lying leg curls and produced **1.5× the hamstring growth**. He also flags a genuine caveat honestly: nearly all stretch research is on untrained subjects over ≤12 weeks, and there is a live hypothesis that stretch-mediated gains plateau early. His answer — even if they plateau, you still want them — is a reasonable practical position rather than a scientific one.

## Decision 2: do you need dedicated upper-pec work?

Yes, and the argument is worth understanding because it is not the one you usually hear.

[18:27] The six-month flat-bench study that grew the middle pec 37% also grew the **upper pec 36%**. A second, Australian study on trained subjects found flat benching grew the middle pec **10%** and the upper pec **7.4%**. On percentages, flat pressing looks like it covers the upper chest fine.

[19:30] Convert to absolute centimetres and it doesn't. The upper pec starts smaller, so a comparable percentage is a smaller real increase. That is the whole case: **flat pressing grows the upper pec less in absolute terms**, and since machine and weighted push-up variations show similar upper-pec activity to flat barbell, the same applies to all of them. This is a genuinely careful piece of reasoning and it inverts a common misreading of the same data.

## Decision 3: which upper-pec option?

[20:31] Well-controlled EMG finds the upper-to-middle pec activity ratio improves when force is directed obliquely upward. Incline pressing does that. Of **seven studies comparing incline to flat, five found greater upper-pec activity** with the incline; most also found reduced middle and lower pec activity and higher front delt activity.

On angle the literature does not converge: one paper favours **30°**, another **44°**, another finds 30° and 45° similar. He attributes this partly to EMG's limits and partly to real individual differences in architecture, leverage and bench setup, and recommends experimenting rather than adopting a number.

He then adds the under-appreciated routes. The upper pec has the best leverage for **shoulder flexion**, so pressing patterns that shift toward shoulder flexion — a **closer grip with elbows tucked**, or a **reverse grip** — may bias it. The data is genuinely split and he says so: **four of eight** grip-width papers favour a closer grip for upper-pec activity; **one of two** reverse-grip papers. One paper found close-grip flat and wide-grip incline produced upper-pec activity that was *not statistically different*, with raw numbers leaning to close grip; the same paper found close-and-reverse-grip incline also not statistically different from wide-grip incline. So the honest summary is a long list of options with no separation between them.

Two he rates lower: the **landmine press** (upward pressing direction is right, but the very close hand spacing restricts pec stretch) and the **Lee's fend press** (restricted stretch, and a plate you can only load so far — do it if you enjoy it).

His recommendation is to pick any of them and use subjective response — perceived upper-pec involvement, pump, next-day soreness — as a tiebreaker, citing Greg Nuckols's report that flat reverse benching makes his upper pecs outrageously sore while incline never does. **He explicitly flags this as unvalidated**: subjective sensations are not validated proxies for activation, and pump and soreness are not strong hypertrophy drivers. He argues only that, with other factors controlled, they may be an indirect read on regional tension. This is the video's clearest instance of a recommendation running ahead of its evidence, and he says so himself.

## Decision 4: do you need lower-pec work?

Probably not. [28:11] In both studies above, the lower pec grew similarly to the middle pec in **both** percentage and absolute terms from flat barbell pressing — the arithmetic problem that justifies incline work does not arise here.

If you want to bias it anyway: decline pressing, machine decline, hands-elevated push-ups, dips, and the fly variations below. Two EMG studies comparing a **15° decline to flat found lower-pec activity similar**; other papers found upper and middle pec and front delt activity *lower* with the decline. So the decline's claim to selectivity is that it reduces everything else, not that it raises lower-pec output. On **dips** he is straightforwardly empty-handed: no published comparison to other chest exercises exists, only a paper comparing dip variations and one unofficial EMG analysis.

## Decision 5: do flies earn a slot?

[31:16] The intuitive argument — remove the triceps and the CNS can drive the pecs harder — is not supported. Two papers found pec activity during the challenging lower portion of a dumbbell fly comparable to flat barbell benching, with one finding higher *average* activity for the bench (driven by the top portion, which by his own shortened-position argument shouldn't matter). Another found pec and front-delt activity similar between barbell bench and machine fly, with the authors concluding the results clash with the isolation premise.

So: **isolation is not essential for long-term pec growth.** It earns a slot for other reasons — sensible variety, and specifically when you want more chest stimulus without more triceps stimulus. He also allows for individuals whose pressing predominantly taxes the triceps regardless of technique, for whom isolation may be genuinely necessary, citing research he himself labels as having notable limitations.

Within flies: cables in line with the arms load the short position (lower fatigue, still stimulatory); a cable set slightly behind the body, dumbbell flies and machine flies load the stretch. He dismisses the "arms straighter stretches the pecs more" argument correctly — **the pecs attach to the upper arm, not the forearm, so elbow bend is irrelevant to pec length**. **Low cable flies** are his upper-pec isolation pick; **high cable flies and decline dumbbell flies** align with lower-pec fibre direction. On the deep-stretch-is-injurious debate he declines to conclude, noting the injury literature on chest movements is low quality and minimal.

On **pullovers** [38:32] he lands on "both, probably": the lats have poor leverage at the top position where the free-weight pullover is challenging, and EMG shows middle and lower pec activity exceeding lat activity there — but the lats are also well stretched, and the deep-squat glute data (90° vs **140° knee flexion**, deeper grew glute max considerably more) shows a muscle can grow well at length despite low leverage and low activity. He wants direct growth measurement and admits it does not exist.

## The study he throws out

[42:40] This is the strongest section. A study of **47 previously untrained men** compared incline-only, flat-only, and both, training **once per week for 8 weeks**. The incline-only group saw the best upper, middle *and* lower pec growth — a result that contradicts most of the video and would be the single best piece of evidence for an incline-only programme.

He rejects it, and shows his working. Pec thickness rose **54–62%** in the incline group and over 40% in some regions for the others, across **eight training sessions** — about **4.6% growth per workout**, as a group average, against a literature averaging 33 sessions for far smaller gains. Further, **only 30 of the 47 completers appear in the hypertrophy measurements**, with no explanation given, which points at measurement error. And the finding that incline grew the *middle and lower* pec more conflicts with multiple EMG papers and with a study finding a lower mid-pec pump from incline than flat benching.

Presenting the study that most supports a popular position and then explaining precisely why he will not use it is the behaviour that makes this channel worth reading despite the creator having no formal credentials.

## Where the evidence is thinner than the confidence

Most of the region-targeting architecture in this video — upper vs middle vs lower pec, grip width, reverse grip, incline angle, decline selectivity, dips, pullovers — rests on **EMG and acute measures, not growth outcomes**. He acknowledges this repeatedly and has appended a long note to the description defending a narrowed position: EMG is unreliable across biomechanically dissimilar exercises (citing a squat-vs-hip-thrust study where EMG failed to predict growth) but may be informative for *subtle* comparisons like incline angles and grip widths, on the strength of the calf-raise case where foot-position EMG predictions were later confirmed by an actual growth study. That is a defensible refinement rather than a dodge, but it remains a mechanism argument doing the work that outcome data would normally do — and it underpins essentially the whole upper- and lower-pec half of the guide.
