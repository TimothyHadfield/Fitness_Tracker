# Is the Barbell Bench Press Enough on Its Own for the Chest?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Is the Bench Press ENOUGH for Chest Growth? (24 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rzNAC1xeZPE)) · 2021-01-28 · 27 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **The bench press is an excellent pec major builder, and the growth-rate data are stronger than most people assume.** Five longitudinal studies measured pec growth from bench pressing alone; three of them produced rates at or above the benchmark rate for elbow flexors, a muscle group that normally grows faster than the quads.
- **The sternal head is close to fully covered.** The clavicular and costal heads grow meaningfully too — but whether they grow *as much* is unsettled: two studies from the same lab say yes, a study in trained lifters says slightly less.
- **The question cannot actually be answered yet**, and he says so. No study ran longer than 24 weeks; four of five used untrained subjects; and **no study has ever compared bench-press-only to bench-press-plus-other-chest-work at matched volume.** Without that comparison, "enough" is untestable.
- **Incline beats decline as the angle worth adding.** A 47-subject trial found the incline group gained more clavicular head thickness than flat or a split of both, while sternal and costal gains were equal. Decline has EMG showing no advantage over flat and **no longitudinal research at all**.
- **The complement he recommends is stretch, not angle** — machine or cable chest flies, which load the pec at a longer length than the bench press reaches.
- Verdict: bench pressing alone will grow the chest well; adding other exercises "at the very least would likely not harm" growth and could enhance it, and helps with long-term stagnation and enjoyment.

## What the pec major does, and whether the bench press does it

He starts from anatomy because the whole argument depends on whether the bench press produces the movements the muscle has leverage for.

**Origins:** medial clavicle, sternum, ribs, costal cartilages, and the aponeurosis of the external oblique. **Insertion:** all fibres converge on the lateral side of the upper arm. Divisions are not universally agreed — the three-head model (clavicular / sternal / costal, i.e. upper / middle / lower) versus the two-head model (clavicular / sternocostal), the latter supported by distinct nerve supplies: the **lateral pectoral nerve** to the clavicular head, the **medial pectoral nerve** to the sternocostal head.

Functions, with the competing muscle noted in each case:

- **Horizontal flexion.** From ~0–10° the anterior deltoid has slightly better leverage; from **10° to 140° the pec major has the greatest leverage**. This is the pec's signature movement.
- **Shoulder flexion.** Clavicular head substantially, sternal head somewhat — but the anterior deltoid has the greatest leverage throughout, so it is probably more activated.
- **Shoulder adduction.** Sternal and costal heads moderately involved, but lat and teres major far more.
- **Shoulder abduction.** Clavicular head slightly involved past 40°, but anterior and especially lateral deltoid far more.
- **Internal rotation.** The pec major as a whole is highly involved.
- Curiously, at high shoulder flexion angles the **costal head appears to contribute to shoulder extension** — the fact that later justifies the pullover.

Conclusion: adduction and abduction exercises are poor pec choices. Everything else on that list happens in the bench press.

**Three technique implications he derives:**

1. **Grip width.** Grip width fixes the degree of horizontal flexion for the whole set. Wrists, elbows and shoulders aligned before the descent gives 90° of horizontal flexion. Since the pec's greatest leverage is around **40–50° of horizontal flexion**, that suggests roughly a **1.5× to 2× shoulder-width grip** as theoretically optimal.
2. **Bar path and shoulder flexion.** The distance between shoulders and bar at the bottom determines how much shoulder flexion the ascent requires. Since the anterior deltoid out-levers the pec in shoulder flexion, you would not want to *maximise* shoulder flexion for pec development. Touching around mid to lower chest works for most.
3. **Elbow flare.** Flaring lets the pec pull on the upper arm more efficiently and permits greater lateral force (which is the triceps' contribution in the bench press), and for most people naturally produces internal rotation — another pec function.

## The growth data, study by study

This is the substance of the video and the numbers are the point. His benchmark throughout is a **review by Wernbom and colleagues**, which pooled many studies to establish typical rates of cross-sectional area increase: **quadriceps ~0.11%/day** (at ~6 sets/session, 3×/week) and **elbow flexors ~0.20%/day** (at ~5 sets/session, 3×/week), mostly from untrained subjects. He notes Wernbom's own explanation for why elbow flexors out-grow quads — the quads are partly "trained" by daily activity — and argues the pec is more like the elbow flexors in that respect, so **0.20%/day is the fair benchmark**.

**Akagi et al. — correlational.** 18 men with ≥1 year of training. Smith machine bench press 1RM correlated significantly with pec major cross-sectional area (largest value across regions used). Implies that getting stronger on the press tracks with a bigger pec, but it is a correlation, tells you nothing about whether the bench alone suffices, and nothing about regions.

**Brandao et al. — the modest result.** Untrained men, **3–5 sets to failure at 80% 1RM**, 3 min rest, **2×/week for 10 weeks**, 2× shoulder-width grip. Pec CSA at the T3–T4 region rose **9.1%**, or **0.13%/day** — above the quad benchmark, below the elbow flexor benchmark. He argues this could be read as suboptimal, then immediately supplies the mitigating detail: the benchmark rate came from ~5 sets 3×/week, while this study averaged **4 sets 2×/week**. Less work, less growth per day — not necessarily a failure of the exercise.

**Yasuda et al. — the strong result.** Untrained men, **3×10 at 75% 1RM**, 3 min rest, **3×/week for 6 weeks**, grip width unspecified, 1RM retested at week 3 to readjust load. Pec CSA at the muscle belly rose **17.6%**, or **0.42%/day** — slightly more than double the elbow flexor benchmark.

**Ogasawara et al. (thickness) — the long one.** **7 untrained men**, 3×10 at 75% 1RM, 2–3 min rest, **3×/week for 24 weeks**, 2× shoulder-width grip, 1RM retested every 3 weeks. Pec thickness at the third and fourth costal cartilages measured weekly; thickness and 1RM climbed together throughout. **+43% thickness, or 0.26%/day.** He immediately flags the comparison problem: **thickness and cross-sectional area are not the same measurement**, so benchmarking this against a CSA figure may not be legitimate.

Those three all measured a single region, roughly the sternal head. The next two address regional differences.

**Davies et al. — the trained-lifter study.** Men and women with an average of **5 years' training experience**, **4×5 at 85% 1RM**, 5 min rest, **2×/week for 8 weeks**, grip width unspecified, 1RM retested every 2 weeks. Thickness measured in three intercostal spaces: ribs 2–3 (upper / clavicular), 3–4 (middle / sternal), 4–5 (lower / costal).

| Region | Thickness increase | Per day |
|---|---|---|
| Upper (clavicular) | 7.44% | 0.13% |
| Middle (sternal) | 10.06% | 0.18% |
| Lower (costal) | 7.45% | 0.13% |

All below the 0.20%/day elbow flexor benchmark — but again it is thickness, not CSA, and more importantly these were **trained subjects** benchmarked against data from mostly untrained subjects. Read that way the results are good. Regionally: no extreme differences, upper and lower similar to each other, both somewhat below the middle.

**Ogasawara et al. (CSA) — the cleanest regional data.** Untrained men, 3×10 at 75% 1RM, 2–3 min rest, **3×/week for 24 weeks**, 2× shoulder-width grip, 1RM retested every 3 weeks. CSA measured at 25% (upper), 50% (middle) and 75% (lower) of pec length.

| Region | CSA increase | Per day |
|---|---|---|
| Upper | 36.3% | 0.22% |
| Middle | 37.3% | 0.22% |
| Lower | 40% | 0.24% |

Untrained subjects and CSA, so this is the fairest comparison to the elbow flexor benchmark — and **all three regions exceed it**, with essentially **equal rates across regions**.

**Where that leaves the regional question.** Brandao says decent; Yasuda and both Ogasawara studies say excellent. On the clavicular and costal heads, **Davies says slightly less than the sternal head; Ogasawara says equal**. He attributes the discrepancy plausibly to training status — Ogasawara's subjects were untrained, Davies' had five years of experience — without pretending to resolve it.

## Why the question is still open

He devotes a section to this and it is the most disciplined part of the video:

1. **Duration.** Every study ran **24 weeks or less**, and four used untrained subjects. Over a training career, bench-press-only work could stall in ways a 6-to-24-week study cannot detect; varying training variables, including exercise selection, is a standard way past stagnation.
2. **The missing comparison.** No study has run bench-press-only against bench-press-plus-other-chest-exercises **at matched weekly set volume**. Without volume equating, any comparison confounds exercise variety with total work. Until that trial exists, "is the bench press enough" is unanswerable in principle.

That second point is the honest core of the video: he has five studies showing the bench press grows the chest well, and zero studies bearing on whether *only* the bench press is as good as a mixed program.

## What to add, and why

**Flies — for stretch, not for a region.** He notes something usually left out: the bench press already stretches the pec under load, and there is evidence the sarcomeres of the **clavicular, sternal and costal** heads are all stretched far enough to produce meaningful passive tension. Passive tension plus the high active tension in the lowering phase is a large total mechanical tension, the best-characterised growth driver. But some exercises stretch the pec **more** than the bench press does: the **seated machine chest fly**, dumbbell flies, and cable fly variations. He prefers the seated machine version for stability and control, and warns that dumbbell flies have a reputation for shoulder injury — avoid if you have or develop shoulder problems.

**Incline — for the clavicular head.** The EMG evidence is a mess, and he lays out the mess rather than cherry-picking:

- **Lauver et al.** — similar clavicular activation at 30° and 45° incline, both greater than 0° and 15° decline through most of the concentric.
- **Coratella et al.** — greater clavicular activation at 45° incline vs 0° and 15° decline.
- **Rodríguez-Ridao et al.** — compared 0°, 15°, 30°, 45° and 60°; activation greatest at **30°**, with 45° and 60° producing *slightly less than flat*.
- **Stephan and Armstrong** — no difference between 30° incline and 15° decline.
- **Saeterbakken et al.** — similar clavicular activation across 25° incline, 0°, and 25° decline.

Two find an incline advantage, three do not. He explicitly says EMG has noteworthy limitations and we should not get too caught up in it — and goes to the one longitudinal study instead.

**Chavez et al.** split **47 untrained men** into flat, incline, or combination groups, all training **1×/week for 8 weeks**. Flat group: 4 sets to failure on the flat Smith machine bench at 8–12RM per session. Incline group: same, at **44° incline**. Combination: 2 sets of each. Result: **sternal and costal thickness gains similar across all three groups; clavicular head thickness gains greater in the incline group.** Notably, the combination group's **2 sets of incline was not enough** to produce the clavicular benefit — the effect required 4 sets.

His caveats: it used a Smith machine, whose mechanics differ from a barbell. His practical conclusion is appropriately conditional — if at some point you feel your upper chest lags, experiment with incline pressing at various angles. Other clavicular options mentioned without evidence: incline dumbbell press, low-to-high cable fly, decline push-up.

**Decline — no case for it.** Lauver and Coratella both found similar costal head activation between flat and 15° decline; Saeterbakken found similar sternocostal activation between 25° decline and flat. And **there is no longitudinal research on decline pressing at all.**

**Pullovers — theoretically interesting for the costal head.** Because the costal head contributes to shoulder extension at high shoulder flexion angles, and the pullover flexes the shoulder deeply on the eccentric then extends on the concentric, the pullover may recruit the costal head well. Three studies show the barbell pullover highly activates the pec major — but they measured the **sternal or clavicular** head, not the costal head, so the specific claim is untested. He says as much.

## Overreach flags

The grip-width recommendation (1.5–2× shoulder width) is derived purely from where the pec's moment arm peaks in a modelling study — no study has compared chest growth between grip widths. Similarly, the elbow-flare advice is a force-analysis argument, not an outcome finding. And the per-day growth-rate comparisons that anchor the entire evaluation mix **thickness** and **cross-sectional area** measurements against a CSA-derived benchmark; he flags the problem each time it arises but still uses the comparison to reach his verdicts.

## How this sits with the rest of the library

**The verdict conflicts with where the library's chest rankings later landed.** This 2021 video treats the barbell bench press as close to ideal for the sternal region and an excellent pec builder overall — and it is the only note in the library that puts direct longitudinal pec growth data behind that claim. `Jeff Nippard videos/chest-exercises-ranked.md` (2024-03-11) instead crowns the **machine chest press** as best-of-the-best and drops the barbell bench to **A tier**, with one of its three deductions being that the bar stops on the rib cage and so gives a shallower stretch than the alternatives. `Jeff Nippard videos/only-25-exercises-need.md` (2026) puts the bench at **#11** and includes it for movement-pattern value rather than as an optimal hypertrophy tool.

The interesting detail is that **HoH's own reasoning contains the seed of that demotion** — his recommended complement is a fly, chosen precisely because it stretches the pec further than the bench press can. He just doesn't treat depth of stretch as a ranking criterion; by 2024 the field does. Conversely, this note is the strongest counterweight the library holds against the bench press's slide: none of the ranking videos cite any of these five growth studies, and the bench remains the only chest exercise with 24-week data showing region-by-region growth at or above the elbow-flexor benchmark rate.

**On incline vs decline the two sources agree**, and by the same route. Nippard's note calls incline "arguably the better default" on the strength of a study finding it produced the same mid and lower pec growth as flat plus more upper pec growth (Chaves 2020), and holds decline in B tier while noting decline has no long-term research. That is the same shape of argument, from the same kind of trial, that this 2021 video makes from Chavez et al. — three years earlier, with a slightly different reservation (Smith machine, 1×/week).

**On the flat bench across three rankings**, `Jeff Nippard videos/SUMMARY-exercise-selection.md` already records the movement from #1 (2022) to A tier (2024) to #11 (2026), and attributes it to a changing rubric rather than new evidence. This note is worth reading alongside that entry, because it is the closest thing the library has to the *evidence* the rubric changes were being applied to.
