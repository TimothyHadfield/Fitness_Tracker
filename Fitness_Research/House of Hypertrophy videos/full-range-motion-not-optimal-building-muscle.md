# The Partial-ROM Case: Why "Full ROM Wins" Was Testing the Wrong Partial

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Full Range of Motion Is NOT Optimal For Building Muscle (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VCN95VtHvs4)) · 2021-06-03 · 21 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- Before Pedrosa, six studies compared full to partial ROM: **four favoured full ROM, one found no difference, one favoured partials**. But all four pro-full-ROM studies used a partial that trained the muscle at a **short or neutral** length. They test short-length partials, not partials as a category.
- **Pedrosa (n=45 untrained women, 12 weeks, knee extension)** filled the gap with five groups: long-length partial (100°–65° knee flexion), short-length partial (65°–30°), full ROM (100°–30°), a varied group alternating long and short, and a control. The **long-length partial produced the best growth overall**, with the varied group a close second.
- Regional detail matters: at 40% femur length all groups were similar; at 50% and 60% the long-length and varied groups beat the other two; at **70% the long-length group beat everything**, including the varied group.
- Supporting evidence: McMahon found long-length (90°–40°) beat short-length (50°–0°) knee extension partials at every measured vastus lateralis region, and Oranchuk's review of nine isometric studies found long-length isometrics grow more than short-length.
- His proposed mechanism is **greater and more consistent mechanical tension** — both active (voluntary activation is higher at long lengths) and passive (titin) — not metabolic stress or damage, which he explicitly rejects as unsatisfying explanations.
- He closes by saying it is **one study**, it should not be treated as confirmed, and the knee extension's resistance profile may partly explain the result.

## The dismantling of the "full ROM wins" consensus
[01:01] The whole first act is a single argument applied four times. In each of the four studies that favoured full ROM, the partial condition worked the muscle at a **short** length, so the comparison confounds "partial" with "short":

- **Pinto** — preacher curl, full 0°–130° elbow flexion vs partial 50°–100°. The 50°–100° window puts the biceps at short-to-neutral length. Biceps thickness favoured full ROM. He asks: what if the partial had been 0°–50°?
- **Bloomquist** — squats, full 0°–120° knee flexion vs partial 0°–60°, so the quadriceps worked short. Thigh CSA across many regions favoured full ROM. What about 60°–120°?
- **Kubo** — squats, full 0°–140° vs partial 0°–90°. Quadriceps volume was **similar** between groups; glute max and adductor volume favoured full ROM.
- **McMahon** — lower-body exercises, full 0°–90° vs partial 0°–50°, quadriceps short. Vastus lateralis CSA at most regions favoured full ROM.

The conclusion he draws is narrow and correct: these four show a **short-length partial is inferior to full ROM**. They say nothing about a long-length partial.

## Pedrosa
[04:36] 45 untrained women, five groups, knee extension machine, 2 s up / 2 s down, three times a week for 12 weeks. All training groups did 3–6 sets of 7 reps at 60% 1RM with 3 min rest, where the 60% 1RM was **specific to each group's own range of motion** and retested every two weeks to keep loading equivalent. That load-normalisation is the design detail that makes the study worth taking seriously.

Rectus femoris and vastus lateralis CSA were measured at 40, 50, 60 and 70% of femur length. The long-length group had the best overall growth; the varied group was close behind; full ROM and short-length trailed. Region by region:

| Site | Result |
|---|---|
| 40% femur length | All groups statistically similar |
| 50% | Long-length ≈ varied > full ROM, short-length |
| 60% | Long-length ≈ varied > full ROM, short-length |
| 70% | Long-length > all others |

[08:13] Two conclusions he draws: short-length and long-length partials are **not the same thing** and should never have been pooled; and a long-length partial is not inferior to full ROM — at most measured regions it was superior.

## The supporting long-vs-short literature
[08:44] **McMahon** (a different study from the ROM one above) compared knee extension at 90°–40° (long) against 50°–0° (short). Vastus lateralis CSA increases were greater for the long-length group at every measured region. No full-ROM group, so it only establishes the long/short contrast — he says so.

[09:46] **Oranchuk's review** pooled nine isometric training studies and found isometrics held at longer muscle lengths produce greater growth than at short lengths. Isometric evidence is a step removed from dynamic training, but it points the same way.

## Why a long-length partial might beat full ROM
[10:16] He runs through metabolic stress and damage first and then dismisses them, which is the reverse of the usual creator move. Long-length isometric knee extension produces greater oxygen consumption (implying more metabolic stress), and several studies find more muscle damage in the days after long-length training. His objections: those studies compared long-length to **short-length**, not to full ROM, and more importantly the role of metabolic stress and damage in growth is far from established, with several lines of evidence saying more of either does not mean more growth.

[11:16] The mechanism he actually backs is **mechanical tension**, split into two components.

**Active tension.** Force from the contractile machinery, determined by how many fibres are recruited and how much force each produces. Voluntary activation — the fraction of a muscle's force-producing potential the nervous system can access — is measurably higher at longer muscle lengths. In one cited quadriceps dataset, maximal voluntary activation averaged **93.6% at 50° of knee flexion, 96% at 75°, and 98.5% at 100°**. A long-length partial therefore keeps you in the high-activation window for the whole set, whereas a full ROM rep passes through it and then out of it.

**Passive tension.** Force generated when titin is stretched — the elastic-band analogy — detected by the same mechanosensors. He is unusually careful here: a muscle being at a long length does **not** guarantee its sarcomeres are stretched, sarcomere arrangement varies, and sarcomeres at different points within the same muscle sit at different lengths at any given joint angle. He cites data (Cutts) suggesting the quadriceps regions measured *do* reach sufficiently stretched sarcomere lengths for high passive tension. So a long-length partial may deliver consistently high passive tension where full ROM's passive contribution appears and then vanishes.

The summary is that a long-length partial may deliver **more consistent** active and passive tension across the set. He explicitly labels both as hypotheses requiring verification.

## What he says against his own conclusion
[18:42] The limitations section is the strongest reason to trust this video.

1. **The exercise chosen suits the result.** On a knee extension, full ROM is hardest near full extension — where the quadriceps are **short**. So the full-ROM group was maximally challenged at short lengths. Would the same hold on an exercise like the squat, which is hardest when the quads, glutes and adductors are already lengthened? He does not know, and says so.
2. **Incomplete measurement.** CSA was measured at 40–70% of femur length for rectus femoris and vastus lateralis only. Nothing at 10–30% or 70–100%, nothing for vastus medialis or intermedius. He concedes the unmeasured regions could in principle have favoured full ROM or short-length.
3. **One study.** He says outright it would not be sensible to conclude long-length partials are superior, that replication is needed, and that if a viewer wants to experiment there is probably no big downside either way.

## Where this lands against the library
This is a **2021** video and the library position it bears on has moved past it. His numbers at the time — long-length partials beat short-length partials, and match or beat full ROM — are consistent with the library's current holding (long-length beating short-length in 7 of 8, and full ROM in 4 of 5). What this video cannot address is the 2024 null trial on 30 trained lifters, which postdates it by three years. Two things about the gap are worth carrying forward:

- Every study he relies on here is on **untrained** subjects. Pedrosa used 45 untrained women; the McMahon long/short comparison and most of the ROM literature are similarly untrained. A null result in trained lifters is not obviously in conflict with anything he presents.
- His central claim is **narrower** than the title. He argues that long-length partials are not inferior to full ROM and may be superior on some exercises, not that full ROM is a mistake. The clickbait title overstates a conclusion he then spends the last three minutes hedging.

**Overreach flag:** the title asserts full ROM is not optimal on the strength of one 12-week trial in untrained women measuring two of four quadriceps muscles, and he leans on voluntary activation — an acute neural measure — as the mechanistic bridge to long-term growth.
