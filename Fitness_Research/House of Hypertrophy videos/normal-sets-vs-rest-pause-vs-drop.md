# Set Structures Compared: Normal, Rest-Pause, Drop and Cluster

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Normal Sets vs Rest-Pause vs Drop Sets vs Cluster Sets for Hypertrophy & Strength" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uI-eUBq98kk)) · 2023-03-05 · 17 min
**Topic:** Effort & failure

## Bottom line
- **For hypertrophy, no set structure beats normal sets.** The best-designed head-to-head study (Enes 2021, 28 trained men, 8 weeks) found rest-pause, drop sets and normal sets produced comparable growth, and a meta-analysis of the drop-set literature agrees.
- **For strength, normal sets are the practical winner** — not because the structure is special, but because strength tracks accumulated reps at heavy loads, and normal sets are the easiest way to do that. Drop sets are actively unsuited since they reduce load.
- **Cluster sets are the one structure he flags as possibly worse for hypertrophy.** A 2021 meta-analysis found parity, but a later study found normal sets beat clusters for middle and upper pec growth while matching them for strength.
- He rejects the "effective reps" rationale for going beyond failure on mechanistic grounds: the strongest motor-unit modelling study implies **full recruitment and high motor-unit forces are reached before failure**, so nothing extra is unlocked by pushing past it.
- Where these techniques earn their place is **time**, not stimulus. A single drop set matched three normal sets of biceps curls for growth at ~2 minutes versus ~7.

## The core study
Enes et al. recruited **28 trained men** (average back squat 1RM 139 kg), assigned to normal, rest-pause or drop-set groups, training twice weekly for **8 weeks** on back squat, leg press and leg extension.

- **Normal:** 4 sets × 12 reps at 70% 1RM, 2 min rest.
- **Rest-pause:** 10 reps at 75% 1RM, 20 s rest, 6 more reps — 3 such sets, 2 min between.
- **Drop:** 10 reps at 75% 1RM, immediate drop to 55% 1RM, 6 more reps — 3 such sets, 2 min between.

Total reps were equated, which is why the normal group did an extra set. Loads rose 5% in week five. Assistance was given if prescribed reps were missed.

**His criticism is the sharpest thing in the video.** Performing six more reps at 75% 1RM only 20 seconds after ten reps at that same load is extremely hard; he suspects the rest-pause group needed assistance frequently, meaning the study may have quietly included **forced repetitions**. The researchers did not report how often assistance was given. He is even-handed about it — he expects the other groups needed help on late sets too.

**Results.** All three groups gained back squat strength, tending to favour rest-pause. Hypertrophy (lateral thigh thickness at proximal, middle and distal sites) was similar across groups at the proximal and middle regions; only rest-pause appeared to gain at the distal region.

He then takes apart his own headline result. The **individual data** show a few subjects in the drop-set and normal groups *lost* size distally, which he argues dragged those group averages down and manufactured the apparent rest-pause advantage. His alternative explanation is that different people simply do not show identical regional hypertrophy from the same training — evidenced by the within-group spread — so it is likely chance that the two comparison groups happened to contain distal non-responders. He flags this as an underrated point: the common assumption that everyone grows the same regions from the same exercise is not supported by these data.

On how subjects *lose* size: he says he knows of no validated explanation, notes it is not unique to this study (a partial back squat study also observed decreases in lower thigh regions), floats a compensatory-mechanism idea and labels it "very much speculative."

## Strength: nothing structural, just heavy reps
Rest-pause won on strength because that group accumulated more repetitions at heavier loads. He supports this with a study showing 2–4 reps at 90% 1RM produced greater 1RM gains on bench and squat than 8–12 reps at 70%. Since heavy-rep accumulation is achievable with normal sets, cluster sets and rest-pause alike, no structure is inherently superior — but **rest-pause is only practical with moderate to lighter loads**, so normal sets are the better vehicle for genuinely heavy work. Drop sets are unsuited by definition.

## Hypertrophy: the mechanistic argument against "effective reps"
He anticipates the objection that the study's protocols were not how people actually use these techniques — real drop sets often use multiple drops within one set, real rest-pause often uses more mini-sets. He addresses it with data rather than assertion: a **meta-analysis of the drop-set literature, including studies with multiple drops per set, still found parity with normal sets**.

For rest-pause there is only one other study: 18 trained individuals, four days a week for six weeks, normal (3×6 at 80% 1RM) vs rest-pause (reps to failure at 80% 1RM, 20 s rest, repeat until 18 total reps). Hypertrophy tended to favour rest-pause. **He then disqualifies it**, because the normal group appears not to have applied progressive overload — so by the later weeks they would have been training far from failure, whereas the rest-pause group's failure-based protocol progressed automatically. His conclusion: this does not qualify as evidence that rest-pause is superior.

The mechanistic case is the more interesting half. Mechanical tension is the primary hypertrophy stimulus, and maximising it means high motor-unit recruitment with high forces from those units. The common claim is that going beyond failure is what achieves full recruitment. **He argues this is wrong**, citing what he calls the strongest motor-unit modelling study to date (Potvin & Fuglevand): full recruitment and high motor-unit forces are achieved **before** failure in a normal set, with higher-threshold units already fatiguing slightly before that point. He uses this to explain why training to failure generally matches stopping shy of it.

Against the fallback position — that going beyond failure accumulates *more* effective reps — he raises two objections:
1. Those extra reps occur under increasing fatigue, which itself impairs recruitment and force output, likely balancing out the gain. He notes that in very long normal sets, central fatigue may cause higher-threshold units to **drop out of recruitment entirely** before failure.
2. There may be a **threshold** number of effective reps beyond which more adds nothing — and all these set structures may already clear it. This would explain the null results despite drop sets and rest-pause technically containing more effective reps.

He also disposes of metabolic stress and muscle damage as explanations: drop sets and rest-pause generate more of both, but he holds there is no compelling evidence either is a powerful hypertrophy driver.

## Cluster sets
Clusters differ from rest-pause in that reps are generally **not** taken to or near failure, and very low rep counts per mini-set are common (e.g. a 6RM load performed as three mini-sets of two with 30 s between).

- **Strength:** a 2021 meta-analysis found clusters and normal sets comparable — logical, since clusters allow rep accumulation at heavier loads.
- **Hypertrophy:** the same meta-analysis found parity, but he lists the limitations precisely — only **five studies**, two of which had the normal-set group using very low rep counts, one of which did not have the normal group train to failure, and two of which lasted only five weeks.
- A **post-meta study** (22 trained individuals, 8 weeks, bench press) compared normal (4×5 at 85% 1RM, 5 min rest) with cluster (4×5 at 85% 1RM, 30 s between each rep, 3 min between sets). Strength gains were similar; **lower pec growth was similar but middle and upper pec growth was superior for the normal group.**

His verdict is appropriately conditional: cluster designs that minimise proximity to failure probably compromise hypertrophy; designs that get some reps close to failure are probably fine.

## Where this lands against the library
- **Drop sets:** he treats them as *equal* to normal sets, not weakly supported — the meta-analysis is the reason. His positive case for them is time efficiency, not superiority. That is compatible with "weakly supported as a superior technique" but not with "weakly supported as a technique."
- **Rest-pause:** two studies favour it, both of which he neutralises on protocol grounds (forced reps in one, no progressive overload in the comparison group in the other). Net verdict: not superior, viable, time-efficient.
- **Cluster sets:** the only structure he suggests may be *inferior*, and only in low-effort implementations.
- **Long-length partials do not appear in this video at all.** He does not compare any of these structures against them, so this note offers no support for or against the library's ranking of partials as best-supported.
- **Accentuated eccentrics** are also absent.

## Time efficiency: the real use case
Since these structures do not out-grow normal sets, their value is compression. A single drop set with multiple load reductions, or a single rest-pause set with multiple mini-sets, replaces several normal sets. Because they are fatiguing, he recommends confining them to **isolation exercises**. The demonstration: a Japanese study (in the drop-set meta-analysis) compared biceps curls performed as one drop set against three normal sets to failure — **similar biceps gains, ~2 minutes versus ~7**.

## Flagging the overreach
Very little here. The one place mechanism carries more weight than it should is the motor-unit argument: a **modelling** study is used to rule out a physiological pathway by which going beyond failure could help. He is upfront that it is a model, and the outcome data happen to agree with it, so the conclusion is not at risk — but the confidence in "mechanistically I don't think there's any valid way through which rest-pause or drop sets would be superior" runs slightly ahead of a simulation.
