# Static Holds and Heavy Negatives: Testing Mentzer's Claim That Lifting Is a Waste of Time

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The FASTEST Way to Build Muscle (Says Mike Mentzer)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sCW_AMJJN3k)) · 2023-11-06 · 29 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- Mentzer's protocol was a **static hold at the fully contracted position held to failure, then a very slow lowering with a load heavier than you could lift**. Arthur Jones made the same claim more bluntly: take the negative out and "you have little or nothing of benefit left."
- **The contracted-position hold is the weakest part of the claim.** Isometrics do build muscle, but the same research finds holds at *long* muscle lengths build more, and lengthened partials beat contracted partials in the ROM literature. Mentzer's premise — that 100% of the muscle can only be activated when fully shortened — is wrong on three separate mechanisms.
- **The lowering phase does contribute a lot**, and the single study isolating it found eccentric-only and normal training out-grew concentric-only. But that study probably had subjects training far from failure, which specifically penalises the lifting phase. Where concentric-only work is taken close to failure, it grows muscle well.
- **Overloaded eccentric-only training and accentuated-eccentric training are effective but not superior.** One trial comparing overloaded eccentric-only to normal training found similar growth; of 11 accentuated-eccentric studies, the majority found no advantage over normal training.
- **Mentzer's fatigue argument is backwards.** Eccentric-overload work causes substantially more damage, soreness and strength loss than concentric work — not less. That fatigue does fall away over ~10 weeks via the repeated bout effect.
- **On tempo**, 0.5–8 s per rep produces similar growth when sets are taken near failure. Two studies favour a 4 s lowering over 1 s on the preacher curl; three others find no consistent advantage. Use what you prefer.

## Decision 1: Should you swap normal sets for a contracted-position hold?

No — and the reasoning Mentzer gave for it does not survive contact with the physiology.

Mentzer's stated logic was that the fully contracted position "is the only position of the exercise where 100% of the muscular bulk can be activated." Dhim gives three reasons this is false:

1. **Sarcomere overlap.** At extreme shortening, myosin heads can be crowded to the point where many cannot reach and bind actin. Fewer cross-bridges, less active force — the opposite of maximal.
2. **Voluntary activation is not 100%, and it is often *higher* at long lengths.** He cites a study finding subjects could access only 84.5% of quadriceps force-producing potential, against ~95% for elbow flexors and calves; a couple of studies indicate voluntary activation is easier to achieve at longer muscle lengths.
3. **Passive force.** Titin and other elastic structures generate force when stretched. That contribution exists at long lengths and is absent when the muscle is fully shortened.

The training data agree. Static holds near the contracted position do stimulate hypertrophy, but the same research finds holds at longer lengths generate more. A 2014 leg extension study and a 2021 Japanese preacher curl study both found lengthened partials beat contracted partials, and he notes three studies where a lengthened partial beat a *full* range of motion outright (with the caveat that this is emerging and needs replication).

The important separation here: the *feeling* of a hard squeeze at peak contraction is not a readout of fibre force. He makes that point explicitly, which is the right correction to a very common training heuristic.

## Decision 2: Is the lowering phase where the growth actually comes from?

Partly — but the headline study that appears to prove it has a specific flaw.

The study in question is a Japanese trial in **53 previously untrained people**, randomised to concentric-only, eccentric-only, normal, or control. All trained groups did the preacher curl with a lengthened partial ROM, **3 sets of 10, twice a week for 5 weeks**, with the same dumbbell load progressed from 30% to 100% of maximal isometric strength; an investigator returned the weight for the two single-phase groups. Elbow flexor growth was greatest in the normal and eccentric-only groups, with minimal gain in the concentric-only group. Read at face value, that is Arthur Jones vindicated.

Dhim's objection is proximity to failure. Because all groups used the same absolute load, and eccentric strength is roughly 40% higher than concentric strength, the load that was demanding for the eccentric group was likely well short of failure for the concentric group across a good chunk of the study. Since the concentric phase is where fibre recruitment and force need effort to climb, training far from failure penalises that group specifically. He points to a set of studies where concentric-only training was taken harder — typically on machines that unloaded the lowering — and hypertrophy was much better. His speculation: if the Japanese subjects had trained close enough to failure, concentric-only would likely have out-grown the result shown, and the normal group would probably have gone further still.

This is the video's best moment of self-correction and it is worth flagging as a quality marker: he presents the study that most supports the video's premise, then dismantles it on a design detail rather than on vibes.

## Decision 3: If you *do* overload the lowering, is it better than normal training?

Effective, but not better.

Two categories to keep apart:

- **Eccentric-only overloaded** — you lower a load you could not lift. This is what Mentzer's method approximates: he had clients use a load heavier than their 6–10RM, held it to fatigue at the contracted position, then lowered slowly. The static hold pre-fatigues the muscle so that lifting the weight is essentially impossible, which puts the following descent in roughly the same physiological state as true eccentric overload.
- **Accentuated eccentric** — the lifting phase is loaded normally and the load is *increased* for the lowering. Flywheel machines, weight releasers, and the 2-1 method (lift with two limbs, lower with one) all sit here.

In the studies that unloaded the lifting phase, eccentric-only overloaded work produced *slightly* more hypertrophy than concentric-only. But that is not the comparison lifters care about. Normal training is lifting *plus* an underloaded lowering — and the Japanese study shows an underloaded lowering still builds respectable muscle. The one paper he is aware of comparing normal training to eccentric-only overloaded training, in untrained older individuals, found **similar growth**. On accentuated eccentrics, **11 studies** exist and the majority find similar hypertrophy to normal training.

His interpretation is a **stimulus ceiling**: past some point the interaction between stimulus and fatigue caps what a set can produce, so adding more eccentric force does not add more growth. That is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism, and he labels it as speculation.

Practically: the 2-1 method, eccentric-only chin-ups and Nordic curls, cheat-and-lower, and Mentzer's own protocol are all legitimate ways to train. None of them are a shortcut past normal training.

## Decision 4: Does eccentric overload build a *different kind* of muscle?

Possibly, and this is the most interesting unresolved thread.

Some research indicates concentric-only work preferentially grows the **middle regions** of a muscle, while overloaded eccentric work better grows the **distal regions**. At the cellular level, there is some evidence that concentric work predominantly adds **sarcomeres in parallel** (side by side, increasing cross-section) while overloaded eccentric work predominantly adds **sarcomeres in series** (end to end, increasing fascicle length).

His counterweight: normal training already includes an underloaded lowering, and normal training has been documented to produce impressive distal-region growth and probably serial sarcomere addition — particularly when a full ROM reaches a lengthened position, or when using lengthened partials. Any residual advantage for eccentric overload he judges too small to call practically meaningful. Verdict: on normal training you are probably not leaving much on the table.

## Decision 5: Is the lifting phase the one that wrecks recovery?

This is where Mentzer is flatly wrong, and Dhim says so directly. Mentzer's justification for ditching the concentric was that it "makes the deepest inroad in the recovery ability." The data run the other way: eccentric-overload training causes considerably **more** damage, soreness and post-session strength loss than concentric-only. And when accentuated eccentric training (both phases loaded) is compared to eccentric-only overload, fatigue is no greater — meaning the fatigue is coming almost entirely from the lowering.

The mitigating fact is the **repeated bout effect**. In a study he has covered previously, a session of 5 sets of 15 maximal eccentric-overload reps produced substantial fatigue, damage and soreness lasting at least 5 days; after 10 weeks of the same training, there was very little to none. So the fatigue cost is a start-up cost, not a permanent tax.

## Decision 6: How fast should you lower on normal reps?

Tempo is close to a non-issue provided the set is taken near failure.

- A **2015 meta-analysis** pooling the studies to that point found rep durations of **0.5 to 8 seconds** (lifting and lowering combined) produced similar hypertrophy when reps were taken to or near failure.
- A within-subject leg extension study trained one leg at 1 s up / 1 s down and the other at 3 s up / 3 s down, both to failure. Time under tension differed substantially; growth did not.
- On the specific idea of fast up, slow down: **two studies** found a 1 s lift with a 4 s lowering on the preacher curl tended to beat a 1 s lowering for biceps growth. **Three others conflict** — a Japanese back squat study (2 s lift, 2 vs 4 s lowering) found similar thigh growth; a Brazilian leg extension study (1 s lift, 2 vs 4 s lowering) found similar rectus femoris and vastus lateralis growth but greater vastus medialis growth with 4 s; a US leg extension study (1 s lift, 1 vs 2 s lowering) found similar growth at one anterior thigh region and *greater* growth with the faster 1 s lowering at another.

No unanimous winner. Use the tempo you prefer.

## Where the argument outruns the evidence

The stimulus-ceiling explanation for why accentuated eccentrics fail to beat normal training is inference from a pattern of null results, not a tested mechanism — he flags it as speculation, but it is doing a lot of work in the video's overall verdict. The regional-hypertrophy and sarcomeres-in-series section similarly rests on a handful of studies and is presented as "some research indicating," which is the right calibration but a thin base for a claim about *different kinds* of growth. And the whole argument that the Japanese lifting-vs-lowering study was confounded by proximity to failure is his own back-calculation from the reported loads, not something the authors measured.
