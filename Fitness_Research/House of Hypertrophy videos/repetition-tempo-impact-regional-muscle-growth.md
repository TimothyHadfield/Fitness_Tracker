# Does Rep Tempo Change Where a Muscle Grows?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Can Repetition Tempo Impact Regional Muscle Growth?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ePdrCerRRsc)) · 2021-02-08 · 9 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- Three studies hint that different rep tempos shift growth to different **regions** of the quadriceps, even when total growth is similar.
- The evidence is thin: only one statistically significant regional difference across the whole set, and only two of the three studies used conventional hypertrophy-style training.
- For **whole-muscle** growth, tempo is close to irrelevant. A meta-analysis found rep durations from **0.5 to 8 seconds** produce similar hypertrophy provided sets go to or near failure.
- Practical read: varying tempo won't cost you anything, and might make growth slightly more uniform across a muscle. That is the whole claim.

## The power-vs-strength comparison
Earp and colleagues compared heavy parallel back squats (75–90% 1RM, taken close to failure) against jump squats at 30% 1RM performed explosively and far from failure. Range of motion was matched — both squatted to parallel.

Heavy squatting grew the **upper** region of the vastus lateralis and vastus intermedius more; jump squatting grew the **lower** region of both more. Since depth was controlled, movement speed is the plausible explanation.

The obvious problem, which he does not flag: this compares two entire training styles, not two tempos. Load, proximity to failure and intent all differ alongside speed.

## Tempo inside a normal hypertrophy protocol
Diniz and colleagues split 44 untrained women across three training groups (plus a non-training control): 5s concentric/1s eccentric, 1s/5s, and 3s/3s. All did knee extensions, 6 reps per set at 50% 1RM, 180s rest, 3×/week for 10 weeks, ramping from 3 to 5 sets per session. Cross-sectional area was measured at 30%, 50% and 70% of thigh length across all four quadriceps heads.

Only **one** comparison reached significance: at 50% of thigh length in the rectus femoris, both the 5/1 and 1/5 groups beat the 3/3 group. Several other differences favoured one group or another numerically, and he is explicit that these may be sampling error.

He also raises the confound himself, which is the strongest part of the video: because all groups used the same load and rep count, the group with the 5-second concentric was necessarily training closest to failure and the 1s-concentric group furthest. Fatigue, not tempo, could explain the results. His counter-argument is neat — the 5/1 and 1/5 groups were at *different* fatigue levels yet grew the same at the one significant site, and both beat 3/3. That pattern doesn't fit a fatigue explanation.

## Trained subjects, eccentric speed only
Pearson and colleagues used 13 well-trained men (3+ years lifting, average squat ~2× bodyweight) in a within-subject design: one leg did unilateral leg extensions with a 1s eccentric, the other with a 3s eccentric, both 1s concentric, 2×/week for 8 weeks, 3 sets at 8–10RM rising to 4 sets. Anterior thigh thickness at 40% of thigh length was similar; at 60% it was **statistically greater for the fast eccentric**.

## Where the conclusion outruns the evidence
Three studies, one significant regional difference each at best, two of them in untrained people, and the flagship Earp comparison confounds tempo with load and effort. "Tempo may influence regional growth" is fair; anything stronger is not, and he stops there.
