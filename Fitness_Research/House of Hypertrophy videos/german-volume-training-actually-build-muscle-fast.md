# German Volume Training: Two Trials, and Five Sets Beat Ten

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Does German Volume Training Actually Build Muscle Fast?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kl-u0WmGTD8)) · 2021-10-11 · 12 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- German volume training (GVT) is **10 sets of 10 at ~60% 1RM on no more than two compound exercises per session, 60–90 s rest**. Both trials that have tested it compared 10 sets to 5 sets — and **5 sets won or tied on every measure**.
- **Amirthalingam 2017:** 19 men, ~4 years training, 3×/week for 6 weeks. Bench press and lat pulldown 1RM increased **significantly more in the 5-set group**; leg press 1RM was similar. DEXA arm and trunk lean mass favoured **5 sets**; leg mass was similar. **Neither group** showed significant ultrasound thickness gains in biceps, triceps, anterior or posterior thigh — which he notes is odd given DEXA registered gains, and ultrasound is generally the more reliable of the two for muscle growth.
- **Hackett 2018:** 12 men, ≥1 year training, 3×/week for **12 weeks**, otherwise the same design. Bench 1RM increased significantly **only in the 5-set group**; leg press 1RM in neither. Neither group gained significant lean mass anywhere, and the **10-set group significantly lost lean leg mass between weeks 6 and 12** — he speculates about a failure to recover, with breakdown exceeding synthesis.
- His stronger criticism is structural: **both 5 and 10 sets of the GVT prescription are probably suboptimal**, because the load and the rest interval are both wrong.
- He closes by explicitly not telling anyone to stop: if you enjoy GVT or have had good results with it, the research does not invalidate your experience.

## Why the trials are weak evidence, in his own words
He lists the limitations before drawing conclusions:

**Amirthalingam.** The 5-set group started **weaker** on bench, lat pulldown and leg press, so regression to the mean or simply more room to improve could explain their larger 1RM gains. Only 19 subjects. Only 6 weeks. Diet was at least controlled for: all subjects took 30 g whey post-session and were encouraged to eat 1,000–2,000 extra kcal/day, and both groups landed near **+1,020 kcal/day**.

**Hackett.** Calories were **not tracked at all**, so diet could confound. Hypertrophy was measured by **DEXA only** — the weaker tool. Only **12 subjects**, which he calls an extremely small sample and the likely reason so many comparisons came back non-significant. And no deload was taken before the final measurements, so residual fatigue from a very high-volume protocol could have depressed the 10-set group's strength and lean mass numbers.

That last point cuts against his own conclusion and he raises it anyway.

## The structural problem with the prescription
Two variables in the classic GVT template look wrong independent of the trial results:

**Proximity to failure.** At 60% 1RM most people can perform roughly **20 reps**. A first set of 10 is therefore about **10 reps from failure** — far outside the ≤5 RIR he treats as the useful range for hypertrophy. Fatigue drags later sets closer to failure (some people cannot complete 10 on the final sets), but the early sets remain largely wasted work. His conclusion is that the same time would be better spent on fewer sets taken closer to failure.

**Rest intervals.** GVT uses 60–90 s on compound exercises, where he argues **2.5+ minutes** is better. His example is [Schoenfeld 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/): 16 men with ≥6 months experience, mostly compound exercises, 3 sets to failure at 8–12RM, 3×/week for 8 weeks. Growth in elbow flexor, triceps, anterior quadriceps and vastus lateralis thickness all **favoured 3 minutes' rest over 1 minute**, as did bench press and squat 1RM.

Put together, his verdict is that GVT is **inefficient rather than useless** — it prescribes a lot of sets whose loading and rest make each one less productive than it could be.

**Flagged:** the conclusion rests on **two trials totalling 31 subjects**, neither of which found significant hypertrophy in *either* group by its more reliable measure. That is a floor problem, not a GVT problem: these studies mostly failed to detect growth at all, so their ability to rank 5 versus 10 sets is limited. He acknowledges the small samples but still treats "5 beat 10" as the finding, when "neither protocol produced measurable growth here" is closer to what the data show. The rest-interval and RIR arguments are the load-bearing part of the case, and they come from outside the GVT literature.
