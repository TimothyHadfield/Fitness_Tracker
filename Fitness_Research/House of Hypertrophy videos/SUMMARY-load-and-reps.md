# Load and rep ranges

Fifteen notes on how heavy to lift, spanning 2020 to 2025, and the one domain where the channel's
headline number changed twice on camera before settling. The spine of it is a distinction he never
lets slip: **load decides strength, effort decides size**, and the two questions get different
answers at both ends of the range. Four of these fifteen notes come from videos with **no
retrievable reference list** — they cite on screen only — and that is flagged in place, because one
of the four carries the single most useful argument here.

---

## What rep range actually grows muscle

**The answer he settled on is 6–35 reps per set, taken to or near failure.** Within that band, load
does not decide how much muscle you build. It took three moves to get there and the dates matter.

**May 2020.** [The hypertrophy rep range is roughly 5 to 35, not 6 to 12](best-rep-range-build-muscle.md)
reads Schoenfeld's 2017 meta-analysis, whose included trials span **30–85% of 1RM**, and converts
that to **5–35 reps**. The strength–hypertrophy–endurance continuum, he argues, holds at its ends
and fails in the middle: there is no evidence growth is best achieved exclusively at 6–12. The
mechanism offered is Henneman's size principle — heavy loads recruit high-threshold motor units from
rep one, light loads recruit them progressively as fatigue accumulates, so both routes end at high
recruitment near failure. The note flags that recruitment at failure is an **acute surrogate**: the
hypertrophy claim rests on the meta-analysis, and the motor-unit material is explanation, not
evidence.

**April 2022 — the floor goes up.** [Are sets of 5 reps enough to maximise hypertrophy?](5-reps-maximize-hypertrophy.md)
is him walking back his own number in public. Vann et al. took **15 trained men** (average back squat
**167 kg**, 1.9x body weight), split each man's legs, and trained unilateral leg extension and leg
press **3x/week for 6 weeks**. One leg did **3 sets of 5 at 82.5% 1RM rising to 95% by week 6**; the
other did **sets of 10 at a fixed 60% 1RM**, progressing set count rather than load. Vastus lateralis
cross-sectional area rose in the light leg; **the heavy leg averaged no gain at all** — while doing
*more* weekly sets (**18 vs 15**) and training *closer* to failure (roughly **0–3 RIR** against
roughly **10 RIR**). Neither volume nor effort explains it. New advice: **8–35 reps**.

**September 2022 — it comes back down and stops.** [4 vs 8 vs 12 reps](4-vs-8-vs-12-reps-strength.md)
states the position he has held ever since: **6–35 reps are similarly effective per set; 3–5 reps
work but are less efficient per set and need extra sets to compensate; 1–2 reps may not build muscle
at all.**

**December 2024 — the consolidation.** [The whole rep-range literature in one place](many-reps-muscle-growth-40-studies.md)
is the best single reference in this domain: 48 citations, **five meta-analyses** covering lean mass,
whole-muscle size and fibre cross-sectional area, all converging. He defends **6–35** against the
**5–30** other people quote, on the grounds that 6–35 better describes the reps actually performed in
the included trials. Two boundary conditions are stated explicitly and both matter: the included sets
went **to or very near failure**, and they used a **normal 1–2 second concentric and 1–2 second
eccentric** — the window does not transfer to deliberately slow reps, and he cites a trial where
6–10 reps with a 10-second lift and 4-second lower was *inferior*. The obvious objection, that most
of this is untrained subjects doing two or three exercises, he answers with **two trained-subject
trials running full programmes** at 8–12 versus 20–35 reps to failure, three sets per exercise, with
similar growth.

**August 2025 — no movement.** [Can sets of 3–5 reps maximise growth?](3-5-reps-truly-maximize-growth.md)
covers a bioRxiv preprint: **n=14, 2+ years trained**, within-subject, unilateral leg press and leg
extension, **3–5 vs 20–25 reps to failure**, 3 sets, 2 min rest, twice weekly for 9 weeks with a
mid-study deload. Vastus lateralis thickness rose **7.4%** and **8.5%** at two regions with **no
significant difference between conditions**. He does not lower the minimum. He says he *hopes* it
comes down, and tells anyone already progressing below 6 reps to change nothing.

## Do low reps build muscle? The confounding argument

This is the most useful thing in the domain, and it is not the argument people assume. **He does not
claim low reps fail. He claims the studies saying they work were rigged in their favour and still
only managed a tie.**

[Can sets of 2–4 reps build muscle?](reps-below-5-maximize-muscle-growth.md) tallies six studies
putting 2–5 reps against moderate or high reps. **Five found parity; one found low reps worse.** Four
of the five supporting studies handed the low-rep group **more sets, longer rest, or both**:

- **Mangine** — 33 men with 2+ years training, 8 weeks. Intensity: 4 sets of 3–5 at 90% 1RM,
  **3 min rest**. Volume: 4 sets of 10–12 at 70%, **1 min rest**. Sets matched, rest not — and his own
  rest-interval work says 2.5+ minutes beats one minute on compounds, so the moderate group was
  handicapped.
- **Schoenfeld 2014** — 17 trained men, 8 weeks. **7 sets of 3** at 3 min rest against **3 sets of
  10** at 90 s. Biceps thickness similar. Both confounds at once.
- **Campos** — 27 untrained men, 8 weeks. Low: **4 sets** of 3–5RM, **3 min**. Intermediate: **3
  sets** of 9–11RM, **2 min**. High: **2 sets** of 20–28RM, **1 min**. Low and intermediate matched
  on fibre CSA, both beating high.
- **Chestnut & Docherty** — 19 untrained men, 10 weeks. 4RM group: **6 sets** per core exercise,
  **3 min**. 10RM group: **3 sets**, **2 min**. Twice the sets and 50% more rest.

Almost always the reason was that researchers **equated volume load** (sets x reps x load), which
forces the heavy group into extra sets. He dismantles that justification head-on: reps of 5–35 grow
similarly at matched set counts despite wildly different volume loads, so **volume load is not what
tracks hypertrophy** — while more sets generally *do* produce more growth. Equating volume load did
not remove a confound; it introduced one.

Strip the four out and **two clean comparisons remain, and they disagree.** **Weiss 2000** — 44
untrained men, **4 sets of back squat, 6 minutes of rest**, 3x/week for 7 weeks — found 3–5 reps as
good as 13–15 and 23–25. **Schoenfeld 2016** — 19 trained men, **3 sets, 2 min rest**, 8 weeks, seven
exercises — found 2–4 reps grew *less* vastus lateralis and elbow flexor than 8–12, with triceps
similar. He notes the two may be separated by something as small as **two reps versus three**, or by
trained versus untrained subjects.

The inference, put plainly in [Can sets of 3–5 reps maximise growth?](3-5-reps-truly-maximize-growth.md),
is the part to get right: **the confounds run toward the low-rep group, so those studies cut against
the low-rep case rather than for it.** Handed extra sets and longer rest, 5-or-fewer reps should have
*beaten* higher reps if it were equally potent per set. It only tied. That is why the floor sits at
6 and not at 3.

[Kubo 2021](4-vs-8-vs-12-reps-strength.md) is the cleanest illustration of the compensation
mechanism. **42 physically active but untrained men**, bench press **2x/week for 10 weeks**,
**3 minutes rest for everyone**, volume-load-equated so set counts differed: **4 group, 7 sets of 4
at 90% 1RM**; **8 group, 4 sets of 8 at 80%**; **12 group, 3 sets of 12 at 70%**. Pectoralis major
**volume** — a 3D whole-muscle measure, better than the single-site thickness most studies use — rose
**similarly in all three**. His reading is not "volume load is what matters" but "**low reps are less
efficient per set, and the extra sets paid for it**". The 4-rep group also spent **nearly double** the
per-session bench time.

Three practical consequences: **~8 reps is the efficient middle ground** for concurrent strength and
size; a handful of sub-6-rep sets in a programme is not a problem; and there is a minor safety signal
— in Schoenfeld's 7x3 protocol, **2 of 10 subjects sustained joint-related injuries**.

## Below the floor: what maxing out does and does not do

[Maxing out builds strength without building muscle](maxing-out-effective-building-muscle-one-rep.md)
is the sharpest separation of the two outcomes on the channel, and it is six minutes long.

**Dankel** — **five men** with a year-plus of training worked **both arms to a 1RM on the unilateral
dumbbell curl daily for 21 consecutive days**, back and shoulders against a wall to kill momentum;
the volume arm additionally did **3x10 at 70% 1RM**. **Strength rose equally in both arms.** Elbow
flexor thickness favoured the volume arm at all three measurement sites, and the 1RM-only arm grew
**virtually not at all**.

**Mattocks** — **38 untrained subjects** (17 men, 21 women), unilateral knee extension and chest
press **2x/week for 8 weeks**. The hypertrophy group did **4 sets to failure at an 8–12RM load**, 90 s
rest; the testing group simply warmed up and worked to a **1RM** each session. **1RM gains were
statistically similar between groups.** Thigh thickness favoured the hypertrophy group at every site
— and he is careful that several of those differences did **not** reach significance, then makes the
point that matters more than the p-values: the testing group's raw before-and-after values show **no
increases and in several places slight decreases**, while the hypertrophy group rose on virtually
every measure.

Two designs, one direction. Practising the test makes you better at the test; **1RM training's
strength gains are neural adaptation and motor skill, not tissue**. Note the scope: this is a claim
about *singles*, not about heavy training generally, and he says so explicitly, deferring 2–4 rep
work to the video above. The evidence is thin on its own terms — **n=5**, plus untrained subjects on
two machines — and the "5–35 reps" figure quoted inside that note is his summary of the wider
literature, not a finding from either study.

## How light is too light? Where the load floor sits

[How light is too light?](light-too-light-maximizing-muscle-growth.md) is the load-floor note, and it
**has no retrievable reference list** — the description is a prose summary that names studies without
linking them, so every citation in it is unverified.

**The band he defends is 30–85% of 1RM**, from two meta-analyses, conditional on sets reaching or
nearly reaching failure. The anchor is **Mitchell**: three sets to failure on the leg extension at
**30% or 80% 1RM**, 3x/week for 10 weeks, similar quadriceps volume gains.

One caveat from that note is worth carrying everywhere in this domain: **the same %1RM buys very
different rep counts on different exercises.** 30% 1RM on the leg extension gave roughly **30–40
reps** to failure; 30% on the dumbbell curl gave roughly **60–80**. The 3–5 rep note makes the same
point from the other end, citing a meta-analysis finding about **13 reps at 80% 1RM on leg press
versus about 9 on bench press**. Percentage of 1RM is a much looser proxy for effort than it looks,
and reps are the better currency.

**Below 30% the evidence splits two against two and he refuses to pick a winner.**

- **Jessee** — untrained men and women, knee extension, four sets to volitional failure at **15% or
  70% 1RM**, 2x/week for 8 weeks, sets capped at 90 reps (only 2 of 40 subjects hit the cap on all
  four sets). Anterior and lateral thigh thickness rose **similarly**. *15% may be enough.*
- **Buckner** — near-identical design on the **dumbbell curl**, same loads and duration. Elbow flexor
  thickness rose **more at 70%**. *15% is too light.*
- **Lasevicius** — untrained men, dumbbell curl and leg press, 3–5 sets to failure, 2x/week for **12
  weeks**, at **20/40/60/80% 1RM**, 120 s rest. Elbow flexor and vastus lateralis CSA gains were
  similar at 40, 60 and 80%, and **all greater than 20%** — in both upper and lower body.
- **Van Roie** — older untrained adults, 12 weeks: **one set to failure at 20% 1RM** against **two
  sets at 80%**. Upper leg volume rose similarly; the extra heavy set bought nothing.

Two confounds sit on top: Jessee and Buckner both gave the light condition **30 seconds** of rest
against **90 seconds** for the heavy one, and Van Roie's subjects were old. He floats and discards the
tidy reconciliation — that 15% works for the lower body but not the upper — because Lasevicius found
the 20% deficit in **both**.

The theoretical reason a load *could* be too light is **central fatigue**: a fall in the nervous
system's drive to the muscle, which is *greater* after long light sets than short heavy ones. Yoon's
evidence is **nine men and nine women** holding a biceps contraction at **20% or 80% MVC** until force
fell 10% below target; the 20% hold lasted about **14 minutes**, the 80% about **25 seconds**, and the
20% condition produced more central fatigue. It is a single-joint isometric study in 18 subjects,
never linked to any of the four growth trials — it explains why a floor *should* exist, not why
Buckner and Lasevicius found one where Jessee and Van Roie did not.

**Practical position: keep most hypertrophy work between 30% and 85% of 1RM.** He adds, honestly, that
the video has almost no practical payoff, because nobody trains at 30% anyway — it is miserable.

**The ceiling is softer than the floor.** [The 40-studies video](many-reps-muscle-growth-40-studies.md)
puts the best comparison at 20/40/60/80% 1RM to failure across 3–5 sets: **40–80% similar, 20%
(averaging over 60 reps) grew muscle but less**. Against that, a curl trial whose light condition
averaged **100 reps on the first set** still matched 80% 1RM — partly because loads were not adjusted
until mid-study, so subjects progressed by adding reps. Two 15%-vs-70% studies split, with the
confounds named above. **35 is a safety margin, not a measured cliff**, and he says so.

One asymmetry that surprises people: **higher reps cost more fatigue, not less.** Recovery was slower
after 40% than 80% 1RM in one leg-extension study, with strength dropping in the *untrained* opposite
leg — a central-fatigue signature. He concedes **6–12 is a defensible zone on fatigue-management
grounds**, while refusing to let that become an efficacy claim, and points to three studies where
high reps held up at **18+ weekly sets per muscle**.

A related practical point sits in the same note and is easy to miss: lifters **underestimate how close
to failure they are with light loads**, probably because high reps hurt more and pain masks true
proximity. So with high-rep work, take at least some sets to genuine failure to recalibrate.

## Does load matter if effort is matched?

Inside 30–85% 1RM, no — and the corollary is that **how you progress does not matter much either**.
[Adding load vs adding reps](adding-more-load-repetitions-better-hypertrophy-strength.md) is eighteen
minutes spent demolishing the only dataset that appears to say otherwise.

Nobrega and colleagues did not run a trial; they **pooled two of their own earlier leg-extension
studies**. The rep-overload group (**n=14**, untrained, 3 sets to failure, 2 min rest, **2x/week for
12 weeks**) held load at 80% 1RM and progressed by adding reps. The load-overload group (**n=10**,
untrained, **3x/week for 8 weeks**) adjusted load every set to stay at **9–12 reps to failure**.
Strength was statistically similar (**+31.4%** vs **+38.3%**); **vastus lateralis CSA gains were
significantly greater for the load group**; and while total volume load did not predict gains,
**volume load progression did**.

The teardown is a model. It is **not randomised** — two studies merged after the fact. **Frequency and
duration were not matched** (6 weekly sets over 12 weeks against 9 over 8), and because gains
decelerate rather than accrue linearly, those two errors do **not** cleanly cancel; his conclusion is
that the effect is impossible to disentangle, not that it is harmless. **Samples of 10 and 14 make a
responder lottery plausible** — Hubal trained **585 people** and found individuals landing far above
and far below the typical 10–30% biceps CSA band. **The measurement was one site (~50% femur length)
on one head of one muscle**, with rectus femoris — which leg extensions grow heavily — not measured
at all.

Then the counter-evidence, the strongest part: the authors' mechanism, that volume load progression
drives growth, fails in at least three other datasets. **Schoenfeld's low-vs-high-load trial** in
trained men had *greater* volume load progression in the 25–35 rep group with comparable growth.
**Brigatto** had greater volume load progression in the twice-weekly group with similar thickness
gains. **Souza Junior's two studies** progressed by shortening rest versus adding load, with similar
arm and thigh CSA changes. He closes with a reductio: if shortening rest is as good as adding load,
and adding load beats adding reps, then **shortening rest would beat adding reps** — which nobody
accepts, so a premise is wrong.

**Practical read: either method works.** Pick whichever lets you keep training hard and keep records;
do not restructure a programme around this study. One caveat he flags against himself: his central
rebuttal — that there is no mechanistic reason load progression should win — is **a mechanistic
argument used to discount an outcome measurement**, and absence of a known mechanism is not evidence
the difference is false.

## Does fibre type change the answer? No, twice over

Two notes attack this and they share a core study.

[No, you do not need to match rep ranges to fibre type](optimal-rep-range-each-muscle-true.md) and
[Training by fibre type](should-train-muscles-according-fiber-type.md) both centre on **Schoenfeld
2020**: **26 untrained young men**, within-subject, standing and seated calf raise, one leg at
**4x6-10** and the other at **4x20-30**, both to momentary failure, 2x/week, 90 s rest, exercise
order counterbalanced. **Soleus, medial gastrocnemius and lateral gastrocnemius all grew similarly.**
The soleus - roughly 90% type I, the muscle where the specificity hypothesis makes its strongest
possible prediction - showed no load preference.

Three further legs support the rejection. **Grgic's meta-analysis of five studies** at the fibre level
found **type I and type II growth comparable across loads** (high reps ~20-40 at 30-50% 1RM against
low reps 3-10 at 75-90%), with **wide 95% confidence intervals** he flags himself. **Sixteen
histochemical and autopsy surveys** show most human muscles sit near an even type I / type II split,
so outside the soleus the premise barely applies. And **the popular self-test does not work**: Douris
2006 found a weak **r = -0.48** using indirect fibre typing in untrained women, while Terzis 2008,
using actual **biopsies**, found **r below 0.2** between vastus lateralis fibre composition and reps
at 70% and 85% 1RM.

He raises the limitations unprompted - untrained subjects, only two muscles, thickness at a **single
site (25% of lower leg length)**, **no biopsies taken**, and only 8 weeks. His closing line, that
fibre type "is not a consideration that needs to go into constructing a training program", is broader
than the evidence: what was shown is that **load prescription** need not track fibre type, which says
nothing about recovery, fatigue cost or long-term programming.

The fibre-type story is also the load-bearing casualty in
[Combining rep ranges](benefits-combining-rep-ranges.md). He kills the standard justification for
mixing rep ranges (heavy for fast twitch, light for slow twitch) with Grgic, concedes the direct
hypertrophy data show no difference, and then argues for mixing anyway from **neural recruitment**
(citing data putting roughly **15.5% of quadriceps activation potential** beyond voluntary reach
against about **5%** for elbow flexors and calves), **capillarisation** and **pain tolerance**. All
three are mechanism, correlation or acute markers. The defensible part is **concurrent development of
other attributes**: low reps favour 1RM strength and bone mineral density; high reps favour local
endurance and mitochondrial protein content, which rose more at 30% than 80% 1RM in Lim 2019. On
capillarisation the one study he cites found **no statistical difference** between 20-25 and 8-12
reps, and he reports the null rather than the direction.

## Do heavy and light loads grow different parts of a muscle?

Possibly, on one badly confounded study.
[Do heavy and light loads grow different parts of a muscle?](heavy-light-weights-produce-different-regional-muscle.md)
is the thinnest note in the set and **has no retrievable reference list** — sources are named on
screen only, one of them with no author or year at all.

The point of departure is fair: the load-equivalence literature measured growth at **one region of
one muscle**, so regional differences would have been invisible to it. The one relevant conventional
trial is **Hisaeda 1996**, **11 untrained women**, leg extension, 3x/week for 8 weeks. Total
quadriceps CSA gains were similar, but the heads differed — the **15–20 rep group grew vastus
lateralis more**, the **4–5 rep group grew vastus intermedius and rectus femoris more** — and
significant increases appeared at **30% of thigh length (upper) for the light group and 70% (lower)
for the heavy group**, without saying which heads that applied to. Per-region values were summed
before analysis, which is what hides the regional data.

The groups differed in **four variables at once**: reps, sets (**5–6 vs 8–9**), rest (**90 s vs
as-needed**) and **tempo** (**2s/2s vs as fast as possible**). He singles out tempo as the most
plausible alternative, having covered evidence elsewhere that tempo alters regional growth. Then he
breaks his own fibre-type explanation on the data: the heavy group grew **vastus intermedius**
slightly more than **rectus femoris**, though rectus femoris is the more fast-twitch of the two; and
the light group's largest gain was **vastus lateralis**, though **vastus medialis** is the more
slow-twitch. Both predictions come out backwards.

**Against it:** an isometric triceps study, **12 untrained men**, 100% vs 60% MVC for 10 weeks,
measuring CSA at **20 regions** along the muscle and finding **similar increases at every one** —
limited because it used isometrics and pooled the three heads.

He still recommends load variety. The recommendation is harmless, but it follows from the low cost of
variety, not from a demonstrated regional benefit.

## Should you vary rep ranges over time?

**Permitted, not prescribed.** [Varying rep ranges is fine, but it does not build more muscle](alternating-rep-ranges-improves-muscle-growth-18.md)
is titled "18 studies" and rests on three with MRI or ultrasound. **Schoenfeld**: 19 men averaging 4.5
years training, 8 weeks, constant 8–12 to failure against **2–4 / 8–12 / 20–30** rotated across the
week; elbow flexor, triceps and vastus lateralis thickness similar. **De Souza**: 26 untrained men,
6 weeks, constant vs linear vs daily-undulating, MRI quadriceps CSA similar. **Fink**: 21 untrained
men, **within-subject by arm**, 8 weeks, 80% vs 30% vs alternating two-week blocks, MRI elbow flexor
CSA similar. All null, and **no study found variation inferior**.

He does something better than stopping at "not significant": he goes to **James Krieger's reanalysis
of the individual Schoenfeld data**, where the triceps advantage for the varied group survived removal
of one outlier while the biceps advantage did not, and calls the residual signal random variation in a
19-subject sample — stated as a judgement, not a finding. The one contrary result is **Kraemer**: 27
untrained women, **nine months**, varied beating constant on fat-free mass — by **skinfold calipers**,
which he discounts on measurement while treating the duration as genuinely novel. Two hypotheses are
left open and labelled: that the effect is **duration-dependent** (everything else ran 12 weeks or
less), and that it may differ **in women** (everything else used men). **The video has no retrievable
reference list; every study in it is listed unverified.**

**But the average hides people.** [Individual responses to rep range](best-rep-range-muscle-growth-isn-t.md)
covers the only crossover design here: **24 previously untrained women, mean age 58, BMI 27**,
lower-body machines, three sets each, 3x/week for **24 weeks** — 12 weeks at **8–12 reps** and 12 at
**27–31**, order counterbalanced. Group means matched the rest of the literature. Individuals did not:
one **lost ~5%** thigh lean tissue on 8–12 and **gained just over 10%** on 27–31; another gained
**~13%** on 8–12 and only **~3%** on 27–31. The order-effect objection he answers empirically — **50%
of subjects saw their largest gain in the range trained in the final 12 weeks** — so order cannot
explain the spread.

He then **disagrees with the study authors themselves**, who read the data as showing that switching
ranges delayed a plateau. His worked two-subject example: A gains 0.8 kg then 0.1 kg, B gains 0.1 kg
then 0.8 kg; averaged, growth looks steady across both halves, but nothing was delayed — different
people simply grew at different times. The implication reverses the authors' advice: **for someone
already growing well in a given rep range, switching may cost them.** Rotation is insurance for the
uncertain, not a plateau-breaker. The limitation he does *not* raise, and the note does: separating
true individual response from measurement error and regression to the mean needs repeated exposures to
the same condition or a non-training control arm, and this design has neither. He concedes that
critique in 2024 via the Robinson methodology paper, cited in
[the 40-studies video](many-reps-muscle-growth-40-studies.md), where the practical line becomes: try a
different rep range **only if progress has stalled with everything else dialled in**.

## Strength is a different outcome, and it does follow load

The separation is absolute in these notes, and it runs both ways.

**Strength tracks load.** In [Kubo](4-vs-8-vs-12-reps-strength.md), bench 1RM was similar in the 4 and
8 groups and **both beat 12**, despite identical pec volume growth. In **Schoenfeld 2016**, 2–4 reps
produced **greater bench and squat 1RM gains** than 8–12 even though hypertrophy outcomes largely
favoured 8–12. The 4-vs-8 tie is probably an **untrained-subject artefact**; the low-rep advantage
likely widens with training experience. A strength-oriented programme should therefore be
substantially **5 reps or fewer**, and [Combining rep ranges](benefits-combining-rep-ranges.md) adds
the health argument for keeping some: low muscular strength predicts all-cause mortality independent
of muscle mass, and heavy loading is the more consistent stimulus for bone mineral density.

**Size does not track load, and singles give strength with no size at all** — the
[maxing-out note](maxing-out-effective-building-muscle-one-rep.md) again. If someone asks whether
working up to heavy singles will build muscle, the answer this domain supports is no.

The most interesting wrinkle is Kubo's correlation analysis: bench strength gains correlated with pec
growth **in the 12-rep group but not in the 4 or 8 groups**. His explanation is that neural
adaptations, stimulated more by heavy loads, cloud the size–strength relationship. The implication is
useful — as a lifter maxes out neural adaptation and movement efficiency, **hypertrophy becomes the
remaining avenue to strength**, which makes higher-rep work defensible even for strength athletes. He
then attacks it himself: he has no idea when neural adaptations max out, and Balshaw 2019 finds
continued neural adaptation in well-trained lifters. The conclusion he keeps is deliberately weak.

## Blood flow restriction

**Barely covered in this domain, and there is no dedicated video in it.** The only substantive
treatment is a section of [the 40-studies video](many-reps-muscle-growth-40-studies.md), used to argue
that light loads do not have to reach momentary failure: **BFR studies find failure and near-failure
equivalent**, and a trained-subject study comparing heavy lower-body work at **1–3 RIR** against
high-rep BFR at **1–4 RIR** found **similar hypertrophy, including at the fibre level**. Its reference
list carries Jessee 2018, Buckner 2020, Bjornsen 2021 and Davids 2021 as the BFR papers — the one
place in this domain where BFR claims are actually traceable.

The channel's other BFR data point sits outside these fifteen notes, in the pump video, where **BFR at
20–40% 1RM matches 70–90% loading** (Lixandrao) despite producing an enormous pump — used as evidence
*against* the pump, not as a recommendation for cuffs.

**So this domain supports only a narrow BFR claim**: it lets very light loads produce growth
comparable to heavy loads, and it does not require training to momentary failure. Nothing here
compares BFR to conventional training inside a trained lifter's full programme, and he never
recommends it. For anything more, the library's real BFR material is elsewhere — see the clash
section below.

## Where this clashes with the rest of the library

**First, a negative result worth knowing: the ISSN position stands say nothing about this.** All 27
documents are nutrition and supplementation; every %1RM in that folder is a study protocol inside a
supplement trial. There is no ISSN prescription on load or reps to cite.

**On the band itself he is the outlier at the bottom, and the widest at the top.** Menno Henselmans
holds **5–30** — the exact figure this channel argues against — and has since moved to **4–30, "and
probably higher"**. Jeff Nippard says **3–35**, with a practical instruction to put **~75% of volume
in 6–15 reps** and split the rest above and below. Barbell Medicine give **3–20 for compounds and
6–20+ for isolation**, refusing the 20–35 territory entirely. So his **floor of 6 is contested from
below by all three**, and his **ceiling of 35 is contested from below by Barbell Medicine**.

**On the load floor he is close to consensus.** Menno agrees at **~30% 1RM**. Nippard puts it lower
and vaguer at **20–30%**. Barbell Medicine give no hypertrophy load floor at all — they prescribe RPE,
not percentages.

**On 3–5 reps the disagreement is real and it is about mechanism, not just numbers.** Nippard accepts
low reps as hypertrophically equivalent and objects only on practical grounds — dropout, injury, and
the set count needed to match — and he *programs* roughly **a third of size-leaning powerbuilding
volume in the 1–5 zone**, which this channel would call inefficient. Menno puts the true floor at
**~4 reps** and explains it as a **minimum time under tension per set**, not a per-set efficiency
discount; his reading is that a 4x4 protocol sits right at the edge and still works. Barbell Medicine
simply state 3–20 with no argument attached. Note the mirror-image use of the same tool: Menno runs
the *extra sets* confound argument in reverse, using it to explain away a low-rep **win**, where this
channel uses it to explain away low-rep **parity**.

**On maxing out he has no dissenters.** Menno concurs that a single 1RM does not build as much muscle
as a set of ten despite far higher tension. Barbell Medicine's only nearby claim — that singles above
85% 1RM improve single-rep performance — is a specificity claim sourced to a pilot study. This is the
one position in the domain that survives the whole library intact.

**On fibre type he has two independent dissenters, and his own note only names one.** Menno accepts
the same null findings and disputes the inference: **capillary density**, not fibre type, predicts
reps at a given %1RM; the effect shows up in **recovery** rather than growth per set; and the active
ingredient in the old Russian high-rep type-I findings was **continuous tension**, not rep number.
That clash is documented inside
[Training by fibre type](should-train-muscles-according-fiber-type.md), including the neat detail that
both men are reading different columns of the same Terzis table. The clash the note does *not* name is
Nippard's: he agrees fibre type barely matters for rep selection **except for the soleus**, where he
explicitly prescribes **15–25 rep calf sets** and warns that training calves only at 6–12 may cost
growth. That is a fibre-type-driven rep prescription, and it runs directly against the Schoenfeld 2020
calf study this channel leans on. Barbell Medicine take no position.

**On BFR the three sources are incompatible and this channel has no position at all.** Nippard is
dismissive — BFR does not enhance hypertrophy on its own and helps only alongside training that is
already suboptimal. Barbell Medicine give the fullest protocol in the library (**20–30% 1RM, one set
of 30 then 3x15, 30–60 s rest**, cuff pressure at a perceived 7/10) and treat it as additive rather
than superior — while carrying affiliate links to cuff products. Menno is the most mechanistically
specific: the cuff adds growth only in roughly a **30% 1RM band**, and his killer argument is that
**BFR grows non-occluded muscle too**, which metabolic stress downstream of the cuff cannot explain.

**One caution before treating agreement as corroboration.** Barbell Medicine, this channel and
Nippard all derive their load position from the **same paper** — Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn and Krieger
2017. Three sources, one evidence base.

## Weak points

**The floor rests on an argument, not a demonstration.** The case for 6 reps rather than 3 is
inferential — *the confounds favoured low reps and low reps still only tied* — and it is a good
argument, but the two studies that actually equated sets and rest **contradict each other**, and one
of them (Weiss) is 44 untrained men squatting with 6 minutes of rest, which is nobody's programme. He
is honest about this and says he hopes the number comes down. Two other sources in this library put
the floor lower and neither finds his efficiency argument decisive.

**The ceiling was never measured.** 35 is a safety margin. The only clean data point above it is
Lasevicius's 20% condition at 60+ reps, and a curl trial averaging 100 reps on set one matched 80%
1RM. Anyone asking "is a 40-rep set fine?" should be told that nobody knows, and that the fatigue cost
is higher than they expect.

**Four of the fifteen notes have no retrievable reference list** — the load-floor note, the
regional-growth note, the alternating-rep-ranges note and the 2021 reps-below-5 note. That last one
matters most: **the confounding argument, the single most valuable thing in this domain, is
unverifiable in its original form.** Its studies were reconstructed from the description's prose, and
the citations attached in the note are matched from the library's own record and marked unverified.
The argument is repeated with full reference lists in the 2024 and 2025 videos, and those are the
versions to lean on.

**Sample sizes are small and subjects are mostly untrained.** Vann is n=15 over 6 weeks. The 2025
preprint is n=14, not peer-reviewed, and produced a **null biopsy result its own authors could not
explain** — no fibre CSA increase in either condition despite whole-muscle thickness rising, attributed
to sampling roughly 150 fibres from a muscle holding hundreds of thousands. Nobrega is n=14 vs n=10.
Hisaeda is n=11. Dankel is n=5. The individual-response study is untrained women averaging 58. The
fibre-type rejection is an 8-week untrained calf study.

**Vann is asked to carry more than it can.** Its conditions differed in load, rep count, set
progression *and* proximity to failure simultaneously; any of those could be doing the work. He
downgrades to "less certain" rather than "5 reps do not work", but the title invites a firmer reading
— and he abandoned the 8-rep floor it produced within five months.

**Mechanism keeps standing in where outcome data is missing.** Motor-unit recruitment at failure is an
acute surrogate carrying the 2020 rep-range argument; central fatigue is an 18-subject isometric study
carrying the load floor; the fibre-type explanation for regional growth is disproved on its own data
and then extended to tempo anyway; and the entire hypertrophy case for combining rep ranges is
mechanism sitting beside a null outcome.

**Three internal inconsistencies to know about.** First, the count of confounded low-rep studies is
given as **four** (of five supporting studies) in the reps-below-5 note, **five** in the 40-studies
note, and **six** in the 3–5 rep note, whose sub-heading lists a different set again, including Kubo
2021 and Hisaeda 1996 that the other notes treat separately. Quote the argument, not the number.
Second, the same Schoenfeld 2020 calf study is described as **8 weeks** in the 2020 fibre-type note
and **10 weeks** in the 2023 rep-range note; 8 weeks is what the paper reports. Third, the biceps
fibre-count estimate appears as **170,000–419,000** in the 3–5 rep note and **198,000–419,000** in the
channel's hyperplasia material — a rounding-level discrepancy, but do not quote the lower bound as
settled.
