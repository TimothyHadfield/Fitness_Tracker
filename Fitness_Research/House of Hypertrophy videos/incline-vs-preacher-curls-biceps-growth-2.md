# Incline vs Preacher Curls: The Study That Went Against His Own Hypothesis

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Incline vs Preacher Curls for Biceps Growth (NEW Research)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gS-xsC48xTs)) · 2023-07-17 · 12 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- The first study to compare the two exercises directly found **preacher curls tended to beat incline curls** for elbow flexor growth at all three measured regions — but **no difference reached statistical significance** in any region, in 31 women.
- He had previously argued incline curls should be among the best biceps exercises, because shoulder extension lengthens the long head. He opens by saying the study **raises the possibility he was wrong** and lists five reasons it might not be decisive.
- His reframing is the useful part: preacher curls are hardest **at the start**, where the biceps are longest; incline curls are hardest as the biceps **shortens**. If preacher really is better, the operative variable may not be *being* stretched but **being loaded while stretched**.
- That hypothesis is contradicted by [Nunes 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32823490/), where barbell (hard at long length) and cable (hard at short length) preacher curls produced **similar** growth — though that study measured only one region.
- Practical upshot is deliberately soft: any elbow flexion with **supination** trains the biceps well, the incline group still grew, and he suggests preacher curls as something to try rather than to switch to.

## The study

[00:32] 31 women with some training experience, randomised to incline or preacher curls. The incline group worked from roughly 0° to no more than 110° of elbow flexion; the preacher group from about 0° to 90°. Both did **4 sets of 6–18 reps to failure, three times a week**. Elbow flexor thickness was measured at 50%, 60% and 70% of approximate upper arm length. Growth **tended to favour preacher at all three sites**.

[01:03] He raises the range-of-motion mismatch himself before a viewer can, and argues it is not fatal — the emerging lengthened-partial literature suggests larger ranges may not be necessary for maximising growth, so the shorter preacher range is not automatically a handicap. That is a defensible answer, though it is worth noticing it is also convenient.

[02:04] His other four caveats, in his own order:

1. **Nothing was statistically significant** in any region. He handles this well — he notes that absence of significance does not prove absence of a difference, but that it does keep alternative explanations alive.
2. **n = 31.** With that sample, the preacher group could simply have drawn better responders.
3. **Failure was not defined and sessions were unsupervised.** It is unclear whether all subjects reached true failure or stopped when they felt like it.
4. **The measurement site includes brachialis.** Whatever grew more, we cannot say whether it was the biceps, the brachialis, or both.

He explicitly refuses to bin the study for having limitations, and notes a second group is running a dumbbell-preacher-versus-incline comparison, so he wants to reserve confident statements until then.

## The hypothesis this generates

[05:08] The preacher curl is performed with the shoulder flexed, which *shortens* the biceps — yet it is hardest at the bottom, where the biceps are at their longest in that exercise. The incline curl puts the shoulder in extension, which *lengthens* the biceps — yet it is hardest as the elbow flexes and the biceps shortens. So a preacher advantage would suggest **where the resistance peaks matters more than where the muscle sits**.

Three studies support the general direction. [Pinto 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22027847/) compared mid-range partial preacher curls to full-range and found growth at the 60% region tended to favour full range. [Sato 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34616309/) and [Pedrosa 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/11/2/39) compared partials in the *initial* (stretched) position to partials in the final position: Sato found the initial position tended to grow all three regions more; Pedrosa found similar growth at 50% but greater growth at 70% for the initial position. Taken together, **the bottom half of a preacher curl appears to be the part doing the work**.

[05:39] Then he presents the study that cuts against him. Nunes compared **barbell versus cable preacher curls** — barbell hardest at long length, cable hardest at short — and found elbow flexor growth similar. If load position genuinely didn't matter, incline curls' stretch could still be enough. The caveat he attaches is real: Nunes measured one region only, while the newer work measures three.

## Recommendations, and the speculation

[07:09] Pick a curl you like. Fine-wire EMG ([Naito 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10328159/), [Buchanan 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2600619/)) shows the biceps most active in elbow flexion **with supination**, while brachialis and brachioradialis take over under pronation — so any supinated curl gives a biceps stimulus. The incline group did not lose size; they grew less on average.

[08:09] He then speculates that the **face-away cable curl** could be the single best biceps exercise: shoulder extension for the stretch, plus more consistent tension so the muscle is loaded at longer lengths. **This is mechanism reasoning with no supporting data at all**, and he says so — but it is the one place in the video where a specific exercise recommendation rests on nothing but an argument.

[08:39] On variety, [Costa 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33440446/) had one group do barbell curls Monday/Wednesday/Friday and another rotate barbell, preacher and incline. Growth across three elbow flexor regions tended to favour the rotating group. He flags that both groups also trained pulldown variations, which he thinks probably did not confound it.

[09:41] Finally, the lengthened-partial extension. Partial leg extensions at the initial position beat full range for rectus femoris and vastus lateralis; partial calf raises at the stretched position tended to beat full range ([Kassiano 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37015016/)); a multi-hip machine study points the same way but exists only as a conference abstract. He suggests trying partial incline or standard curls in the initial position, alternating full and partial reps, or finishing a full-range set to failure with lengthened partials. All labelled speculation, and all transplanted from other muscles.

[11:43] On biasing the long versus short head of the biceps: **no strong evidence that it can be done meaningfully.**

**Rigour note:** the whole video exists because a study contradicted his own published hypothesis, and he leads with that rather than burying it. **Overreach to flag:** the face-away cable curl pick, and the transfer of lengthened-partial results from quads and calves to the elbow flexors, are both mechanism arguments standing in for outcome data he does not have.
