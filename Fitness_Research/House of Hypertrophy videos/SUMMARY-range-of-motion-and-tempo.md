# Range of motion, muscle length, stretch and tempo

Eighteen notes on where in the range you load a muscle and how fast you move through it, spanning
February 2021 to October 2025. This is the domain where the evidence moved fastest and where House
of Hypertrophy is at its most useful, because his format — one variable, twelve minutes, every study
named with its sample size and training status — is exactly the format this question needed. It is
also the domain where **he changed his mind**, publicly and in stages, so the notes disagree with
each other by design: a reader who hits the 2021 note and the 2025 note in the wrong order will see
him arguing both sides of lengthened partials. The dates below are the tiebreaker, and the newer
note always wins.

Two structural warnings before the content. First, **two of these eighteen notes have no retrievable
reference list at all** — [Mentzer's static holds and negatives](fastest-way-build-muscle-says-mike-mentzer.md)
and [the 30–60 second rule](most-overrated-muscle-building-principle.md) cite on screen only, their
descriptions carry nothing, and every study in them is reconstructed from auto-captions and marked
unverified. You cannot check them. Second, almost every claim in this domain turns on **what was
measured and where**: whole-muscle volume by MRI, cross-sectional area at named percentages of limb
length, ultrasound thickness at one site, biopsy fibre CSA, or — at the bottom of the ladder — a tape
measure. The lengthened-partials literature is almost entirely a story about measurement sites, so
the distinction is never cosmetic here.

---

## Does full range of motion beat a partial?

Yes, if the partial is at a **short** muscle length. That is the whole of the older literature and it
was mis-read for a decade. [The partial-ROM case](full-range-motion-not-optimal-building-muscle.md)
(June 2021) is the note that made the correction, and its first act is a single argument applied four
times. Six studies had compared full to partial ROM; four favoured full, one found no difference, one
favoured partials — and **in all four pro-full-ROM studies the partial trained the muscle short or
neutral**. Pinto's preacher curl used 50–100° of elbow flexion against a full 0–130°. Bloomquist's
squat used 0–60° against 0–120°. Kubo's used 0–90° against 0–140°. McMahon's lower-body work used
0–50° against 0–90°. **Every one of them cut the stretched half.** The conclusion he draws is narrow
and correct: those four show a short-length partial is inferior to full ROM, and say nothing at all
about a long-length partial.

How badly a short partial fails is regional, and [regional hypertrophy](optimize-muscle-growth-most-people-don-t.md)
(August 2024) is the note that sets it out cleanly. Short-length partials grow the **proximal and
middle regions decently — sometimes as well as full ROM — and fail distally**. In Bloomquist's squat
trial, measured across **six regions of the front thigh**, the shallow group grew only the two most
proximal sites and **actually lost size at the distal thigh**. In McMahon's leg-extension work,
proximal vastus lateralis leaned to full ROM, the middle was similar, and the distal region was
considerably better with full ROM. This is why a single mid-belly ultrasound slice is a bad way to
run any of these studies — it samples the one place the effect is smallest.

Two qualifications keep this from being a blanket rule. **How short the partial is matters a lot.**
[Lengthened partials, region by region](faster-muscle-growth-new-study-epic.md) (September 2025)
lines up the depth data: Bloomquist's 60° squat lost to 120° across regions, but Kubo's **90° squat
matched 140° for quadriceps volume**, and Larsen's 2025 leg press found 100° of knee flexion similar
to an average 154° for overall regional quad growth. Extending a short partial from 60° to 90°
apparently closes most of the quad gap — and he immediately supplies the counter-evidence to his own
point, because in Kubo the **glute max and adductor volume were clearly better at 140°**. Depth still
buys something; it just stops buying quads. And second, across the whole literature summarised in the
2025 note, **ten studies compare shortened partials to full ROM or lengthened partials, and shortened
partials are generally a bit worse but far from useless** — growth happens, and mid-region growth has
sometimes matched.

The bench press is the one clean applied test.
[Full-range bench beat partials](half-bench-press-reps-more-strength-size.md) (March 2022) covers
Martínez-Cava: **49 men with six months' experience, Smith machine, 2×/week for 10 weeks**, full-,
two-thirds- and one-third-range groups, all partials at the **top**, each group loaded relative to
its own range-specific 1RM using velocity-based auto-regulation. **The full-range group gained most
on all three 1RM tests — full, two-thirds and one-third.** Training the partial range was worse at
building strength in that very partial range, which inverts specificity and is the genuinely
interesting result. The one-third group was worst everywhere. He also presents the contradiction: two
Massey studies using a 2–5 inch top-range partial found full range best in women with limited
experience but **no difference at all between full, partial and combination groups in men with some
training experience**, and he says plainly he does not know what explains the conflict.

## Do lengthened partials beat full range of motion?

**No — but nothing has found them worse, and the distal edge is real if small.** This is the question
his position moved on, so take it chronologically.

**June 2021, the opening move.** [The partial-ROM case](full-range-motion-not-optimal-building-muscle.md)
introduces Pedrosa: **45 untrained women, 12 weeks, knee extension**, five groups — long-length
partial (100–65° knee flexion), short-length partial (65–30°), full ROM (100–30°), a varied group
alternating the two, and a control. All did 3–6 sets of 7 at **60% of a 1RM specific to their own
range and retested fortnightly**, which is the design detail that makes it credible. Rectus femoris
and vastus lateralis CSA at 40, 50, 60 and 70% of femur length: **all similar at 40%; long-length and
varied ahead at 50% and 60%; long-length ahead of everything at 70%.** His mechanism is mechanical
tension, active and passive — **voluntary activation rising from 93.6% at 50° of knee flexion to 96%
at 75° and 98.5% at 100°**, plus passive titin force — and he explicitly rejects metabolic stress and
damage. He then spends three minutes hedging: one study, untrained women, two of four quad heads, and
a knee extension whose resistance profile is hardest where the quads are shortest, which flatters the
result.

**April 2022, peak optimism.** [Training at long muscle lengths](triple-hypertrophy-training-muscle-like.md)
covers Sato: **32 people untrained for at least six months, 5 weeks**, dumbbell preacher curls twice
weekly at 3×10, one group at 0–50° of elbow flexion (stretched) and one at 80–130° (contracted), each
loaded from its own mid-range maximal isometric force. Elbow flexor thickness at 50%, 60% and 70% of
upper arm length: **similar at 50% and 60%, significantly greater for the long group at 70%, and
roughly three times greater averaged across the three sites.** He flags the missing arm immediately —
**there was no full-ROM group** — and says the full-versus-lengthened-partial claim still rests on
Pedrosa alone.

**October 2022, the sweep.** [Stretch-mediated hypertrophy](key-more-muscle-stretch-mediated-hypertrophy.md)
generalises the pattern muscle by muscle: quads, glutes, adductors, hamstrings, biceps and triceps
all show more growth trained at longer lengths, **largest distally**, while stating that there was
then **no direct research at all on back, shoulders, calves, abs or chest**. He still refuses to say
lengthened partials beat full ROM off one study.

**September 2024, the turn.** [The first trial in trained lifters](finally-here-new-study-muscle-growth-epic.md)
covers Wolf et al.: **25 trained individuals averaging 4.9 years of experience, twice weekly for 8
weeks**, within-subject, one side of the body on full ROM and the other on lengthened partials across
every exercise, all sets to momentary failure. Elbow flexor and triceps thickness at **45%, 50% and
55% of humerus length grew similarly**. The within-subject design is the strength — one side of your
body does not sleep more than the other. He raises the sharpest caveat himself: **only the 45–55%
regions were measured**, precisely the window where prior work says the effect is smallest. He still
reads the null as informative, because it means the **peak squeeze at the shortened position is
dispensable** — the old assumption that you need the full range for both stretch and contraction does
not survive.

**April 2025, the counterweight consolidates.** [Post-failure partials vs lengthened partials](training-beyond-failure-new-study-epic.md)
puts the two trained-subject nulls side by side — **Wolf's n=25 and a 297-subject full-body trial**,
neither finding an arm or thigh difference — and this is where he stops recommending lengthened
partials generally. He does not simply accept them: Wolf has an open dispute over how the partials
were standardised, the 297-subject trial used low volumes, and **both measured only the mid-region**.
The note's own new study is a first head-to-head of post-failure partials against lengthened
partials: **23 trained individuals averaging 7.3 years, unilateral Smith machine calf raises, 4 sets
twice weekly for 8 weeks**, one leg doing lengthened partials to failure at 10–20 reps and the other
doing full ROM to failure then partials past it, with total reps deliberately matched. Growth
**tended** to favour lengthened partials, and hitting failure twice in one set bought nothing — but
he names three caveats himself: n=23, an analysis that "failed to provide anywhere close to strong
support", and high measurement error. **Only calves survive as a confident recommendation.**

**September 2025, the reconciliation.** [Lengthened partials, region by region](faster-muscle-growth-new-study-epic.md)
tests the distal hypothesis directly: **13 trained lifters, ~6 years experience, 8 weeks**, unilateral
machine preacher curls, full ROM at 0–140° against a lengthened partial at 0–70°, both arms 5 sets of
10–15 to momentary failure. **The 50% region was a dead heat; the 70% region favoured partials at a
Bayes factor of 4.87** — moderate evidence, not a win, and he reads it as *slightly* greater distal
growth. The tally that matters: **six studies now compare full ROM to lengthened partials. All three
in untrained subjects favour partials. All three in trained subjects show similar growth — but two of
those three could not have seen a distal effect**, because they only measured mid-muscle.

**Where it lands.** Lengthened partials are **at least as good as full ROM and occasionally slightly
better distally**; nothing has found them inferior; **full ROM remains a defensible default**. The
untrained/trained split and the mid-region measurement problem between them explain most of the
apparent contradiction. His own recommendation by the end is that both are on the table — use
lengthened partials on some exercises, on final sets, or as extended reps once full-ROM failure hits.

One live hypothesis worth keeping: **the effect may be confined to exercises hardest at the shortened
position**. Calf raises and leg extensions are where untrained trials found partials superior; the two
trained nulls used Bayesian curls, overhead triceps extensions and incline curls, which are already
hardest at moderate-to-long lengths so a lengthened partial adds nothing. He labels this unfalsified
either way. The muscle-specific version is that **calves are the clean case**, because gastrocnemius
force capacity falls off sharply at short lengths, with traps a guess and pecs probably not needing it.

A related question with one honest answer:
[all-long-length curls vs a mix](have-talk-about-biceps-growth.md) (February 2025) is the first study
to ask whether training a muscle *entirely* at long lengths beats a mix. **Seven trained subjects
averaging 5.5 years**, within-subject, one arm doing six sets of a shoulder-extended cable curl and
the other three lengthened plus three shortened, twice weekly to failure at 8–12 reps — then a 6–8
week washout and a full crossover with arms re-randomised, giving the observation count of a
14-subject study. **CSA trended to the all-long arm but stayed inside the region of practical
equivalence; thickness showed nothing; circumference favoured all-long at every site and cleared the
ROPE at 70%.** He gives both readings and leans to "small real advantage obscured by low power". The
practical conclusion is deliberately dull: **include at least one shoulder-extended curl; a mix still
grows you fine.**

## Does training at long muscle lengths matter — as opposed to partial ranges?

**Yes, and he is emphatic that this is a different question.** In the trained-lifter partials trials
both conditions reached the *same maximum* muscle length; the partial merely omitted the shortened
half. **Exercise selection that reaches a longer maximum length is a separate claim and it still
wins.** The cases, gathered across
[training at long muscle lengths](triple-hypertrophy-training-muscle-like.md),
[stretch-mediated hypertrophy](key-more-muscle-stretch-mediated-hypertrophy.md) and
[regional hypertrophy](optimize-muscle-growth-most-people-don-t.md):

- **Seated over lying leg curls** (Maeo 2021) is the cleanest result in the domain. Same 0–90° knee
  range; the seated position's hip flexion stretches the three biarticular hamstrings further.
  **Volume increases were significantly greater in all three, while the biceps femoris short head —
  which crosses only the knee — grew the same.** An internal control behaving exactly as predicted.
- **Overhead over neutral triceps extensions.** Stasinaki 2018 leaned that way non-significantly in
  **nine subjects**; Maeo 2022, within-subject, found significantly greater long head growth overhead
  and unexpectedly greater **medial and lateral** head growth too, which he keeps rather than
  discards on the grounds that having no explanation is not the same as having no effect.
- **Leaning-back over normal leg extensions**, where the rectus femoris — the only biarticular quad
  head — sits longer at 40° of hip flexion than 90°, with the **biggest difference proximally**.
- **Incline over preacher curls**, from a conference abstract he explicitly refuses to comment on
  fully until publication.

For biarticular muscles the pattern is that longer beats shorter with the **largest differences
proximally** — but it rests on three studies and he says so. And he argues against his own pattern by
naming a possible **threshold**: lying leg extensions lengthen the rectus femoris more still than
leaning back, produced more activation, and were **not more effective**. A forthcoming study likewise
found shoulder-neutral cable curls similar to shoulder-extended when tension was highest at the start.
So "longer is better" has a ceiling somewhere, location unknown.

Squat depth is the applied version.
[Squat depth](partial-squats-superior-deep-squats-size-strength.md) (January 2022) reads Bloomquist
(**17 men untrained for six months, 0–60° vs 0–120°, 3×/week for 12 weeks, six thigh regions**) and
Kubo (**17 men untrained a year, 0–90° vs 0–140°, ~8RM, 2×/week for 10 weeks, muscle volume**)
together: **60° is insufficient for anything; 90° is adequate for quads but leaves glute max and
adductors on the table.** Strength is pure specificity across four studies — train the depth you want
to be strong at — with one exception, Bazyler's **17 strong men (average full squat 146.9 kg)**, where
a combination of full plus 100° partials beat full-only on **both** full and partial 1RM, volume
matched as load × range × reps. Athleticism splits by training age: deeper won for jumps in untrained
and lightly trained subjects (Bloomquist, Hartmann), but **Rhea's college athletes got the biggest
vertical jump and 40-yard sprint gains from 55–65° partials**, which is one study against two. On
safety, Hartmann's review found no greater injury risk from deep squats and argued **partials may
carry more risk because they demand much heavier loads**.

## Does stretching between sets do anything?

Nothing in these eighteen notes tests it — inter-set stretching and FST-7 live in the Programming
domain, in [stretching between sets](stretching-between-sets-help-build-more-muscle.md),
[two new studies](stretch-between-sets-more-hypertrophy.md) and
[the FST-7 failure](stretching-between-sets-transform-muscle-strength.md), where the original
positive finding does not replicate. The one adjacent item in the channel's Mechanisms section is
[an hour of daily calf stretching](stretching-builds-muscle-similar-lifting-weights.md), whose
protocol is the whole story.

What this domain establishes instead is that **the stretch that matters is stretch under load** —
tension at a long muscle length inside a working set, not passive lengthening between them.
[Stretch-mediated hypertrophy](key-more-muscle-stretch-mediated-hypertrophy.md) is the muscle-by-muscle
map of that claim, and it is worth noting where it is thin: the **chest section has no direct
hypertrophy data at all** and builds its conclusion on Martínez-Cava's bench *strength* study, which
measured no growth. He labels it "very indirect", and it is the one place in an otherwise scrupulous
video where the reasoning outruns the outcome data. Credit where it is due in the same note: he
**discards** a 2020 Brazilian squats-versus-hip-thrusts paper reporting far more glute growth with
squats — a result that supports his own thesis — because that research group's data validity has been
seriously questioned.

Two techniques sit in the same conceptual space and both come out inert.
[Constant tension](constant-tension-key-building-more-muscle.md) (June 2021) tests the bodybuilding
idea that never locking out keeps the muscle loaded and accumulates metabolites. Applied **without**
shortening the range, it does nothing: two Tanimoto studies — **24 untrained men across five machine
exercises**, and **16 untrained men on the leg extension for 12 weeks** — found statistically similar
thickness or CSA gains everywhere, and the leg extension is the right test because locking the knee
still leaves quad tension, so the only real difference between conditions was the 1-second pause.
Applied **by** cutting to a mid-range partial, it is unresolved: **Goto (44 men with 1+ years
training, skull crushers, 45–90° elbow flexion only, 8 weeks) found the partial produced significantly
greater triceps CSA at 60% of upper arm length, while Pinto (30 untrained men, preacher curl, 50–100°,
10 weeks) found full ROM better for biceps thickness.** Two well-matched studies pointing opposite
ways, and his verdict is that together they tell us nothing. The parsimonious reading of the whole
video is that **muscle length is doing the work and continuity of tension is incidental**.

[Isometrics](they-said-worst-way-build-muscle.md) (October 2025) is the same logic at zero velocity.
Varovic 2025, the **first isometric-versus-dynamic trial in trained individuals — 23 subjects
averaging 4.2 years, within-subject, one leg each**, found **long-length isometric knee extensions
(held at ~125° of flexion, 30 s per set at RPE 10) matched full-range dynamic knee extensions
(125–10°, also 30 s per set) for quadriceps growth over 6 weeks**, at 3–5 sets, 90 s rest, twice
weekly, thickness measured at **three regions each of anterior and lateral thigh**. Some measures
leaned isometric, notably the upper anterior thigh. Across six head-to-head studies, four found
similar size gains, one found regional differences, and the only clearly negative one is **Rasch and
Morehouse 1957, which measured upper-arm circumference**. Two useful boundaries: **maximum stretch is
not required** — most supportive studies used 90–100° of knee flexion — and the evidence base is
machine or immovable-object isometrics, so **he refuses to extend it to planks and wall sits**, which
is the video's weakest link precisely because he declines to bridge it.

## What do eccentrics actually contribute?

[Mentzer's static holds and heavy negatives](fastest-way-build-muscle-says-mike-mentzer.md) (November
2023) is the long answer, and the note to be most careful with, because **it has no retrievable
reference list** — the 11 accentuated-eccentric studies, the n=53 Japanese trial and the 84.5%
voluntary-activation figure are all named aloud and unverified.

**The contracted-position hold is the weak half of Mentzer's protocol.** His premise that the fully
contracted position is the only one where 100% of the muscle can be activated is wrong on three
mechanisms: sarcomere overlap crowds myosin heads at extreme shortening so fewer cross-bridges form;
**voluntary activation is not 100% and is often higher at long lengths** (84.5% for quadriceps against
~95% for elbow flexors and calves); and passive titin force exists only when stretched. The training
data agree — holds at long lengths beat holds at short ones, and lengthened partials beat contracted
partials. **The feeling of a hard squeeze at peak contraction is not a readout of fibre force**, which
is the useful correction.

**The lowering phase does contribute — and the study that appears to prove it is confounded.** The
Japanese trial (**n=53 untrained, preacher curl lengthened partials, 3×10, twice weekly for 5 weeks**,
concentric-only vs eccentric-only vs normal vs control, same absolute load progressed 30% to 100% of
maximal isometric strength) found minimal growth in the concentric-only group. His objection is
proximity to failure: **eccentric strength is roughly 40% higher than concentric, so a load demanding
for the eccentric group was well short of failure for the concentric group.** Where concentric-only
work is taken near failure — on machines that unload the lowering — it grows muscle well. This is the
video's best moment: he dismantles the study that most supports his own framing, on a design detail.

**Overloading the eccentric is effective but not superior.** Keep two things apart: *eccentric-only
overloaded* (lowering a load you cannot lift, which is what Mentzer's pre-fatiguing hold approximates)
and *accentuated eccentric* (normal lift, extra load on the lowering — flywheels, weight releasers,
the 2-1 method). The one trial comparing normal training to eccentric-only overload, in untrained
older individuals, found **similar growth**; of **11 accentuated-eccentric studies, the majority found
no advantage** over normal training. His explanation is a **stimulus ceiling** — past some point the
stimulus-fatigue interaction caps what a set delivers — which he correctly labels speculation.

**And Mentzer's fatigue argument runs backwards.** Eccentric overload causes considerably *more*
damage, soreness and post-session strength loss than concentric work, and when accentuated eccentric
training is compared to eccentric-only overload the fatigue is no greater, meaning it comes almost
entirely from the lowering. The mitigating fact is the **repeated bout effect**: 5 sets of 15 maximal
eccentric-overload reps produced fatigue and soreness lasting at least five days initially and almost
none after 10 weeks. The cost is a start-up cost, not a permanent tax.

The open thread is whether eccentric overload builds a *different* kind of muscle — some evidence that
concentric work preferentially grows middle regions and adds **sarcomeres in parallel**, while
overloaded eccentric work grows distal regions and adds **sarcomeres in series**. His counterweight is
that normal training already contains an underloaded lowering and already produces impressive distal
growth, especially through a full range or lengthened partials. Verdict: **you are not leaving much on
the table with normal reps.**

## How much does tempo matter?

Almost not at all, and **unlike range of motion this answer has not moved since 2021**.

The decisive methodological point belongs to
[time under tension is overrated](time-under-tension-overrated-10-studies.md) (May 2021). The three
studies that appear to show slow tempo builds more muscle — **Usui (16 untrained men, back squat,
3×10 at 50% 1RM, 10 weeks), Watanabe 2013 (3×8 at 50% 1RM, 12 weeks) and Watanabe 2014 (3×12 at 30%
1RM, 12 weeks)**, all 3s/3s against 1s/1s — share one fatal design flaw: **load and rep count were
fixed, so the slow group was necessarily closer to failure.** At 50% 1RM with 1-second reps the fast
group was a very long way from it, which is why Usui's normal-speed group grew almost nothing. These
measure effort, not tempo.

When effort *is* controlled, the difference vanishes. **Lacerda — ten untrained men, within-subject,
one leg at 2 s per rep and the other at 6 s, unilateral knee extension, 3–4 sets to failure at 50–60%
1RM, 14 weeks** — found similar rectus femoris and vastus lateralis CSA gains, with set durations of
**25–38 s** on the fast leg and **37–52 s** on the slow. **Chaves — untrained men, 3 sets to failure
at 70% 1RM for 8 weeks, a deliberate 4-second rep against a self-selected tempo averaging 0.9 s up
and 0.9 s down** — found similar vastus lateralis CSA. **Schoenfeld's meta-analysis of four
to-failure studies puts 0.5 to 8 seconds per rep at parity**, and that is where the library's
0.5–8 second range comes from.

The mechanism backs it. **Morton 2019, in men with 2+ years of training**, ran four leg-extension
conditions to failure — 80% 1RM at fast and slow tempos (26 s and 31 s per set) and 30% 1RM at fast
and slow (54 s and 76 s) — and found **similar slow- and fast-twitch recruitment by glycogen depletion
across a threefold spread in time under tension**. The rep-range literature corroborates: 5–35 reps to
failure grow similarly, which necessarily means wildly different TUTs are equivalent.

[The 30–60 second rule](most-overrated-muscle-building-principle.md) (August 2022) is the kill shot on
the popular number, and also **the second note here with no reference list** — its description carries
only timestamps and music credits. Lacerda's fast sets ran **25–38 seconds, below the supposed
threshold, and grew the same**. The sharpest supporting argument is from a different literature
entirely: **loads from 30% to 80% 1RM produce similar hypertrophy to failure, but 30% yields 30–40
reps and 80% yields 7–12** — if TUT drove growth, light-load training should win outright, and it
does not. He offers a **floor near 9 seconds per set** (Schoenfeld's 7×3 versus 3×10, with the 9-second
figure coming from the author's Instagram remark, and only because that group did more than twice the
sets) and a **ceiling around 84–140 seconds** back-calculated from the super-slow study. Both are
single-study inferences wearing the clothes of numbers.

[SuperSlow training](superslow-training-produce-super-fast-gains.md) (March 2022) handles the one
tempo with evidence against it. The famous **Westcott 2001** result — SuperSlow gaining ~50% more
strength across just over 70 untrained subjects aged 25–82 — **tested each group at its own tempo**,
which is two different tests, not a comparison. **Keeler 2001 (14 untrained women, eight exercises,
3×/week for 10 weeks, both groups tested at a true 1RM with no deliberate tempo) had the regular group
gain much more on 5 of 8 exercises** and trend ahead on the rest. The only hypertrophy comparison is
**Schuenke 2012: 19 untrained women, 6 weeks, leg press, squat and leg extension, regular at 6–10 reps
and 80–85% 1RM with 1–2 s reps against SuperSlow at 40–60% 1RM with 10 s up and 4 s down — vastus
lateralis slow- and fast-twitch fibre CSA both favoured the regular group.** He rules out the load
explanation himself, since 30–80% 1RM to failure grows similarly. The best part is the mechanics:
**the force-velocity argument for slow reps is a misapplication.** Force-velocity is established in
unfatigued fibres or against different loads; with **one fixed load, force = mass × acceleration**, so
moving slower *reduces* force. **Schilling 2008** measured it — a national-level weightlifter squatting
170 kg produced lower mean and peak propulsive force at 10s/4s than at maximal speed. His proposed
reason SuperSlow underperforms anyway is **central fatigue** (Yoon 2007: a 20% MVC hold lasted ~14
minutes and produced more central fatigue than an 80% hold lasting ~25 seconds), which he labels a
hypothesis built on one 19-subject study.

[The best rep speed](best-rep-speed-size.md) (October 2025) closes the file. A meta-analysis of
**14 studies and 278 subjects**, restricted to trials manipulating **one phase in isolation**, found
**no clear advantage either way**. The eccentric estimate leaned faster at **SMD 0.06 — about 0.9
percentage points of growth, 5% versus 5.9% — with a credible interval comfortably spanning zero**.
A post-hoc split by failure hints that slower eccentrics may edge it in to-failure training (6 of 6 of
those studies used ordinary free weights or machines, against 3 of 5 non-failure studies using
isokinetic dynamometers), and he argues it then backs off. **Both conditions grew 5–6%**, matching the
literature-wide norm.

Whether tempo shifts *where* a muscle grows is the weakest note in the domain.
[Regional tempo effects](repetition-tempo-impact-regional-muscle-growth.md) (February 2021) has three
studies and one significant regional difference between them. **Diniz — 44 untrained women across
5s/1s, 1s/5s and 3s/3s knee extensions, 6 reps at 50% 1RM, 10 weeks, CSA at 30/50/70% of thigh length
across all four quad heads — found exactly one significant comparison**, rectus femoris at 50% where
both 5/1 and 1/5 beat 3/3. **Pearson — 13 well-trained men, within-subject, 1 s versus 3 s eccentric
for 8 weeks — found similar anterior thigh thickness at 40% and significantly greater growth for the
FASTER eccentric at 60%.** And the flagship Earp comparison sets heavy parallel squats against
explosive jump squats at 30% 1RM far from failure, confounding tempo with load and effort. "Tempo may
influence regional growth" is as far as it goes.

**Practically: pick the tempo you prefer, keep it roughly between half a second and eight seconds per
rep, take sets to or near failure, and do not manage time under tension as a variable. Avoid genuine
super-slow work. Slowing tempo is a legitimate progressive-overload lever when you cannot add load.**

## Weak points

**The Wolf sample size is inconsistent inside this domain.** Both
[the trained-lifter trial note](finally-here-new-study-muscle-growth-epic.md) and
[the 2025 counterweight note](training-beyond-failure-new-study-epic.md) give **n=25** (4.9 years
average experience). But the library-editorial section of the
[2021 partial-ROM note](full-range-motion-not-optimal-building-muscle.md) refers to "the 2024 null
trial on **30** trained lifters". **Report n=25 as the figure two notes give, and treat the "30" as
unreconciled** — the library's cross-source layer flags this as a known defect, and it should be
checked against the preprint rather than silently resolved.

**A date discrepancy on the same meta-analysis.** The Schoenfeld repetition-duration meta-analysis is
cited as **2015** in [time under tension is overrated](time-under-tension-overrated-10-studies.md)
(matching its reference list, *Sports Med* 45(4):577-85, 2015) and as **"a 2016 Schoenfeld
meta-analysis"** in [the 30–60 second rule](most-overrated-muscle-building-principle.md). Same study,
one year wrong; the 2015 date is the verifiable one.

**Two notes cannot be checked at all.** [Mentzer](fastest-way-build-muscle-says-mike-mentzer.md) and
[the 30–60 second rule](most-overrated-muscle-building-principle.md) have **no reference list in the
video description**, and both are load-bearing — the first is the domain's only real treatment of
eccentrics, the second supplies the TUT floor and ceiling numbers. Every study in them is
auto-caption-reconstructed and marked unverified, including the **11 accentuated-eccentric studies**
(none named), the **n=53** Japanese trial, and the **84.5%** voluntary-activation figure. Separately,
[the best rep speed](best-rep-speed-size.md) cites a **meta-analysis of 111 studies** for the
"typical hypertrophy is ~5%" benchmark that does not appear in its own reference list, and
[all-long-length curls](have-talk-about-biceps-growth.md) rests on a **single unpublished preprint**,
its only citation.

**A minor exercise-description drift.** The 2021 note describes McMahon's ROM study as **lower-body
exercises**, while [regional hypertrophy](optimize-muscle-growth-most-people-don-t.md) labels the same
pair of McMahon 2014 papers as **leg extension**. Low stakes, but if a reader is chasing the protocol
the two notes will not match.

**Where his conclusions outrun his evidence.** The chest section of
[stretch-mediated hypertrophy](key-more-muscle-stretch-mediated-hypertrophy.md) builds a hypertrophy
conclusion on a **strength** study with no growth measurement, and
[half bench press reps](half-bench-press-reps-more-strength-size.md) repeats the move — he says he is
"almost certain" full-range benching grows more, on **zero bench-press hypertrophy data**, which does
not exist. [Constant tension](constant-tension-key-building-more-muscle.md) reclassifies Pedrosa
post hoc as a constant-tension study to keep a technique alive that his own data (two null Tanimoto
trials, a flat Goto/Pinto contradiction) suggest is inert. [All-long-length curls](have-talk-about-biceps-growth.md)
leans on an **arm-circumference** result from **n=7** in the same breath as calling circumference the
crudest measure available. The bench-press note's powerlifting extension — that competition arching
is effectively a top-range partial, so benching with less arch might raise a competition 1RM — is a
long inference from mechanically defined Smith-machine stopping points to altered scapular positions,
and he does not flag the gap. The [isometrics](they-said-worst-way-build-muscle.md) bridge from
machine work to planks and wall sits is pure mechanism with no outcome data. And in
[squat depth](partial-squats-superior-deep-squats-size-strength.md) the 133% lumbar bone-density and
thicker-cartilage figures are cross-sectional observations of weightlifters reported secondhand
through a review — they show adaptability, not causation.

**Watch the titles.** [Full range of motion is NOT optimal](full-range-motion-not-optimal-building-muscle.md),
[TRIPLE hypertrophy](triple-hypertrophy-training-muscle-like.md) and
[Faster muscle growth… Epic](faster-muscle-growth-new-study-epic.md) all sell conclusions considerably
harder than their own contents support — one 12-week trial in untrained women, a 5-week study with no
full-ROM group, and a moderate Bayesian regional difference in 13 people respectively. The bodies of
those videos are measured throughout; the packaging is not, and a reader skimming headlines will
mis-read the domain in exactly the direction he later corrected.

**Population, duration and measurement limits apply everywhere.** Almost the entire tempo literature
is **untrained subjects** — he says so, and bridges to trained lifters by mechanism plus two rep-range
studies. The ROM studies run weeks to a few months, with nothing on what years of lengthened partials
would do; his own guess is visibly more distal development, and he says claiming certainty would be
lying. And repeatedly, the studies that report nulls turn out to have measured **one mid-belly site**,
which is the single most consequential methodological fact in this domain.
