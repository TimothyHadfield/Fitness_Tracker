# How Many Sets? The Swelling Objection to High Volume, Tested

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How Many Sets for Muscle Growth? (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=21m6wO96KEM)) · 2026-02-16 · 25 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- **The new study (Alvarez et al.) is the first to compare muscle swelling across different set numbers.** 13 trained men, crossover, three workouts a week apart at 7, 14 and 21 total sets. Result: **no significant differences in swelling between set numbers, and swelling had essentially resolved by 24 hours** in every condition.
- That removes the main objection to the volume literature. The charge was that studies measuring muscle size 48–72 hours after the last session were reading residual swelling from high-volume groups as growth. On this evidence they were not.
- **Dhim reports no effect sizes, confidence intervals or point estimates for the new study** — only the null result and the authors' quoted conclusion. That is the single biggest gap in the video's evidence reporting.
- The background is **Enes et al. 2023** (31 trained men; 22 fixed weekly sets vs progressions reaching 42 and 52 weekly sets, with growth trending better at the highest volumes — still the highest weekly set count in the literature) and the **Pelland et al. meta-regression** (35 studies; more sets, more hypertrophy, **no plateau and no inverted U**).
- The strength/hypertrophy mismatch — hypertrophy rising indefinitely while strength appears maximised at **~5 weekly sets** — is explained by the strength analysis containing nearly double the studies, with many non-overlapping ones on untrained subjects showing large low-volume strength gains. Restricting to trained subjects with matched size and strength measures, higher sets produce both.
- **His recommendation is not "do 40 sets."** It is: train as much as you feel you can handle while maintaining high effort per set, adjust over time, and if you want to try very high volumes, do it on one or two muscle groups and add sets gradually.

## Why volume was in dispute

The starting point is **Enes et al.**, published October 2023 and, by Dhim's account, one of the most controversial hypertrophy papers of recent years.

**Design.** 31 well-trained individuals trained back squat, leg press and leg extension twice a week. Everyone performed **22 sets in the first week** — 11 sets per session split across the three movements. Then:

- **Fixed group:** 22 weekly sets for the whole study.
- **Four-set group:** +4 sets every two weeks, reaching **42 weekly sets** in the final week.
- **Six-set group:** +6 sets every two weeks, reaching **52 weekly sets** in the final week.

All sets were aimed at 2 reps from failure in the 6–12 rep range, except the final set on each exercise, which went to volitional concentric failure.

**Result.** Growth, assessed as vastus lateralis cross-sectional area and thickness, tended to be better with the highest volumes. Back squat 1RM increases followed a similar pattern. The 52 weekly sets reached by the six-set group remains the highest weekly volume reported in the literature.

**The confound Dhim flags himself.** The higher-volume groups differed from the fixed group in two ways at once: a higher *average* number of sets, and the *act of progressively adding* sets. Which one drove the extra growth is unknown. He illustrates the problem cleanly — a group holding 20 weekly sets for 11 weeks and a group ramping 10 to 30 over 11 weeks both average 20, and nobody knows whether they would grow the same. He says the current evidence is insufficient to answer this and defers it to a future video. That is an honest hanging thread rather than a resolved point.

**The objection.** Critics argued the extra growth in the high-volume groups was not real hypertrophy — not added contractile units — but muscle swelling. This was not an absurd suggestion: intense workouts cause transient increases in muscle size, and some papers document swelling detectable for days afterwards. In Enes et al., muscle size was measured **72 hours after the final session**, and sceptics did not accept that as long enough.

## The meta-regression that widened the argument

Just over a year later, the **Pelland et al.** meta-regression pooled **35 studies** and found more sets producing greater hypertrophy with **no sign of a plateau or an inverted-U**. Dhim calls this the strongest available insight into the sets–hypertrophy relationship, and it is the anchor for everything that follows.

The x-axis is fractional sets per muscle group per week — working sets only, not warm-ups. Across the pooled studies the average was about **10 reps per set**, and **most sets were taken to failure**. Because many of the constituent studies also measured muscle size 48–72 hours after the last session, the same swelling objection was raised against the whole regression: perhaps lower volumes were enough to maximise real hypertrophy, and the upward slope was an artefact.

The swelling argument itself is not new — Dhim traces it back to at least 2018 — but since these two papers it has attached almost entirely to them.

**The gap in the literature.** No study had ever compared swelling responses between different set numbers. So nobody knew how large swelling differences between low and high volumes were, or how long swelling from different set numbers took to subside. The new paper exists to fill exactly that gap.

## The new study, in detail

**Alvarez et al.**, the first study to compare muscle swelling across set numbers.

- **Sample:** 13 trained individuals, average **5.1 years** of training experience.
- **Design:** within-subject crossover, three workouts separated by a week, **counterbalanced in randomised order**. Each subject completed all three conditions.
- **Conditions:** back squat, leg press and leg extension at **7 total sets**, **14 total sets**, or **21 total sets** — the only difference between workouts.
- **Load and reps:** each exercise began at **90% of the subject's 10RM**, targeting **8–10 reps** per set, with load adjusted as needed to hold the rep range.
- **Rest:** 2 minutes between sets, 3 minutes between exercises.
- **Effort:** the authors state subjects were motivated to exert maximal effort on each exercise, applied uniformly to minimise variability in exertion.
- **Measurements:** muscle thickness and echo intensity of the anterior thigh (comprising rectus femoris and vastus intermedius) at **two sites**, taken pre-workout, immediately post-workout, and at **24, 48 and 72 hours**.

**Result.** There were **no significant differences in response between the different set numbers**, and swelling had more or less fully subsided **24 hours** after the workout in every condition. The authors' conclusion, quoted in the video: residual muscle swelling and/or oedema is unlikely to be a major confounding factor in hypertrophy assessments, at least when post-training measurements are taken 24 hours or later after the final session.

**On the effect size.** Dhim gives none. No point estimates for thickness change at any timepoint, no confidence intervals, no standardised effect sizes, and no discussion of whether 13 subjects gave adequate power to detect a difference that exists. This is a null result being used to close an argument, and null results are exactly where those numbers matter most. It is the clearest reporting weakness in an otherwise careful video, and anyone relying on this conclusion should read the paper rather than the summary.

**On the sample size**, he does make the right methodological argument. Thirteen subjects sounds dismissible, but this was a crossover: each subject performed all three conditions, so there is no between-subject variability across conditions. At any given sample size a crossover has considerably more power, and 13 subjects in a crossover is typically better than 40 split across three groups. He still declines to call it conclusive proof, only that it should be taken seriously.

**Context from other studies.** At least five other studies in trained individuals have examined swelling in the days after a workout. **Two** found measurements back at baseline by 24 hours; **three** found some swelling still present at 48–72 hours. Dhim's handling of the divergence is good: the studies are not identical and there are several possible explanations, but crucially **none of the five compared different set numbers**, so none of them supports the specific claim that higher set numbers produce more prolonged swelling. That is what made the new study necessary.

## The argument that would survive even a positive finding

The strongest section of the video is the one arguing that *even if* the new study had found greater and more persistent swelling from higher sets, it would not follow that swelling confounds the sets–hypertrophy literature.

Every swelling study, including the new one, measures the response to **a single workout**. The studies feeding the meta-regression had subjects train for weeks, with size measured at the end of the whole program. The bridge between those two is the **repeated bout effect**: as training continues, the body accrues adaptations that reduce damage, fatigue and swelling.

The obvious objection — these swelling studies used trained subjects, who already have those adaptations — misses that accustomedness is exercise-specific. Trained lifters get unusually sore from a new exercise or a new set of variables, and that soreness diminishes as the weeks pass. The subjects in a single-workout study are trained in general but unaccustomed to that particular session.

Two supporting studies:

- **Lau et al.:** untrained subjects performed **10 sets of 6 maximal eccentric curls** on a dynamometer, where every rep is performed at the subject's moment-in-time eccentric 1RM. Substantial damage and swelling persisted for days, with some measures not back to baseline **7 days** later. Repeating the session **4 weeks later**, damage markers including swelling were substantially reduced, and there was **no swelling at 48–72 hours**.
- **Margaritelis et al.:** untrained subjects performed **5 sets of 15 maximal eccentric leg extensions once a week for 10 weeks**. The first session produced considerable damage and fatigue, with many measures unrecovered at 5 days. As the weeks passed there were **progressive** reductions in all damage markers and faster performance recovery. Swelling was not measured; Dhim infers it would have followed the same pattern, and labels that as inference.

The second study is the one that matters for the specific counter-claim, circulating on social media, that these adaptations are largely exhausted after the second workout. Ten weekly sessions with continuing improvement is direct evidence against that.

**Conclusion as stated:** it is very unlikely that swelling is meaningfully contributing to the observed sets–hypertrophy relationship, so higher sets likely do genuinely produce more hypertrophy. He explicitly notes that "very unlikely to contribute meaningfully" is weaker than "contributes zero", and that he cannot assert the stronger claim with confidence. That distinction is worth preserving.

## The strength mismatch

In the same meta-regression, **strength appeared to be maximised at merely 5 sets per muscle group per week** — a sharp contrast to the hypertrophy curve.

The argument built on this: if higher sets genuinely added contractile units, strength should keep rising too; since it does not, hypertrophy must also plateau around 5 sets, and the extra "growth" is swelling or sarcoplasmic hypertrophy.

Dhim's rebuttal, drawn from a Stronger by Science article by Greg Nuckols and presented as his interpretation of it:

- The **strength analysis contained nearly double the studies** of the hypertrophy analysis.
- There were overlapping studies, but **many non-overlapping ones**, and much of the non-overlapping strength data came from **untrained subjects showing large strength gains at low volumes** — expected, given learning effects and neural adaptations.
- The cleaner test is to restrict to **studies on trained individuals that took related strength and size measurements** (quad growth alongside back squat 1RM, for example). Restricted that way, **higher sets generally produce both greater size and greater strength.**

This is a re-analysis argument rather than new data, and it depends on the reader accepting the restriction as principled rather than convenient. The rationale — that untrained low-volume strength gains are dominated by neural learning and say little about contractile tissue — is sound.

## What he actually recommends

He is emphatic that "higher sets tend to be better on average" does not translate into "train with very high sets", and says people repeatedly make that inference for him. The qualifiers:

- **Diminishing returns are visible in the curve.** Respectable gains are available at lower volumes, which he calls good news for anyone not trying to maximise everything.
- **Credible intervals widen markedly beyond ~25 weekly sets per muscle**, because fewer studies go there. Moderate-to-high beats low with reasonable certainty; the gap between very high and high-ish may shrink as data accumulate.
- **Average study duration was ~10 weeks.** A moderator analysis showed an even stronger sets–hypertrophy relationship in longer studies — which, since swelling responses diminish over weeks, is further evidence against the swelling explanation. But what happens over years is unknown, and one possibility is that moderate volumes eventually arrive at the same place.
- **Many constituent studies used incomplete programs**, training only one or a few muscle groups. Some high-set studies did train a larger proportion of the body, so he does not claim full-body very-high-volume training is impossible — only that the evidence base is skewed.
- **These are averages.** Some individuals may genuinely do as well or better on lower volumes.

**The recommendation:** if you are already progressing well, change nothing. Otherwise, unless you are convinced you respond better to lower volumes, train as much as you feel you can handle while still exerting high effort on each set, and adjust as you go — try less if you want to test whether progress holds, try more as the repeated bout effect improves your recovery. Tolerance can differ between muscle groups. If you have never tried very high volumes and want to, do it as **muscle group specialisation** — one or two muscles — and **ease in by progressively adding sets across weeks**, which is, as he notes, structurally similar to the Enes protocol.

## Relevance to the volume ceiling dispute

For the library's purposes, this video does not raise or lower a ceiling — it removes one of the two main reasons for doubting that the ceiling is high. The specific contribution is narrow and should be recorded narrowly: **swelling, measured after a single workout in trained men, does not differ between 7, 14 and 21 sets and is gone by 24 hours.** Everything else in the video is context around that one finding, plus a re-analysis argument about the strength curve that originates with Nuckols rather than with new data.

**Overreach flag:** the conclusion that higher sets "genuinely" produce more hypertrophy rests on a single crossover study with 13 subjects, reported without effect sizes or power discussion, applied to a question about multi-week training programs it did not study. Dhim hedges the wording carefully, but the hedge is doing more work than the evidence.
