# Shrinking Rest Intervals Week by Week Is a Legitimate Way to Overload — Just Not a Better One

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Decrease Rest Between Sets (Overtime) For More Hypertrophy and Strength?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5GpunWbrzMk)) · 2022-01-27 · 10 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Two studies by Souza-Junior and colleagues compared holding rest at **2 minutes** against gradually cutting it from **2 minutes to 30 seconds** over 6–8 weeks in trained men. Neither found a statistically significant difference in 1RM squat, 1RM bench, or thigh and arm cross-sectional area.
- The volume-load data is the interesting part. The constant-rest groups overloaded by **adding load**; the decreasing-rest groups overloaded by **cutting rest**, with load roughly flat (and in one case dropping in weeks 3–4). Same outcomes either way.
- So **decreasing rest is a usable progression variable** — not superior, not inferior, over the short term.
- Both studies are small (**n≈21–22 total, ~11 per group**), 8 weeks, in trained men aged roughly 20–22, and both come from the same research group. This is a null result, not a demonstrated equivalence.
- He explicitly refuses to read the numerically-better percentages in study two as a real effect, because they were not statistically significant and the samples are too small to trust the point estimates. That restraint is the right call.

## The two studies
**Study one.** 21 men with at least a year of training experience. For the first two weeks both groups trained identically — 6 sessions per week, 3 sets per exercise at a 10–12RM load, 2 minutes of rest. For the remaining six weeks both moved to 4 sets per exercise at an **8–10RM load**, still 6 sessions per week. The constant group kept resting 2 minutes. The decreasing group walked their rest down from 2 minutes to 30 seconds across those six weeks. Both groups adjusted load whenever needed to stay inside 8–10 reps.

At eight weeks: no statistically significant between-group difference in squat or bench 1RM, and none in thigh or arm cross-sectional area.

**Study two.** 22 men, same entry criterion, 8 weeks, 6 sessions per week, 4 sets per exercise at an 8–10RM load. Constant group at 2 minutes throughout; decreasing group held 2 minutes for two weeks then tapered weekly to 30 seconds. The wrinkle: **all subjects supplemented creatine** — 20 g/day loading for seven days, then 5 g/day maintenance. Again, no statistically significant differences in squat or bench 1RM or in thigh and arm cross-sectional area.

## The volume-load reading, which is the actual finding
Because sets per exercise were fixed at four, changes in volume load in study one reduce cleanly to changes in load. The constant-rest group's volume load climbed steadily on both squat and bench across the six weeks — they were adding weight inside the 8–10 rep window. The decreasing-rest group's volume load barely moved — they were not adding weight; they were removing rest.

Study two shows the same pattern with a sharper edge. The decreasing-rest group's bench volume load **dropped sharply in weeks 3 and 4**, meaning they had to reduce load to hold 8–10 reps as rest shrank, then stabilised. Squat volume load stayed fairly flat with slight late reductions.

His conclusion: progressively overloading by cutting rest — **even when that forces a load reduction** — produced the same strength and size outcomes as progressively overloading by adding load. That is a genuinely useful reframe of what counts as progression.

## Is 2 minutes the right comparator?
The obvious objection is that the control condition might be a straw man. He addresses it directly. From his rest-interval guide: on compound lifts, 2+ minutes appears better for hypertrophy than shorter rests, and for strength in trained lifters 2+ minutes may beat shorter durations. Beyond two minutes, the evidence thins:

- **Ahtiainen 2005** found 2-minute rest produced similar quadriceps cross-sectional area gains to 5-minute rest — but he flags that the 2-minute group performed roughly **one extra set per exercise on average**, which confounds it.
- **Willardson & Burkett 2008** found 2 minutes produced similar squat 1RM gains to 4 minutes with everything else equal.

So 2 minutes is probably a fair control, but on thin evidence. He says so.

## What we still don't know
- Both studies ran **8 weeks**. Rest cannot keep shrinking indefinitely — 30 seconds is already short. What happens when someone simply sits at 30 seconds for months afterwards is untested.
- Nobody has tested **alternating** blocks of rest-reduction and load-increase over a long training career, which is the way most people would actually use this.
- Subjects were trained men averaging 20–22 years old. Nothing here speaks to women, older lifters, or beginners.

**Flagged:** the framing that cutting rest is "no less effective" than adding load rests on two null results from what appears to be the same research group, with about 11 subjects per group — underpowered enough that a real difference could easily hide. He acknowledges the sample-size problem for the percentages that favoured the decreasing-rest group, but the same logic undercuts the headline equivalence claim just as hard.
