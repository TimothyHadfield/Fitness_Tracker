# The Case Against "Trained Lifters Stop Growing"

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "\"You CANNOT Gain Much More Muscle\" (16 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yEulhkqn9SE)) · 2022-08-29 · 17 min
**Topic:** Evidence & method

## Bottom line
- This is a **rebuttal video**, not a video about genetic ceilings. Its target is Counts et al. 2017, a review arguing that upper- and lower-body muscle growth plateaus within roughly three months and that trained individuals are unlikely to see further appreciable growth.
- His method is to assemble study after study in **trained men with documented lifts** who nonetheless gained. Percentage gains in the 5–11% thickness range across 6–9 week studies are his repeated evidence that "no further appreciable growth" is false.
- He identifies specific design limitations in the studies behind the review: **no nutritional control**, **one exercise per muscle**, **no rep range variation**, **no progression in set number**. All four are things he holds are needed to sustain long-term growth.
- Trained lifters can add lean mass without a surplus, and often lose fat while doing so — provided protein sits at **1.6–2.2 g/kg**. For deliberate recomposition he cites a 2021 meta-analysis putting the point where lean mass gains cease at roughly a **500 kcal deficit**, so keep the deficit smaller.
- The video ends on a placebo study in national-level powerlifters (fake steroids, PRs across the board) used to argue that a plateau mindset is itself limiting — the weakest link in the chain.

## How he frames the question — and how it differs from the library
This is worth recording carefully because the framing is not the one our library uses.

**Our position:** the natural ceiling is expressed as **FFMI 22–24, maybe 25** — a trend rather than a hard law, since pre-steroid Mr. Americas and average college offensive linemen breach it.

**His position:** he never mentions FFMI, body composition indices, or any absolute ceiling number. He approaches the question entirely through **rate of gain over time in trained subjects** — does growth continue, at what percentage, and under what conditions. His argument is about *the shape of the curve*, not *where it terminates*.

The two framings are not in conflict but they answer different questions. He is refuting "growth stops at three months"; our material addresses "how much muscle can a natural lifter ultimately carry". He concedes the ceiling exists in passing — "the more and more trained you get the more difficult it is to attain body recomposition", "so long as you're not super advanced and really close to your genetic ceiling" — but he never attempts to locate it and offers no number.

**Practical consequence for the library:** this video supports the "trend not law" reading by demonstrating continued gains in demonstrably strong lifters, but it contributes **no independent evidence on where the ceiling sits**. It should not be filed as corroboration of the FFMI figures.

## The rebuttal to Counts et al.
He opens by restating criticisms from a previous video. The experiments the review leaned on:
- **Never mentioned calorie or protein intake** — both crucial for long-term growth.
- Trained each muscle with **only one exercise**, when exercise variety may be needed to sustain gains and grow more regions of a muscle.
- Used **no rep range variation**, when some evidence suggests variety supports long-term growth.
- Held **set numbers constant**, when increasing volume is an effective way through plateaus in trained individuals.

His stated motive for making this second video is that viewers took the previous one the wrong way — some concluding that PEDs are therefore necessary. That is a candid admission that his first attempt miscommunicated, and it is why this video is structured as reassurance.

## Trained lifters gain lean mass without a surplus
Four studies, all with subjects' actual lifts reported — his consistent way of proving "trained" means trained.

- **Cribb 2006 (Australia).** Trained men (bench ~84 kg, squat ~80 kg — he notes drily that they skipped leg day), 10 weeks, no increase in calories over pre-study intake, likely not in a surplus. Whey group gained lean mass while losing fat; casein group gained less lean mass with little fat change. Both hit 2–2.2 g/kg protein. **He immediately qualifies this**: it appears to show whey beats casein, but another study (Wilborn) found them similar, so the difference may just be small samples. He is citing it for the recomposition point, not the protein-type point.
- **Kreipke 2015 (USA).** 32 trained men (squat 120 kg, bench 102 kg, deadlift 158 kg), 4 weeks, multi-ingredient supplement vs placebo. Both gained lean mass with little fat change; the **placebo group tended to lose fat**. Calorie and protein intake unreported — he says so.
- **Colquhoun 2018 (USA).** 28 trained men (squat 138 kg, bench 103 kg, deadlift 163 kg), matched weekly sets at 3× vs 6× per week frequency. Both increased fat-free mass similarly (the 6× advantage non-significant); fat decreased in the 3× group. Calorie and protein intake again unreported.
- **Gomes 2019 (Brazil).** 23 trained men (bench 103 kg, squat 132 kg), bro split vs full body at matched weekly sets per exercise. Both increased lean mass similarly on roughly maintenance calories and 1.9 g/kg protein.

His recomposition protocol: keep the deficit **under 500 kcal** (Murphy 2021 meta-analysis places the lean-mass-gain cutoff around there) and protein at **1.6–2.2 g/kg**.

## Sleep
None of the studies behind Counts et al. mentioned sleep. His evidence is Wang 2018: 36 individuals classed as obese, calories cut 29–43%, one group sleeping normally (6–7 h) and one sleeping an hour less five nights a week (5–6 h, catching up to 7–8 h on the other two). Both lost similar total mass, but **the normal-sleep group lost mostly fat and the restricted group lost mostly muscle**. He states the limitations without prompting: **neither group lifted weights, and the subjects were untrained and obese**. He supplements with mechanistic data (sleep restriction lowers muscle protein synthesis, raises cortisol, lowers testosterone — Lamon 2021).

## Trained lifters still respond to training
Four more studies, chosen so that each also teaches a variable:
- **Brigatto 2019 (Brazil).** 20 trained men (bench 94 kg, squat 125 kg), 1× vs 2× weekly frequency at matched weekly sets. Both gained comparably: **5–11% thickness** in triceps, biceps, vastus lateralis and anterior quadriceps.
- **Schoenfeld 2015 (New York).** 18 men (bench ~101 kg, squat ~121 kg), 8 weeks, 8–12 reps to failure vs 25–35 reps to failure. Both gained comparably, **5–9% thickness**.
- **Schoenfeld 2016 (New York).** 13 men (bench 93 kg, squat 118 kg), 8 weeks, 3 min vs 1 min rest. Both gained, **but 3 minutes was better** — consistent with the wider literature for compound exercises, where 2.5–3 minutes tends to beat shorter rests. He uses this as his best illustration that *optimising variables* meaningfully changes outcomes in trained people.
- **Rauch 2020.** 17 men (squat 140 kg, bench 110 kg), 9 weeks, fixed programme vs auto-regulated exercise selection from a wide list, all other variables matched. **Lean body mass gains were greater in the auto-regulated group.** His interpretation — trained individuals have a good sense of what works for their anatomy — is plausible but speculative, and he says "presumably."

## The mindset section
The final segment is the weakest. Maganaris et al.: powerlifters (bench 189 kg, squat 257 kg, deadlift 260 kg) were given what they were told were fast-acting steroids but were actually placebo. All hit PRs — **+9.5 kg bench, +12.2 kg squat, +10.9 kg deadlift** — which the authors noted moved every subject from nationally to internationally ranked.

**This is a strength result, not a hypertrophy result, and it is acute.** No muscle was built in the time between the placebo and the lift. He uses it to argue that expecting a plateau is itself detrimental, which is a reasonable motivational point but does not bear on whether trained lifters can gain more muscle. It is the one place in the video where the evidence does not support the claim it is attached to.

## What to take from it
The core claim survives: trained men with 100 kg+ bench presses reliably added measurable muscle across 6–10 week studies, frequently without a surplus and sometimes while losing fat. That is a sound refutation of "no further appreciable growth." His per-study limitation flagging is thorough — unreported nutrition, small samples, untrained populations, non-significant differences all get called out as they come up.

Two soft spots. First, **percentage thickness gains over 8 weeks in already-trained-but-not-elite subjects do not establish anything about the far end of the curve**, which is the question most viewers actually have; the "you cannot gain much more muscle" title invites that question and the video answers a narrower one. Second, the placebo strength study is doing rhetorical rather than evidential work.
