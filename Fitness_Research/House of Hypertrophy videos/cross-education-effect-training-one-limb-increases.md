# Cross-Education: Training One Limb Makes the Other Stronger, But Barely Bigger

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Cross-Education Effect (Training One Limb Increases Strength + Size of the Opposite Limb)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v9HA9wCsubs)) · 2020-11-18 · 23 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Training one limb produces a real, sizeable strength gain in the **untrained** opposite limb: **+11.9%** across 31 studies in Manca's meta-analysis, against roughly **+27.6%** in the trained limb (his own hand-tally of 26 of those studies).
- The effect is **much larger for strength than for size**. Of 11 studies measuring size in the untrained limb, **4 found growth and 7 did not** — and 3 of those 7 failed to grow the *trained* limb either, so their protocols were simply inadequate.
- Where untrained-limb growth appeared it was **small and frequently not statistically significant**: e.g. +7.2% untrained vs +17.4% trained elbow flexor CSA in the one study that reported clean numbers.
- **Lower body transfers more than upper body** (+16.4% vs +9.4% untrained-limb strength) and **eccentric-only training transfers most** (+17.7% untrained-limb eccentric strength).
- The strongest practical application is **immobilisation**: across five casted-arm studies, trainers gained 4.6% strength and 0.2% size in the immobilised arm while controls **lost 13.3% strength and 3.8% size**.
- Mechanism for size is unresolved — the two candidates (systemic hormones, mirror activity) both have problems he identifies himself. Mechanism for strength is neural and better studied but still unsettled.

## What this topic is
[00:00] Cross-education is the increase in strength, and possibly size, of a limb you never trained, produced by training the opposite limb. Synonyms in the literature include contralateral effect, cross-limb transfer, interlimb transfer and bilateral transfer. It is worth noting the topic is essentially absent from the rest of this library, so the numbers below are the baseline.

## Strength transfer: the numbers
[01:00] **Manca's meta-analysis** pooled 31 studies with an average duration of about 5.5 weeks (range 3–12 weeks). Untrained-limb strength increased **11.9%**.

The meta-analysis did not report the trained limb, so he went and collected the figures himself from 26 of the 31 (five inaccessible or not reporting it), averaging **27.6%**. He flags clearly that this is a crude average and not how a meta-analysis would do it — which is exactly the right caveat, and he supplies it unprompted. So the headline framing is: **the untrained limb gets roughly 40% of the trained limb's strength gain.**

Sub-analyses (untrained-limb figures from the meta-analysis; trained-limb figures from his own tally):

| Condition | Trained limb | Untrained limb |
|---|---|---|
| Upper body | +24.3% | +9.4% |
| Lower body | +30.9% | +16.4% |
| Isometric | +22.9% | +8.2% |
| Concentric only | not available | +11.3% |
| Eccentric only | too variable to average (n=3) | +17.7% |
| Isotonic | +33.1% | +15.9% |

Two takeaways: **lower body transfers more than upper**, and **eccentric overload produces the largest relative cross-education effect**. The meta-analysis also found some correlation between trained- and untrained-limb strength gain, implying the transfer scales with how much you gained on the trained side.

He is upfront that the 31 studies are heterogeneous in body region and contraction mode, and that the study durations are short (3–12 weeks), so none of this speaks to long-term training.

## Size transfer: much weaker
[05:05] There is no meta-analysis on cross-education and muscle size, so he assembled the literature himself: **11 studies, 4 finding growth in the untrained limb, 7 not**. Of the 7 nulls, **3 failed to grow the trained limb either** — those protocols were too weak to test the question. The remaining 4 grew the trained limb and not the untrained one.

The four positive studies, with what they actually showed:

- **Brown** — 14 elderly untrained men, unilateral elbow flexion, 2–4 sets, 3×/week, 12 weeks. Trained elbow flexor CSA **+17.4%**, untrained **+7.2%**. The only study of the four with clean reported numbers.
- **Bezerra** — 10 untrained subjects, maximal isometric knee extension with the right leg, 3×/week, 6 weeks. Numbers not reported, only a graph. Right leg gained significant quadriceps CSA; the left leg showed a **slight increase that was not statistically significant**.
- **Magnus** — 23 untrained subjects, unilateral shoulder exercises, 3×/week, 4 weeks. Trained side gained significant supraspinatus and anterior deltoid thickness. Untrained side: **no supraspinatus growth**, and some anterior deltoid growth that was **not statistically significant**.
- **Beyer** — 17 untrained men, unilateral leg extension, 3 sets, 3×/week, 4 weeks. Trained leg significant on all measures. Untrained leg showed **non-significant** increases, mainly in vastus lateralis thickness and CSA.

That is the honest picture: three of his four "positive" studies are non-significant trends. He characterises the result as growth being possible but slight, and notes all four were on untrained subjects — his expectation is that trained lifters would see less or none. That is speculation, and he labels it.

## Immobilisation: the strongest use case
[08:39] The studies above all let subjects use the untrained limb in daily life. A review by Andrushko covers the five studies where the untrained arm was **casted**. All five had a control group with one arm immobilised and no training of either arm. He notes there are, as far as he knows, **no leg immobilisation studies**.

Averaged across the five:

| | Trained group | Control group |
|---|---|---|
| Trained / non-immobilised arm strength | +26% | −2.5% |
| Immobilised arm strength | **+4.6%** | **−13.3%** |
| Trained / non-immobilised arm size | +3.9% | −0.6% |
| Immobilised arm size | **+0.2%** | **−3.8%** |

So training the free arm did not merely slow the losses in the casted arm — it produced a small strength **gain** and held size essentially flat. This is the one place in the video where the effect is unambiguously practically useful. Subjects were untrained; he expects trained individuals would at best attenuate losses rather than gain.

## Mechanisms for the size effect (both weak)
[11:12] **Hypothesis 1: systemic hormones.** Unilateral training releases growth hormone, IGF-1 and testosterone into circulation, which could act on IGF-1 and androgen receptors in the untrained limb, initiating PI3K–Akt–mTOR. He kills it with two objections: the **single-joint** exercises used in cross-education studies barely spike anabolic hormones at all (multi-joint work does), and even where spikes occur there is little correlation between acute hormonal responses and hypertrophy — programmes designed to spike hormones do not outgrow programmes that don't. This is the same position he argues at length in his acute-hormone video, and it is consistent with the library's holding on acute spikes.

[14:44] **Hypothesis 2: mirror activity.** During unilateral training, the corresponding untrained muscle shows slight activation. Magnus measured the untrained (immobilised) biceps at **3.1%** of the trained biceps' activation, and the untrained triceps at **6.1%**. Andrushko found the untrained wrist flexors at **5.6% of MVC** during unilateral wrist flexor training. His own objection: this level of activation is trivial, comparable to what people experience in ordinary daily tasks, which do not cause hypertrophy.

He concludes both hypotheses have real limitations, neither can be ruled out, and much more research is needed. Given how small the size effect is in the first place, that is proportionate.

## Mechanisms for the strength effect (neural, better studied)
[16:47] Since untrained-limb growth is minimal, growth cannot explain an 11.9% strength gain — so the explanation has to be nervous-system adaptation. He spends several minutes on upper/lower motor neuron anatomy, the corticospinal tract and alpha motor neurons before arriving at three candidate mechanisms:

1. **Cross-hemisphere transfer of adaptation.** A review by Frazer identifies strong connections between the dorsal premotor cortex, supplementary motor area and cingulate cortex on one side and their homologues on the other. Adaptations occurring in the trained hemisphere could partially transfer across those connections to the hemisphere controlling the untrained limb.
2. **Corticospinal excitability or inhibition.** More excitatory input, or less inhibitory input, to corticospinal neurons means more signal reaching the untrained muscle. He reports that although some evidence finds increased excitability, Frazer's review notes **numerous papers finding no effect** — and that the evidence is actually stronger for **decreased inhibition** than increased excitation.
3. **Interhemispheric inhibition.** One study found untrained-limb strength gain accompanied by a **31% decrease** in inhibition from the trained hemisphere to the untrained one. He immediately pairs it with studies (including a second Manca paper) finding **no effect** on interhemispheric inhibition.

He closes by saying the strength mechanisms are better studied than the size mechanisms but still not settled.

## Where this lands against the library
Genuinely additive — nothing else in this library covers cross-education. Two things to carry forward:

- **The practical claim is narrow.** For a healthy lifter this is a curiosity, not a programming lever; you would not train one arm to grow the other. The real application is **injury and immobilisation**, where the casted-arm data is strong enough to act on.
- **It matters as a methodological constraint.** Within-subject unilateral study designs are common in hypertrophy research (both arm-hormone trials in his testosterone video use one). The finding that cross-education transfers substantially to **strength** but barely to **size** is precisely what makes such designs acceptable for hypertrophy outcomes and questionable for strength outcomes.

**Overreach flag:** the "4 of 11 studies found untrained-limb growth" headline is generous — three of those four reported non-significant increases, and he presents them as evidence for growth before conceding the amount "does not seem to be much."
