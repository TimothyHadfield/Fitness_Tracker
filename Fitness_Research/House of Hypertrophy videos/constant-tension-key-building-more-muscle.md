# Constant Tension: No Better Than Locking Out, Unless the Partial Is at Long Lengths

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Is Constant Tension the Key to Building More Muscle?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=14hQqiHoVoU)) · 2021-06-16 · 10 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- **Constant tension applied without shortening the range of motion is equivalent to normal reps.** Two Tanimoto studies found no difference in growth at any measured site.
- **Constant tension applied by cutting to a mid-range partial is unresolved.** Two well-matched studies directly contradict each other: Goto found the partial superior for triceps, Pinto found the full ROM superior for biceps.
- The single hypothesis that survives is that **constant tension may be worth something when the partial sits at long muscle lengths** — where a separate study (Pedrosa) found partials beating full ROM. He flags it as speculative.
- The hypoxia correlation in the Goto study is **correlational within one group**, and he says so.
- The whole video is about a bodybuilding technique whose rationale is metabolic stress, and his verdict is that the technique adds nothing on its own — **position in the range of motion, not continuity of tension, is what does any work here**.

## The technique and its premise

Constant tension means not locking out the joints, so the muscle stays loaded for the whole set. The rationale is that preventing the muscle from "breathing" reduces oxygen content and accumulates metabolites, which some research links to hypertrophy. He states from the start that this link is "far from crystal clear" — so the mechanism is on probation before the studies begin.

## Constant tension by shortening the range of motion

**Goto et al.** — 44 men with 1+ years of training, triceps skull crusher, 3 sets of 8 at 8RM relative to each group's own range, 3×/week for 8 weeks. Full group went from full extension to maximal flexion; partial group worked **45-90° of elbow flexion** only. **Triceps cross-sectional area at 60% of upper arm length increased significantly more in the partial group.**

The researchers also measured **hypoxia** (muscle oxygen decrease) after the constant-tension sets, and found a correlation: the subjects with the largest oxygen drops had the largest CSA increases. He states directly that **correlation does not prove causation**, which is the right guard on a finding that could easily have been sold as mechanism.

**Pinto et al.** — 30 untrained men, preacher curl, 2-4 sets of 8-20 reps, 2×/week for 10 weeks. Full group worked 0-130° of elbow flexion; partial group **50-100°**. **Biceps thickness favoured the full ROM.**

These two are close enough in design to be compared and they point in opposite directions. His conclusion is that together they **cannot tell us anything** about mid-range partials — which is more honest than picking the one that supports the technique.

## Constant tension without shortening the range of motion

The more interesting design question: can you keep tension continuous while still using a full range? Two studies by **Tanimoto et al.** say yes, and that it doesn't help.

**Study 1** — 24 untrained men, five machine exercises (squat, chest press, lat pulldown, ab crunch, back extension), 3 sets to failure each session.

- Normal group: full ROM, **85-90% 1RM**, 1s up / 1s down / **1s pause** — the pause lets the muscle unload. ~8 reps to failure.
- Constant tension group: no lockout on squat, chest press and pulldown, **50-60% 1RM**, 3s up / 3s down, **no pause**. Also ~8 reps to failure.

He pre-empts the obvious objection — different loads and tempos — by citing the two relevant literatures: **loads permitting 5-35 reps to failure are similarly effective**, and **rep durations from 0.5 to 8 seconds are similarly effective**. Given both, the confound is probably not material. That is a defensible move rather than a hand-wave, though it does mean the comparison relies on two prior bodies of evidence holding.

**Results:** statistically similar thickness increases at chest, biceps, triceps, abdominal, subscapular, quadriceps and hamstrings. Some percentages favoured the normal group, and he handles this well: percentages are averages, and since the results were statistically similar, **within-group variation exceeded between-group variation**. No claim either way.

**Study 2** — 16 untrained men, leg extension, 3 sets to failure, 3×/week for 12 weeks. Both groups worked 90-0° of knee flexion. The constant-tension group used 3s/3s with no pause at ~50-60% 1RM; the normal group used 80% 1RM with 1s/1s and a **1s pause** that unloads the quads. He explains why the leg extension is the right exercise for this test: **locking out the knee still leaves considerable quadriceps tension**, and at the bottom the quads stay contracted provided you don't pause — so the difference between conditions really is the pause. **Quadriceps cross-sectional area increases were statistically similar.**

## The long-length escape hatch

He ends by importing a third partial-ROM study, **Pedrosa et al.**, covered in a previous video: leg extension partials **at long muscle lengths** produced **greater** growth than a full ROM. His observation is that a long-length partial is, much of the time, technically a constant-tension technique. So a constant-tension partial might be superior — provided it sits at long lengths rather than in the mid-range.

**This is where the conclusion outruns the evidence, and he flags it himself.** The Pedrosa study was not designed as a constant-tension study; reclassifying it as one is a post-hoc reinterpretation. The more parsimonious reading of the whole video is that **muscle length is doing the work and continuous tension is incidental** — the Goto/Pinto conflict and the two null Tanimoto studies are exactly what you would expect if constant tension per se were inert. He says more research is needed before concluding, which is correct, but the framing leaves the technique more alive than his own data support.
