# A Bigger Pump Does Not Mean More Growth

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Pump Is the BEST Indicator for Hypertrophy (True or False?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PchAiNNkSh4)) · 2022-02-10 · 12 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Training in a way that maximises the pump does **not** reliably produce more hypertrophy. Several literatures point the way the pump hypothesis predicts; several others point the opposite way, and the ones that point the opposite way are the better-controlled comparisons.
- The cleanest disconfirmations: **30% 1RM produces a far bigger pump than 80% 1RM but similar growth**; **blood flow restriction** produces a huge pump but growth similar to heavy non-BFR training; **2.5+ min rest** beats 90 s or less on compound exercises despite giving a smaller pump.
- The evidence *for* the pump is entirely indirect — drop sets, short rests and constant-tension work all beat their comparators in some studies, but **none of those studies measured the pump**. The pump is his inferred explanation, not a measured variable.
- The only direct measurement is a correlational study: subjects with the largest acute quadriceps swelling after session one had the most growth at six weeks, but Spearman's rho was only **0.4–0.6**, which he states plainly.
- Practical residue: the pump is a useful *feedback signal* for which muscle region you are loading (a technique change that shifts the pump probably shifted the recruitment), and it is pleasant. It is not a target.

## What the pump actually is
During contraction, veins draining the muscle are compressed while arteries keep delivering blood, so plasma accumulates in and around the fibres. Metabolite accumulation — lactate among others — draws additional fluid into the fibre itself, intensifying it. Maximising it therefore requires high enough activation to meaningfully compress the veins, plus many or long contractions with minimal rest. The mechanistic case for it mattering is osmosensing: fibre swelling is detected as a threat to membrane integrity and may trigger anabolic and anti-catabolic signalling.

## The evidence that looks supportive
Three training literatures produce both a bigger pump and (sometimes) more growth, which invites the inference:

- **Drop sets.** Varović found 2–5 drop sets per session on the leg extension (each with two load reductions, 120 s between) produced greater quadriceps growth than 2–5 straight sets to failure. Fink found a drop set with three load reductions beat three straight sets on the triceps pushdown.
- **Short rest with isolation work.** Fink found 30 s between sets produced greater arm cross-sectional area gains than 3 min, across three biceps and three triceps exercises.
- **Constant tension.** Goto found partial-ROM skull crushers that avoided lockout produced greater triceps cross-sectional area than full-ROM skull crushers.

He is explicit each time that the pump was never measured in any of these, and that "perhaps the greater pump explains it" is speculation.

The one direct test is Hirono: leg extensions, 3×8 at 80% 1RM, 60 s rest, 3×/week for 6 weeks, with quadriceps thickness measured immediately and at 5, 10 and 15 min after the first session as a swelling proxy. Acute swelling at every time point correlated with six-week hypertrophy — but at rho 0.4–0.6, which he notes is not a strong relationship.

## The evidence that breaks it
**Load.** Jenkins showed 3 sets to failure at 30% 1RM produced greater acute quadriceps cross-sectional area than at 80% — a bigger pump, as expected from the higher rep count and metabolite accumulation. Yet a substantial body of work shows 30% and 80% 1RM to failure produce similar hypertrophy. Bigger pump, same growth.

**Rest intervals, compound exercises.** Several studies show 2.5 min or more between sets beats 90 s or less for hypertrophy, with all else equal. Longer rest almost certainly means a smaller pump. This directly contradicts the isolation-exercise short-rest result above, and he presents both.

**Constant tension, the other studies.** Pinto found no significant biceps thickness difference between partial-ROM constant-tension preacher curls and full-ROM. Two Tanimoto studies across a range of exercises found constant tension no better than locking out. Three studies against one.

**Blood flow restriction.** BFR with light loads to failure gives an enormous pump — arterial inflow partially restricted, venous return fully occluded. Lixandrão's meta-analysis found BFR at 20–40% 1RM produced hypertrophy similar to non-BFR at 70–90% 1RM. Far bigger pump, same result.

## Where he lands
Absence of a dose-response does not prove absence of causation — some small part of the swelling response could still contribute, and the osmosensing mechanism is real. But no one needs to train to maximise the pump in order to maximise growth. He keeps the pump as a **regional recruitment cue**: if changing pull-up technique to hold scapular retraction at the top produces a bigger upper-back pump, that is reasonable evidence the technique change recruited the upper back better.

**Flagged:** the supportive half of the argument rests entirely on studies where the pump was inferred rather than measured, and he says so each time rather than letting the inference stand. The one exception is that he does not resolve the conflict he creates himself — short rest wins for isolation work, long rest wins for compounds — beyond noting the isolation rest-interval literature "isn't crystal clear."
