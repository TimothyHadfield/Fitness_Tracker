# Volume, frequency, effort and training to failure

Twenty-eight notes on how much to do, how often to do it, and how hard — the largest and most
contested domain on this channel, spanning 2020 to 2026. This is the one place in the library where
the underlying trials genuinely disagree with each other rather than merely being underpowered, and
the summary below deliberately preserves that disagreement instead of averaging it away. Two of the
twenty-eight videos have **no retrievable reference list at all**, and both of them are load-bearing:
the 27-study HIT video and the minimum-dose video. Where a number below comes from one of those,
it is flagged, because you cannot check it.

---

## First: what does he count as a set?

The volume literature founders on definitions before it founders on data, so pin this down first.

His operational definition, stated identically across the channel, is a **hard set of 5–35 reps
taken within about 3 reps of failure** ([volume load](volume-load-not-drive-muscle-hypertrophy.md)),
narrowed to **8–12 reps within 3 of failure** in the 2021 guide because that is what the pooled
studies used ([2021 ultimate guide](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md)).
Warm-ups never count. A set of 30 reps at 6 RIR does not enter the tally at all, and that filter is
what makes "count your hard sets" a prescription rather than a tautology.

Until 2024 he counted **traditionally**: any exercise where the muscle is a prime mover counts a
full set for that muscle, so one bench press set is one chest set, one triceps set and one front
delt set ([2023 sets guide](many-sets-per-muscle-examine-science.md)). Stabiliser work counts
nothing. He was unusually good about reporting both figures — his splits video repeatedly shows that
"12 weekly sets" for arms in these designs is often **24 or more** once compounds are counted
([splits](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md)).

Then the 2024 Pelland meta-regression tested three counting schemes against the data and the
**fractional method won** — a direct set counts **1.0**, an indirect set **0.5**
([2024 meta-regression](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md)). Three sets of rows plus
three of curls is **4.5** biceps sets fractionally, **6** by the total method, **3** direct-only. The
evidence favouring fractional was "strongly or very strongly" in its favour, and the same result
held in the per-session analysis ([per-session](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md)) and the
frequency analysis ([frequency](often-should-train-muscle-new-study-epic.md)).

**This matters for every number in this file.** Almost all pre-2024 research counted the total way,
so historical set recommendations and the meta-regression's numbers are not on the same scale — and
his own **10–20** recommendation is delivered without saying which scale it uses. Since it postdates
the paper, 10–20 fractional sets is meaningfully more work than 10–20 counted the old way, and he
does not spell that out.

**What is not the metric: volume load.** Sets × reps × load is unrelated to hypertrophy, and the
[dedicated note](volume-load-not-drive-muscle-hypertrophy.md) breaks the link four ways. **30% 1RM**
produces far more volume load than 80% with equal growth (Lim, 21 untrained men, 10 weeks). High-rep
training adds volume load about **three times faster** — ~**31,000 kg** at 25–35 reps against
~**10,700 kg** at 8–12 over 8 weeks — with no growth advantage. Failure produces more volume load
than 1–3 RIR with equal growth (Santanielo, within-subject, ~5 years trained). And the decisive one:
**Lasevicius** had untrained men do unilateral leg extension at 30% 1RM, one leg **3 sets to
failure** and the other **~7 sets stopping ~14 reps short**, arranged so total volume load matched —
and the failure leg grew far more. If volume load were the driver, that result is impossible.

## How many sets per week?

There are two answers on this channel and he knows it: **the analysis says more is more; the
recommendation says 10–20**.

### The recommendation

**10–20 weekly sets per muscle**, for most people, after training quality is fixed — and he is
explicit about the order of operations: exercise selection first, then proximity to failure and rest
intervals, then volume as the last lever ([2024 meta-regression](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md)).
For beginners the older figure is **9–12** ([2023 sets guide](many-sets-per-muscle-examine-science.md)).
Adjust by feedback: burnt out with minimal progress, do less; recovering well, push past 20. If you
want to try very high volumes, do it as **muscle-group specialisation** on one or two muscles and
**add sets gradually** rather than jumping ([swelling study](many-sets-muscle-growth.md)).

He is candid that 10–20 is his prior and not the paper's output. **The curve does not bend at 20.**

### The analysis

**Schoenfeld/Ogborn/Krieger 2017** pooled **15 studies** and found roughly **0.37% more growth per
added weekly set**, with **9+** beating fewer than 9 and **10+** beating 5–9 beating under 5. He
accepts the direction and immediately narrows it on two grounds: mostly untrained subjects, and
almost no arm above **9–12** weekly sets. The defensible reading is "**9–12 beats less, in
beginners**" ([2021 ultimate guide](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md)).

**Pelland et al. 2024/2026** is the current anchor: **35 studies, 1,000+ subjects**. The best-fitting
model was a **square-root curve** — no plateau, no inverted U — climbing all the way to **43 weekly
sets per muscle**, with later sets worth roughly **one third** of the first ones
([2024 meta-regression](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md)). That one-third figure, not
the 43, is the practical finding: a respectable chunk of the available growth is bought by the first
few sets, which is why low-volume trainees should not despair.

**Enes 2023** is the single most aggressive trial: **31 trained men, 12 weeks**, back squat + leg
press + leg extension twice weekly, session one at **6–8 reps** and session two at **10–12**, all
sets at **2 RIR except the last set of each exercise** which went to failure, rest **≥2 minutes**,
protein **>1.6 g/kg**. Everyone began at **22 weekly quad sets**; one group held there, one added
**4 sets every two weeks to 42**, one added **6 to 52** ([Enes note](2-2x-more-muscle-new-study-surprising.md)).
Both progressing groups out-grew and out-gained the fixed group on vastus lateralis CSA, thickness
and squat 1RM. **52 weekly sets remains the highest weekly volume in the literature.**

His handling of the 42-vs-52 comparison is the careful passage: the six-set group's averages were
higher but its **confidence interval was much wider** and overlapped the four-set arm, which he
reads as greater between-subject variation. Superiority of 52 over 42 cannot be asserted. The
title's "2.2x more muscle" never appears in the spoken content.

### Why he overrides his own curve

Three reasons, and the first is the strongest thing in the domain.

**Incomplete programs.** A fair number of the pooled studies trained **one or a handful of muscle
groups**, so systemic fatigue and recovery demand do not resemble a real programme. He says
explicitly that he "probably should have done a better job at emphasizing" this in earlier videos
([per-session](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md)). It bites hardest on the headline result:
**Enes trained quads only**, and that is the very study he cites as showing benefit at very high
volume with long rests.

**Thin data at the top.** Credible intervals widen conspicuously past **25 weekly sets**, and he
volunteers knowledge of **two unpublished studies that failed to find benefit at very high volumes**
— disclosing evidence against the exciting result.

**Duration.** Average study length is **~10 weeks**. A moderator analysis found the volume–growth
relationship *stronger* in longer studies, but he raises the alternative he cannot exclude: over a
long enough horizon, moderate volume may arrive at the same endpoint as high.

His calibrated position, worth quoting exactly: **confident that moderate beats low even in a
complete programme, not confident that very high beats moderate.**

### Is there a plateau? Six trials, and the rest interval that may explain them

As of late 2021 he counted **exactly six trials** in trained lifters testing above 9–12 weekly sets,
and they split three-three ([2021 ultimate guide](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md)).

Favouring the highest volume, **all with short rests**:

- **Brigatto** — 27 men, ~3.5 years trained, **16 vs 24 vs 32** weekly sets, 2 non-consecutive days,
  8 weeks, 50% compound, **60 s rest**. Biceps, triceps and VL thickness best at **32**.
- **Radaelli** — 48 men with calisthenics experience, biceps at **6/18/30** and triceps at
  **9/27/45**, 3 days a week, **6 months** (much the longest), **90–120 s rest**. Highest volume won.
- **Schoenfeld 2019** — 34 men, ~4.4 years trained, biceps and triceps at **6/18/30**, quads at
  **9/27/45**, 3 days a week, 8 weeks, **90 s rest**. Highest volume won everywhere.

Finding a plateau, **all with long rests**:

- **Heaselgrave** — 49 men, ≥1 year trained, biceps at **9/18/27**, 6 weeks, **3 min rest**. Best at
  **18**. Design asymmetry he flags himself: the 9-set group did all nine in one session.
- **Ostrowski** — 35 men, 1–4 years trained, triceps at **7/14/28**, 2 days a week, 10 weeks,
  **3 min rest**. **14 was sufficient**; the extra 14 bought nothing.
- **Aube** — 35 men, ≥3 years trained, quads at **12/18/24**, 2 days a week, 8 weeks, 100% compound,
  **2 min rest**. Statistically similar across all three. **12 was sufficient.**

That is a **threefold disagreement in the same population**. His explanation is rest interval, and
the correlation is perfect: **60–90 s in every high-volume win, 2–3 min in every plateau**. The
mechanism is **Longo** — 3 leg-press sets at 3 min beat 3 sets at 1 min for quad CSA, but **4–5 sets
at 1 min matched 3 sets at 3 min**. Longer rests make each set count for more; shorter rests mean
you need more sets to buy the same growth. If that holds, the six trials are one finding in two
currencies: **30–45 weekly sets with short rests, 12–18 with long rests**
([2023 sets guide](many-sets-per-muscle-examine-science.md)).

He used this to attack the **2022 Baz-Valle review for pooling short- and long-rest studies**, which
had found biceps and quads flat between moderate and higher volumes with triceps favouring **>20**.

**He then retired the hypothesis.** The 2025 per-session meta-analysis ran inter-set rest as a
moderator and the volume–growth relationship **held across both shorter and longer rests**. He calls
it out and abandons his own position ([per-session](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md)). He
had already declined to bank the 2024 moderator that pointed his way, on the grounds that the
authors called it exploratory and that accepting it would force him to conclude that very high
volumes with one-minute rests are optimal ([2024 meta-regression](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md)).
The honest summary: the rest hypothesis was always a **post-hoc regrouping of six trials**, never a
tested comparison, and the one analysis that tested it did not support it.

The 2021 recommendation of **30–45 sets with short rests** should therefore not be quoted as live.
He himself hedged it into near-uselessness at the time — "probably not practical or sustainable for
most people" — a dose only defensible under conditions he advises against.

### Where the Barbalho retraction bears

The "less is more" leg of this dispute was largely two papers. **Barbalho's twin trials** — trained
men and untrained women, every muscle trained **once weekly** at **5, 10, 15 or 20 sets**, so weekly
sets equalled per-session sets — concluded **5–10 sufficed and 15–20 were slightly worse**.

**Both are retracted**, probably because the data were not legitimate, and many studies from that
group contain atypical and improbable data (per Vigotsky and Stronger by Science). He states this
plainly and **removes them from an argument they would have supported** — he was at that moment
building a case for a per-session ceiling ([splits](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md)).
**Do not quote the "5 beat 20" result as evidence anywhere.**

What survives against high volume is thinner than it looks: the three long-rest plateau trials
(**8–10 weeks, n=35–49**), the two German Volume Training trials, and the two unpublished nulls he
volunteers.

### German Volume Training: five sets beat ten, sort of

GVT is **10 sets of 10 at ~60% 1RM** on no more than two compound exercises per session with
**60–90 s rest**. Both trials that have tested it compared 10 sets against 5, and 5 won or tied on
every measure ([GVT](german-volume-training-actually-build-muscle-fast.md)).

**Amirthalingam 2017** — 19 men, ~4 years trained, 3×/week for 6 weeks. Bench and lat pulldown 1RM
rose significantly more in the **5-set** group; DEXA arm and trunk lean mass favoured 5 sets. But
**neither group showed significant ultrasound thickness gains anywhere**. **Hackett 2018** — 12 men,
≥1 year trained, 3×/week for **12 weeks** — bench 1RM rose only in the 5-set group, neither group
gained significant lean mass, and the **10-set group significantly lost lean leg mass between weeks
6 and 12**.

His structural criticism is better than the trials: at **60% 1RM** most people manage roughly **20
reps**, so a first set of 10 sits about **10 reps from failure**, far outside the useful range; and
**60–90 s rest on compounds** is short where he argues **2.5+ minutes** is better (Schoenfeld 2016,
16 men, 3 sets to failure at 8–12RM, 3×/week for 8 weeks — every measured site favoured **3 minutes
over 1 minute**). GVT is inefficient rather than useless.

**Read the trials for what they are.** Two studies totalling **31 subjects**, neither of which
detected significant hypertrophy in *either* group by its more reliable measure. That is a detection
failure, not a ranking. "5 beat 10" is a weaker finding than the note's title implies.

### The swelling objection, tested

The strongest remaining objection to the whole volume literature was that studies measuring size
**48–72 hours** after the last session were reading residual fluid from the high-volume groups as
growth. It is not an absurd charge — several papers document swelling detectable for days, and Enes
measured at **72 hours**.

**Alvarez et al.** is the first study ever to compare swelling *across set numbers*
([swelling](many-sets-muscle-growth.md)). **13 trained individuals, average 5.1 years' experience**,
within-subject **crossover**, three workouts a week apart in counterbalanced randomised order:
back squat, leg press and leg extension at **7, 14 or 21 total sets**, each exercise starting at
**90% of the subject's 10RM** targeting **8–10 reps**, **2 minutes** between sets and **3 minutes**
between exercises. Anterior thigh thickness and echo intensity at **two sites**, measured pre, post,
and at **24, 48 and 72 hours**.

Result: **no significant differences between set numbers, and swelling essentially resolved by 24
hours** in every condition.

His methodological defence of n=13 is right — in a crossover each subject serves in all three
conditions, so 13 in a crossover typically beats 40 split across three groups. But **he reports no
effect sizes, confidence intervals, point estimates or power discussion**. This is a null being used
to close an argument, and a null is exactly where those numbers matter most.

The stronger half of the video is the argument that would survive even a positive finding. Every
swelling study measures the response to **a single workout**, while the meta-regression's studies
ran for weeks; the bridge is the **repeated bout effect**. **Lau** had untrained subjects do
**10 sets of 6 maximal eccentric curls**, with some measures unrecovered at **7 days** — and on
repeating the session **4 weeks later** there was **no swelling at 48–72 hours**. **Margaritelis**
ran **5 sets of 15 maximal eccentric leg extensions weekly for 10 weeks**: unrecovered at 5 days
after session one, progressive reductions in every damage marker across the ten weeks — which
directly refutes the claim that these adaptations are exhausted after the second workout.

Context he supplies: at least **five other swelling studies in trained individuals** exist — **two**
found baseline by 24 hours, **three** found some swelling at 48–72 hours — but **none of the five
compared different set numbers**, which is what made the new study necessary.

### The strength mismatch

In the same meta-regression, **strength appeared maximised at merely ~5 weekly sets per muscle**,
and at **~2 sets per muscle per session** ([per-session](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md)) —
a sharp contrast to a hypertrophy curve still rising at 43. The argument built on this: if extra
sets added contractile tissue, strength should keep rising.

His rebuttal, drawn from Greg Nuckols at Stronger by Science ([swelling](many-sets-muscle-growth.md)):
the **strength analysis contained nearly double the studies**, with many non-overlapping ones on
**untrained subjects showing large strength gains at low volumes** — expected, given learning and
neural adaptation. Restrict to **trained subjects with matched size and strength measures** (quad
growth alongside squat 1RM) and higher sets produce both. This is a re-analysis argument, not new
data, and it depends on accepting the restriction as principled.

## How few sets can you get away with?

His minimum-effective figure is **2–4 working sets per muscle per week**, at **6–12 reps**, taken to
or very near failure — a defensible floor, not an optimum, which he still puts at **12–20**
([minimum dose](build-muscle-while-barely-training-using-science.md)). **This video has no
reference list; everything in it is unverifiable.**

The most useful thing in it is a correction. The widely quoted minimum-dose meta-analysis was
published with a **set-counting error**; using the fixed values obtained from one of the paper's own
authors, **fewer than five weekly sets per muscle yields 53% of the hypertrophy of more than ten**.
He says explicitly that other numbers from this analysis circulating in other videos are wrong.

> **Library conflict.** The minimalist material held elsewhere in this collection puts **1–4 weekly
> sets at ~64%** and **5–9 sets at ~84%** of maximum gains. Those are the *uncorrected* figures from
> the same underlying analysis. His **53%** comes from the author-issued correction — but it cannot
> be traced from the source, because the video cites nothing.

"Fewer than five" is vague, so he unpacks it: most pooled studies actually used **2–4** weekly sets,
and he leans to **four** as the practical floor. Then he states the limitation immediately — **most
of those studies used untrained subjects** — offers the one counterweight (a paper finding **three
weekly sets** produced measurable gains in trained men) and then the counterweight to that (older
untrained adults who **barely grew on two weekly quad sets but grew much better on eight**).

Two other minimum-dose rules. **Rest 2.5–3 minutes, not less** — so a time-efficient session is
mostly waiting, and his fix is structural rather than shorter rests: antagonist supersets, drop sets,
rest-pause and the 3/7 method, each of which he says roughly halves session time. And **failure
probably does matter at these set counts**, which reverses his usual position — see below.

The other end of the floor question is what minimal volume *maintains*. **Bickel 2011** had **70
previously untrained** people train quads three times weekly, three sets per exercise, for 16 weeks
— **27 weekly sets** — then split them for 32 weeks into detraining, **one-third volume** (3 sets
once weekly) or **one-ninth volume** (1 set once weekly). Older adults lost some size in **both**
maintenance groups; younger adults gained a little on one-third and **largely just maintained** on
one-ninth ([Mentzer](scientifically-dismantling-mike-mentzer-claims.md)). High volume built the
size; minimal volume could not add to it.

### One set versus three

The HIT case is that one set per exercise is enough. Pooled, it is not — but the gap is smaller than
the argument implies. A 2017 meta-analysis of **15 studies, 14 of them one set versus three sets per
exercise**, favoured higher volume for muscle mass with an **effect size difference of 0.24 —
small**; the strength equivalent (9 studies, 7 of them one versus three) was **0.18 — trivial**
([HIT](1-set-failure-hit-vs-high-volume.md)). Three sets does not give three times the gains. The
fair caveat in the other direction: those trials mostly ran **12 weeks or less**, and trivial-to-small
differences may compound over years. **This video has no reference list — all 27 studies are named
aloud only and none could be verified.**

### The variable that may beat any absolute number

Three findings point at **change relative to your own habitual volume** rather than any target.

**Scarpelli** — 16 men with ≥2 years' training, within-subject, unilateral leg extension and leg
press, 8 weeks. One leg trained at **20% more weekly sets than that individual had been doing**; the
other at a **prescribed ~22 weekly sets**. Vastus lateralis CSA gains were **significantly greater in
the +20% leg** — and for **8 of the 16**, the prescribed leg represented an increase of **30–120%**
over habit, a much bigger jump, and it still lost ([splits](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md)).

**Aube's responder split** — after finding no difference between 12, 18 and 24 weekly quad sets, the
researchers sorted by growth: high responders had raised their weekly sets by an average of **6 over
their pre-study habit**, moderate responders by **4**, low responders by **1–2**. This is a post-hoc
subgroup defined on the outcome, with a non-randomised exposure; he presents it as suggestive
without flagging that structure.

So his plateau prescription is a **20% bump, not a doubling**. And if you are currently progressing,
**nothing in any of these videos requires a change**, whatever your volume.

## How often should you train a muscle?

**At least twice a week** is his working answer, and he is careful that it is a hedge rather than a
finding ([per-session](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md)).

### With weekly sets equated, frequency does almost nothing

The Pelland frequency analysis (**35 studies, 1,000+ subjects**) found **no consistent or large
effect** ([frequency](often-should-train-muscle-new-study-epic.md)). **1× → 2×** produced a slight
numerical increase with credible intervals compatible with negligible effects; **beyond 2×,
nothing**; no meaningful difference between trained and untrained. He says outright he expected
higher frequency to win at high volumes and is **"genuinely surprised"** it is not clearly
supported. Nine biceps sets in one session versus three sets on each of three days grew the same,
despite the one-session version necessarily using much lighter loads on the later sets.

The standard rescue — that high frequency only wins at recoverable volumes — was **tested directly
and failed**. The researchers split studies by whether volumes were plausibly recoverable, defined
as **≤3 sets per session at 3×/week, ≤5 at 2×/week, ≤12 at 1×/week**, and neither bin showed a clear
benefit of higher frequency.

He then argues those thresholds are themselves too low, with five studies: trained individuals
recovering from **4 sets in 24 hours** across four consecutive daily sessions (House); the majority
recovered by **72 hours** after **7 sets** (McLester); trained subjects replicating a **12-set** chest
session at **72 hours** (Miranda); untrained subjects progressing loads on **6 quad sets 3×/week**
(Rønnestad); well-trained subjects progressing on **11 quad sets 2×/week** (Enes). Note the evidence
type: **performance recovery and load progression, not growth.**

### Below 12 weekly sets the picture is clean; above it, chaos

Schoenfeld's **2016** meta-analysis (7 studies) found 2× beat 1×, and it is still quoted. It was
superseded by the **2019** update — **13 studies, no frequency effect at all** — and the qualifier he
supplies and most summaries do not is that **11 of the 13 used ≤12 weekly sets per muscle**
([splits](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md)). Below that line, **bro splits are
not inferior**, and any architecture you will adhere to is fine. Three illustrations: **23 trained
men** (bench 103 kg, squat 132 kg) at **10 weekly sets** per muscle, bro split **1×/week** versus
full body **5×/week**, similar lean mass at 8 weeks (Gomes); untrained subjects doing **6 leg
extension sets in one day versus 2 across three days**, similar quad thickness at 11 weeks (Ochi);
**18 trained men** at 1× versus 2×, no difference at 6 weeks (Yue).

Above 12 weekly sets, five studies give four answers:

| Study | Subjects | Comparison | Weekly sets | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Evangelista 2021 | Untrained men | Bro split 2× vs full body 4× | 16/muscle (triceps 40–48 with compounds) | **No difference** at 8 wk |
| Brigatto 2019 | Trained men (bench 94 kg) | Split 1× vs 2× | 16/muscle (arms 24) | **No difference** at 8 wk |
| Zaroni 2019 | Trained men (bench 99 kg) | Bro split 1× vs full body 5× | 15/muscle (arms 30) | **Full body superior** at 8 wk |
| Bartolomei 2021 | Trained men (bench 96 kg) | Bro split 1× vs full body 3–4× | 15 chest, **20 quads** | **Bro split superior** for VL and traps at 10 wk |
| Lasevicius 2019 | Trained men (squat 161 kg) | Upper/lower 2× vs full body 3× | **36 quads**, 12 arms | **Upper/lower superior** at 10 wk |

His verdict — "utterly confusing" — is the honest one. Zaroni and Bartolomei compared bro splits to
full body in trained men benching around 95–100 kg and reached **opposite** conclusions on vastus
lateralis. And note the two results that break the per-session-ceiling story outright: Bartolomei's
winning bro-split group did **20 quadriceps sets in one session** with 2 min rest, and Lasevicius's
winning group was the *lower*-frequency one at **36 weekly quad sets**.

### Sets per session: is there a ceiling?

He kept weekly and per-session volume strictly separate, which is the most useful structural move in
the domain, and his answer moved twice.

**2021 position:** the ceiling exists but **scales with rest interval**, and should be *lower* with
long rests precisely because each long-rest set does more. With short rests he had direct evidence —
the winning groups in Brigatto, Radaelli and Schoenfeld were doing **15–16 sets per muscle per
session**. With long rests he had only acute proxies: **Ogasawara's** rat gastrocnemius study
(electrically induced sets of 10, 3 min rest) where protein synthesis rose to **10 sets** and was
*lower* at 20; and **Damas** in humans at ~2 min rest, where **12 quad sets** produced only slightly
more myofibrillar protein synthesis than 8. He states the limitations plainly: rats in one, acute
protein synthesis in both. Net: **a mechanistic estimate of ~10–12 sets with long rests, against a
measured 15–16 with short rests** ([2021 ultimate guide](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md)).

**2022 position:** he pushed it lower, to a tentative **6–10 sets per muscle per session** with 2.5–3
min rest, adding Krieger's paywalled analysis (plateau beyond **6–8 sets** with 2+ min rest) while
raising two confounds against it himself — untrained subjects cluster at the low-set end, and the
high-set end comes from studies with high *weekly* volumes, so the downturn could be under-recovery.
He labelled it speculation and immediately supplied the counterexample: **Bartolomei, 20 quad sets in
one session at 2 min rest, and the bro split still won**
([splits](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md)).

**2025 position:** the per-session meta-analysis (**35 studies, 1,000+ subjects**) found sets per
muscle per *session* rising with hypertrophy on a concave curve with **no clear plateau past 20
sets**, and the relationship held across both shorter and longer rests. The researchers' own
statistical criterion put the point of **greater uncertainty at 11 sets per session** and **31 sets
per week** ([per-session](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md)). He retires the rest-interval
interaction here.

He also checks the three analyses against each other, which is the right move: if growth plateaued at
a low per-session count, higher frequency would *have* to be superior for higher weekly volume to
help. It isn't, and there is no low plateau, so the picture is internally consistent.

**Bottom line on the per-session number: a bare figure quoted without its rest interval and its
counting method is close to meaningless.** A source quoting 5–6 is assuming long rests; ~10 is
reading the same acute data; 15 is describing short-rest work; 11 is the 2025 uncertainty threshold,
not a ceiling.

### Does frequency matter independently of volume?

Mostly no, and the popular mechanism for saying yes is broken.

**The MPS argument fails twice** ([MPS](high-frequency-training-more-frequent-muscle-protein.md)).
The time-course everyone quotes — trained lifters back to baseline by ~10 h, untrained still elevated
at **40–50 h** — is **mixed** muscle protein synthesis, which sums every protein in the fibre, and
mixed and **myofibrillar** MPS do not track each other: Burd found that 24 hours after four
leg-extension sets to failure, **mixed MPS was unchanged while myofibrillar MPS was significantly
elevated**. Second, damage contaminates the signal — **Damas 2016** found myofibrillar MPS
elevations correlate with hypertrophy **only after damage has been attenuated**. The cleanest
damage-free readings put myofibrillar MPS still elevated at **48 hours** (Damas, week 10) and
**72 hours** (Miller), with neither study measuring further out. Run the original argument on those
numbers and it argues for training a muscle every second or third day — the opposite of what it is
deployed for. He refuses that conclusion too, which is the right handling.

**Frequency is a container for volume.** When a 2018 strength meta-analysis pooled the studies that
did *not* equate weekly sets — so higher-frequency groups simply did more work — **strength gains
were greater with higher frequency** ([HIT](1-set-failure-hit-vs-high-volume.md)). The practical
value of frequency is that it lets you *add* weekly sets.

**Trials that look like frequency studies often are not.** The clearest case: **21 previously
untrained** subjects trained one side **2×/week at 6–8 weekly sets** and the other **3×/week at
9–12** — so the two variables moved together by design
([2× vs 3×](training-muscle-2x-vs-3x-week-growth.md)). Every muscle tended to grow more on the 3×
side, by **1–3%** for quads, hamstrings and biceps and **4–5%** for triceps, and he says plainly the
extra sets were probably the driver, since **9+ weekly sets beats fewer than 9** and the 3× condition
crossed that line while the 2× did not. The same study killed the fibre-type hypothesis in passing:
subjects with more fast-twitch fibres did **not** do better on lower frequency, and the slow-twitch
group grew just as well overall.

**The one clean within-subject frequency trial** is Neves: **24 trained men** (unilateral leg press
1RM averaging **203 kg**), one leg doing **9 sets in one session weekly**, the other **3 sets on
three days**, weekly sets identical at nine ([within-subject frequency](best-training-frequency.md)).
Neither strength nor quad CSA differed statistically, but effect sizes and confidence intervals
leaned toward **3×**, and the individual data showed some men clearly better at higher frequency
while one gained markedly more strength on his once-weekly leg. He discards the study's other group,
which equated **total tonnage** and thereby forced the 3× condition to hold back short of failure.
His headline is not "3× beats 1×" but that **frequency response varies between individuals**, and he
demotes two meta-analyses to get there — a strong methodological claim resting on one n=24 trial.

**Individual variation is his standing explanation for the null** ([frequency](often-should-train-muscle-new-study-epic.md)):
people differ in between-session recovery and in how well they dissipate fatigue within a session, so
pooling washes it out. He marks it as speculation and links a paper explaining that the existing
literature is not designed to detect individual response at all — which also makes the hypothesis
currently untestable.

### Do you need 48 hours between sessions?

No. Two trials have compared the same programme run **24 hours** apart against **48–72 hours**
([24-hour rest](long-should-wait-train-muscle-again.md)). **Yang 2018**: 30 active men, 3×/week for
**12 weeks** — 10RM strength across a range of exercises and lean mass increased similarly.
**Carvalho & Santos**: 21 **trained** men, 3×/week for **7 weeks** — neither group gained measurable
fat-free mass at all, with 1RM gains slightly and non-significantly favouring the 24-hour group,
which he declines to bank.

The frequency literature backs it up: **5×/week per muscle** implies well under 48 hours between
hits and comes back fine (Zaroni, where the full-body group's hypertrophy **tended to be superior**).
**Colquhoun** gave trained subjects **12 weekly sets each** of squat and bench split across **3 or 6
sessions**, so the six-times group repeatedly trained on 24 hours' recovery, and fat-free mass and
strength were comparable ([Mentzer](scientifically-dismantling-mike-mentzer-claims.md)).

**The honest gap is connective tissue.** How much resistance training damages tendons, how long
repair takes, and how training variables change that is not clear, so he refuses to answer whether
24-hour training raises the risk of accumulating tendon damage. His three partial mitigations —
high-frequency programmes usually run lower per-session set counts, tissue resilience varies, and
deloads may be what dissipates connective-tissue fatigue regardless — are offered instead of an
answer, not as one.

### And overtraining is hard to produce on purpose

**Of 22 studies that deliberately set out to induce overtraining or non-functional overreaching from
resistance training, 10 found nothing meeting the definition** (Grandou 2020)
([Mentzer](scientifically-dismantling-mike-mentzer-claims.md)). Ratamess 2003 is the worked example:
mostly full-body training **five days a week on 24 hours' recovery** with maximal strength tests
every Friday — bench and squat dropped in week two, then rebounded and **improved**.

Fatigue from multiple hard sets also resolves faster than assumed, and faster with training age.
**Six bench sets to momentary failure** left a neuromuscular fatigue marker **virtually fully
recovered at 48 hours** (Refalo 2023 — one marker, one paper, and he says so). **Margaritelis** is the
stronger one: **5 sets of 15 maximal eccentric contractions** caused soreness, damage and fatigue for
at least **5 days** after session one, and by **week 10** produced virtually none, with near-full
recovery inside **24 hours**. HIT's recovery model is calibrated to the untrained state — **recovery
capacity is an adaptation, not a constant**.

## How close to failure?

**Within 3 reps of failure, failure included if you want it.** That is his settled line, held since
2020 and unmoved by anything since ([2020](should-train-failure-muscle-growth.md),
[2021](training-failure-must-hypertrophy.md), [2023](must-train-failure-build-muscle.md),
[2024](hard-should-workout-new-study-epic.md), [2025](train-easier-size-strength-new-study-interesting.md)).
He never argues failure adds *nothing*; he argues the last **1–3 reps add nothing detectable in
growth outcomes**, which is a weaker and more defensible claim.

### The four longitudinal studies

- **Lacerda 2020** — **10 previously untrained men**, unilateral leg extension, **3–4 sets at 55–60%
  1RM**, 2–3×/week for **14 weeks**. One leg to failure every set; the other at **2 RIR on set one,
  1 RIR on set two, 0 RIR on the last**. Rectus femoris and vastus lateralis CSA similar, with raw
  percentages slightly favouring **non-failure**. Individual data: for RF, **4 responded better to
  non-failure, 3 to failure, 3 similarly**; for VL, **4 better to non-failure, 6 similarly**.
- **Nóbrega 2018** — **32 previously untrained men**, four leg conditions crossing load (**80% vs
  30% 1RM**) with failure vs non-failure, 3 sets, 2×/week, **12 weeks**. VL CSA similar across **all
  four**. Non-failure stopped where subjects *felt* about to fail, which the data suggest was **~3–1
  RIR**.
- **Sampson & Groeller 2016** — **28 previously untrained men**, elbow flexion, **4 sets at 85% 1RM**,
  3×/week for **12 weeks**. Failure group averaged **6 reps**; the two non-failure groups did **4
  reps (~2 RIR)** at different tempos. Elbow flexor CSA similar across all three. Confounds he
  states: differing tempos, and the non-failure groups did one extra set to failure weekly.
- **Santanielo 2020** — the one that carries the argument. **14 men, average 5.1 years' training**,
  74.4 kg bodyweight, leg pressing **237.5 kg** at baseline. Unilateral leg press and leg extension,
  2×/week for **10 weeks**, with **individualised set counts** (each subject's habitual weekly quad
  sets **+20%**). VL CSA similar; the non-failure leg averaged **1.6 fewer reps**, roughly 1–3 RIR.

### The best-designed study since

An **18-subject within-subject trial averaging 7.7 years' experience**, half of them competitive
powerlifters or bodybuilders ([2024 failure study](hard-should-workout-new-study-epic.md)). One leg
to momentary failure; the other at **2 RIR on leg press and 1 RIR on leg extension**. Twice weekly,
**8 weeks**, **10–17 weekly quad sets**, rest **4 minutes** on leg press and **2 minutes** on leg
extension, nutrition app-guided to **1% monthly weight gain**. Quadriceps growth **similar**.

Five features make it the strongest test to date: the most experienced sample yet; **verified RIR
accuracy** (subjects off by **less than one rep** on both 1-RIR and 3-RIR pre-tests); a within-subject
unilateral design; **individualised set counts** raised 20% halfway through; and published supervision
footage of both a failure set and a 2-RIR set.

The regional finding is where he kills his own neat story. **Vastus lateralis** slightly favoured the
failure leg and **rectus femoris** slightly favoured non-failure, and the researchers' explanation —
leg press always first, failure there blunting the leg-extension stimulus — is attractive. He then
notes **Santanielo used the same exercise order and found nothing of the sort**, and concludes the
small regional differences remain unexplained.

### The meta-regression that appeared to favour failure, dismantled

The **2023 proximity-to-failure meta-regression** found hypertrophy improving non-linearly as you
approach failure — real, and at the time of the video **a preprint**
([failure meta-regression](must-train-failure-build-muscle.md)). The parts nobody quoted:

- The benefit of nearing failure **shrank with heavier loads** — his mechanistic reading is that
  nearing failure matters because it forces full recruitment, and heavy loads already force it.
- It shrank with **higher frequency**, **more weekly sets per muscle**, and **longer study
  durations**, with the **null inside the confidence interval each time**. Once volume and frequency
  are decent, the data leave open that there is no failure benefit at all.
- **Studies using a strict definition** — momentary or concentric failure — showed **less** benefit
  than studies using vague "volitional failure". That is the wrong way round if failure is the driver.

He also works a case where the RIR estimates the regression runs on are demonstrably wrong.
**Martorelli**: two curl groups at 70% 1RM, one doing 3 sets to failure and the other 3 sets of
seven, baseline maximum at that load **10.37 reps**, so the estimate is **3.37 RIR**. Two errors
follow — cumulative fatigue means sets two and three were closer than 3.37, and the load was not
updated until **week five**, so as subjects got stronger the estimate became an increasing
underestimate. He concedes fairly that the regression's inclusion of cluster and rest-pause protocols,
which he would have excluded, did not swing the result.

The three meta-analyses preceding it (**Grgic 2022, Refalo 2023, Vieira 2021**) all found no
significant hypertrophy difference between failure and non-failure. He accepts the standard objection
— "non-failure" is a bucket containing 1 RIR and 10 RIR — which is why the meta-regression was the
right fix.

### Beyond 3 RIR: unresolved, not permitted

Only **four** longitudinal studies bear on whether 4, 5, 6+ RIR can also be optimal, and they
disagree ([beyond 3 RIR](leaving-more-3-reps-reserve-vs-training.md)).

- **Martorelli 2017** — **89 previously untrained women**, barbell curl, 2×/week for 10 weeks, 2 min
  rest. Failure (3 sets at 70% 1RM) vs **3 sets of 7** (~**5–6 RIR** falling to 4–2 by set three) vs
  **4 sets of 7**. Elbow flexor thickness appeared greatest in the failure group **regardless of the
  extra set**. The cleanest evidence against >3 RIR.
- **Lasevicius 2022** — **25 previously untrained men**, unilateral leg extension, 2×/week for
  8 weeks. High-load failure (3 sets at 80%, ~12 reps) matched high-load **~5 RIR** — but only when
  the 5-RIR leg did **5–6 sets** instead of three. **Low-load ~14 RIR** grew least and could not be
  rescued by extra sets at all.
- **Karsten 2021** — **18 men with 2–5 years' training**, 2×/week for 6 weeks. Failure (4×10, **2 min
  rest**) vs **8×5 at ~5 RIR** (**1 min rest**). Despite **four extra sets**, 5 RIR underperformed —
  directly contradicting Lasevicius. Three explanations he refuses to choose between: training status,
  the failure group's 30 s rest-pause top-up when a set fell short, and **the unequal rest intervals**,
  which he treats as strongest — the result may be a rest finding, not an RIR finding.
- **Carroll 2019** — **15 men averaging 7.7 years' training**, 3×/week for 10 weeks, RM loads vs
  **70–90% of those loads** (~2–6 RIR on two days, ~4–14 RIR on the third). VL CSA **favoured the
  lighter group** — but both groups also did **sprint training twice weekly**, so the added
  conditioning may have eaten the failure group's recovery.

His verdict is **agnostic, not permissive**: "we unfortunately cannot conclude if leaving more than
three repetitions in reserve can optimize muscle hypertrophy," so err toward **≤3 RIR**. The pattern
where higher RIR survives is that **extra sets compensate** — which means on a **set-equated** basis
failure probably beats 5 RIR.

He also **retracted his own claim** that velocity-based research licensed >3 RIR, binning a
6,000-word script after concluding the RIR figures in those studies required too much extrapolation.

**Where this clashes with the library.** Menno Henselmans goes furthest of anyone in the collection,
arguing **4–6 RIR can match 0 RIR** and rejecting the effective-reps model outright — that full
motor-unit recruitment arrives nearer **80% of 1RM** than 85%, that fibre-type activation climbs
gradually, and that EMG curves *taper* toward failure. House of Hypertrophy does **not** endorse
that. His equivalence is conditional on adding sets, and the trained-subject null he defends is
bounded at **3–1 RIR**. Where the two agree is that the discrete five-effective-reps cut-off is not
supported; where they part is the practical instruction.

### The 2025 varied-RIR trial

The first study to compare a **fixed** proximity to failure against a **varied** one
([varied RIR](train-easier-size-strength-new-study-interesting.md)). **31 trained individuals**,
upper/lower programme, two four-week blocks. Fixed group: **1 RIR on every set**. Varied group:
**4 → 3 → 2 → 1 RIR** across each block. Deloads between and after, at halved frequency and volume.

Vastus lateralis CSA and triceps thickness showed **no significant differences** (triceps leaning
toward varied), and bench and squat 1RM likewise (men's bench leaning toward fixed). He refuses to
read anything into either lean, and **does not change his recommendation** — default **0–2 RIR** for
hypertrophy.

The limitations are his own: **no true-failure group** (he infers it would not have won, and labels
the inference), only week four fully supervised, individual RIR accuracy varying a fair bit even
where averages were decent, accuracy tested on **two lifts only**, and a single small sample. Where
he *is* comfortable applying it: **strength-primary training** (proximity to failure matters much
less), **very high-volume phases or specialisation blocks** where easier sets make the volume
manageable, and **low-energy days** where the alternative is skipping.

### The exception: at very low volumes, failure probably does matter

The null holds at **6–19 weekly sets per muscle**. At roughly **2 weekly sets**, Giessing found
failure produced better muscle-mass gains than stopping just short — with imprecise
body-composition measures, which he flags ([2024 failure study](hard-should-workout-new-study-epic.md)).
The [minimum-dose video](build-muscle-while-barely-training-using-science.md) reverses his usual
position on exactly that ground: the four studies establishing the null all used **six or more**
weekly sets, and at 2–4 sets there may be nothing to spare.

Summarised in one line ([varied RIR](train-easier-size-strength-new-study-interesting.md)): **with
low volumes, failure may be superior; with moderate-to-high volumes — which are better for growth
anyway — 1–2 RIR is likely sufficient; for strength, well short of failure is fine.**

## Can anyone actually judge RIR?

Better than assumed under instruction, badly in habitual practice.

**Halperin's meta-analysis pooled 12 studies** and found an average **underestimation of about
1 rep** — far better than lifters assume — but with individuals out by **10–13 reps**
([RIR accuracy](become-laser-accurate-leaving-reps-reserve.md)). Loads spanned **50–90% 1RM**, mostly
compound lifts. Five meta-regressions give five usable rules:

1. **Do not pre-plan.** Estimates made *before* a set are worst; made *during* one, better; made
   *near failure*, best. Don't decide in advance that this is a set of eight.
2. **Heavier loads are much easier to judge.** Past roughly **12 reps** underestimation grows
   systematically — more room for error, and greater discomfort masking the read. Since **~30–85%
   1RM** is similarly effective, someone training by RIR should sit on the heavier half of that
   window.
3. **Later sets are slightly more accurate**, plausibly because earlier failure sets refreshed
   calibration. Practical implication he offers as an implication rather than a finding: **do your
   failure sets first**.
4. **Lower-body estimates are slightly worse.** Push a little harder than feels right on legs.
5. **Training years barely help.** His hypothesis — flagged as untested — is that what matters is
   experience **with failure specifically**, not experience in general.

Against that, **Hackett 2017** had **81 adults** stop at rep 10 on chest press (70% 1RM) and leg press
(80% 1RM) and estimate their remainder: they commonly **predicted 5–6 more when they could do 10**,
and training experience barely helped ([2020 failure](should-train-failure-muscle-growth.md)). The
2025 trial measured a **worst average error of −1.5 reps** on the 3-RIR squat estimate, improving to
about **−1** on retest ([varied RIR](train-easier-size-strength-new-study-interesting.md)).

The reconciliation is the useful part: **people judge proximity reasonably well when they are
actually trying to, and badly when they are just training.** Which makes occasional failure sets
**calibration rather than stimulus**, and means an unaudited "3 RIR" silently degrades into junk
volume for anyone who never checks. His definition throughout is **technical failure** — the point
where no further rep with correct execution is possible — which is what makes the question tractable
in the first place.

## What happens if you go past failure?

Nothing extra, on the outcome data and on the mechanism.

**Set structures compared head to head** ([set structures](normal-sets-vs-rest-pause-vs-drop.md)):
Enes 2021 assigned **28 trained men** (average squat 1RM **139 kg**) to normal (**4×12 at 70% 1RM**),
rest-pause (**10 reps at 75%, 20 s, 6 more**, three such sets) or drop sets (**10 at 75% dropping to
55%, 6 more**, three such sets), twice weekly for **8 weeks** with total reps equated and **2 min**
between sets. Strength gains tended to favour rest-pause; thigh thickness was similar proximally and
mid-thigh, with only rest-pause gaining distally. He then takes apart his own result: the
**individual data** show a few subjects in the drop and normal groups actually **lost** distal size,
dragging those averages down and manufacturing the rest-pause advantage. His alternative — that
people simply do not show identical regional hypertrophy from the same training — is well supported
by the within-group spread and is an underrated point in its own right.

**The mechanistic case against "effective reps" past failure.** Citing **Potvin & Fuglevand's**
motor-unit model — seven simulated units from lowest to highest threshold during a **50% MVC
isometric hold to failure over 100 seconds**, where unit 120 enters at **70 s**, peaks around **95 s**,
and at the actual moment of failure **no new units are recruited and no unit produces more force** —
he argues full recruitment and high motor-unit forces arrive **before** failure. A separate EMG study
puts maximal recruitment at **3–5 reps before failure** for the shoulder and trapezius musculature
([2021 failure](training-failure-must-hypertrophy.md)). Two objections to the fallback that going
past failure accumulates *more* effective reps: those extra reps occur under fatigue that itself
impairs recruitment — in very long sets central fatigue may drop high-threshold units out of
recruitment entirely — and there may be a **threshold** number of effective reps beyond which more
adds nothing, which all these structures already clear. **Flag: this is a model of isometric
contractions**, and he says so; the outcome data happen to agree, so the conclusion is not at risk,
but the mechanism is doing more work than a simulation should carry.

**Drop sets** ([drop sets](science-drop-sets-vs-normal-sets-hypertrophy.md)) split by exercise type
across the five studies that existed in 2021: **promising on isolation, no advantage on compounds**.
**Fink 2018** is the strongest — a **single** triceps pushdown drop set (12RM to failure then three
consecutive 20% reductions) twice weekly for 6 weeks **beat three normal sets** on triceps CSA in
16 men. **Ozaki 2018** — a single dumbbell curl drop set (80% dropping through 65/50/40/30%) matched
three normal sets in nine untrained men, at **~2 minutes against ~7**. **Varović** — 16 men with under
a year's training, leg extension, 3×/week for 8 weeks — found VL similar everywhere and rectus
femoris **greater for drop sets at 30% and 50% of muscle length** but similar at 70%: drop sets grew
*more regions*, not more muscle. Both compound studies (**Angleri 2017**, 16 men 4+ years trained,
12 weeks; **Enes 2021**) were null. He offers two mechanisms for the split — technique breakdown and
central fatigue — then undercuts his own explanation in one sentence, noting that compounds
generating more central fatigue is "not completely supported by the research itself." The pattern is
also **confounded with training status**: both compound studies used the most trained subjects.

**A meta-analysis of the drop-set literature, including protocols with multiple drops per set, still
found parity with normal sets.** So the honest verdict is **equal, and time-efficient** — not
superior.

**Rest-pause** has only two supportive studies and he neutralises both: the Enes protocol probably
included **forced repetitions** (six more reps at 75% 1RM twenty seconds after ten at the same load
is extremely hard, and assistance frequency was never reported), and the other study's normal-set
comparison group **appears not to have applied progressive overload**, so by the later weeks they
were far from failure ([set structures](normal-sets-vs-rest-pause-vs-drop.md)).

**Cluster sets** are the one structure he flags as possibly *worse*. A 2021 meta-analysis found
parity on both strength and size, but with only **five studies**, two using very low rep counts in the
normal-set group, one where the normal group did not train to failure, and two lasting five weeks. A
post-meta study (**22 trained individuals, 8 weeks, bench press**: normal 4×5 at 85% with 5 min rest
versus cluster 4×5 at 85% with **30 s between each rep**) found similar strength and lower-pec growth
but **superior middle and upper pec growth for normal sets**. His conditional verdict is right:
cluster designs that minimise proximity to failure probably compromise hypertrophy; designs that get
some reps close to failure are probably fine.

**Cheat reps** ([cheat reps](cheat-reps-vs-strict-reps.md)): the first hypertrophy trial found
**no difference**. **25 untrained subjects**, within-subject, unilateral dumbbell curls and
pushdowns, **4 sets of 8–12 to failure**, 2×/week for **8 weeks**, one arm swinging every rep and the
other strict. Elbow flexor and triceps thickness at the **55% and 65%** regions increased similarly —
momentum "neither helped nor hindered". He does not abandon strict form: safety is **untested**
(5 of 30 recruits dropped out, none for injury, which he correctly calls absence of evidence), and
the real cost is that **cheating makes a heavier load ambiguous between getting stronger and cheating
harder**. Note the scope: this tested cheating on *every* rep, **not** cheat reps as an intensity
technique past failure, which remains untested.

**And effort has a psychological component that is measurably large but almost entirely acute.**
Deceived into believing they had taken an immediate-acting anabolic steroid, **11 nationally ranked
powerlifters** every one hit a personal best, averaging **+9.5 kg bench, +12.2 kg squat, +10.9 kg
deadlift** — and when some were later told the pills had been placebos, **their strength fell back to
baseline** while the still-deceived roughly held ([belief](gain-strength-muscle-underrated.md)).
Ariel & Saville's **15 trained varsity athletes** gained **2 to 10 times** more during four weeks on
a placebo "Dianabol" tablet than in the preceding seven weeks. **Psyching up works acutely** —
preparatory arousal, motivational self-talk and first-person imagery all raised maximal force in a
review, with no clear winner. But the hypertrophy evidence is **one 10-week pilot**: 40 athletes on
an **identical** programme, half told it was individualised to their force-velocity profile, who
gained more squat strength and rectus femoris thickness. He calls out its small effects and
confounders himself, and there is **no research at all on psyching up and hypertrophy**.

## Where the trials disagree

Five genuine conflicts sit inside this set, and none of them should be averaged into a single number.

**Volume: 12–18 versus 30–45.** Six trials in trained lifters, split three-three, in the same
population over similar durations. The candidate explanations are **rest interval** (perfect
correlation, later disconfirmed by a moderator analysis), **frequency** (all three plateau trials
split their sets over only two days, so their top groups were doing **12–14 sets per session** and
may have been capped by session structure rather than weekly volume), **which muscle was measured**,
and **how sets were counted**. His own final position is that the rest hypothesis was a post-hoc
regrouping and that the one analysis testing it failed to support it — so the disagreement is
currently unexplained, not resolved.

**Frequency above 12 weekly sets.** Five studies, four answers, with Zaroni and Bartolomei directly
contradicting each other in near-identical populations on the same muscle. Below 12 sets the
literature is clean and null. His hypothesis — that individual differences only begin to matter above
12 weekly sets — is untested but explains the pattern better than anything else offered.

**Beyond 3 RIR.** Lasevicius says 5 RIR works when given 2–3 extra sets; Karsten says it does not, in
trained men, given four extra sets. One is confounded by **unequal rest** (2 min vs 1 min), the other
by **training status**; Carroll, the third result favouring higher RIR, is confounded by **concurrent
sprint training**. Nothing resolves, and he says so.

**Individual response.** Hammarström's data is reported two different ways in two notes on this
channel — as **13 of 34 clearly benefiting from 18 weekly sets and 3 from 6**
([2023 sets guide](many-sets-per-muscle-examine-science.md)), and as a **near 50-50 split**
([HIT](1-set-failure-hit-vs-high-volume.md)). Damas's within-subject data splits roughly
**32% / 32% / 36%**; Van Vossel's mostly follows the average, favouring the higher volume. All three
used **untrained** subjects and two also varied frequency alongside set count.

**Swelling.** Two studies in trained subjects find size back at baseline by **24 hours**; three find
some swelling at **48–72 hours**. None of the five compared set numbers, which is why Alvarez was
needed — and Alvarez is n=13 reported without effect sizes.

## Weak points

**Two of the 28 videos have no retrievable reference list**, and both carry load-bearing numbers.
[The 27-study HIT video](1-set-failure-hit-vs-high-volume.md) sources every one of its studies on
screen only — the 0.24 and 0.18 effect sizes, the 2021 failure meta-analysis, the motor-unit model,
the 50-50 individual-response split, all unverified. [The minimum-dose video](build-muscle-while-barely-training-using-science.md)
is the same, and its **53%** correction — a figure that directly contradicts the 64%/84% numbers held
elsewhere in this library — cannot be checked at all. Treat both as summaries of his argument, not as
traceable chains to the literature.

**The 10–20 recommendation is not what the analysis shows, and the counting scale is unstated.** He
says so — but a viewer skimming hears "the data says 10–20" when the curve does not bend there, and
nowhere does he note that 10–20 **fractional** sets is more work than 10–20 counted traditionally.

**The swelling null carries more weight than its reporting supports.** n=13, no effect sizes, no
confidence intervals, no power discussion, applied to a question about multi-week programmes it did
not study. The hedge is doing more work than the evidence.

**The 2021 30–45 set figure was never usable** and should not be quoted forward: three trials of
6–8 weeks (Radaelli's 6 months excepted) in samples of 27–48, ultrasound-measured, with no test of
whether that dose stays productive — then hedged as impractical. Add his own 2023 caveat: in the
short-rest trials **proximity to failure is hard to verify**, and one study running trained subjects
at **28 weekly isolation sets per arm muscle** (46 including compounds) with short rest had some
subjects growing well, a number gaining very little, and **some losing size**.

**The individual-differences case is built almost entirely on untrained subjects.** Van Vossel,
Damas and Hammarström are all untrained; two also confounded frequency with set count. Transfer to
trained lifters is asserted rather than shown, and none of these analyses separate true responder
heterogeneity from measurement error and within-subject noise — the standard trap in this
literature, which he addresses only in passing ([within-subject frequency](best-training-frequency.md)).

**The GVT conclusion outruns its data**, and **acute proxies do real work where he says they
cannot**: the 2021 long-rest per-session ceiling of 10–12 sets rests on **rat protein synthesis** and
**acute human MPS**. He uses Burd's myofibrillar MPS result as evidence for multiple sets in
[the Mentzer video](scientifically-dismantling-mike-mentzer-claims.md) having argued in
[the MPS video](high-frequency-training-more-frequent-muscle-protein.md) that acute protein synthesis
cannot support training recommendations — an inconsistency caveated more thinly when the evidence
points his way. Two other mechanisms are models, not measurements: the motor-unit argument against
training past failure ([set structures](normal-sets-vs-rest-pause-vs-drop.md),
[2021 failure](training-failure-must-hypertrophy.md)) simulates **isometric** contractions, and the
repeated-bout explanation for why 24-hour training works
([24-hour rest](long-should-wait-train-muscle-again.md)) was **not measured in either trial**.

**Two post-hoc responder analyses are used as prescriptions.** Aube's high/moderate/low split is
defined on the outcome with a non-randomised exposure, and he presents it as suggestive without
flagging that structure; the "+20% over habitual volume" advice rests on that plus one trial with
n=16.

**Errors and discrepancies visible in the notes themselves.** Enes 2021 is reported as **28 trained
men** in [the set-structures note](normal-sets-vs-rest-pause-vs-drop.md) and **18 men with 2+ years'
training** in [the drop-set note](science-drop-sets-vs-normal-sets-hypertrophy.md) — the same study,
