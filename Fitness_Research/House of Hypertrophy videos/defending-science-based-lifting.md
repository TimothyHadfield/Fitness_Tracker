# What the Backlash Against Science-Based Lifting Gets Right, and What It Gets Wrong

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Defending Science-Based Lifting" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=88bT1no39pM)) · 2025-11-19 · 13 min
**Topic:** Evidence & method

## Bottom line
- His central move is a **distinction between research and the communication of research**. Most of the backlash, he argues, lands on content creators who cite badly — not on the literature. He explicitly declines to defend the creators.
- He concedes a lot: small samples are endemic, individual studies should change nothing about your training, **85% of the 20 most-cited strength and conditioning meta-analyses contained at least one statistical error**, and he found an effect-size error himself in a 2022 protein meta-analysis.
- What he will not concede: that lifting research has produced nothing of value. His examples are all things the audience already believes — creatine works and is safe, BCAAs/leucine are not essential, protein timing is minor next to daily total, short rest is not required.
- On "science flip-flops": apparent contradictions usually have mundane explanations — evidence quality differs (rat vs human), methods and populations differ, and small-sample studies will disagree by chance even with identical designs. The fix is meta-analysis, which now exists for **volume, proximity to failure, frequency, rest intervals and rep tempo**.
- On "you're overcomplicating it": training hard and reading the literature are not mutually exclusive, and nothing in the research says the basics don't work. If it makes you overthink, stop reading — he treats this as a temperament difference, not a hierarchy.

## Part I: The criticisms are mostly aimed at creators, not research
[00:30] His worked example is the **stability claim** — the online position that maximising stability maximises hypertrophy. He grants the extreme case (benching on a Swiss ball is a bad idea) but points out that machines are generally *more* stable than free weights, and meta-analytic evidence finds similar hypertrophy between the two, including in trained lifters. The same applies to the "bench press is suboptimal" claim: four studies compare barbell bench to other exercises with growth measured, and chest growth is fairly similar. Overall triceps growth was better with skull crushers — unsurprisingly — but one paper found the **lateral head grew more with bench press**.

[02:02] This leads to his sharpest point. Claims like these usually rest on mechanistic data, and some creators argue mechanistic data outranks outcome data. That assumes we already know the mechanisms. The Roberts et al. review he quotes says the opposite: future work "will continue to confirm or refute which of the discussed mechanisms are obligatory for rather than coinciding with" hypertrophy. He allows the escape hatch — maybe outcome data fails to show the predicted effect for reason X — but insists those are testable hypotheses, and acting certain before testing is disingenuous. His second example is **isometrics**: widely implied to be inferior on no cited evidence, when most of the evidence does not find worse hypertrophy with them.

## Part II: What the research has already bought you
[03:34] The argument here is that the value of research is invisible once absorbed. Creatine's reputation rests on it having been extensively studied. BCAA and leucine supplementation being non-essential, protein timing mattering less than daily intake, short inter-set rest not being required — each is a belief the audience holds *because* of research. He then names questions only research can answer: what stimuli and sensors initiate hypertrophy, what mechanical tension versus metabolites versus damage each contribute (tension currently looks most potent), and whether human hyperplasia occurs.

[05:36] He grants the fair version of a common criticism — research reports averages — and answers that averages are not the ceiling. He points to a review on physiological differences between higher and lower responders, and says the best work on individual differences has appeared in roughly the last 18 months, including a within-participant paper on volume.

## Part III: Flip-flopping, and the honest version of it
[06:08] Four reasons studies conflict: unequal evidence quality, differing methods or populations, and — the one he stresses — **small samples producing results that miss the true effect through chance and low power**. Hence: don't weight individual studies heavily, and don't change your training on one. He closes the section with the position that shifting conclusions are a feature, not a defect: "the essence of science is to have no allegiance to a particular outcome."

## Part IV: The concession section
[08:12] This is the part that makes the video worth keeping. He hands over the finding that **85% of the 20 most highly cited strength and conditioning meta-analyses had at least one statistical error** — then immediately supplies the mitigations: errors vary in severity, all the analysed papers predate 2019 (one from 2004), and most were not hypertrophy-focused. He does not stop there. He describes catching an error himself in a 2022 protein meta-analysis that used **d_z effect sizes** — dividing by the pooled change-score SD rather than the pooled pre-intervention SD, which he argues is the wrong denominator for judging magnitude. He contacted researchers; **James Steele** is redoing the analysis.

[10:45] His case that quality is rising: recent hypertrophy meta-analyses he rates highly, a failure-versus-1–2-RIR study in highly trained lifters that found similar growth and published training footage, a range-of-motion study with **just under 300 subjects**, and a volume study in progress targeting **120**.

## Where the argument is weakest
Two soft spots. First, the "research has delivered" list is selected from findings that are both settled and comfortable — creatine, protein timing, rest intervals — which does not test the harder claim that research resolves the contested variables people actually argue about. Second, he asserts the recent meta-analyses were "done to what I think is an exceptional standard" without showing his work, and defers the defence to future videos; that is a quality judgement from a self-taught reviewer offered without the evidentiary support he demands of others in the same video. He is otherwise unusually rigorous here — quoting the review that undercuts confident mechanism claims, volunteering an error he found in a paper on his own side, and stating the limitations of the 85%-error finding rather than letting it stand as a rhetorical gift.
