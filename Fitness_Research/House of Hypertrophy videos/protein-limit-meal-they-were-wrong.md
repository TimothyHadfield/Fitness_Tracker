# The Per-Meal Protein Limit: 100 g in One Sitting Beats 25 g

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Protein Limit For a Meal (They Were WRONG)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uyFCg_0kJuA)) · 2024-04-01 · 11 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **His verdict: there is no meaningful per-meal protein ceiling.** The 20-25 g figure came from studies whose design guaranteed it, and the best current data show a **100 g dose producing a larger and longer-lasting muscle protein synthesis response than 25 g**.
- **Oxidation rates after 100 g were small** — directly against the "anything over 20-25 g is burned off" claim.
- The **20 g result was an artefact of small training doses**: one or two exercises for one muscle group. Train the full body and **40 g beats 20 g**.
- On actual growth over weeks, the picture is modest: **three protein servings a day is slightly better than two**, and **more than three adds nothing** (three studies).
- **Total daily protein is the variable that matters**, and training matters more than either.

## Where 20 g came from, and why it doesn't hold

The 2013 study that anchored the whole distribution debate ([Areta](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3650697/)) gave 24 trained men **80 g of whey over 12 hours** after leg extension training, split three ways: 10 g every 1.5 h, 20 g every 3 h, or 40 g twice 6 h apart. MPS was best with **20 g × 4**. A second study ([Witard](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24257722/)) found MPS more or less maximised at 20 g of whey after lower-body training, with 40 g adding very little.

He identifies two design features that predetermined the answer:

1. **The training stimulus was tiny.** One or two exercises for a single muscle group. A full-body session recruits far more tissue and should raise the dose needed. That prediction is confirmed: a 2016 study ([Macnaughton](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4985555/)) had subjects train the whole body, and **40 g increased MPS more than 20 g**.
2. **The total dose was unrealistically low.** 80 g across 12 hours is below what most lifters eat, and MPS was only measured to 5 hours post-exercise — a window too short to see whether a large dose simply works for longer.

## The 100 g study

The [Trommelen](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10772463/) paper fixes both problems. Active men trained the **full body**, then consumed **0 g, 25 g, or 100 g of milk protein**. MPS was **greater and longer-lasting after 100 g**, and **relative oxidation rates were small** — the excess was not simply burned.

He flags the one real limitation himself: milk protein is roughly **20% whey and 80% casein**, so digestion is slow, and a faster protein might behave differently. He notes the authors give reasons why they think the finding is not exclusive to slow-digesting protein, without pretending that settles it.

He also stops the inference at the right place: **this is still MPS, not hypertrophy.** He cites a review documenting established cases where MPS data fails to track actual muscle growth, and lists why acute MPS is a poor proxy for eating behaviour — the measurements are all post-training, people eat protein before training too, eat on rest days, and eat protein alongside other foods that alter digestion.

## What the growth data actually show

Three studies over weeks, all modest:

- **2020, untrained, 12 weeks** ([PMID 32321161](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32321161/)). Three roughly even protein meals versus a distribution where only two meals counted as sufficient servings. Strength and total lean soft tissue gains **favoured three servings, but the difference was far from large**. He notes both groups ate only **1.3-1.45 g/kg/day**, which he calls not terrible but low enough that a higher intake might change the result.
- **2021, 10 male rowers bulking, 8 weeks** ([PMID 33361498](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33361498/)). Three vs six servings a day with total protein and calories equated. **All measures, including fat-free mass, comparable.** He states the sample size plainly.
- **24 elite rugby players, 6 weeks** ([PMID 26132746](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26132746/)). Three daily shakes taken **with** meals (four sufficient servings) versus **between** meals (six sufficient servings). **Lean mass gains similar.**

Net: **three servings slightly better than two, nothing beyond three.** He describes this as "not plentiful or super strong data" and commits to updating if that changes — an accurate characterisation of a base of three small trials.

## How this sits against the library

Our library holds that **absorption is a red herring** (~95% of ingested protein is absorbed, with only ~10% of a meal's amino acids reaching muscle), that **~20 g gives a near-maximal MPS response**, and that the **ISSN gives 20-40 g per meal with no ceiling**. His verdict is consistent with the ISSN position and pushes past it:

- He never argues from absorption at all — he goes straight to oxidation, which is the correct target. The 100 g oxidation result is the direct evidence against the "wasted protein" claim.
- He accepts the **~20 g near-maximal MPS finding as real for the conditions tested**, then shows those conditions were unrepresentative. That is a refinement of the library position rather than a contradiction: 20 g is near-maximal *after one exercise for one muscle*, not after a full session.
- The **ISSN's no-ceiling stance is vindicated**, and the 100 g data suggest the upper bound of 40 g is conservative rather than a limit.
- **Practical upshot he lands on:** you can build well on fewer, larger meals. Daily total is what matters, and training matters more than distribution.

**Where the conclusion runs slightly ahead of the evidence:** the headline "they were wrong" rests on a single acute MPS study, and he himself argues two minutes later that MPS does not reliably predict hypertrophy. The long-term data he then presents show a *small* advantage to three servings over two — which is a mild argument *for* some distribution, not against it. He does not resolve that tension so much as let the acute result carry the title.
