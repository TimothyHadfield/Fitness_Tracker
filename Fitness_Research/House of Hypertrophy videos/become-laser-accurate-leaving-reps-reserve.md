# Getting Reps-in-Reserve Estimates Right: Five Things That Move Your Accuracy

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Become Laser Accurate When Leaving Reps in Reserve (New Research)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qmjr8kMaUH8)) · 2021-11-15 · 10 min
**Topic:** Effort & failure

## Bottom line
- Halperin and colleagues pooled **12 studies** on how well people estimate their proximity to failure. On average, subjects **underestimated by only about 1 rep** — but the spread was enormous, with some individuals out by **10–13 reps**.
- **Do not pre-plan.** Estimates made *before* a set are worse than estimates made *during* one, and estimates made closer to failure are the most accurate of all.
- **Heavier loads are much easier to judge.** Sets taken past ~12 reps produce systematically bigger underestimations.
- Accuracy improves in **later sets** of a session, and is slightly worse on **lower-body** than upper-body exercises.
- Years of training experience barely helped. Dhim's read is that what matters is experience *with failure specifically*, not experience in general — and he labels that as his inference, not the study's finding.

## What "reps in reserve" means here
[00:00] The video follows on from his conclusion that stopping 0–3 reps from failure produces similar hypertrophy to training to failure. Failure means **technical failure**: the point where no further rep with correct execution is possible. On a barbell curl, that's when you can no longer flex the elbows while keeping a fairly rigid posture. Once you start swinging the back or driving with the legs, the spinal erectors and legs are moving the weight, the elbow flexors are doing no additional work, and their recruitment may actually fall.

That definition matters because it makes the RIR question tractable — but it also creates the practical problem the video is about. If you think you left three in the tank and you actually left five or more, the prescription silently becomes junk volume.

## The evidence base
[02:31] The 12 pooled studies had mixed designs. Loads spanned **50–90% of 1RM**. Most used compound exercises, with back squat and bench press most common; leg press, chest press, trap bar deadlift and biceps and triceps isolation appeared in a few. The estimation protocols split three ways:

- Predict, *before* the set, how many reps you could do with a given load.
- Stop mid-set (commonly at rep 10 with 70% 1RM), report how many more you think you could get, then continue to failure.
- Report the moment you *feel* you've hit a given RIR (e.g. 3), then continue to failure.

In every design the subjects eventually went to failure, so the prediction could be checked against the actual number.

[04:03] **Result: a 1-rep average underestimation.** That is far better than most lifters assume. But the distribution is the story — a fair number were out by 1–3 reps, and some were out by 10–13.

## The five meta-regressions
[04:35] **Timing of the prediction.** Accuracy improved the closer to failure the estimate was made. Pre-set predictions were the worst; within-set predictions better; within-set predictions made near failure best. Practical translation: don't decide in advance that this will be a set of eight. Just rep until you genuinely feel you've reached the proximity you want, then stop.

[05:35] **Load.** Accuracy was much better with heavier loads and fewer reps. Past roughly 12 reps, underestimation grew. Two reasons: more reps means more room for error, and lighter high-rep sets produce greater perceived exertion and discomfort, which masks your read on how close failure actually is. Since roughly **30–85% of 1RM is similarly effective for hypertrophy**, someone who wants to train by RIR should sit on the heavier side of that window.

[06:05] **Set order.** Later sets were slightly more accurate. His explanation is mechanistic but plausible: subjects had already hit failure on earlier sets, which refreshed their calibration. The application is neat — if you mix failure sets and RIR sets in a session, **do the failure sets first**. Four sets of skull crushers: take the first two to failure, then use RIR on the last two.

[07:06] **Body region.** Lower-body estimates were slightly worse than upper-body. Push a little harder than feels right on legs.

[07:37] **Training status.** Barely mattered — years of experience did not meaningfully improve prediction. Dhim admits he isn't sure why, then offers a hypothesis he explicitly flags as untested: many "trained" subjects may have had little experience *training to failure*, and splitting the sample on that variable rather than on training years might reveal the effect. His recommendation follows from it: deliberately take a few sets per exercise to failure, or run occasional blocks where most sets go to failure, to keep your reference point calibrated.

## Reconciling this with the library
Our library records lifters given their habitual "10-rep" weight and told to go to failure hitting 16–20+ reps. That is not in conflict with a 1-rep average error here — it is a different question. Halperin's studies asked people to make a **deliberate estimate under instruction**, usually mid-set, often near failure. The 16–20+ rep finding is about **self-selected training loads in habitual practice**, where nobody is being asked to estimate anything. The pair together say something useful: people can judge proximity to failure reasonably well *when they are actually trying to*, and badly when they are just training as usual.

**Overreach flag:** the "do your failure sets first" recommendation rests on a slight between-set accuracy trend in a meta-regression, not on a study designed to test set order — he presents it as a possible implication rather than a finding, which is the right framing.
