# How Many Sets Per Week: Six Trials That Disagree, and the Rest Interval That May Explain Why

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The ULTIMATE Guide to How Many Sets Optimize Muscle Hypertrophy" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MW99hv5M1Yg)) · 2021-10-04 · 25 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- His answer is **not a single number**. It is conditional on rest interval: with **long rests (2+ min), 12–18 weekly sets per muscle** looks sufficient for trained lifters; with **short rests (≤90 s), 30–45 weekly sets** can be better. He argues these are the same finding expressed in different currencies, because longer rests make each set more effective.
- **Beginners: 9–12 weekly sets per muscle.** This is the only claim he treats as well supported, and it comes from the Schoenfeld/Ogborn/Krieger meta-analysis — whose included trials mostly used untrained subjects and mostly did not go above 9–12 sets, so it cannot speak to anything higher.
- In trained lifters there were, as of late 2021, **exactly six trials** testing more than 9–12 weekly sets. **Three favoured the highest volume (Brigatto, Radaelli, Schoenfeld 2019), three found a plateau at 12–18 (Heaselgrave, Ostrowski, Aube).** The three that favoured high volume all used 60–90 s rests; the three that found a plateau all used 2–3 min rests. That split is the spine of the whole video.
- He keeps **weekly and per-session volume strictly separate**, and this is the most useful thing in the video. His per-session ceiling is not a fixed number either: **15–16 sets per muscle per session was still productive with short rests**, while acute data (rat and human protein synthesis) hint at a ceiling of roughly **10–12 sets per session with long rests**. A per-session cap quoted without its rest interval is close to meaningless.
- If you are progressing, change nothing. If you have plateaued or are a low responder, **raise weekly sets by about 20%** — Scarpelli's within-subject trial found a 20% increase beat jumps of 30–120%.
- He is explicit that the rest-interval explanation is **his hypothesis**, not a finding, and that the per-session ceiling rests on **acute protein-synthesis measures in rats and humans, not growth**. Credit where due: he flags both limitations himself.

## What he counts as a set

Worth pinning down, because volume arguments usually founder on definitions. In this video a set is **8–12 reps taken to within 3 reps of failure** — the operational definition used by essentially every study he reviews. Any exercise where a muscle is a **prime mover** counts a full set for that muscle: one bench press set counts one for chest, one for triceps, one for front delts. Isolation work counts for its target. He does not use fractional counting, and he does not count a muscle acting as a stabiliser. Comparisons with other sources' set counts should be made with this in mind.

## The beginner case, and why the meta-analysis stops there

The 2017 Schoenfeld/Ogborn/Krieger dose-response meta-analysis pooled 15 studies and reported that each additional weekly set was associated with about a **0.37% greater gain in muscle size**, with 9+ weekly sets beating fewer than 9. He accepts the direction but immediately narrows the claim on two grounds: most included studies never had anyone perform more than 9–12 weekly sets, and most used previously untrained subjects. So the defensible reading is "**9–12 weekly sets beats less, in beginners**" — and he adds a mechanistic reason to stop there: beginners gain fastest but also sustain the most muscle damage and take longest to recover, so more may not be usable.

This is a good example of his habit of extracting a smaller claim than the headline supports. It is also the point where his number is *lower* than the meta-analysis's own trend line, which is not the direction a volume enthusiast would err in.

## The six trials in trained lifters

**The three that favoured more sets — all with short rests:**

- **Brigatto et al.** — 27 men, ~3.5 years training, 16 vs 24 vs 32 weekly sets per muscle, split over 2 non-consecutive days, 8 weeks, 50% compound / 50% isolation, **60 s rests**. Biceps, triceps and vastus lateralis thickness gains were greatest at 32 sets.
- **Radaelli et al.** — 48 men with calisthenics experience, biceps at 6 vs 18 vs 30 weekly sets and triceps at 9 vs 27 vs 45, 3 days a week, **6 months** (much the longest of the six), **90–120 s rests**. Biceps and triceps thickness favoured the highest volume.
- **Schoenfeld et al. (2019)** — 34 men, ~4.4 years training, biceps and triceps at 6 vs 18 vs 30 weekly sets and quads at 9 vs 27 vs 45, 3 days a week, 8 weeks, **90 s rests**. Biceps, triceps, rectus femoris and lateral thigh thickness all favoured the highest volume.

**The three that found a plateau — all with long rests:**

- **Heaselgrave et al.** — 49 men, ≥1 year training, biceps at 9 vs 18 vs 27 weekly sets, 6 weeks, **3 min rests**. Biceps thickness favoured **18**. Note the design asymmetry he points out: the 9-set group did all 9 in one session, while 18 and 27 were split over two days.
- **Ostrowski et al.** — 35 men, 1–4 years training, triceps at 7 vs 14 vs 28 weekly sets, 2 days a week, 10 weeks, **3 min rests**. 14 and 28 were similar, both beating 7. So **14 was sufficient**; the extra 14 bought nothing.
- **Aube et al.** — 35 men, ≥3 years training, quads at 12 vs 18 vs 24 weekly sets, 2 days a week, 8 weeks, 100% compound, **2 min rests**. Anterior thigh thickness at two sites was statistically similar across all three. **12 was sufficient.**

Read as a set, the three "more is better" trials point at 30–45 weekly sets and the three "plateau" trials at 12–18. That is a threefold disagreement in the same population, and it is exactly the disagreement this library has been carrying unresolved.

## His explanation: it is the rest interval

He offers three hypotheses and ranks them.

**Individual differences** he accepts as partly true but insufficient — it would be a coincidence for recruitment to sort responders so cleanly three-and-three.

**Rest intervals** he considers the strong hypothesis, and the correlation is clean: 60–90 s in all three high-volume-wins trials, 2–3 min in all three plateau trials. The mechanism comes from **Longo et al.**, who found that on the leg press at 80% 1RM to failure, **3 sets with 3 min rest** beat **3 sets with 1 min rest** for quadriceps cross-sectional area — but **4–5 sets with 1 min rest** matched **3 sets with 3 min rest**. Longer rests make each set count for more; shorter rests mean you need more sets to buy the same growth. He notes honestly that this was a compound exercise and that the rest-interval literature for isolation work is unclear.

If that holds, the six trials are not contradictory at all. They are measuring the same thing in two different units.

**Frequency** is the third hypothesis and it complicates the first two. All three plateau trials distributed weekly sets over only **two** days. Their highest groups (24–28 weekly sets) were therefore doing **12–14 sets per muscle per session**. If a per-session ceiling exists, those groups may have been capped by session structure rather than by weekly volume — and might have grown more had the same sets been spread over three or more days.

## Weekly volume versus the per-session ceiling

This is the section worth carrying into the rest of the library, because the per-session ceiling is quoted here in a form that explains why other sources quote it four different ways.

His position is that the ceiling **exists but scales with rest interval** — it should be lower with long rests, precisely because each long-rest set does more.

- **With short rests**, he has direct evidence: the high-volume groups in Brigatto, Radaelli and Schoenfeld were doing **15–16 sets per muscle per session** and grew the most. So 15–16 sets per session is *inside* the ceiling when rests are short.
- **With long rests**, there is no direct trial, and he says so. Two acute studies are all he has. **Ogasawara et al.** stimulated rat gastrocnemius with sets of 10 electrically induced contractions using 3 min rests: muscle protein synthesis rose progressively up to **10 sets per session** and was *lower* at 20 sets — putting a ceiling somewhere between 10 and 20. **Damas et al.**, in humans with ~2 min rests, found 12 quadriceps sets in a session produced only slightly more protein synthesis than 8 — suggesting **12 or more sets per session is near the ceiling**.

He states the limitations plainly: rats in one case, acute protein synthesis rather than long-term growth in both. The honest summary is that his long-rest per-session ceiling is a **mechanistic estimate of roughly 10–12 sets**, not a measured one, whereas the short-rest figure of 15–16 is grounded in actual thickness outcomes.

That reconciles a good deal. A source quoting "5–6 sets per session" is almost certainly assuming long rests and hard sets; one quoting "~10" is reading the same acute data he is; one quoting "15" is describing short-rest, high-frequency work. The number is not the disagreement. The training context is.

## If you have plateaued

He is unusually restrained here: for most viewers who are currently progressing, **nothing in this video requires a change**, whether their current volume is above, within or below his ranges.

For plateaued or low-responding lifters he offers two studies.

**Aube's post-hoc responder split.** After finding no statistical difference between 12, 18 and 24 weekly quad sets, the researchers sorted subjects by anterior thigh growth. High responders had raised their weekly sets by an average of **6 more** than they had been doing before the study; moderate responders by **4**; low responders by only **1–2**. Read as "increasing your sets helps", it is suggestive. It is worth flagging that this is a post-hoc subgroup analysis defined on the outcome, and the exposure — how far your assigned volume exceeded your habitual volume — is not randomised, so it is closer to a hypothesis generator than evidence. He presents it as suggestive but does not caveat the post-hoc structure.

**Scarpelli et al.** is the stronger design: 16 men with ≥2 years training, unilateral leg extension and leg press, **within-subject**. One leg trained at **20% more weekly sets than that individual had been doing before the study**; the other trained a **prescribed ~22 weekly sets**. After 8 weeks, vastus lateralis cross-sectional area increases were **significantly greater in the +20% leg**. The detail that makes it useful: for 8 of the 16 subjects, the prescribed leg represented an increase of **30–120%** over their habitual volume — a much bigger jump than 20% — and the +20% leg still won. Bigger increases were not better.

So his plateau prescription is a **20% bump**, not a doubling.

## Individual differences are real

He closes on **Hammarström et al.**: 16 men and 18 women trained unilateral leg press and leg extension at 7–10RM three times a week for 12 weeks, one leg with **1 set per exercise (6 weekly quad sets)** and the other with **3 sets per exercise (18 weekly sets)**. Most participants gained more quadriceps cross-sectional area on the 3-set leg, as the rest of the video predicts — but **a few did slightly better on the 1-set leg**. Within-subject design, so this is not noise between people; it is a genuine spread in dose-response.

## What this changes for the library

- The **43-sets-a-week** figure from Pelland's meta-regression postdates this video and is not addressed here. But his framework predicts that a pooled estimate stretching that high will be dominated by short-rest trials — which is testable against Pelland's included studies, and worth doing.
- The **"10 weekly sets is enough"** position and the **"5 beat 20"** finding are not among his six trials. He treats the trained-lifter evidence base as those six and no more, as of October 2021.
- The **per-session ceiling** should be recorded in this library **with its rest interval attached**. Quoting 5, 5–10, 6–8 or ~10 as bare numbers is what produced the apparent conflict.

## Where the conclusion outruns the evidence

The 30–45 weekly set recommendation rests on three trials of 6–8 weeks (Radaelli's 6 months excepted) in samples of 27–48, measuring thickness by ultrasound, with no trial having tested whether that volume remains productive beyond a couple of months — and he then hedges it into near-uselessness on practical grounds ("probably not practical or sustainable for most people"). If a dose is only defensible under conditions he himself advises against, it is closer to a mechanistic curiosity than a recommendation.
