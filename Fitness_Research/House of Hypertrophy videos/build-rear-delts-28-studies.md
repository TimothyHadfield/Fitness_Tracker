# Rear Delts: Ranking Pulls and Isolation Work From Leverage Data

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How to Build Your Rear Delts (28 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uyoFw2OKe7k)) · 2023-07-31 · 24 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- There are **no studies directly measuring rear delt hypertrophy across exercises**. Everything here is inferred from moment-arm (leverage) data, fine-wire and surface EMG, and the long-muscle-length literature. He says so, and the rankings are explicitly labelled speculation.
- **Wider-grip horizontal pulls rank highest among compounds**, because they involve shoulder *horizontal* extension, where the rear delts have the greatest leverage of any muscle. Narrow-grip pulls involve shoulder extension, where the **teres major** has the best leverage and the rear delts only decent leverage.
- Full compound ranking: wide-grip horizontal pulls > narrow-grip horizontal pulls > narrow-grip vertical pulls > wide-grip vertical pulls (which involve shoulder adduction, where rear delts have minimal to no leverage), with supinated vertical pulls placed somewhere between the last two.
- Isolation ranking (his hypothesis): **cable reverse flies and dumbbell "power" raises** > face pulls and machine reverse flies > dumbbell reverse flies — driven entirely by which variations load the rear delts at a **long** muscle length.
- Practical advice is unusually restrained: **recreational lifters can skip isolation work**, train the pulls they like, reassess rear delt development after several months to a year, and add isolation only if unsatisfied. Anyone maximising should add isolation on top of pulls.

## The tool: leverage, not EMG
[01:32] The analytical backbone is the moment arm — the perpendicular distance between a muscle's line of force and the joint it moves. The door-hinge analogy: pushing near the hinge is hard (short moment arm), pushing far from it is easy (long moment arm). Longer moment arm means more torque and more potential to generate the movement, and he cites research suggesting the nervous system preferentially recruits muscles with the best leverage for a given motion.

He then does something that separates this from the usual "science-based" exercise-ranking video: **he attacks his own tool.** Measuring moment arms is imprecise, methods vary between labs, and the two studies of latissimus leverage in shoulder extension disagree sharply — one finds strong leverage, one much weaker. Even when the second study's upper-lat region (its best-levered region) is plotted, the divergence remains. Neither study accounted for the lats wrapping around the posterior chest wall, so neither estimate is exact. And people are not anatomically identical, so leverage varies between individuals. He keeps the tool anyway, on the grounds that it still gives a quantifiable insight, but the caveats are stated before the conclusions are drawn.

## Horizontal pulls
[01:00] **Narrow grip** (close-grip barbell rows, seated rows, inverted rows, most dumbbell rows) means you are predominantly doing **shoulder extension**. The muscles with leverage for that motion, per the 2019 Australian review (Hik & Ackland): **teres major has the largest leverage throughout**; lats are disputed (see above); **rear delts have fairly good leverage** across three studies; teres minor consistently low. Horizontal pulls also involve scapular retraction, so traps and rhomboids join in, and the lats may contribute to retraction since most people have some lat–scapula attachment.

His summary: teres major and possibly lats take the bulk of the stimulus, but the rear delts are **respectably targeted**, and these movements contribute meaningfully to rear delt development.

[05:08] **Wide grip** (wide-grip barbell rows, seated rows, inverted rows, dumbbell rows) shifts you towards **shoulder horizontal extension**. Kuechle's classic analysis finds the **rear delts have the greatest leverage** for this motion, with infraspinatus and supraspinatus close behind, and teres major and lats far lower. Fine-wire EMG supports it: rear delt activity is slightly higher during horizontal extension motions than during shoulder extension and rowing motions, and other fine-wire analyses find relatively high rear delt recordings during horizontal extension.

He takes a detour to explain why fine-wire matters: the needle electrode minimises crosstalk from neighbouring muscles and follows the tissue as it moves, neither of which surface EMG does. He still flags a flaw — one of those studies did not appear to equate relative loading between exercises.

## Vertical pulls
[07:11] **Narrow grip** pull-ups and pulldowns are also predominantly shoulder extension, so the same leverage picture applies. The difference is range: vertical pulls span roughly 180° to 0° of shoulder angle, while horizontal pulls run about 75° to negative angles — meaning **horizontal pulls allow shoulder hyperextension**, which vertical pulls do not. The leverage data does not extend beyond 120°, but he argues this is not a serious problem because both narrow-grip variants are hardest below 120°, where the rear delts have solid leverage. Vertical pulls add scapular depression and downward rotation, recruiting other muscles.

[09:12] **Wide grip** vertical pulls shift towards **shoulder adduction**, where teres major, lats and subscapularis have the leverage and **most studies find the rear delts have minimal to none**. In practice, most people use a moderately wide grip — adduction in the scapular plane — where rear delt leverage estimates range from very small to fairly decent. A 2002 US surface EMG analysis found **narrow-grip pulldowns produced higher rear delt activity than wide-grip**, consistent with the leverage picture.

He handles this sEMG result well. He states he is highly critical of surface EMG and that there are cases where it fails to predict hypertrophy — then gives a case where it succeeded: sEMG predicted that toes-out calf raises bias the medial gastrocnemius and toes-in the lateral, and a long-term hypertrophy study subsequently found exactly that. His verdict here is that the pulldown finding is *probably* closer to correct because the leverage data agrees, which is the right epistemic weighting.

[12:18] He ranks **narrow-grip horizontal pulls above narrow-grip vertical pulls** despite both being shoulder extension, on the grounds that horizontal pulls reach hyperextension and thus bias the rear delts further. He acknowledges the obvious objection — vertical pulls involve more total ROM — and answers that greater range does not itself appear to be associated with more growth, which is where the muscle-length literature comes in later.

[13:19] **Supinated grip** (chin-ups, supinated pulldowns) is also predominantly shoulder extension. The 2002 sEMG study found supinated pulldowns produced *lower* rear delt activity than close-grip pulldowns, comparable to the wide-grip condition. He speculates heavy biceps involvement may reduce shoulder extensor activity, says plainly he does not know whether the finding is valid, and places supinated pulls either alongside or above the wide-grip variants depending on whether it is.

## Do you need isolation work?
[14:21] For a recreational lifter after notable rather than maximal improvement, training with some vertical and horizontal pulls may be sufficient even if they are not the top-ranked ones. His protocol: train the pulls you like, then after some months or a year evaluate your rear delt development, and add isolation work if you are not satisfied. For anyone maximising, add isolation on top.

The supporting logic is the **Kassiano** hip thrust study: 33 untrained women, three times a week for 10 weeks. One group trained leg press and stiff-legged deadlift; the other added a barbell hip thrust — which is not strictly an isolation exercise, but predominantly grows the glute max — and thus performed more total sets. **Glute max growth was superior in the group that added it.** He generalises that adding a targeted exercise is a good way to train a muscle more while keeping fatigue in check. Note that the added-exercise group also did more volume, so the study cannot separate "added a targeted exercise" from "did more sets" — he doesn't flag this.

[16:23] Two sEMG studies (Franke; Botton) find **machine reverse flies produce higher rear delt activity than seated rows**, even though the rows were performed such that shoulder horizontal extension occurred. He finds this genuinely puzzling — same shoulder motion, different result — and speculates the elbow motion in rows, and possible scapular retraction by some subjects, could explain it. He repeats the caution about sEMG.

## Ranking the isolation exercises
[17:55] Candidates all involving shoulder horizontal extension: machine, dumbbell and cable reverse flies; face pulls (technically compound); dumbbell "power" raises. His ranking, and the reasoning:

- **Bottom: dumbbell reverse flies** — not bad, but only most challenging at the *shortened* end position.
- **Middle: machine reverse flies and face pulls** — the machine provides resistance more evenly through the range. Face pulls are also hardest shortened, but add external rotation, in which the rear delts are also involved, so he treats the combination as possibly beneficial.
- **Top: cable reverse flies and dumbbell power raises** — both involve horizontal extension *and* attain a great stretch, being most challenging around the **lengthened** position.

[19:28] The entire ranking rests on the long-muscle-length literature, which he summarises: Maeo's seated vs lying leg curl (seated stretches the hamstrings more, grew them more); Maeo's overhead triceps extension vs pushdown (overhead stretches the long head more, grew the triceps more); Pedrosa's leg extension (a lengthened partial beat full ROM for rectus femoris and vastus lateralis); Kassiano's calf study (a stretched-position partial beat full ROM for medial and lateral gastrocnemius); and a conference abstract from Japan on a multi-hip machine, which he flags as **abstract only, not yet published**. He says more research is needed on whether long-length partials always beat full ROM.

[21:00] He addresses the counter-argument directly: some have argued rear delts specifically should *not* respond better to stretch, based on research into rear delt sarcomere operating lengths. His response is that the sarcomere-length literature has a range of limitations that make it unusable for deciding whether a given muscle benefits from stretch, and he links an article rather than resolving it. In the absence of other evidence, the rankings are his current speculation and he will update them if research changes.

## Postscript: seven segments
[22:33] The deltoid is conventionally three heads but research — documented as early as **1734** — supports **seven segments**, visible in lean individuals. Functional data suggests segment 1 is the front head, segments 2–3 the side head, and **segments 4–7 the rear head**. It is not known whether particular exercises preferentially grow particular segments, so he cannot be confident that his recommended exercises train all four rear segments.

**Overreach flag:** the entire exercise hierarchy is built from moment-arm modelling, EMG and analogy to the muscle-length literature in other muscles — **not one hypertrophy outcome study on rear delts exists**, and the stretch-based isolation ranking extrapolates from quadriceps, hamstring, triceps and calf data to a muscle whose sarcomere operating range he admits is contested.
