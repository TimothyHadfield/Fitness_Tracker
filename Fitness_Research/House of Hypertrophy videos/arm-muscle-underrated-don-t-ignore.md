# The Brachialis: What One Direct Study and a Lot of Indirect Data Can Tell You

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "This Arm Muscle is Underrated (Don't Ignore it)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wbNpypPuYLo)) · 2023-09-18 · 10 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- The brachialis is the **second largest elbow flexor** and not far behind the biceps. It sits beneath the biceps at the lower upper arm, so growing it pushes the biceps out and sharpens the biceps/triceps separation.
- **Exactly one study has measured brachialis hypertrophy directly.** O'Hagan and colleagues (1995) found underhand machine preacher curls grew the biceps by **8.3%** cross-sectional area and the brachialis by **37.6%**. Every other conclusion in this video is built on indirect data — pump, combined elbow-flexor measurements, moment arms and EMG.
- The brachialis is a pure elbow flexor with leverage close to the biceps throughout the range, so it is recruited well in **all** curl variations. It's not something you need a special exercise for.
- Curls that are hardest at **long muscle lengths** (preacher, face-away cable, certain machines) are ranked above curls hardest in the mid-to-shortened range — but **long-length partials on any curl may equalise them all**.
- **Grip:** neutral or overhand curls likely bias the brachialis relative to the biceps, because the biceps loses supination leverage while the brachialis's leverage is unchanged. This is a refinement, not a requirement.

## What the direct and indirect evidence says
[01:02] O'Hagan's classic Canadian analysis is the anchor and the only one of its kind: underhand machine preacher curls, biceps +8.3% CSA, brachialis +37.6% CSA. Dhim is upfront that this is a single study and that we cannot conclude the brachialis grows more than the biceps from curling in general.

[01:33] Two indirect lines fill the gap. A pump analysis on **dumbbell underhand preacher curls** found the brachialis pumped similarly to the biceps. He handles this carefully: the pump is fluid accumulation and is **not** a strong cause of growth, but within a given exercise, regions that pump most may end up growing most — so this hints at a comparable stimulus, somewhat against the O'Hagan result.

[02:03] A Spanish paper compared **dumbbell preacher curls to incline curls**, measuring elbow flexor growth across three regions. The measurement combined biceps and brachialis, so no separation is possible. Growth tended to favour preacher curls, and the gap was largest at the **70% region** — where, the authors noted, the brachialis is about **twice as thick** as at the 50% region. Dhim's inference is that a decent chunk of the preacher curl's advantage there may be brachialis, and he flags it as speculation.

[03:35] The mechanistic backing: the brachialis's **moment arm is close to the biceps' throughout elbow flexion**, and leverage is one factor the nervous system uses in deciding which muscles to recruit for a task (neuromechanical matching). So expect the brachialis to be trained effectively by every curl.

## Long muscle lengths, and the range-of-motion equaliser
[04:37] Preacher curls are hardest near the *start* of the curl, where the elbow flexors are long. Incline and standing curls are hardest at neutral-to-short lengths. Given the consistent finding that challenging muscles at long lengths produces more growth, he ranks long-length-biased curls above the rest — while explicitly saying standard and incline curls are not useless, since neutral-to-short training still produces measurable growth.

[05:38] Then the more interesting move. Three studies find **long-length partials produce more overall growth than a full range of motion**, and long-length partials beat short-length partials. A Japanese paper comparing **partial preacher curls at long versus short lengths** found elbow flexor growth superior across all three measured regions for the long-length condition. If that holds, then performing long-length partials on *any* curl challenges the brachialis at long lengths and renders all curl variations similarly effective for it.

Three practical routes: use long-length partials outright; use integrated partials (alternate a full rep with a long-length partial); or take a set of standard or incline curls to failure through a full range and then squeeze out extra partial reps at the long end.

## Grip
[08:40] The biceps has strong supination leverage, and it's greatest with an underhand grip — the best-controlled data shows higher biceps activity underhand than overhand. The brachialis's elbow flexion leverage is **unaffected by grip**. So with a neutral or overhand grip, biceps contribution falls while brachialis contribution stays put, raising its relative involvement; and the brachialis has been documented as better activated with an overhand grip. There is **no research comparing neutral to overhand** directly, so no ranking between those two.

His caveat is the right one: the only study that found large brachialis growth used an *underhand* grip. If you're not chasing the last ounce of brachialis mass, neutral and overhand curls aren't essential.

## Anatomical footnote
[09:41] The brachialis is traditionally described as single-headed, but cadaver work has found individuals with two, three, four or even five or more heads. In two-headed individuals the deep head appears to initiate elbow flexion and the superficial head to provide power once flexed — both are involved throughout, so none of the recommendations change.

**Overreach flag:** the exercise ranking rests on one 1995 hypertrophy study, a pump measure, and combined biceps-plus-brachialis measurements from which brachialis contribution is inferred rather than observed — Dhim says so himself at each step, but the video's confident framing ("the best brachialis exercises") outruns what a single direct study can support.
