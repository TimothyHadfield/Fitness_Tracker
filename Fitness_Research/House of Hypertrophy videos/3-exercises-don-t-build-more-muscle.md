# The Big Three Aren't Required: Machines and Body Weight Match Them

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "These 3 Exercises DON'T Build More Muscle" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yPGXO_y0cis)) · 2023-08-21 · 8 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- Four trials comparing free weights (squat, bench) against machine or body-weight alternatives found **no hypertrophy advantage for the free-weight version** in any measured muscle.
- The strongest of them, a 2023 Spanish trial in **36 trained men over 8 weeks**, found similar quadriceps, pec major and rectus abdominis growth between barbell and machine programmes.
- **No study in the video actually tested the deadlift.** He says so, then argues from mechanism that it can't be essential — the one place here where he leaves outcome data behind.
- Verdict is permissive, not prescriptive: the Big Three are highly effective, just not irreplaceable.

## The comparative trials
**Hernández-Belmonte et al. (2023, Spain)** assigned 36 trained men to free weight (barbell back squat, bench press, prone row, seated overhead press) or machine (hex-bar squat, chest press, seated row, machine shoulder press), 3 sets per exercise, 3 sessions a week, 8 weeks. Reps were performed with maximal intended velocity and each set stopped at a **20% velocity loss**, which prior work suggests puts subjects at roughly 50% of their maximum reps per set. Quadriceps growth at both the 30% and 60% measurement sites, plus pec major and rectus abdominis, increased similarly in both groups.

**Aerenhouts & D'Hondt (2020, Belgium)** had untrained men train free-weight-only (barbell squat, dumbbell bench, deadlift), machine-only, or combined. Chest, thigh and upper-arm circumference gains were similar across all three. He flags that circumference is an imprecise growth measure, and that none of the sites is really deadlift-relevant.

**Schwanbeck et al. (2020, Canada)** put 36 trained individuals on free weight (barbell squat, bench, stiff-leg deadlift) or machine/Smith alternatives; quadriceps and biceps growth were similar. Again he notes the measurements don't map onto the deadlift or bench.

For body weight, he reuses the two push-up trials: **Kikuchi & Nakazato (2017)**, 18 trained men, high reps to failure, chest and triceps growth comparable between bench press and push-ups; and **Kotarsky et al. (2018)**, 23 men, progressive push-up variations versus bench press, where raw percentages favoured push-ups. He is careful here — none of the Kotarsky gains reached significance in a four-week study, and he raises the possibility that the push-up group simply had better genetics.

## The deadlift gap
No study compared the deadlift's hypertrophy against alternatives. His argument is mechanistic: muscle damage and metabolite accumulation are not primary growth drivers, mechanical tension is, and tension can be produced by barbells, dumbbells, machines or body weight provided the movement recruits the target muscle and sets are taken to or near failure. That reasoning is consistent with the rest of the library, but it is a mechanism argument standing in for a trial that doesn't exist — worth flagging.

He adds that the deadlift's reputation for outsized fatigue is **not supported** by the current evidence, then immediately notes that evidence is only two studies. If it did turn out to be more taxing, he'd suggest trap-bar variations, leg press or hip thrusts.
