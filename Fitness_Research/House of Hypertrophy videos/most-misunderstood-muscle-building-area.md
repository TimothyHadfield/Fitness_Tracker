# CNS Fatigue: What It Actually Is, and Which Common Beliefs Survive

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Most MISUNDERSTOOD Muscle Building Area" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wbt7iV-xYWA)) · 2023-08-14 · 22 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **CNS fatigue is a specific, measurable thing**: reduced electrical drive from brain and spinal cord to the muscle, lowering fibre recruitment and firing rates. It is quantified via **voluntary activation**, or inferred from strength loss in an untrained contralateral muscle. It is not "feeling tired," and it is not peripheral fatigue.
- **Heavier loads do not produce more CNS fatigue — lighter loads do.** Longer contraction durations produce more CNS fatigue immediately after, and 40% loads to failure produced more than 80% loads, including contralateral strength loss that 80% loads did not cause.
- **The deadlift is probably not uniquely fatiguing.** Two of three studies fail to support the belief; the third found the deadlift recovered slowest but "far from substantially" so.
- **Training to failure produces more overall fatigue, but the only study measuring CNS fatigue specifically found it similar** to non-failure training. The extra fatigue appears to be peripheral and damage-related.
- **Hand grip strength is not a valid CNS fatigue indicator** — at least two studies find no correlation. Vertical jump is his speculative preference, but he says no study has properly tested it.
- **Recovery capacity is trainable.** The repeated bout effect is dramatic: in a Greek study, week-1 sessions caused 5+ days of damage and strength loss; by week 10 the same session was almost fully recovered from within 1–2 days.

## Definitions first
[01:02] He starts by fixing the term, and the fix does most of the work in the video. Muscles produce force because of electrical signals from the CNS. **CNS fatigue is a reduction in that drive**, cutting the number of fibres activated and their firing rates. Two ways to measure it:

1. **Voluntary activation** — how much of a muscle's force-producing potential the nervous system can access. A drop indicates CNS fatigue.
2. **Contralateral inference** — if you trained only your right biceps and your left biceps has also lost strength, CNS fatigue is implicated.

And the essential distinction: **peripheral fatigue** — changes inside the muscle that lower force — also reduces performance and is not CNS fatigue. Most of the video's corrections come from people conflating the two.

## Is CNS fatigue always present days after lifting?
[02:34] No. **Two studies on trained individuals** using a range of compound exercises, mainly four sets each in the 4–6 rep range with heavier loads, found **no CNS fatigue at 24 or 48 hours**.

But **several studies do find it**. Two involving 10 sets of back squats in the 5–10 rep range (trained subjects, one with a subset of untrained) found CNS fatigue persisting **48–72 hours**. High-volume maximal eccentric training produces CNS fatigue lasting several days.

Since **volume** is the variable that differs most between the null and positive findings, he infers more sets means more CNS fatigue — and says outright he expects this to be unsurprising to viewers.

## Do heavier loads produce more CNS fatigue?
[03:36] This is the section that flips a common belief, and the evidence is reasonably tight.

**Immediately after training:**
- Dumbbell curls at 5, 10 or 20 reps to or near failure: **no significant difference** in CNS fatigue.
- Two leg extension studies: CNS fatigue immediately after tended to be **greater with a 40% load than an 80% load**.
- The pattern appears to be **contraction duration**, not load: more CNS fatigue after holding a 20% contraction for 13 minutes than an 80% contraction for 30 seconds; more after a 30% hand-muscle contraction lasting ~400 s than after 45%, 60% and 75% contractions of shorter duration. Longer-duration aerobic work also generates more CNS fatigue immediately after.

**In the days after** (overall fatigue, not CNS-specific):
- One study found progressively worse recovery of muscle performance moving from ~4 to ~12 reps, with incrementally more damage at higher reps.
- Leg extension to failure at 30% recovered more slowly than at 80%.
- The clinching design: subjects trained one leg at 40% or 80% to failure a week apart. Recovery was slower after the 40% load, **and strength in the opposite leg dropped immediately and at 24 hours after the 40% session but not after the 80% session** — the contralateral inference for CNS fatigue.

[06:10] Then the part that matters practically: **does it matter?** If you want to minimise neuromuscular fatigue while maximising stimulus, lower reps do it better. But your body adapts to fatigue, and if the fatigue from high-rep work were counterproductive it should show up as compromised growth — and it doesn't. Reps up to 35 produce similar hypertrophy. He backs it with a New York study: trained subjects, three sets per exercise, three times a week for 8 weeks (~18 weekly sets for quads directly and 18 indirectly for biceps and triceps), one group to failure at **8–12 reps** and one at **25–35 reps**. Elbow flexor, triceps and quad growth were **similar**. So the fatigue is real and doesn't cost you gains.

## Is the deadlift uniquely fatiguing?
[07:14] Three studies:

1. **10 trained men**, deadlift and squat on separate occasions, **8 sets of 2 at 95% 1RM**. CNS fatigue similar at 5 and 30 minutes after. His own two criticisms: voluntary activation was measured in the **quads**, which is defensible since CNS fatigue is systemic but he'd have preferred an overlapping muscle like glute max; and it was measured only at 5 and 30 minutes, not in the days after.
2. **12 trained men**, deadlift, back squat and bench press on separate occasions, **4 sets to failure at 80% 1RM**. Muscle swelling, stiffness, soreness, performance and damage in the days after were **comparable across all three**. Doesn't measure CNS fatigue directly, but tests the popular assumption.
3. **10 recreationally trained men** performed a workout including the deadlift for two sets per exercise, then repeated it at 24 and 48 hours. The **deadlift had the lowest performance recovery** — but "far from substantially" so.

Verdict: two of three fail to support the belief, and he rates study 2 as the best designed (well-trained lifters, multiple fatigue measures). He then declines to close the case — he says two studies aren't strong proof, he doesn't think the anecdotes should be fully discounted, and further research should clarify it. He also offers a list of plausible reasons the deadlift *feels* more fatiguing without endorsing any.

## Does training to failure produce more CNS fatigue?
[09:48] Failure training undeniably produces **more overall fatigue** — a recent study on trained individuals found slower recovery after six bench press sets to failure than stopping 1–3 reps short.

But there appears to be exactly **one study measuring CNS fatigue from failure training**: trained men with 1+ years of experience did six back squat sets at a 10RM load, on one occasion all to failure and on another only five reps per set. Muscle damage was higher after the failure session, but **CNS fatigue both within the session and in the days after was similar**. He notes this contradicted his own default assumption, calls it preliminary, and says he'll see whether it replicates. The implication: the extra fatigue from failure is peripheral and damage-related, not central.

[11:20] Then the same "does it matter" move. Getting close to failure is needed to maximise a set's stimulus, but 0–3 RIR generally produces similar growth to failure. He addresses the meta-regression that circulated claiming failure is superior: it used **indirect equations and estimates** to infer how far from failure the non-failure groups actually were, and those estimates were likely not accurate. High-quality individual studies and an arguably better-controlled meta-analysis find similar growth from 0–3 RIR.

Three cases where he'd still avoid failure: if you're also doing **cardio or sprint training** (preliminary data suggests better adaptations without failure), for **older individuals** (though not consistently established), and when **lifestyle recovery is poor** — high stress, poor sleep, or dieting.

## Can you detect CNS fatigue?
[12:52] **Hand grip strength: no.** At least two studies find it fails to correlate with CNS fatigue, possibly because the hand muscles are too small to be sensitive to it. This is worth carrying forward as a straightforward debunk of a common gym-app metric.

**Vertical jump: maybe.** The logic is that CNS fatigue is largely a reduction in fast-twitch recruitment, and explosive movements are powered by fast-twitch fibres. He is explicit that **no study has properly assessed this**. The closest evidence is a study where post-workout vertical jump reduction correlated with back squat performance reduction but not other exercises — and those correlations were **between people**, not within a person over time, which is the question that matters.

**Subjective feeling: not terrible.** No data on subjective fatigue vs CNS fatigue specifically. For overall fatigue, one study found a strong correlation between subjective fatigue and squat performance, another found some correlation with muscle damage, but other papers find poorer associations.

[15:11] His practical position: tracking fatigue is fine if you enjoy it but isn't essential. Recovery problems announce themselves through **performance stagnation, regression, and subjective feeling**, and you adjust from there.

## Recovery capacity is trainable
[15:24] The **repeated bout effect** is the most useful finding in the video. A Greek study had untrained subjects do maximal eccentric quadriceps contractions — 5 sets of 15, once a week for 10 weeks. Week 1 produced substantial damage, soreness, stiffness and strength loss lasting **at least five days**. As weeks progressed the magnitude shrank, and by **week 10 virtually full strength recovery occurred within a day or two**.

[16:26] He then takes on the counter-argument that trained individuals don't recover CNS function faster, which rests on a study of young trained men, middle-aged trained men and middle-aged untrained men doing **10 sets of 10 at 60% 1RM** on the back squat. All showed CNS fatigue in the days after, and the trained and untrained middle-aged groups showed similar magnitudes. His two objections are good ones: the "untrained" middle-aged men were **oddly strong** — back squat strength not far off the trained group and **greater fat-free mass** — and none of the trained subjects would have been habitually running 10×10, so unfamiliarity with the protocol likely drove much of the fatigue. He supports this with research showing some CNS fatigue measures recover faster after a second workout, and reasons that since the Greek study's subjects went from 5+ days of impairment to 1–2 days, and CNS fatigue is known to persist 3+ days after maximal eccentric work, reduced CNS fatigue is plausibly part of that improvement.

[17:59] The ceiling case: **13 trained men** with stated 10RM strength levels and FFMI trained a programme **every day for four days**, each set to ~9.5 RPE (0–1 reps short of failure), **four hard sets for chest and four for quads per session**. Bench press and back squat performance held constant across all four days — recovery inside 24 hours. He is careful that the point is not that you should train this way, only that this recovery capacity is developable.

[19:00] **Individual differences.** In the Greek study's individual data, nearly all subjects followed the average pattern, but **around two to four individuals** still experienced fairly notable fatigue after the week-10 session despite improving. And in the four-day study, the researchers noted the strongest individuals — bench press 10RM at 1.25× bodyweight — **failed to replicate day-one performance**, hinting at a strength threshold beyond which recovery gets harder. He flags this as something he'd like corroborated.

**Overreach flag:** the vertical jump recommendation is presented as his preferred detection method while he simultaneously states no study has tested it, and the "recovery improves as you adapt" conclusion partly rests on inferring CNS fatigue reduction in a study that did not measure CNS fatigue.
