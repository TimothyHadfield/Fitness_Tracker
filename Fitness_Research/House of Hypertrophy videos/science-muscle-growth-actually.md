# The Five Ways a Muscle Can Get Bigger

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Science of What Muscle Growth Actually Is" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Gw6Y-aw4hs)) · 2021-08-02 · 13 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Part 1 of a five-video series. It is definitional, not practical: it names and distinguishes the mechanisms, and explicitly defers the evidence for each to later videos. Nothing here tells you how to train.
- In humans a muscle gets bigger by increasing **cross-sectional area**. By his count there are five underlying routes: **myofibrillar hypertrophy, sarcoplasmic hypertrophy, myofibrillar packing, hyperplasia, and an increase in muscle length**.
- The first three are the same phenomenon at different ratios — they differ only in whether myofibrils and sarcoplasm grow at the same pace, or one outpaces the other.
- **Hyperplasia in humans is "far from clear"** and he labels its contribution tentative. **Increased muscle length may be limited.** So the fibre cross-sectional-area routes are what most likely drive real-world growth.
- He is careful about a subtlety most explanations skip: **myofibrillar packing does not necessarily increase fibre size at all** — myofibrils can grow denser without the fibre getting wider.

## The hierarchy first
[01:00] Whole muscle → fascicles → muscle fibres → myofibrils. Myofibrils are arrays of **sarcomeres**, and sarcomeres generate force: the myosin head extends from the myosin filament and pulls actin toward the M line. Everything inside the fibre that is not myofibril counts as **sarcoplasm** — mostly water, plus glycogen, sarcoplasmic proteins and non-contractile organelles.

Because fibres sit inside fascicles and fascicles inside the muscle, anything that widens a fibre widens the fascicle and then the whole muscle. That chain is what makes all five routes count as "growth."

## 1. Myofibrillar hypertrophy
[02:01] Myofibrils and sarcoplasm grow **at the same pace**, so their relative proportions are unchanged. His worked example: a fibre that is 85% myofibril / 15% sarcoplasm increases cross-sectional area by 30% and is still 85/15.

[03:34] Two sub-routes for myofibril growth: an increase in the **number** of myofibrils, or an increase in the **cross-sectional area of existing ones** — the latter thought to occur by adding sarcomeres above or below existing ones, called **sarcomeres in parallel**.

## 2. Sarcoplasmic hypertrophy
[04:04] The sarcoplasm grows **faster** than the myofibrils, so its relative share rises: 85/15 becomes, say, 80/20 after a 30% increase in fibre area. He makes the point that is usually lost in the "fake vs real muscle" argument — **myofibrils have not shrunk**. They may have grown too, just more slowly; or they may not have grown while the sarcoplasm did. Candidate drivers named for later videos: glycogen and sarcoplasmic proteins.

## 3. Myofibrillar packing
[05:05] The mirror image: myofibrils grow **faster** than the sarcoplasm, so 85/15 becomes 90/10.

[06:06] The careful part. In his packing example he deliberately does not say the fibre grew, because **myofibrils can grow without increasing fibre cross-sectional area**. He offers two ways packing could still widen a fibre: myofibrils growing until they push the fibre's outer wall outward, or myofibrils growing while the sarcoplasm also grows — just more slowly, which keeps it within the packing definition. If sarcoplasm matched the myofibril growth rate, it would by definition be myofibrillar hypertrophy again.

[07:10] His own mini-review: these three are one continuum, distinguished only by **the ratio of myofibril to sarcoplasm growth**.

## 4. Hyperplasia
[07:40] An increase in the **number** of fibres in a muscle, by manufacturing entirely new fibres or by existing fibres splitting in two. More fibres per fascicle means greater fascicle area and greater whole-muscle area.

He states plainly that the research on whether this occurs in humans is **far from clear**, and calls its status as a growth mechanism **tentative**. Detail deferred to video 5.

## 5. An increase in muscle length
[08:42] Muscle attachment points on bone do not move, so a longer muscle cannot visibly lengthen — it **bulges outward instead**, which registers as cross-sectional area.

[09:12] The chain of causation: there is reasonable evidence resistance training can lengthen **fascicles** in humans; if fascicles run the muscle's length, longer fascicles mean a longer muscle. Below that, myofibrils lengthen by building and adding sarcomeres end to end — **sarcomeres in series**. He flags that he could not find concrete evidence that the sarcoplasm expands lengthwise to support this, though presumably it does; and he notes that if both expand lengthwise at the same rate, this could technically be classed as myofibrillar hypertrophy.

[10:45] Two anatomical complications he raises and does not dodge: some fascicles do not run the full length of a muscle, so lengthening them may not lengthen the muscle; and some fibres do not run the full length of their fascicle, so lengthening those fibres may not lengthen the fascicle. He also flags that the achievable increase in muscle length **may be limited**.

## What this leaves you with
[11:45] Hyperplasia is tentative, length increases may be limited — so the three fibre-cross-sectional-area routes are most likely what commonly drives whole-muscle growth. The rest of the series compares them and asks whether any can be trained for specifically.

## Note on evidence quality
This is a taxonomy built from review papers and a small number of primary sources, and the argument runs almost entirely through **mechanism** — none of the five routes is here connected to an outcome measure of muscle growth from a training study. That is appropriate for a definitional video, but it means nothing in this note is actionable on its own, and the two most eye-catching mechanisms (hyperplasia, length increase) are the two he flags as poorly supported in humans.
