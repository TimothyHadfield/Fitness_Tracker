# Turkesterone and the Size of the Placebo Effect

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Turkesterone Works...But It's Not for the Reasons You Think It Does" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QGxg5-Di5vk)) · 2022-02-03 · 10 min
**Topic:** Evidence & method

## Bottom line
- He does **not** review turkesterone's own evidence. The video's argument is that turkesterone's anecdotal track record can be explained without any pharmacological effect at all — so anecdote cannot be used as evidence for it.
- His stated position on the evidence: there is **not a single long-term human study** on how turkesterone affects hypertrophy or strength. The human research most often cited examined structurally similar compounds (ecdysteroids), not turkesterone.
- He is explicit that he is **not** claiming turkesterone is all placebo, and that future data could validate the claims. The title's "works" is deliberate: he thinks people genuinely get results, and thinks expectation is a sufficient explanation.
- Ariel & Saville 1973: 6 trained athletes given a sham Dianabol pill gained **2–10× more 1RM strength in just over half the time** than in the preceding drug-free block (e.g. squat +2.65 kg over 7 weeks, then +18.94 kg over 4 weeks on placebo).
- Maganaris 2000: 11 nationally ranked powerlifters given saccharin tablets described as fast-acting steroids all hit PRs — bench +9.5 kg, squat +12.2 kg, deadlift +10.9 kg — moving every subject from nationally to internationally ranked. When some were told the pills were placebo, their lifts fell straight back to baseline.

## The argument
The video is a methods lesson dressed as a supplement review. His framing: anecdotes about turkesterone come with an array of confounders — the user may have consciously or unconsciously changed training effort, nutrition or sleep on starting it — and expectation itself is one of those confounders. Controlled trials exist precisely to isolate these; pooled Reddit and YouTube-comment reports cannot.

He then argues that the confounder people most underweight is expectation, and that the ergogenic literature on placebo is far stronger than most lifters assume. He also does something worth crediting: he credits the source of the two studies openly, saying he only knows them because Greg Nuckols of Stronger by Science wrote them up, and states he has no affiliation.

## The 1973 study
Fifteen male varsity athletes with at least two years of training trained for seven weeks. Those making the largest 1RM gains across bench, seated press, standing press and squat were promised free steroids for a subsequent four-week block. Six were selected and given a daily 10 mg "Dianabol" tablet — actually a placebo.

Over the initial seven drug-free weeks the six gained on average +4.35 kg bench, +0.73 kg standing press, +2.27 kg seated press, +2.65 kg squat. Over the four placebo weeks: +13.28 kg bench, +7.58 kg standing press, +5.30 kg seated press, +18.94 kg squat.

## The 2000 study
Eleven nationally ranked powerlifters, average 1RMs of 189 kg bench, 257 kg squat, 260 kg deadlift. The lead author was their own coach, and ran the study because the athletes had asked him about steroids.

Five minutes before a 1RM test, subjects took two saccharin tablets described as immediate-acting anabolic steroids. All hit PRs, averaging +9.5 kg bench, +12.2 kg squat, +10.9 kg deadlift over baseline — which the authors characterised as moving each subject from nationally to internationally ranked. They then took more "steroid" tablets through a training week and reported greater vigour and heavier loads than ever.

The second test session is the striking one. Some subjects were told before testing that the pills had been placebo all along; they lifted **7.5 kg less on bench, 11 kg less on squat and 12.5 kg less on deadlift** than in session one — back to baseline. Those still unaware roughly held their gains.

## What he actually concludes
Two things. First, that with any supplement — steroids included — a strong expectation of an effect means some share of the observed gains is attributable to expectation alone. He immediately guards this: steroids demonstrably work mechanistically, and he cites Bhasin 1996, where 600 mg/week testosterone with **no lifting at all** produced greater quadriceps area, triceps area and fat-free mass gains than natural lifting. Second, that when a supplement has no long-term human data and a large pile of anecdotes, there is no way to tell how much of the anecdote is placebo — "it might just very well be most of it."

He closes on a broader note about expectations limiting performance in any domain, which is untethered from the two studies and reads as inspirational filler.

## Where this sits against the library
The library holds that turkesterone has zero human evidence and that tested products contained 0.15% of label. His position is compatible but narrower and more precise: **zero long-term human hypertrophy/strength trials on turkesterone specifically**, with the commonly-cited human work being on ecdysterone, a structurally similar compound — an important distinction, since ecdysterone human data does exist (the Isenmann/German ecdysterone paper is in his reference list). He makes no claim at all about product label accuracy or dosing; that part of the library position comes from elsewhere. He also declines to call turkesterone useless, which the library's framing arguably does.

**Overreach flag:** the two placebo studies are 6 and 11 subjects, both unblinded by design and both nearly 25 and 50 years old; treating effect sizes that large as the expected magnitude of supplement placebo is a stretch he does not caveat.
