# Biceps Training: Which Curl, How Hard, and Whether Pulling Counts

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The ULTIMATE Guide to Biceps Growth (73 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Df22YJ64uv4)) · 2023-04-12 · 63 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

> **Reference warning.** This video's description contains **no reference list**. The 73 studies are cited on screen only and are not retrievable. Every study below is described from the transcript; author names come from auto-captions and are **unverified** unless the library resolves them independently. See the References section.

## Bottom line
- **Effort dominates exercise choice.** In the one study here that isolates the variable, the same barbell curl produced a large hypertrophy gap between training to failure and stopping 5–6 reps short — and adding an extra non-failure set closed almost none of it.
- **Nearly every curl works.** Machine, cable, barbell, dumbbell, EZ-bar, preacher — five studies show comparable elbow flexor growth, because all of them load elbow flexion with supination.
- **His speculative pick is a curl with the shoulder extended** (incline dumbbell or face-away cable) for the stretch. He labels this a hypothesis, not a finding. **The library now holds direct evidence against half of it** — see the cross-reference section.
- **Pulling is not enough.** Dumbbell rows produced just under **50% less** elbow flexor growth than dumbbell curls, and horizontal pulling is mechanically poor for the long head specifically.
- **The peak is genetic** and biceps anatomy is far more variable than textbooks suggest — roughly **36% of upper limbs have three, four or five biceps heads**.
- **He rejects sEMG for exercise comparison outright**, with a table of conflicting results and two clean cases where activation and growth point opposite ways. Then he leans on a fine-wire EMG study to build the supination argument — the one place his own standard slips.

## Decision 1: does it matter which curl you pick?

Mostly no. Five studies, all showing comparable elbow flexor growth:

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 2020, Canada | 36 trained; machine curl + machine preacher vs EZ-bar curl + preacher, 8 wks | Comparable growth at ~65% upper arm length |
| 2020, Brazil | Cable vs barbell preacher curl, 10 wks | Comparable growth at ~50% |
| 2013, Japan | Dumbbell curl only | **+13.3%** elbow flexor CSA |
| 2016, Brazil | Cable preacher curl only | **+16–20%** CSA across upper, middle and lower regions |
| 2011, Brazil | Barbell curl as the only direct biceps work | Growth at all three regions measured |

He correctly warns against comparing percentages across studies with different subjects, regions and variables. The mechanical reason he gives is the important part: **all of these load elbow flexion with supination**, which is what the biceps do. If you want the simple answer, it is "use the curl you like."

One measurement caveat he repeats throughout: most of these studies measure **elbow flexor** size at a point on the upper arm, which includes the brachialis. Averaging six MRI studies, the biceps is only **46%** of elbow flexor volume, against brachialis **37%** and brachioradialis **17%**. So "elbow flexor growth" is roughly half biceps, and every result above should be read with that dilution in mind. He is unusually consistent about flagging this.

## Decision 2: is there a best curl anyway?

His speculation is a **curl with the shoulder extended** — incline dumbbell curl or face-away cable curl — because shoulder extension stretches the biceps, particularly the long head, which crosses the shoulder joint.

Three lines of evidence, and he grades each:

1. **Isometric training at long vs short muscle lengths** — three studies favouring long. All three were on the **quadriceps**, so transfer to the biceps is assumed, not shown.
2. **Range-of-motion studies on the preacher curl.** Two studies compared a partial at the *initial* (stretched) position against a partial at the *final* position, with load adjusted since you can lift more in the final position. One found elbow flexor growth across three regions tending to favour the initial-position partial; the other found growth similar at 50% but superior at 70%, favouring initial-position overall. Both found **considerably more gain at the 70% region** with the stretch-position partial. A third study compared full ROM against a mid-range partial preacher curl and found full ROM better at ~60%. His own caveat is right: the preacher curl is performed in shoulder *flexion*, so this shows the biceps respond to stretch, not that a more stretched exercise would be better still.
3. **The triceps analogy** — the study finding overhead cable extensions produced greater triceps long head volume growth than pressdowns, *and* unexpectedly greater combined medial and lateral head growth.

He then handles the counter-argument, which is the mark of the channel: some argue biceps sarcomere length–tension data says the biceps should *not* grow more from stretch. He declines to draw conclusions from one line of literature with known limitations, and points out the identical argument was made about the triceps and was falsified by the overhead extension result.

**Incline curl vs face-away cable:** the claim that cables are better because they hold force production more constant through the range gets dismissed on evidence. **Four studies of variable resistance versus normal resistance all found similar hypertrophy**, two of them on the biceps (a 2018 machine preacher curl study measuring arm circumference; a 1995 preacher machine curl study measuring biceps CSA). If a constant resistance profile mattered, variable resistance should win. It doesn't. Pick whichever you prefer.

## Decision 3: how hard do the sets need to be?

[21:02] This is the section he calls the most important tip, and the study earns it.

**Martorelli 2017** (name unverified): **89 previously untrained women**, barbell curl only, twice a week for 10 weeks, three groups, all at 70% 1RM with 2 minutes rest and 1RM retested at week 5:

- **Failure:** 3 sets to failure
- **Non-failure, equal sets:** 3 sets of 7
- **Non-failure, plus one:** 4 sets of 7

At 70% 1RM subjects could manage 12–13 reps, so the non-failure groups stopped **5–6 reps short** on the first set (somewhat closer on later sets from cumulative fatigue). Elbow flexor growth at ~60% upper arm length was **superior for the failure group by a large margin**. The extra set bought the non-failure-plus-one group only a slight improvement, leaving it far off the failure group.

Same exercise, same load, same rest — the only variable is proximity to failure, and it dominates. Adding volume did not compensate.

He then softens it correctly rather than overselling: other research finds **1–3 reps from failure** matches failure, so stopping just short is fine. But he adds the practical problem — people systematically underestimate proximity to failure, believing they stopped 2 reps short when they were 5 — and recommends gaining genuine experience training to failure to calibrate RIR judgement. That is a good, non-obvious point and it is well placed.

The theoretical frame is mechanical tension: passive tension (from stretch) plus active tension (contractile force), detected by mechanosensors. He explicitly notes that muscle damage and the pump are **not** supported as strong hypertrophy drivers.

## Decision 4: do you need more than one curl?

His answer is a soft yes, resting on regional hypertrophy. The chain has four links and he rates each honestly:

- **The biceps do not grow evenly across regions.** The 2011 Brazilian barbell curl study found largest gains at the 50% region, less at 60% and 70%. Possible reasons: regions do not shorten equally during elbow flexion; fibre-type proportions differ by region (and fast-twitch fibres have greater hypertrophic potential); and there may be subgroups of fibres with distinct functions.
- **Different people grow different regions from the same exercise.** A 2017 study (caption: "Dankel") had **five trained men** curl for 21 days: one grew the 50% region most, one grew 60% and 70% equally, one grew only the 60% region, one grew best at 70% but well everywhere, one grew increasingly more toward 70%. He immediately caps the implication — **the differences are slight, most people grow all regions well**, and nobody's arm ends up looking odd.
- **Costa 2021**, varied vs non-varied. Non-varied performed the barbell curl on Monday, Wednesday and Friday; varied performed barbell curl, cable preacher curl and incline dumbbell curl respectively, over 8 weeks. The varied group tended to grow all three elbow flexor regions more, particularly 50% and 60%, with slightly more total growth. **He gives three caveats himself**: the lat pulldown variations also differed and confound it; the groups were different people, and people differ in regional response; and the varied group performed the incline curl, which by his own hypothesis is the best exercise — which could be the whole effect.
- **A 2019 US acute study**: nine sets of neutral-shoulder cable curls versus three sets each at neutral, extended and flexed shoulder. Echo intensity rose similarly at the middle region but more at the lower region with the varied session. He rates this himself as **not high quality** — echo intensity is not hypertrophy and its meaning is ambiguous.

His conclusion is proportionate: the single most hypertrophic exercise is still probably the shoulder-extended curl, but it may not develop every region optimally, and there is no real downside to running two or three biomechanically different curls.

## Decision 5: can you bias the long or short head?

Not the peak — that is genetic, and he makes the point well by contrasting Arnold's and Sergio Oliva's very different biceps shapes despite comparable development, and notes Arnold attributes his own to heredity.

For head bias the widely stated logic is shoulder extension → long head stretched → long head biased; shoulder flexion → long head shortened → short head biased. He finds it plausible and then says the thing most people won't: **there is no research measuring long and short head hypertrophy separately, so it cannot be confirmed.**

His most useful contribution is dismantling the "seesaw" assumption — that biasing one head starves the other. Two counter-cases:

- **Seated vs lying leg curl:** the biceps femoris long head grew more with seated curls (more hip flexion, more stretch), but **short head growth was similar between the two**. Biasing one did not cost the other.
- **Rectus femoris in leg extensions:** partly shortened at the hip by the ~90° seated position, yet leg extensions produce substantial rectus femoris hypertrophy.

So both heads likely grow well on either curl. On the other proposed levers — shoulder external/internal rotation, grip width — he reports the claims conflict with each other and that he knows of no high-quality evidence either way.

## Decision 6: does back training count?

Partly, and less than people assume.

- Compounds alone do produce measurable growth: a 2016 US study in trained men where lat pulldown and cable row were the only biceps-involving exercises (3 sets of 8–12 to failure, 2 min rest, 3×/wk, 8 wks) produced **+5%** elbow flexor thickness.
- **Mannarino 2018** (name unverified) compared dumbbell rows to dumbbell curls, averaged across the 25%, 50% and 75% regions: curls were superior, with **rows producing just under 50% less growth**. Critically, **both were performed with a supinated grip** — rows are normally pronated or neutral, so real-world rows are likely worse still.
- **Horizontal pulls are mechanically poor for the long head.** During the lowering phase the shoulder flexes while the elbow extends; during lifting the reverse. The long head therefore shortens at one joint while lengthening at the other, in both directions. He supports the pattern with two cases where the same geometry predicts minimal growth and growth is minimal: **rectus femoris in the squat**, and **triceps long head in the bench press**.
- **Vertical pulls** are the contested case. Two studies found lat pulldowns produced elbow flexor thickness gains similar to barbell curls, and that adding curls to pulldowns added nothing. He raises three limitations and one integrity concern: both papers share an author who **has previously been involved in studies that are most likely fraudulent** (he notes no signs these two are, and remains cautious); both used untrained subjects; and thickness was measured at only one region. He adds that both used a **pronated** grip, which by the supination argument would understate biceps involvement — and speculates the elbow flexor gains from pulldowns may reflect more brachialis, and those from curls more biceps.
- **Chin-ups** have no direct research. He speculates they are excellent (supinated grip plus elbow flexion) with the caveat that the back may be the limiting factor.

## The sEMG section

[45:51] He presents a table of published and unofficial surface EMG comparisons of curl variations, notes they conflict with each other (one finds straight bar above EZ bar, another finds them equal), and rejects the whole approach for exercise comparison, citing two Vigotsky papers. His two clean examples are the best short case against sEMG in the library:

- High sEMG from the **rectus femoris during squats**, yet squat training produces no significant rectus femoris hypertrophy.
- Higher quadriceps sEMG during **short-length** leg extension isometrics, yet greater hypertrophy from **long-length** isometrics.

He is careful to say sEMG has other valid uses; it just does not predict which exercise builds more muscle.

## Flag: where his own standard slips

The supination argument is built on a **1998 fine-wire EMG study** — biceps long and short head activity highest in supination, brachialis and brachioradialis highest in pronation — plus papers reporting that the brachioradialis signals the biceps to reduce activity during pronated elbow flexion. That argument then does real work: it drives his discounting of pronated-grip pulldowns and rows, and his enthusiasm for chin-ups.

Fine-wire EMG is a better instrument than surface EMG, and comparing grip positions within one movement is exactly the "subtle comparison" case he defends elsewhere. But it is still **activation, not growth**, in a video that spends three minutes explaining why activation does not predict growth. He does not acknowledge the tension. It is the one place where his conclusions rest on a proxy he has elsewhere ruled out.

**Agreements**

- **The best-exercise pick.** His speculative single best — a curl with the shoulder extended — matches the library's top rank. `Jeff Nippard videos/biceps-exercises-ranked.md` puts the **face-away (Bayesian) cable curl** in S tier, and `Jeff Nippard videos/only-25-exercises-need.md` calls it "the only exercise he knows of that trains the biceps at long muscle lengths while keeping high tension through the whole ROM." `Menno Henselmans videos/new-science-maximize-biceps-growth.md` also picks Bayesian curls. Three sources, three routes, same answer.
- **The peak is genetic.** `Menno Henselmans videos/4-biceps-training-myths-confusing-everyone.md` Myth 2 is "you cannot train your biceps peak." Identical position.
- **Rows count for about half.** The figure "dumbbell rows count about half" appears in three Menno notes, and `4-biceps-training-myths-confusing-everyone.md` explicitly warns that **no study in its reference list is identified as its source**. This video supplies a strong candidate: a 2018 dumbbell row vs dumbbell curl trial measuring three regions, with rows at **just under 50% less growth**. Caption name "Mannarino," unverified — but it is the same comparison, the same direction and the same magnitude, and it is worth chasing to close that gap.
- **The Gentil integrity concern.** `Menno Henselmans videos/4-biceps-training-myths-confusing-everyone.md` flags Gentil 2015 as unreplicated with an integrity concern. This video raises the same concern independently, about the same author, regarding the pulldown-versus-curl studies. Two sources converging on a research-integrity caution is worth recording.
- **Effort over everything.** The Martorelli result sits comfortably with the library's 0–3 RIR position, and his warning that lifters overestimate how close to failure they are echoes `Jeff Nippard videos/junk-volume.md`, where 47% of lifters left 6–10+ reps in reserve.

**Disagreements**

- **Supination.** This is the sharpest clash in the library on arms. He treats supination as a predominant biceps function and a supinated grip as the higher-tension position, and builds his critique of pronated pulling on it. `Menno Henselmans videos/4-biceps-training-myths-confusing-everyone.md` Myth 1 is the flat contradiction: "**supinated grips are not the best biceps position — supination *shortens* the biceps.**" The two are arguing from different quantities — he from activation, the other from muscle length — and the flag above applies: his evidence here is acute, and by his own stated standard acute evidence does not settle exercise selection. Unresolved, and worth watching.
- **Incline curl vs preacher curl.** His central hypothesis is that more shoulder extension means more stretch means more growth. The library has since acquired direct evidence against the incline-curl half of it. `Jeff Nippard videos/biceps-exercises-ranked.md` reports **Zabaleta-Korta 2023** ([PMID 37559762](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37559762/)) with preacher curls out-growing incline curls, under the principle "high tension in a decent stretch beats a huge stretch with no tension"; `Menno Henselmans videos/full-rom-dead-stretch-mediated-hypertrophy-lengthened.md` reports the same result, and `Menno Henselmans videos/optimized-exercises-blow-arms.md` gives the mechanism — **the incline curl leaves the arm vertical at the bottom, unloading the biceps exactly where it is most stretched**.

  Two things save most of his position. He explicitly framed it as speculation, and this video is contemporaneous with the study that undercuts it. And the criticism applies to the *incline dumbbell curl* specifically, not to the shoulder-extended family: the **face-away cable curl**, which he treats as equally good, maintains tension at length and is precisely the fix the library recommends. So the correct update is that his exercise class survives and his tolerance of the dumbbell version of it does not.

- **Exercise variety.** He argues from Costa 2021 for two or three biomechanically different curls. `Menno Henselmans videos/mike-mentzer-right-about-arm-training.md` reports **Valério 2026** finding no advantage to arm exercise variety or extra volume (~3% growth in all groups), concluding one good isolation exercise per muscle. His own three caveats on Costa — pulldown confounding, different subjects, the incline curl in the varied arm — largely anticipate that result, so the disagreement is smaller than it looks, but the library's later evidence points the other way.
- **Scope note, not a clash.** The library ranks the hammer curl highly for "peak" via the brachialis. This video **deliberately excludes** the brachialis and brachioradialis, deferring them to a separate video, so its silence is not disagreement.
