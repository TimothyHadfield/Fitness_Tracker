# Trap Bar vs Conventional Deadlift: More Quads, Same Glutes and Erectors

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Trap Bar Deadlift vs Conventional Deadlift (In-Depth Comparison)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qTb6Q1FhtTw)) · 2020-08-12 · 11 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- Once you correct for the fact that people lift **8.4% more** on the trap bar, the two lifts impose **similar hip and spinal flexion moments** — so glutes and spinal erectors are involved similarly. The trap bar's larger **knee flexion moment survives the correction**, so it involves the quads more.
- **Hamstrings likely favour the conventional deadlift**, on a passive-tension argument rather than a moment argument: the conventional stretches the hamstrings more. The quads are correspondingly stretched more on the trap bar.
- **Low handles ≈ conventional range of motion; high handles are ~22% shorter.** He treats the two handle heights as roughly comparable in muscle involvement, since the smaller moment arms are offset by the heavier loads used.
- **For athletes the trap bar is clearly superior**: greater average force, power, velocity and time spent accelerating at 90% 1RM, for both handle heights.
- **The trap bar is not a squat.** ~78° of knee flexion at the start versus 100-120° for a parallel squat, and the hip moment meaningfully exceeds the knee moment — the opposite of the squat, where the two are comparable.
- **This is entirely biomechanical modelling and acute data.** No study compares long-term hypertrophy between the two lifts, and he says so.

## The correction that carries the video

The one comparison of peak joint moments ([Swinton 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26840440/)) tested trained powerlifters on both lifts at 10-80% of their **conventional** deadlift 1RM. At 80%, the conventional produced greater peak spinal flexion and hip flexion moments; the trap bar produced a greater peak knee flexion moment.

Taken at face value, that reads as "conventional for posterior chain, trap bar for quads." He rejects the face value, and the reasoning is the best part of the video: **both conditions used the same absolute load**, set by the conventional 1RM. The same study found subjects could lift **8.4% more on the trap bar**. So a true like-for-like comparison — 80% of each lift's own 1RM — requires scaling the trap bar moments up by 8.4%. Doing that **erases the spinal and hip differences** and leaves the knee difference intact.

The conclusion he draws is therefore narrower than the raw data suggests: quads more on the trap bar; **glutes and spinal erectors similar**; hamstrings decided by something other than moments.

He spends nearly two minutes first explaining what an external moment is, worked through a biceps curl with actual numbers (15 kg × 9.8 m/s² × 0.3 m moment arm = 44.1 Nm). It is a genuine attempt to make the rest of the video auditable rather than taken on trust.

## Passive tension, and where the argument becomes inference

Moments are not the only determinant of muscle involvement. He adds **passive tension** — force generated by a muscle when stretched. The hamstrings are stretched more in the conventional deadlift (more hip flexion, less knee flexion), so they contribute more force and are plausibly more involved. The vastii are stretched more in the trap bar's greater knee flexion, reinforcing the quad conclusion.

**This is the flag:** the hamstring claim has no data behind it at all — no moment difference survives the 8.4% correction, so the entire hamstring case is a length-tension argument. He states it with appropriate hedging ("likely"), but a reader should not treat "conventional works hamstrings more" as a measured finding.

## High handles versus low handles

Range of motion: with **low handles the bar travel is near identical to conventional** ([Lake 2017](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/5/4/82)); with **high handles it is ~22% less** ([Lockie 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/med/28394830)). High handles therefore involve roughly 22% less ROM than low handles.

On muscle involvement he reasons that high handles reduce knee flexion, hip flexion and (for some) trunk lean, shrinking every moment arm — but heavier loads can be used, which should roughly restore the moments. The residual difference is that the low handles stretch the quads slightly more. **Whether that translates to more quad growth is unknown**, and he says so directly.

## Practical considerations

Two anatomical cases where the shorter ROM is an advantage rather than a compromise: people whose femur contacts the pelvis at the bottom of a conventional pull, and people who can only reach the bar by rounding their back. For both, the high handles allow a more comfortable and mechanically better start position.

For **lower back pain or injury**, he favours the trap bar (either handle height) on a fatigue argument: with the load in front of you, fatigue tends to pull you forward and shift load onto the lower back; with the load beside you, the lower back is less likely to pick up the slack, and any shift goes to the quads instead.

## Athletic performance

At 90% 1RM in 11 trained men, the low-handle trap bar produced greater **average force, power, velocity and time spent accelerating** than the conventional ([Lake 2017](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/5/4/82)). The high-handle version beat the conventional on force, power and velocity at 1RM ([Lockie](https://europepmc.org/article/med/28394830)). He labels this acute data but still concludes the trap bar is "probably superior" for athletes — a reasonable inference, though it remains an inference from bar-speed measures to on-field transfer.

## Is it a squat?

The recurring claim that the trap bar deadlift is really a squat gets three data points against it. Knee flexion at the start of a high-handle trap bar pull averages **~78°**, versus **100-120°** for a parallel squat and **~140°** for a deep squat. In Swinton's moment data the **hip flexion moment is meaningfully larger than the knee flexion moment**, whereas in a squat at ~80% 1RM with 120° knee flexion, peak hip and knee moments are **comparable** ([Swinton 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22505136/); [Escamilla 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11404665/) for a medium-stance squat). It is a hip-dominant lift with more quad involvement than a conventional deadlift — still a deadlift.
