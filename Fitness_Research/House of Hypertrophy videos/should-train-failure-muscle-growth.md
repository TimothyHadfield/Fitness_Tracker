# Failure Isn't Required, But 3 RIR Is the Line

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Should You Train to Failure for Muscle Growth? (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KJ8QVg6yE08)) · 2020-06-17 · 7 min
**Topic:** Effort & failure

## Bottom line
- A within-subject trial (**10 untrained men, one leg to failure, one leg not, 14 weeks**) found **statistically similar** rectus femoris and vastus lateralis growth, with raw percentages slightly favouring the **non-failure** leg.
- The pattern across the wider literature is a threshold: **~3 reps in reserve or fewer matches failure**; studies where the non-failure group left ~5+ reps tend to favour failure.
- Individual responses split three ways in the same study — some grew more with failure, some with RIR, some equally.
- **Almost none of this data is from trained lifters**, and he says so plainly rather than generalising.
- People systematically **underestimate their remaining reps**, which is the practical case for occasionally going to failure — as calibration, not stimulus.

## The unilateral trial
Ten untrained men had one leg assigned to failure and the other to non-failure on the unilateral leg extension, training five times a week for 14 weeks with one leg per workout, alternating so each leg received the same total sessions. Weeks 1–2: 3 sets at 50% 1RM. Weeks 3–8: 3 sets at 60%. Weeks 9–14: 4 sets at 60%. 1RM was retested every two weeks from week 3 to keep the failure leg's load accurate.

The non-failure prescription was derived from the failure leg's own performance: total reps in the previous failure session divided by number of sets. If the failure leg managed 9, 7 and 5 reps, the non-failure leg did 3 sets of 7. That produces a **rising effort profile within the session** — roughly 2–3 RIR on set one, 1–2 on set two, 1–0 on the last set — which is worth noting, because "non-failure" here still means genuinely hard sets.

Ultrasound cross-sectional area of rectus femoris and vastus lateralis increased statistically similarly, with percentages slightly favouring non-failure.

## The individual data
He highlights the per-subject responses, which is the most useful thing in the video. For rectus femoris: 4 responded better to non-failure, 3 better to failure, 3 similarly. For vastus lateralis: 4 better to non-failure, 6 similarly. He draws the sensible conclusion — response varies by person and possibly by muscle within a person, and the only route to knowing your own is a training log over time.

## Where the threshold sits
Fitting this to the rest of the literature, he argues that leaving **around 3 reps or fewer** produces results comparable to failure, while studies whose non-failure arm left **~5 or more** tend to find failure superior. He then presents the study that contradicts him (the captions render the name as "Karin"; the closest match in his reference list is Karsten et al., so treat the attribution as unconfirmed) and explains rather than dismisses it: those subjects also performed **sprint training twice weekly**, so the failure group was plausibly under-recovered, and without the sprinting the result might have gone the other way. That is speculation, and he labels it as such.

His stated major limitation: **there isn't enough research on trained individuals**. He lists all three possibilities (trained lifters might respond equally, better to failure, or better to RIR) and concludes we don't know.

## The calibration problem
Hackett et al. (2017) had 81 adults of varying experience stop at rep 10 on chest press (70% 1RM) and leg press (80% 1RM), estimate remaining reps, then continue to failure. Subjects consistently underestimated — commonly predicting 5–6 more reps when they could do 10. **Training experience barely helped.** His practical recommendation: train to failure occasionally so your reference point for what failure feels like stays current, which keeps your RIR estimates honest.

He closes with an exercise-selection caveat: on multi-joint lifts, fatigue-driven form breakdown makes failure less attractive, especially for beginners, whereas single-joint work like curls and skull crushers has little skill component and takes failure fine.
