# Can You Redistribute the Load Within a Compound Lift? One Study, and a Careful Demolition of It

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How to Change Muscle Activation With Any Compound Exercise (Maybe)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LT-2PJlyUm4)) · 2022-01-20 · 11 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- One study (Stronska) found that **six weeks of isolation training for whichever bench-press muscle showed the lowest relative EMG raised that muscle's EMG on the bench**, usually with a corresponding drop in the others.
- The "maybe" in the title is the whole video. He spends four of eleven minutes explaining why the study's central measurement **cannot support the claim its authors made** — and then explains why he keeps it anyway.
- Surface EMG does not measure muscle activation. It approximates neuromuscular excitation, and even that imperfectly, since changing muscle length or joint angle alters the recording at constant excitation.
- Normalising to a maximum isometric contraction makes **between-muscle comparisons meaningless**, because the reference contraction is arbitrary. That is exactly how the researchers assigned subjects to groups.
- What survives: a **within-subject, same-exercise, same-electrode** comparison showing consistent directional change in all three groups. That is suggestive. It is not evidence of growth.

## The study

[Stronska et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32460639/) recruited **27 men averaging a 105.2 kg barbell bench press**, all benching at least 120% of bodyweight. Baseline testing was two 4-second maximal isometric bench contractions with elbows at 90° and upper arms perpendicular to the floor, plus three reps at **85% 1RM**, with surface EMG on pectoralis major, anterior deltoid and triceps.

For each subject the researchers identified which of the three showed the **lowest relative activation**, forming three groups. Each then trained that muscle in isolation: three exercises per session, **four sets of 10–15 reps to failure, three times a week for six weeks** — the anterior deltoid group did upright rows, lateral raises and front raises; the pec group did dumbbell, machine and cable flies; the triceps group did barbell skull crushers, cable overhead extensions and incline skull crushers. All three also did three sets at a 4RM bench weekly so strength would not drift.

Afterwards all three groups had increased bench 1RM similarly, and the EMG tests were repeated. On the 85% 1RM reps: the **anterior deltoid group** raised anterior deltoid relative activation with significant decreases in pec and triceps; the **pec group** raised pec activation with significant decreases in triceps and anterior deltoid; the **triceps group** raised triceps activation with a significant pec decrease and no significant anterior deltoid change.

Stated plainly, the study claims that exclusive isolation work for one muscle raises its relative contribution to a compound lift and lowers the others'.

## Why the measurement can't carry that claim

**Problem one: what surface EMG records.** It is routinely described as measuring "muscle activation". It does not. Muscle activation is calcium binding to troponin, enabling cross-bridge cycling. Surface EMG approximates **neuromuscular excitation** — neurons firing at the fibres they innervate — and there are intermediate steps between the two that should not be collapsed. And it only *approximates* even that: when true neuromuscular excitation is held constant, changing muscle length or joint angle changes the recording ([Vigotsky et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5758546/)).

**Problem two: the normalisation, and this is the serious one.** Each muscle's bench-press recording is expressed as a percentage of that muscle's maximum isometric contraction. His worked example: if the triceps produces 100 units during the reference isometric and 85 units during the 85% 1RM bench, you call it 85% MVIC. But if a *different* triceps isometric had been chosen as the reference and produced 150 units, the same 85 units becomes 57% MVIC. The percentage is a property of the reference contraction as much as of the exercise.

It follows that comparing percent-of-MVIC **across different muscles** tells you nothing about which muscle is contributing more — the denominators are arbitrary and unrelated. And that comparison is precisely what defined the study's groups: the "anterior deltoid group" were the subjects whose anterior deltoid %MVIC was lower than their pec or triceps %MVIC during the bench tests. The equipment and method were not adequate to establish that at all.

## Why he keeps it

Two reasons, both narrow and both fair.

**Consistency of direction.** Every group's EMG went up for the muscle it trained. Three independent groups, three matching results.

**The comparison is within-subject, same exercise, same electrode placement.** The before-and-after contrast holds muscle length, joint angle and electrode position roughly constant — which is exactly what the length/angle objection above threatens. The %MVIC problem breaks cross-muscle comparisons; it damages a within-muscle, pre-versus-post comparison far less.

So his conclusion is that isolation training for a muscle **may** raise its recruitment in a compound lift, and no more than that.

## Open questions he names

Subjects performed **36 weekly sets** for the target muscle — enormous. Would a normal dose do it? And they **ceased training everything else**, so the effect could be reduced training of the competing muscles rather than increased training of the target. The realistic version — raise volume on the muscle you want, cut it on the others, keep training everything — is untested.

His practical suggestion, offered as an experiment rather than a recommendation: if you want the back squat to involve the glutes more, run a period of added glute isolation while minimising other work; likewise for deadlifts if you want less spinal erector and more glute or hamstring.

**Overreach flag:** the video is about EMG amplitudes, not muscle. Nothing here shows that a changed recruitment pattern produces different regional or total growth, and surface EMG amplitude is not a validated predictor of hypertrophy ([Vigotsky et al. 2022](https://andrewvigotsky.com/static/studies/Vigotsky_2022_Sports_Med_sEMG_hypertrophy.pdf)) — a paper he cites in this very description. He never claims growth, which is to his credit, but the premise depends on the acute proxy being meaningful.

**Cross-reference:** he returns to this same Stronska study seven months later in [Underrated Exercise Selection Principles](underrated-exercise-selection-principles-muscle-growth.md), where it appears as supporting evidence with the normalisation critique dropped — and where he defends the study's validity on the grounds of the maximal isometric normalisation he dismantles here. Read the two together; this is the more careful treatment.
