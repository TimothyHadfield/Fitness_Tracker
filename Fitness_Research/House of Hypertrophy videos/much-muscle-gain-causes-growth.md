# How Much Muscle Can You Actually Gain — and What Drives It

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How Much Muscle Can You Gain? (& What Causes Growth?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZWYf_7jAWEM)) · 2025-12-02 · 15 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- A new review estimates lifetime natural fat-free mass gain at roughly **5 kg (low responder), 12 kg (average), 21 kg (high responder)** for men. Women gain at a **similar relative rate** but roughly **70% of the absolute** amount, because they start with less.
- Short-term reference point: the average male gains about **1.5 kg fat-free mass in ~10 weeks**; a high responder ~3 kg, a low responder ~0.4 kg over 10–12 weeks.
- **Acute anabolic hormone spikes do not drive hypertrophy.** Post-workout testosterone, GH and IGF-1 elevations are within normal daily variation, last under 90 minutes, and are orders of magnitude smaller than exogenous doses. Training designed to maximise them does not produce more growth.
- **Mechanical tension is the primary and essential driver.** But he warns explicitly against building a program on speculation about what maximises tension when outcome data says the variable doesn't matter.
- **Metabolic stress is not the primary driver**, and current evidence questions whether it is even additive — though he refuses to say it has zero role, and gives concrete reasons why. **Cell swelling / the pump** has no evidence of direct hypertrophic benefit.

## How much muscle, over what timeframe
The review builds its estimate in two stages. First, short-term: a meta-analysis of resistance training in healthy adult males found the average response was **+1.5 kg fat-free mass in around 10 weeks**. Trained subjects saw numerically lower gains, though — interestingly — the difference was not statistically significant. He immediately flags why that is not reassuring: "trained" is a very broad bucket, and nobody expects a 10-year lifter and a 2-year lifter to respond alike.

He also flags the measurement caveat properly: **fat-free mass is not skeletal muscle mass**. The estimate assumes muscle primarily drives FFM change, which is defensible over the long term but is an assumption.

Second, long-term: the researchers inferred a ceiling from **fat-free mass index** ranges in natural bodybuilders. FFMI is fat-free mass divided by height in metres squared — an 85 kg lifter at 15% body fat and 1.8 m gives 22.3. Reported natural bodybuilder FFMI ranges of **22.7–25**, combined with typical heights and baseline FFM, imply a total change of **12.5–20.2 kg**, or **2.5–4 kg per year over five years**.

Putting the two together gives the schematic: **21 kg / 12 kg / 5 kg** for high, average and low responders.

**Overreach flag — which he raises himself.** This is a merge of two literatures, each with its own limitations, and it rests on debatable assumptions. The x-axis stops at five years and the extent of further growth after that is contested. Real trajectories are lumpy, not smooth. And he cautions strongly against labelling anyone a **true** low responder — they may respond better to different training variables or better non-training factors.

## Anabolic hormones: the idea is dead
The historical hook is testosterone: isolated in the 1930s, and the classic trial showing **600 mg/week testosterone enanthate with no training** produced larger increases in some hypertrophy proxies than natural training ([Bhasin 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8637535/)). That effect is why people care about their own hormone spikes — and why some build programs (big compound lifts, many sets, moderate loads, short rest) around maximising the post-workout surge.

The evidence in the review dismantles it in two steps:

1. **[Wilkinson 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16972050/)** — unilateral leg extension and leg press 3×/week for 8 weeks. **No** change in systemic anabolic hormones in the 90 minutes post-workout, yet fibre CSA increased. So hormonal elevations are not *required*. But with no comparison group, this alone doesn't rule out that spikes *help*.
2. **[West 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19910330/)** — a low-hormone condition did a unilateral arm isolation exercise; a high-hormone condition did the same curl followed by a high volume of lower-body work, producing much larger acute hormone elevations. **Hypertrophy was similar.** Other studies also fail to find superiority for high-hormone protocols, and correlational work fails to link acute elevations to growth ([West & Phillips 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22105707/), [Mitchell 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24130904/), [Morton 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27174923/)).

The review's conclusion: acute increases in testosterone, GH or IGF-1 after exercise do not influence muscle protein synthesis or hypertrophic outcomes in men or women. The explanation is unglamorous — training-induced changes sit inside normal daily hormonal variation, are orders of magnitude below exogenous administration, and typically resolve within 90 minutes.

## Mechanical tension — and a warning about it
Mechanical tension is force generated by muscle fibres: **active** (from contractile elements) or **passive** (from components under stretch). Mechanosensors transduce that force into signalling that culminates in hypertrophy. The review calls it the primary and essential driver.

Proximity to failure is closely tied to it — as you approach failure, muscle fibre activation progressively rises, exposing more fibres to greater tension. He is careful not to overrun this: it does **not** mean you must train to failure, and stopping **1–2 reps short** can be sufficient in certain scenarios.

The most useful paragraph in the video is his caution against the tension framework itself. People hypothesise that some training style maximises mechanical tension and rebuild their whole program around it, **even where studies measuring actual growth fail to support that variable being superior**. His rule: prefer growth-outcome research over speculation about tension. This is a self-aware correction on a channel that trades heavily in mechanism.

## Metabolic stress: not the driver, probably not additive
Three lines of evidence in the review:

1. Bodybuilding-style 8–12 rep sets produce more metabolic stress than heavy training, yet **3–5 reps produces similar gains**, particularly where the low-rep condition does more sets to equate volume load.
2. **Blood flow restriction** creates a relatively hypoxic, high-metabolite environment and works well even at very light loads — but the plausible mechanism is **premature fatigue causing earlier recruitment of more fibres**, i.e. mechanical loading, not metabolites per se. And if metabolic stress were additive, adding BFR to moderate/heavy training should add growth. Studies applying BFR **during** the set, **within** rest intervals, or **after** the session all fail to find more growth ([Teixeira 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33900254/), [Dankel 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27480315/), [Madarame 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28448687/)).
3. Modalities like HIIT and sprinting produce similar or greater metabolite accumulation than lifting, yet produce **less** hypertrophy.

Then he adds four qualifications the review did not: separating metabolic stress from tension in humans is extremely difficult; there are **over 4,200 metabolites in human serum** and most are unexplored; a small threshold amount of metabolic stress that essentially all training styles exceed cannot be ruled out; and more extreme levels not yet tested in humans might matter. He also notes **capillarisation** as a possible indirect route by which high-metabolic-stress styles could help. This is the clearest example in the video of him arguing against his own tidy conclusion.

## Cell swelling and the pump
The pump originates in post-exercise hyperaemia and the opening of vascular and capillary beds, increasing muscle size acutely. The review concludes there is **a lack of evidence for a direct hypertrophic benefit of cell swelling**. His additions: the pump may still matter for enjoyment and adherence; and when comparing two exercises done with matched variables, a greater pump at a given muscle region *might* indicate more tension there and so be a growth indicator — but the evidence on that is mixed.

**Muscle damage** is not covered by the review; he notes the evidence linking it to growth is likewise unpromising.
