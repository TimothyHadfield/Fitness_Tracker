# Upper-body exercise selection

Twenty-eight notes on what to do for chest, back, shoulders, biceps, triceps and forearms, organised
by muscle rather than by upload order. They span 2020 to 2025, and the creator changed position on
several of them in that time — where two notes disagree, the newer one is named in place. The single
most important thing to carry into every claim below is that the amount of *measured hypertrophy*
behind these recommendations varies enormously by muscle: the chest and triceps have real growth
trials, the biceps have several, the lats and delts have almost none, and the forearms have none at
all.

---

## How to read this source

House of Hypertrophy is the most densely cited channel in this library, and the least verifiable at
exactly the points where it is most confident. **Twenty-six of its videos have no retrievable
reference list**, and the upper-body exercise-selection set is where that bites hardest: the
[73-study biceps guide](ultimate-guide-biceps-growth-73-studies.md), the
[63-study triceps guide](ultimate-guide-building-triceps-63-studies.md), the
[36-study lats guide](build-lats-ultimate-guide-36-studies.md), the
[72-study chest guide](grow-chest-most-detailed-guide-internet-72.md), the
[33-study chest exercise ranking](best-chest-exercises-building-muscle-33-studies.md), the
[forearm tier list](bigger-forearms-ranking-best-exercises-using-science.md), the
[upper-chest clip](blow-upper-chest-exercises.md) and the
[pullover clip](pullovers-not-train-lats-uh-oh.md) all cite on screen only. Where a claim traces to
one of those, it is flagged below as unverified, because the reader cannot check it. In the biceps
guide the transcript is auto-captioned as well, so even the author names are mangled guesses.

The creator is self-taught with no formal credentials, which he says himself, and he is unusually
careful in practice: he presents the study that contradicts him, states sample sizes and training
status, and retires his own positions. Three of the notes below exist *because* a study went against
something he had argued.

The distinction that matters most in this domain, and the one to keep sharp at all times:

- **Measured hypertrophy** — ultrasound, MRI or CSA change after weeks of training. This exists for
  the bench press, push-ups, machine presses, skull crushers, overhead extensions, curls, rows,
  pulldowns and lateral raises. Nothing else.
- **EMG / activation** — electrical excitation during a single session. Almost the whole upper-chest,
  Smith machine, dips, decline, grip-width and behind-the-neck case rests on this. He states
  repeatedly that it is not a validated predictor of growth, then uses it anyway, under a self-made
  rule that **EMG is more trustworthy for near-identical variations of one exercise than for
  comparisons between dissimilar exercises**. That rule is his own generalisation from two data
  points and has never been tested.
- **Leverage / moment-arm modelling** — cadaveric and computational estimates of a muscle's
  mechanical advantage, combined with "neuromechanical matching" (the nervous system recruits the
  best-levered muscle). **The entire lat, rear-delt and side-delt hierarchy is built on this and
  nothing else**, and each of those notes says so.

## The selection principles that recur

Five ideas do the work across every muscle.

**Effort beats exercise choice, and it is not close.** The cleanest demonstration in the set is in
the [biceps guide](ultimate-guide-biceps-growth-73-studies.md): **89 previously untrained women**,
barbell curl only, twice a week for 10 weeks, all at 70% 1RM with 2 min rest. Three groups — 3 sets
to failure, 3 sets of 7, or 4 sets of 7. At that load subjects could manage 12–13 reps, so the
non-failure groups stopped **5–6 reps short**. Elbow flexor growth at ~60% upper arm length was
**superior for the failure group by a large margin**, and **the extra set bought almost nothing**.
Same exercise, same load, same rest — proximity to failure dominated, and volume did not compensate.
He then softens it correctly: 1–3 reps from failure matches failure elsewhere, but lifters
systematically believe they stopped 2 short when they stopped 5. **Reference list: none.**

**Loading is permissive.** **6–35 reps taken to or near failure grow similarly**, so exercise choice
is rarely constrained by how heavy you can go. The
[triceps guide](ultimate-guide-building-triceps-63-studies.md) tests this against the "triceps are
fast-twitch" claim and rejects it: Schoenfeld's 18 trained men found **8–12RM similar to 25–35RM**
for triceps thickness, and a second Schoenfeld trial (19 trained men) found **2–4RM slightly worse
than 8–12RM**. The [shoulder note](build-3d-shoulders-25-studies.md) adds that you can progress by
**adding reps at a fixed load** rather than adding load, and that tempos from **0.5 to 8 seconds per
rep** work similarly — which is why lateral raises never need to get heavy.

**Equipment barely matters.** Machines, Smith machines, dumbbells, barbells and bodyweight all
produce comparable growth where they have been compared. The
[chest ranking](best-chest-exercises-building-muscle-33-studies.md) has the strongest single instance
— a direct hypertrophy comparison of machine chest press against flat barbell bench finding **similar
pec growth** — and demolishes the "machines are more stable so recruitment is higher" argument by
pointing out that the study behind it **strapped the leg and torso down on a leg extension**, which
is nothing like sitting on a bench. The [overhead press note](should-overhead-press-muscle-growth.md)
repeats the demolition and adds that **seated barbell pressing did not out-activate standing** in
trained lifters, and that the free-weight hormone argument fails because acute hormone spikes do not
predict growth.

**Muscle length is the one predictor he trusts, and it comes in three flavours.** The
[lateral raise note](have-talk-about-lateral-raises.md) supplies the most useful framework in the
whole set by splitting the "long lengths grow more" literature into three distinct questions that are
usually mashed together:

1. **Resistance challenge** — where in the range the exercise is hardest. **Two studies, both null**:
   the cable-vs-dumbbell lateral raise trial, and Nunes 2020 on barbell vs cable preacher curls.
2. **Range of motion** — full ROM vs lengthened partials. Three untrained studies favoured lengthened
   partials; **two 2024 trained-subject studies, one with just under 300 subjects, found them broadly
   equivalent.**
3. **Biarticular muscles** — moving the *other* joint to lengthen the muscle. **This is where the
   effect has survived**: seated over lying leg curls, leaning-back over normal leg extensions,
   straight-leg over bent-leg calf raises — each with an internal control muscle that does not cross
   the second joint and grows equally either way.

Category three is what justifies overhead triceps extensions and shoulder-extended curls. Categories
one and two are much weaker than the tier lists built on them imply, and he says so.

**A two-joint muscle is poorly trained when one of its actions opposes the compound.** This is the
sharpest mechanical rule in the set and it explains two of the best-evidenced findings on this page:
the triceps long head is barely grown by pressing, and the biceps long head is barely grown by
horizontal rowing. Both are cases where the muscle shortens at one joint while lengthening at the
other, so its length hardly changes and it cannot contribute much force.

## Chest

**The bench press is a genuinely good pec builder, and this is the best growth data anywhere in the
upper body.** [Is the bench press enough for chest growth](bench-press-enough-chest-growth-24-studies.md)
assembles five longitudinal trials and benchmarks them against Wernbom's pooled elbow-flexor rate of
**0.20% CSA per day**:

- **Ogasawara**, 7 untrained men, 3x10 at 75% 1RM, 3x/week for **24 weeks**: **+43% pec thickness,
  0.26%/day.**
- **Yasuda**, untrained men, 3x10 at 75%, 3x/week for 6 weeks: **+17.6% CSA, 0.42%/day** — double the
  benchmark.
- **Brandão**, untrained men, 3–5 sets to failure at 80%, 2x/week for 10 weeks: **+9.1% CSA,
  0.13%/day** — which he attributes to less weekly work, not a failure of the exercise.
- **Davies**, trained men and women (~5 years), 4x5 at 85%, 2x/week for 8 weeks: upper **7.44%**,
  middle **10.06%**, lower **7.45%**.
- **Ogasawara (CSA)**, 24 weeks: upper **36.3%**, middle **37.3%**, lower **40.0%** — all three
  regions above the elbow-flexor benchmark.

He flags every time that **thickness and cross-sectional area are being compared against a CSA
benchmark**, which is not strictly legitimate. The honest core of that video is its admission that
**no study has ever compared bench-press-only against bench-press-plus-other-chest-work at matched
volume**, so "is the bench enough" is unanswerable in principle.

**Every horizontal press is roughly interchangeable.** The
[72-study chest guide](grow-chest-most-detailed-guide-internet-72.md) makes the mechanical case —
barbell, dumbbell, Smith, machine and push-up all load shoulder horizontal flexion with the pecs
stretched at the bottom — and offers second-order tiebreakers rather than a ranking: **dumbbells
train the triceps slightly less** (you cannot produce lateral force without dropping them);
**dumbbells and cambered bars allow a deeper stretch**; **floor presses cap the stretch and are
therefore likely suboptimal**; and **failure is safer on a machine or Smith than on a free barbell
without safeties**. One velocity-loss study in trained lifters found pec growth best in the **50%
velocity-loss group**, roughly 70–80% of maximum possible reps.

**Push-ups match the bench press, and the constraint is load, not stimulus.** Two trials.
**Kikuchi 2017** — 18 men with at least a year of training, 3 sets to failure at a resistance
equivalent to **40% of bench 1RM**, twice weekly for 8 weeks — found **similar pec and triceps
thickness gains**, with one unexplained oddity: biceps thickness rose significantly only in the bench
group, which he declines to explain. **Kotarsky 2018** — 23 novices, 4 weeks — found **neither group
significant**, with raw percentages favouring push-ups. Both are covered in
[the 2020 note](bench-press-vs-push-ups-which-best.md) and in far more detail in the
[2021 ultimate comparison](push-ups-vs-bench-press-muscle-hypertrophy.md), which supplies the
arithmetic that makes the answer usable: **growth holds from about 30% to 80% of 1RM**, a 30% load is
roughly a **35-rep set**, and **a standard push-up loads about 64% of body weight** (Ebben 2011;
kneeling ~49%). So **once you can do about 35 clean push-ups you are at the bottom of the useful load
range** and need external load. His preferred fix is **weighted push-ups**, not harder variations,
because harder variations redistribute load between chest, front delts and triceps in ways you did
not choose — feet-elevated shifts toward upper chest and front delt, single-arm variants are
triceps-dominant. Push-ups also beat the bench on two muscles: **rectus abdominis and external
oblique** (a push-up is a plank) and **serratus anterior** (free scapular protraction, which a bench
press deliberately prevents).

**Do you need incline work? Yes — and the reason is arithmetic, not activation.** This is his
cleanest piece of reasoning in the domain, in
[How effective is the flat bench press for upper, middle and lower pec](effective-flat-bench-press-upper-middle-lower.md).
Ogasawara's regional percentages were near-identical, but baseline upper-pec CSA was about **15 cm2**
against **25–27 cm2** for middle and lower — so the same percentage bought **5.5 cm2 versus about
10 cm2**. Flat pressing does not skip the upper chest; **it grows a smaller region by the same
proportion, and the absolute gap persists**. By the same logic **lower-pec work is unnecessary**:
that region has no arithmetic problem, growing comparably to the middle in both percentage and
absolute terms.

**Which upper-pec exercise is entirely unresolved, and entirely EMG.**
[Blow up your upper chest](blow-upper-chest-exercises.md) — a clip of the chest guide — counts the
literature: **5 of 7 studies favour incline over flat** for upper-pec activity, usually at the cost
of mid and lower pec and with higher front delt activity; **4 of 8 grip-width papers favour a closer
grip**; **1 of 2 reverse-grip papers** favours reverse. The mechanism is that the upper pec has the
best leverage for **shoulder flexion**, which a close tucked grip or a reverse grip biases toward.
Angle does not converge: **30 degrees, 44 degrees, or no difference between 30 and 45**. The two
head-to-head comparisons he leans on are **non-significant results from a single paper**, read
directionally. His recommendation is therefore honest — pick one you like — and his tiebreaker is
subjective response (pump, soreness, perceived involvement), which he explicitly flags as **not a
validated proxy**, citing Greg Nuckols's report that flat reverse benching wrecks his upper pecs
while inclines never do. Two he rates lower: the **landmine press** and the **Lee's fend / Svend
press**, both for restricted stretch and capped loading.

**Where his position changed on incline.** In 2021, Chavez et al. — 47 untrained men, incline vs flat
vs both, **once weekly for 8 weeks** on a Smith machine — was
[treated as "the one longitudinal study"](bench-press-enough-chest-growth-24-studies.md) and the basis
for recommending incline, with the notable detail that the combination group's **2 sets of incline
was not enough**; the clavicular benefit required 4. By
[2024](grow-chest-most-detailed-guide-internet-72.md) he takes it apart: **54–62% thickness gains
across eight training sessions** is about **4.6% growth per workout**, against a literature averaging
33 sessions, and **only 30 of the 47 completers appear in the hypertrophy data** with no explanation.
Throwing out the study that best supports his own recommendation is the most creditable ninety
seconds in the domain — and the recommendation survives anyway, on the arithmetic.

**Decline has no case.** Two EMG studies found a **15-degree decline no better than flat** for
lower-pec activity, and others found upper, middle and front delt activity *lower* with the decline —
so its claim to selectivity is that it reduces everything else, not that it raises lower-pec output.
**No longitudinal decline research exists at all.** **Dips** are worse off:
[the chest ranking](best-chest-exercises-building-muscle-33-studies.md) states there is **no
published EMG or hypertrophy comparison of dips to any other chest exercise**, only a comparison
between dip variants, and he declines to conclude anything.

**Flies are not required.** The intuitive argument — remove the triceps and the nervous system can
drive the pecs harder — is not supported: two papers found pec activity during the challenging lower
portion of a dumbbell fly **comparable to flat benching**, and another found pec and front delt
activity similar between barbell bench and machine fly, with the authors noting the clash with the
isolation premise. Flies earn a slot for **programme-design** reasons — chest volume without triceps
volume — not because they stimulate the pecs harder. Within flies: cables in line with the arms load
the short position; a cable set slightly behind the body, dumbbell flies and machine flies load the
stretch; **low cable flies** are his upper-pec pick and **high cable flies and decline dumbbell
flies** the lower-pec one. He kills the "straighter arms stretch the pecs more" argument cleanly —
**the pecs attach to the upper arm, not the forearm, so elbow bend is irrelevant to pec length**.
Note that the 2021 note recommended flies for a different reason: **stretch**, specifically the seated
machine fly, which reaches a longer pec length than the bench press can.

**His speculative picks are the cambered bar bench press, deficit push-ups and stretched-position
partials**, all chosen purely for stretch depth, and he is explicit that **no chest data supports
this** and that two of his own cited studies found more stretch did not mean more growth: deep versus
90-degree squats grew glutes more but quads the same, and partial skull crushers beat full ROM for
the triceps. His stated confidence is "I don't anticipate these being inferior."

## Back and lats

**This is the domain with no growth data at all, and he says so twice, unprompted.**
[Build your lats: the ultimate guide (36 studies)](build-lats-ultimate-guide-36-studies.md) contains
**zero direct measurements of lat growth**. Its 36 studies are moment-arm analyses, mostly isometric
surface EMG, hypertrophy trials **on entirely different muscles** used analogically, and one Reddit
anecdote about chest soreness from very wide pull-ups. **The video has no retrievable reference
list** — sources appear on screen, unnamed, identified only by year and country. Anyone citing it for
a lat exercise ranking is citing a chain of inferences, not an outcome.

**His organising move is good, and it is the part worth keeping: classify by joint action, not by
exercise name.**

- **Close and neutral grips, in both planes** (close-grip pull-ups and pulldowns, chin-ups, close-grip
  barbell rows, seated rows, inverted rows, tucked one-arm rows) are **shoulder extension**, where
  teres major and the lats have the largest moment arms. Because these are hardest near the
  contracted position and the upper lat has better leverage at low shoulder angles, he infers an
  **upper-lat bias**.
- **Wide-grip vertical pulls** shift toward **shoulder adduction**, where teres major, all three lat
  regions and the mid and lower pec retain leverage while the rear delt's drops off rapidly —
  inferred **middle and lower lat bias**.
- **Medium-grip vertical pulls** blend the two and may **cover all lat regions reasonably well**.
  This is his most practically useful conclusion, and it comes from the geometry rather than from any
  study.
- **Wide-grip rows are the one common back exercise he actively argues against for lats.** These are
  shoulder horizontal extension, where the best leverage sits in the **rear delts, infraspinatus and
  supraspinatus**, and the lats are "a lot lower."

**Minimum viable back programme: one shoulder-extension compound plus one shoulder-adduction
compound — or medium-grip vertical pulls alone.** He rates the probability of excellent long-term lat
development on that as "extremely high," and treats isolation work as optional unless you are
maximising or your compound selection is skewed. His case for isolation is an honest extrapolation: a
bench press study found **individuals differ in relative recruitment** of chest, triceps and
shoulders, so someone might get a poor lat stimulus from a compound that works well for someone else.

**Close-grip vertical and horizontal pulls may develop the lats comparably**, despite very different
ranges — vertical pulls move the shoulder roughly 170 to 0 degrees, rows roughly 75 to 0, sometimes
into hyperextension. His argument is that **more range is not automatically better** (three studies
where partials at long muscle lengths beat full ROM) and that vertical pulls, while stretching the
lats more, **are not challenging at the most stretched position**. Note that this sits awkwardly
beside the long-length principle driving his triceps and biceps picks; here he argues the other way.

**Are barbell rows inferior?** Two sEMG studies say maybe — a 2009 Canadian study found greater lat
activity with **inverted rows** than barbell rows (and greater spinal erector activity with the
barbell), and a 2005 Japanese study found slightly greater lat activity with **seated rows** (and
greater trap activity with the barbell). His response is the best passage in the video: he gives the
pro-argument (a barbell row's torso-stabilisation demand diverts effort), then the counter-argument
(the findings may simply reflect sEMG's known limitations, and a barbell row should still be limited
by shoulder extension strength), and then concludes **he does not know**. The only concrete reason to
avoid barbell rows is **spinal erector fatigue** if you already train that area heavily — a
programming reason, not a hypertrophy one.

**Pullovers: machine > cable > free weight, on resistance profile alone.**
[Pullovers do not train lats?](pullovers-not-train-lats-uh-oh.md) — a clip of the lat guide — lays out
the case against them fairly. Two isometric studies show **lat activity highest at low shoulder
elevation and falling progressively as the arm goes overhead**, which is exactly where a free-weight
pullover is hardest, and several sEMG studies report the **middle and lower pec out-activating the
lats** there. He refuses the conclusion on the grounds that **lat leverage overhead is limited, not
zero**, and limited activity plus a deep stretch may still be a real stimulus. His evidence is the
back squat — hardest at the bottom, where the glute max is stretched, poorly levered and shows low
sEMG, yet a Japanese trial found **deeper squats grew the glute max more**. Cable pullovers keep
tension through a greater active range including the low elevation angles where lat activity is
highest; machines get both, **assuming the machine maintains resistance throughout**. He also floats
**high rows with the arm slightly across the body** as possibly the best isolation-adjacent option,
since the lats wrap the rib cage so crossing the body lengthens them — and states plainly that
whether this beats a machine pullover is **unknown**. Separately, the
[chest guide](grow-chest-most-detailed-guide-internet-72.md) lands on "both, probably": the pullover
trains pecs and lats, and he wants direct growth measurement that does not exist.

## Shoulders

### Front delts

**Overhead presses are among the best front delt builders and are not essential.** The
[2025 overhead press note](should-overhead-press-muscle-growth.md) is almost entirely moment-arm and
EMG data, which he flags three separate times. The usable content is the **plane-of-motion**
distinction:

- **Sagittal plane** (narrow barbell grip, dumbbells travelling forward) is **shoulder flexion**,
  where front delt leverage far exceeds side delt. This is the variation for maximising front delt
  while minimising side delt.
- **Frontal and scapular planes** (wide grip, behind-the-neck, dumbbells beside the head,
  lateral-handle machines) are **shoulder abduction**, where front and side delts have comparable
  leverage — though **above 100 degrees of abduction the front delt matches or exceeds** the side
  delt, and overhead pressing holds the arms **externally rotated**, which raises front delt abduction
  leverage while barely affecting the others.

His verdict on necessity: front raises plus horizontal pressing, incline pressing or dips cover the
front delts; lateral raises deliver a better side delt stimulus; triceps options are abundant. So the
overhead press earns its place on **efficiency** — front delts, side delts and two triceps heads in
one movement — not on uniqueness. **Behind-the-neck pressing** he defends, quoting a paper concluding
that for people with normal trunk stability and adequate shoulder range, overhead pressing is safe for
shoulder and spine whether in front of or behind the head. **Upper pec involvement is minimal**: upper
pec activity peaks at a **30-degree incline** and falls as the angle approaches vertical.

### Side delts

Two notes cover this and the newer one supersedes the older on one specific point.
[How to build 3D shoulders (25 studies)](build-3d-shoulders-25-studies.md) (2023) establishes the
leverage picture, and
[Bigger side delts: ranking the best exercises](bigger-side-delts-ranking-best-exercises-using.md)
(2024) turns it into a tier list. **Neither contains a single side delt hypertrophy trial**, and both
say so.

What the compounds do:

- **Bench presses and horizontal pushing:** in horizontal flexion the side delts have **no leverage at
  all** — they have leverage for the opposite motion.
- **Narrow-grip rows:** shoulder extension, **no side delt leverage**.
- **Wide-grip rows:** horizontal extension, where **rear delts have up to three times** the side
  delts' leverage.
- **Narrow-grip vertical presses:** shoulder flexion, where **front delt leverage is typically three
  to four times** the side delt's.
- **Wide-grip presses or dumbbells beside the head:** shoulder abduction, where front and side delts
  are **fairly comparable** — good, but probably not enough to maximise.

**Lateral raises are where the side delt has the best leverage of any muscle**, dominating from
**31 to 90 degrees** of abduction. The 2024 tier list ranks by **where in the range the exercise is
hardest**:

- **S tier: leaning or lying dumbbell lateral raises, cable lateral raises with a high (hand-height)
  setup, and good machine lateral raises** — all three shift peak resistance toward a lengthened side
  delt. His shortcut for finding the hardest point: **with free weights, where the moving segment is
  parallel to the ground; with cables, where cable and segment form 90 degrees.**
- **A tier: the standard dumbbell lateral raise** — it works, since the side delt has excellent
  abduction leverage throughout, but it is hardest at 90 degrees where the muscle is moderately
  shortened — **and internally rotated "empty the can" raises**, which EMG puts level with neutral,
  with external rotation slightly worse.
- **A-minus: super-ROM (Larsen-style) raises past 90 degrees**, because the extra range sits at short
  lengths, most people must externally rotate to clear the humeral head, and past 90 degrees the front
  delt matches or exceeds the side delt — **and upright rows**, which he predicts will surprise
  viewers.
- **B tier: externally rotated raises** (external rotation appears to raise the *front* delt's
  abduction leverage) **and abduction-dominant overhead presses**.
- **C tier: raises with the elbow bent to 90 degrees** — flagged low confidence, one study, ranked
  against his own intuition. **D tier: flexion-dominant overhead presses.**

**Two upgrades for the standard dumbbell raise:** continue past failure with **progressively shorter
partials** (your range shrinks rep by rep, so you increasingly load long lengths), or do **lengthened
partials** at the bottom only.

**Two things he refuses to endorse:** the **scapular plane** over the frontal plane (leverages barely
differ, and if anything the scapular plane brings in more front delt), and **seated or braced raises
for stability** (the stability research strapped subjects down on a leg extension; back squats are
less stable than hack squats with similar growth; seated overhead pressing did not out-activate
standing).

**Where he was wrong, and says so.**
[We have to talk about lateral raises](have-talk-about-lateral-raises.md) covers the trial he himself
helped run: **24 trained individuals, average 7.1 years' experience**, within-subject, one arm cable
and one arm dumbbell lateral raise, **5 sets to failure at 12–16 reps, twice weekly for 8 weeks**,
range matched at 0–90 degrees, cuffs used to remove grip fatigue. **Side delt growth was similar at
both the proximal and distal regions.** He had predicted cables would win on resistance-profile
reasoning, and the prediction failed. His caveats are unprompted, and the most important is that
**total growth in both conditions was small and not far off measurement error**, so the trial may
simply have been underpowered. His practical line is worth keeping in spirit: none of these choices
costs you anything — "it's pretty hard to do something tragically wrong."

**Upright rows and impingement.** Both shoulder notes spend several minutes dismantling the safety
case against upright rows, and it is the only shoulder section resting on outcome data:

1. Compression from above should damage the **upper** side of the supraspinatus tendon; damage is
   found predominantly on the **underside**.
2. **Hooked acromions do not predict shoulder problems**, and smaller acromiohumeral distances do not
   correlate with pain in people who have subacromial pain.
3. The common "stay below 90 degrees" advice is **backwards** — the smallest acromiohumeral distances
   occur between **40 and 75 degrees**, so if impingement worked as claimed, all lateral raises would
   be dangerous.
4. **Internal rotation increases or does not affect** acromiohumeral distance.
5. The **Hawkins-Kennedy test** has poor specificity and little diagnostic value.
6. **Subacromial decompression surgery does not outperform placebo surgery** for pain, function or
   quality of life, across a body of literature including a Cochrane review.

He supports the field's shift from "impingement" to "subacromial pain", cites a 2021 paper calling
the impingement theory antiquated, and closes correctly: pain is multifactorial, someone reports pain
from every exercise ever invented, and **pain is not proof of danger** — if it hurts, use one of the
many alternatives.

### Rear delts

[How to build your rear delts (28 studies)](build-rear-delts-28-studies.md) contains **no rear delt
hypertrophy trial** and labels its rankings speculation. It is also the note where he most visibly
**attacks his own tool**: moment-arm measurement is imprecise, methods vary between labs, the two
studies of lat leverage in shoulder extension **disagree sharply**, neither accounted for the lats
wrapping the posterior chest wall, and individuals differ anatomically. He keeps the tool, but the
caveats come before the conclusions.

**Compound ranking:** wide-grip horizontal pulls > narrow-grip horizontal pulls > narrow-grip vertical
pulls > wide-grip vertical pulls, with supinated vertical pulls somewhere between the last two. The
logic: **wide-grip rows are shoulder horizontal extension, where the rear delts have the greatest
leverage of any muscle** (Kuechle), supported by **fine-wire** EMG — which he takes more seriously
than surface EMG because the needle minimises crosstalk and follows the tissue as it moves. Narrow
grips are shoulder extension, where **teres major has the best leverage** and the rear delts are
merely respectable. Wide-grip vertical pulls are adduction, where **most studies give the rear delts
minimal to no leverage**, and a 2002 sEMG paper found narrow-grip pulldowns out-activating wide — a
case where he accepts an sEMG result *because the leverage data agrees*, which is the right
weighting. He also ranks narrow-grip horizontal pulls above narrow-grip vertical pulls because rows
reach **shoulder hyperextension**, which vertical pulls do not.

**Isolation ranking**, driven entirely by where the exercise is hardest: **cable reverse flies and
dumbbell "power" raises** (horizontal extension attained around the lengthened position) > **machine
reverse flies and face pulls** (the machine spreads resistance more evenly; face pulls are hardest
shortened but add external rotation, which the rear delts also perform) > **dumbbell reverse flies**
(hardest at the shortened end position).

**Practical advice is unusually restrained:** recreational lifters can skip rear delt isolation
entirely, train the pulls they like, **reassess after several months to a year**, and add isolation
only if unsatisfied. Anyone maximising should add it on top of pulls. The supporting evidence is
Kassiano's hip thrust study (33 untrained women, 10 weeks — adding a targeted exercise improved
gluteus maximus growth), and note that the added-exercise group **also performed more total sets**,
so the study cannot separate the two — a confound he does not flag.

One footnote both shoulder notes carry: the deltoid divides into **seven segments** rather than three,
documented as early as **1734**, with segment 1 the front head, 2–3 the side head and **4–7 the rear
head**. Nobody knows whether particular exercises preferentially grow particular segments, so he
cannot claim his picks train all four rear segments.

## Biceps and elbow flexors

Seven notes cover this and it is the most argued-over set in the library, partly because he changed
his mind twice.

**Nearly every curl works.** The [73-study guide](ultimate-guide-biceps-growth-73-studies.md) tables
five studies — 36 trained subjects on machine versus EZ-bar curls (8 weeks, comparable at ~65% upper
arm length); cable versus barbell preacher curl (10 weeks, comparable at 50%); dumbbell curls alone
(**+13.3% CSA**); cable preacher curls alone (**+16–20% across three regions**); barbell curl alone
(growth at all three regions) — and gives the mechanical reason: **all of them load elbow flexion
with supination**. The measurement caveat he repeats throughout is important and rarely stated
elsewhere: averaging six MRI studies, **the biceps is only 46% of elbow flexor volume**, against
brachialis 37% and brachioradialis 17%, so "elbow flexor growth" is roughly half biceps and every
percentage above should be read with that dilution in mind.

**His pick is a curl with the shoulder extended** — incline dumbbell or face-away (Bayesian) cable
curl — on the reasoning that shoulder extension lengthens the long head, which crosses the shoulder.
[The most effective biceps exercise](most-effective-biceps-exercise-not-think.md) lays out the three
supporting lines and grades each: three **isometric long-versus-short-length studies, all on the
quadriceps**; two partial-ROM preacher curl studies (**Sato 2021** and **Pedrosa 2023**) where the
**stretched half beat the shortened half**, with the difference concentrated at the **70% upper-arm
region**; and the triceps overhead-extension analogy. He handles the counter-argument — that biceps
sarcomere length-tension data says the biceps should *not* respond to stretch — by noting the
identical argument was made about the triceps and was falsified by Maeo. **No study has ever compared
incline or face-away curls against another curl for growth**, and he frames the whole thing as a
hypothesis.

**Then the evidence arrived, and it is messy.** Four studies now compare shoulder-extended against
shoulder-flexed curls, and the notes track his reading in real time.

**2023** ([Incline vs preacher curls, new research](incline-vs-preacher-curls-biceps-growth-2.md)):
**31 women with some training experience**, 4 sets of 6–18 to failure, three times weekly. Growth
**tended to favour preacher at all three regions** — but **nothing was significant**, sessions were
unsupervised, failure was undefined, and the measurement site includes brachialis. He opens by saying
the study raises the possibility he was wrong. His reframing is the useful part: **preacher curls are
hardest at the start where the biceps are longest; incline curls are hardest as the biceps shortens**
— so if preacher really wins, the operative variable is not *being* stretched but **being loaded
while stretched**. That hypothesis is immediately contradicted by **Nunes 2020** (barbell versus cable
preacher curls, hardest at opposite ends, **similar growth** — one region only).

**2025** ([Incline vs preacher curls, new study](incline-vs-preacher-curls-biceps-growth.md)):
**Kassiano 2025**, 63 previously untrained women, 3 sets of 8–12 to momentary failure, twice weekly
for 8 weeks, 0–120 degrees of elbow flexion. **The 50% region favoured incline, 60% was similar, 70%
favoured preacher.** A **conference abstract only** (Kobayashi, 21 previously untrained adults,
within-subject, 12 weeks) separates the muscles: **biceps volume greater with incline** (differences
at 20–40% of biceps length), **brachialis greater with preacher** (70–80%), **brachioradialis greater
with preacher** (60%). A blood-flow-restriction study (**21 men averaging two years' training, three
weeks**) found no regional differences, and he discounts it on duration. His tally: **biceps 2 for
incline and 2 null; brachialis 3 pointing to preacher; brachioradialis 1 for preacher.** He notices
and rejects the obvious alternative reading — that the two null studies used trained subjects and the
two favouring incline used untrained — preferring to weigh study quality, which is a defensible
judgement rather than a settled one.

**2025** ([Bigger biceps: this new study is interesting](bigger-biceps-new-study-interesting.md)): the
first trial to **match the resistance profiles** — 15 untrained men, within-subject, Bayesian cable
curl versus preacher curl **both set to peak at roughly 60 degrees of elbow flexion**, 3–5 sets of
8–12, twice weekly for 10 weeks, with **biceps and brachialis measured separately** rather than lumped
as elbow flexors. **No significant difference anywhere; both arms grew at every region.** He reads
the null as underpowered rather than empty (n=15), and notes that the Kobayashi abstract puts the
largest biceps difference at **20–40% of biceps length** while this study's highest measurement site
was **55%** — the effect may live above where they looked.

**Where this lands:** **include a shoulder-extended curl** — worst case you build the same amount of
muscle, best case slightly more — and **combining a shoulder-extended curl with a preacher curl is
defensible** for complete elbow flexor development, since the regional data hint that the preacher
serves the brachialis and brachioradialis better. His closing position is the honest one: **the
differences between curl variations are small and nowhere near the top of the list of things that
drive growth.**

**The brachialis deserves its own note.**
[This arm muscle is underrated](arm-muscle-underrated-don-t-ignore.md) reports that **exactly one
study has measured brachialis hypertrophy directly** — O'Hagan 1995, underhand machine preacher
curls, **biceps +8.3% CSA, brachialis +37.6%**. Everything else is pump data, combined elbow-flexor
measurements and moment arms. The practical content: the brachialis is a **pure elbow flexor whose
moment arm stays close to the biceps' throughout the range**, so **every curl trains it well** and it
needs no special exercise. **Neutral or overhand grips likely bias it relative to the biceps** —
the biceps loses supination leverage while the brachialis's leverage is unchanged — but there is **no
research comparing neutral to overhand**, and the one study showing large brachialis growth used an
**underhand** grip. He also notes the long-length-partial escape route: if partials at long lengths
beat full ROM, doing them on *any* curl challenges the brachialis at long lengths and levels the
variations.

**Does pulling count for the arms?** His position moved, and the newer note is more permissive.

**2021** ([Are rows and lat pulldowns enough](rows-lat-pulldowns-enough-biceps-growth.md)):
**Mannarino**, 10 untrained men, within-subject, dumbbell row versus dumbbell curl, 4–6 sets of 8–12
to failure, twice weekly for 8 weeks, measured at 25%, 50% and 75% and averaged — **the curl arm
gained about 11%, the row arm about 5%**. Both used a **supinated** grip, which should if anything
have helped the rows. **Gentil 2015**, 29 untrained men, lat pulldown versus barbell curl, 10 weeks —
**about 6% each, no significant difference**, but measured at a **single site 10 cm from the cubital
fossa**. He refuses to pool the two studies across papers and explains exactly why: volume differed
(4–6 sets versus 3), measurement differed, and Hubal's 243 men and 342 women show individual response
spread so wide that two small samples are unlikely to be comparable. His conclusion — rows and
pulldowns are not enough — is **one inferential step removed from the data**, and he says so: no group
in Costa trained biceps with compounds only.

**2025** ([Compound exercises are enough for the biceps](compound-exercises-enough-biceps.md)): a
two-experiment paper. Experiment two — **9 previously untrained men**, within-subject, dumbbell row
versus dumbbell curl (again with a **supinated grip on the row**), 4–6 sets of 8–12 to failure, twice
weekly for 8 weeks — found **growth at 75% greater with curls, growth at 25% similar**. So **back work
is not a null stimulus for the arms**. What survives is the weaker and better-supported claim that
**compounds cannot maximise elbow flexor size**, because they miss the upper region and cap how much
arm volume you can add without also fatiguing the back. He also confirmed with the authors that
**participants overlap** with the same team's 2019 paper. The same note handles **the pump as a
proxy** and refuses to endorse it: swelling predicted growth at one region and not the other here,
partially in a bench press paper (flat produced more mid-pec swelling than incline, matching the
growth literature; upper-pec swelling was similar, which does not), and correctly in a calf paper —
roughly half the time. A useful number from it: untrained subjects went from **8.6% acute pec
swelling to 14.2% real growth in 8 weeks**, so a beginner genuinely out-grows an early pump inside
two months.

**Three smaller points from the guide worth keeping.** The **peak is genetic** — he makes the point by
contrasting Arnold's and Sergio Oliva's very different biceps shapes at comparable development.
**Anatomy is more variable than textbooks suggest**: roughly **36% of upper limbs have three, four or
five biceps heads**. And **no research measures long and short head hypertrophy separately**, so head
biasing cannot be confirmed — his best contribution there is dismantling the **seesaw assumption**
that biasing one head starves the other, using the seated-versus-lying leg curl case where the biceps
femoris long head grew more while the **short head grew equally**.

## Triceps

**The triceps are roughly 2.5 times the size of the biceps**, so they dominate arm size — and they
are the one upper-body muscle where the exercise-selection evidence is genuinely good.

**Pressing does not build the long head. This is the best-evidenced claim in the whole domain.**
Brandão 2020 — **43 untrained men**, four groups (bench only, skull crusher only, bench then skull
crusher, skull crusher then bench), **80% 1RM to failure, 3 min rest, twice weekly for 10 weeks**,
sets ramping 3 to 4 to 5 across the block, MRI cross-sectional area by head — is covered in
[Is the bench press enough for triceps hypertrophy](bench-press-enough-triceps-hypertrophy.md) and
again in [Build your triceps faster](build-triceps-faster-science-simplified.md):

- **Lateral head:** favoured the **bench** groups over skull-crushers-only.
- **Long head:** significantly favoured the **skull crusher** groups over bench-only.
- **Medial head:** statistically similar, but roughly **double** in the skull crusher groups.
- **Whole triceps:** statistically similar, but bench-only gained about **half** the percentage of
  the three groups doing skull crushers.
- **Doing both grew all three heads.** The bench press is not redundant — it is the exercise doing the
  lateral head work.

**The mechanism is clean:** the long head is the only head crossing the shoulder, and pressing
requires shoulder flexion, so a strong long-head contraction would fight the lift. **Wakahara 2013**
supports it from another direction — the neutral-grip dumbbell bench press, which moves in the same
plane as close-grip barbell benching, showed high lateral and medial head activity, low long head
activity, and after 12 weeks of training it **long head thickness did not increase**. That kills the
"just use a close grip" fix, and there is **no research at all** comparing wide to close grip benching
for triceps hypertrophy, only inconsistent EMG. The same logic extends to incline, decline, machine
presses, push-ups and dips, and — tentatively, with no direct research — to **overhead presses**, per
the [overhead press note](should-overhead-press-muscle-growth.md), which adds that the lateral and
medial heads should do well in any press.

**Two cautions on Brandão.** He twice **overrides a non-significant result** by pointing at percentage
differences and calling it type II error, in a study with about **10–11 subjects per cell**. And the
two combined-exercise groups performed **double the total set volume**, so only bench-only versus
skull-crusher-only is genuinely volume-matched.

**Which isolation exercise: the overhead extension.**
[The best triceps hypertrophy exercise](best-triceps-hypertrophy-exercise.md) covers Maeo as it broke:
**21 adults**, within-subject, one arm overhead cable extension and the other cable pushdown, **range
of motion matched at 0–90 degrees**, supinated grip, **5 sets of 10, 2 min rest, twice weekly for 12
weeks**, **alternating which arm went first each session**, MRI muscle volume. Results: whole triceps
**1.4x**, long head **1.5x**, **medial and lateral combined 1.4x** — all favouring overhead,
**despite heavier absolute loads on the pushdown throughout**. The medial and lateral result has **no
accepted explanation**; the paper's two speculations (the long head sitting on the descending limb of
its force-length curve overhead, and restricted blood flow raising metabolic stress) he treats
sceptically, the second because metabolic stress generally fails to track hypertrophy.

**Stasinaki 2018** (**n=9, 6 weeks**) was null on the long head, and he handles the comparison
properly: a third the subjects, half the duration, and — critically — it **always ran pushdowns first
each session**, so residual fatigue could have blunted the overhead arm, whereas Maeo alternated,
which he calls the perfect approach. Its one useful signal is regional: long head thickness at 50%
and 60% and CSA at the **lower** portion favoured overhead, while CSA at the **upper** portion
favoured **pushdowns**, hinting that the two exercises grow different regions. He labels that a
hypothesis, not a prescription.

**Practical:** **if you do one isolation exercise, do an overhead extension.** If you do more,
overhead plus pushdown may develop the long head more evenly, and overhead plus skull crusher is also
defensible. Skull crushers stretch the long head more than pushdowns but less than overhead
extensions, and there is **no direct comparison** between them. The free-weight overhead version rests
on a **five-person study** — a very thin basis. **Costa 2021** offers modest support for variety
(22 subjects, three times weekly for 8 weeks, pushdowns only versus pushdown / seated overhead
extension / kickback across the week; triceps thickness across three regions **tended to** favour the
varied group), and he immediately concedes the effect could be **entirely the seated extension**
training the long head at a longer length rather than variation as such.

**Two of his own positions were retired here.** The
[63-study triceps guide](ultimate-guide-building-triceps-63-studies.md) (2021) had only the n=9 null
and hedged correctly — "more research would be needed to confirm" — and he **adopted Maeo by 2023**.
That same guide also treats the pushdown's hardest-at-lockout profile as a **virtue**, complementary
to the skull crusher; by the [2024 chest guide](grow-chest-most-detailed-guide-internet-72.md) he
argues that **tension near the shortened position adds little**. Both readings cannot stand, and the
newer one is his current view.

**What the 2021 guide still contributes**, with its reference gap noted. The fibre-type argument for
heavy triceps work fails (5–35 reps to failure grow similarly, and 2–4RM was slightly worse than
8–12RM). The **recovery objection to high frequency does not survive**: Chen's eccentric-only protocol
showed large triceps force loss and soreness, but the identical protocol two weeks later produced far
less, and **Akagi** found triceps shear modulus **no different from an untrained control group and no
different from baseline** by week 6 of skull crusher training. **Frequency is genuinely unresolved**,
with five studies pointing four ways — Brigatto found 1x = 2x at 16 weekly sets, Saric 3x = 6x at 12,
Lasevicius **2x better than 3x** at 24, Schoenfeld **3x better than 2x** at 15, and Zaroni **5x better
than 2x** at 30. He refuses to pick, and offers Damas's within-subject design as the explanation:
**6 subjects responded better to high frequency and volume, 7 better to low, 6 similarly.** Its volume
recommendation of **30–45 weekly sets with short rests, or 14–28 with long rests**, is far above his
own standing 10–20 per muscle, is explicitly conditional on short rests and trained subjects, and he
questions its long-term sustainability himself. On hypoxia techniques he lands on "probably fine, not
established": two studies for (Goto's constant-tension partial-ROM skull crushers, where **the degree
of hypoxia in individual subjects correlated with their CSA increase**, and Fink's drop sets, the only
study at the time finding drop sets superior), three lines against (Tanimoto's no-lockout chest press,
and two blood-flow-restriction bench press papers).

## Forearms

**No study has ever measured forearm hypertrophy from any exercise.** Both forearm notes state this
outright, and the [12-minute tier list](bigger-forearms-ranking-best-exercises-using-science.md)
closes with "please don't consider these rankings as 100% definitely true." The
[39-minute guide](build-forearms-faster-most-detailed-guide-web.md) is the better of the two and is
the only note in this entire set with a complete, properly resolved reference list.

**Do you need direct forearm work?** Partly. Vertical pulls, horizontal pulls and deadlifts all demand
sustained static finger flexion, which trains the flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus; curls
load the wrist flexors isometrically and lateral raises the wrist extensors. But the argument for
direct work follows from effort-primacy: **in compound lifts the forearms are rarely the limiting
factor, so they never approach their own failure point** — and if they are the limiting factor, you
are compromising the exercise's real target, so use straps and train forearms separately.

**The whole ranking runs on one anatomical fact and one transferred principle.** The fact: **every
anterior-compartment muscle except the two pronators contributes to wrist flexion, and all of them
have good moment arms for it** (Gonzalez 1997) — so **one movement pattern trains nearly all the
mass**, and the anterior compartment is the larger half. The principle: **training at longer muscle
lengths builds more muscle**, imported from Maeo 2021 on hamstrings and Kassiano 2023 on calves.
Everything else is joint geometry. He is explicit that **all of the long-length research is on
untrained subjects**, which means his top-ranked exercises are recommended to trained lifters on
untested grounds, and he foregrounds the trained-subject lengthened-partials study that cuts against
him rather than burying it.

**Wrist flexion.** The flexors are longest with the wrist extended, so **standing wrist curls sit in C
tier** — they travel only neutral-to-flexed and leave the fingers flexed. **Forearm-supported wrist
curls across a bench reach genuine wrist extension and hit A tier.** **Adding fat grips, or letting
the load roll down to the finger bases and then flexing fingers and wrist together, moves either up a
tier** — S tier for the supported version — because the **flexor digitorum superficialis and
profundus are the two largest forearm muscles** (Holzbaur 2007) and they **cross the fingers as well
as the wrist**, so they only reach full length when both joints extend. Fat grips hold the extended
finger position throughout; the roll-to-fingertips version reaches greater peak finger extension but
only momentarily, and he declines to separate them. He also raises the objection against himself —
does adding finger flexion compromise the other wrist flexors? — and answers no on the seated-leg-curl
precedent, while saying plainly that it is not an identical comparison.

**Wrist extension** is the mirror image: standing wrist extensions are A tier, **supported extensions
across a bench are S tier**, reaching genuine wrist flexion. Here **finger flexion around the bar is
good**, since several extensors attach to the fingers and are longer when the fingers are flexed — so
fat grips would be counterproductive.

**The load objection, answered well:** you cannot go as heavy on finger-flexion wrist curls, but
**Maeo 2023 found overhead extensions out-grew pushdowns despite heavier loads on the pushdown
throughout** — heavier does not mean more stimulus. And the practical caveat is one worth carrying:
**if you dislike the top-ranked variation, the lower-ranked ones still work**; meaningful hypertrophy
is documented at shorter muscle lengths, and taking a movement you will actually do to near failure is
not a wasted set.

**What he tells you to skip**, which is the most distinctive part of the guide. **Grip training,
radial and ulnar deviation, and pronation/supination work are all classified as non-essential.**
Isometric grip work trains finger flexion, already covered by wrist curls; five of the seven deviation
muscles are already trained, and the two exceptions (abductor pollicis longus, extensor pollicis
brevis) are small enough that growing them has "a trivial impact on your physique." He spends roughly
five minutes telling you what not to bother with. Where grip work is done anyway, the same length
logic ranks **fat-grip holds and plate pinches above normal holds and farmer's walks**, and **pure
finger curls with the wrist held extended** above hand grippers.

**The brachioradialis section overturns standard advice, and he flags it as controversial.** The
folklore — that neutral or pronated grips (hammer curls, reverse curls) bias the brachioradialis —
**fails to replicate**: **2 studies favour pronated, 3 find no difference, and 4 favour supinated**,
with the most recent (10 competitive bodybuilders performing cable curls at three grips) finding
**both biceps and brachioradialis activity tending higher supinated**. His explanation for why lifters
feel the opposite is genuinely interesting: **during pronated curls the load pulls the wrist into
flexion, so the wrist extensors work hard to resist — and they sit right next to the
brachioradialis**, so the sensation may be misattributed. The supporting growth evidence is one
uncontrolled study (Erskine 2014, where biceps, brachialis and brachioradialis all grew significantly
and similarly from preacher curls, which compared nothing). He says he is "completely open to being
utterly wrong," and gives the sensible out: if pulls and at least one curl have not given you the
brachioradialis you want, add curl volume or experiment with neutral and pronated grips **"even though
the present evidence doesn't support it."** For *which* curl, the
[tier list](bigger-forearms-ranking-best-exercises-using-science.md) leans on an **unpublished
conference abstract** finding better brachioradialis growth from preacher than incline curls,
significant only at the 60% region; the guide leaves the question genuinely open between "harder at
the top" (Murray 1995 moment arms put the brachioradialis's leverage advantage highest at high elbow
flexion angles) and "harder at the bottom" (long-length training generally), and answers "no published
research can settle this — pick the curl you prefer."

## Weak points

**The reference problem is worst where the confidence is highest.** Eight of these twenty-eight notes
have **no retrievable reference list at all**, and they include every one of his flagship long-form
guides. The [73-study biceps guide](ultimate-guide-biceps-growth-73-studies.md) is the worst case: no
list, and an **auto-captioned** transcript, so even the author names ("Martorelli", "Mannarino",
"Dankel", "Mao") are caption guesses. **The Martorelli failure-versus-non-failure result — the single
most consequential finding in that video, and arguably in this whole domain — is unverifiable.** The
same applies to the [63-study triceps guide](ultimate-guide-building-triceps-63-studies.md) (its
30–45 weekly set figure, its five-study frequency table and its fibre-subpopulation argument), the
[36-study lat guide](build-lats-ultimate-guide-36-studies.md) (sources named only by year and
country), the [72-study chest guide](grow-chest-most-detailed-guide-internet-72.md) (which says the
references "do not fit" and points to an off-site page), the
[33-study chest ranking](best-chest-exercises-building-muscle-33-studies.md), the
[upper-chest clip](blow-upper-chest-exercises.md) (so the 5-of-7, 4-of-8 and 1-of-2 counts cannot be
checked), the [pullover clip](pullovers-not-train-lats-uh-oh.md), and the
[forearm tier list](bigger-forearms-ranking-best-exercises-using-science.md). Where the sections above
give a number from one of these, treat it as reported rather than verified.

**Three exercise hierarchies contain no hypertrophy study on the target muscle.** The
[lat guide](build-lats-ultimate-guide-36-studies.md), the
[rear delt guide](build-rear-delts-28-studies.md) and both side delt notes
([2023](build-3d-shoulders-25-studies.md),
[2024](bigger-side-delts-ranking-best-exercises-using.md)) rank exercises on moment arms, isometric
sEMG and analogy to quad, glute, hamstring, calf and triceps trials. Each says so — the lat guide
twice, unprompted — but **the tier-list format communicates far more certainty than the underlying
evidence supports**, which is the single most important thing to carry into any answer about back or
shoulder exercise selection. The lat regional-bias claims are the weakest link of all: a moment-arm
model chained to isometric sEMG **at a joint angle the exercise never actually reaches** (20 degrees
of abduction, for the wide-grip adduction claim), then to regional growth nobody has measured.

**The one prediction from that method that got tested, failed.** The 2023 and 2024 side delt notes
both argued cables should beat dumbbells because the cable is hardest where the delt is longest. The
[trial he helped run](have-talk-about-lateral-raises.md) found **no difference**. He reports the
failure himself, and it is the strongest available warning about how much weight to put on
resistance-profile reasoning generally — including his S-tier calls for leaning and lying lateral
raises, which rest on exactly the same logic and have never been tested.

**The EMG rule is his own invention.** "EMG is more trustworthy for near-identical variations than for
dissimilar exercises" is a plausible principle generalised from **two data points** — the calf-raise
foot-position case where EMG predicted growth correctly, and the squat-versus-hip-thrust case where it
did not. It is doing enormous work: it licenses the whole upper-pec grip and angle section, the
brachioradialis conclusion, the lateral-raise rotation rankings, the Smith machine verdict and the
behind-the-neck press call. It has never been validated.

**He breaks his own EMG standard once, in the same video where he sets it.** The
[biceps guide](ultimate-guide-biceps-growth-73-studies.md) spends three minutes rejecting sEMG for
exercise comparison — with two excellent examples, high rectus femoris sEMG in squats that produces
no significant rectus femoris growth, and higher quad sEMG in short-length leg extension isometrics
that produce less growth than long-length ones — and then builds the **supination argument** on a 1998
fine-wire EMG study. That argument then does real work: it drives his discounting of pronated-grip
pulldowns and rows and his enthusiasm for chin-ups. Fine-wire is a better instrument, and comparing
grips within one movement is his own "subtle comparison" case — but it is still **activation, not
growth**, and he does not acknowledge the tension.

**Type II error is invoked to rescue results that went the wrong way.** In
[Is the bench press enough for triceps hypertrophy](bench-press-enough-triceps-hypertrophy.md) he
twice overrides non-significant whole-triceps and medial-head results by pointing at percentage
differences, in a study with **10–11 subjects per cell** — exactly the situation where percentages are
unstable in either direction. He does the same in
[Bigger biceps](bigger-biceps-new-study-interesting.md), reading an n=15 null as underpowered rather
than empty. The reading is defensible in both cases and he is transparent that it is *his* reading,
but it is the same move each time and it always favours his prior.

**Recommendations that rest on nothing.** The **grip-width (1.5–2x shoulder) and elbow-flare** advice
in [the 2021 bench note](bench-press-enough-chest-growth-24-studies.md) is derived purely from where
the pec's moment arm peaks in a modelling study; no study has compared chest growth between grip
widths. The upper-pec fix in
[the flat bench regional note](effective-flat-bench-press-upper-middle-lower.md) rests entirely on
moment-arm anatomy and is **presented as the practical conclusion without being labelled untested** —
one of the few places he does not flag his own inference. The
[chest ranking](best-chest-exercises-building-muscle-33-studies.md) promises "the best chest
exercises" and names two — the cambered bar and deficit push-ups — with **zero direct evidence**, on a
mechanism (stretch) that its own cited studies show does not reliably transfer. And in
[the 2023 incline-vs-preacher note](incline-vs-preacher-curls-biceps-growth-2.md) the **face-away
cable curl** pick is explicitly mechanism reasoning with no supporting data at all. Add to that
[the 2021 triceps guide](ultimate-guide-building-triceps-63-studies.md)'s fibre-subpopulation
argument, which justifies "2–3 isolation exercises at varying shoulder angles" by extrapolating twice
from a study that examined elbow extension, supination and pronation — not exercises, not shoulder
angles, not growth.

**Errors and internal contradictions in the underlying notes**, worth knowing before quoting a number:

- **Costa 2021 is described four different ways** across four notes — "22 detrained men (no training
  in the prior 6 months)" in the [triceps guide](ultimate-guide-building-triceps-63-studies.md),
  "22 trained men... nine weeks" in
  [rows and pulldowns](rows-lat-pulldowns-enough-biceps-growth.md), "22 people... 8 weeks" in
  [build your triceps faster](build-triceps-faster-science-simplified.md), and "trained men... 8
  weeks" in [Bigger biceps](bigger-biceps-new-study-interesting.md). The rows note already flags the
  eight-versus-nine-week slip; **the trained/detrained conflict is not flagged anywhere** and it
  matters, because that study is the main support for exercise variety in both arms.
- **Maeo's subject composition differs between notes** — "21 untrained adults (14 men, 7 women)" in
  [the best triceps exercise](best-triceps-hypertrophy-exercise.md) versus "21 men" in
  [build your triceps faster](build-triceps-faster-science-simplified.md).
- **Chavez is summarised differently in the two chest notes.** The
  [2021 note](bench-press-enough-chest-growth-24-studies.md) says the incline group gained more
  clavicular thickness while **sternal and costal gains were similar across all three groups**; the
  [2024 guide](grow-chest-most-detailed-guide-internet-72.md) says the incline-only group saw the best
  **upper, middle *and* lower** pec growth. Those are different results from the same study, and the
  second is the version he uses to argue the study is physiologically implausible.
- **The Davies data appears in two incompatible framings.** In
  [the 2021 bench note](bench-press-enough-chest-growth-24-studies.md) and
  [the regional note](effective-flat-bench-press-upper-middle-lower.md) it is a straightforward
  trained-lifter regional study (4x5 at 85% 1RM, 5 min rest, 8 weeks, n=10); in
  [the chest ranking](best-chest-exercises-building-muscle-33-studies.md) the same design appears as
  the **normal-set arm of a cluster-set comparison**, an arm the earlier notes never mention. It is
  also a **university thesis rather than a peer-reviewed paper**, which only the regional note says.
- **Mannarino is dated 2018 in the biceps guide** and **2021** in the reference lists of the two notes
  that cite it properly. The 2018 date comes from auto-captions and should not be quoted.
- **Two of his own notes disagree about the supraspinatus.** The
  [2023 shoulder note](build-3d-shoulders-25-studies.md) gives the supraspinatus the best leverage
  from 0–30 degrees of abduction; the
  [2024 side delt ranking](bigger-side-delts-ranking-best-exercises-using.md) says the "supraspinatus
  does the first 15 degrees" textbook claim **is misleading** and that it does not appear to activate
  earlier than the side delt — which is the load-bearing premise for training the delt at long lengths
  at all. **The 2024 version is newer.**
- **Regional curl findings point opposite ways across notes.** The
  [biceps guide](ultimate-guide-biceps-growth-73-studies.md) reports a 2011 Brazilian barbell curl
  study finding the **largest gains at the 50% region**, less at 60% and 70%; the
  [2025 compound note](compound-exercises-enough-biceps.md) reports curls growing the **75% region
  more than the 25%**. Both may be true of different exercises and different subjects, but the notes
  never reconcile them, and the regional attributions elsewhere in the biceps set depend on which is
  right.
- **The rep-range floor is stated as 5–35 in the 2021 triceps guide** and as **6–35** everywhere
  newer. The channel landed on 6–35; the older figure should not be quoted as current.
- The [forearm guide](build-forearms-faster-most-detailed-guide-web.md) flags two of its own reference
  problems honestly: an "Ackland et al." entry that resolves to a systematic review of
  **glenohumeral** moment arms inside a forearm video (probably a mis-paste), and a study it dates to
  1986 that PubMed dates **1987**.
- **The rear delt isolation case has an unflagged confound**: in
  [the rear delt guide](build-rear-delts-28-studies.md), the Kassiano group that added a hip thrust
  and grew more glute max also **performed more total sets**, so "adding a targeted exercise helps"
  cannot be separated from "doing more volume helps."

**Where the whole set is thinnest, in one line each.** Chest: everything regional is EMG. Back:
everything is leverage, and there is no growth study on any back exercise for any back muscle.
Shoulders: no side or rear delt hypertrophy trial exists except the cable-versus-dumbbell null.
Biceps: the best evidence runs on untrained subjects and mostly cannot separate biceps from
brachialis. Triceps: this is the strong one — real MRI outcomes, within-subject designs, and a
mechanism that predicted a result before it was tested. Forearms: nothing has ever been measured.
