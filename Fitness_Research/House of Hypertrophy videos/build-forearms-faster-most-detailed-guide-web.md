# Training the Forearms: Wrist Curls, Grip Work and the Brachioradialis

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Build Your Forearms FASTER: The Most Detailed Guide on the Web" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u6DJVoGSrh4)) · 2024-07-01 · 39 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **There is no research comparing wrist curl variations for hypertrophy.** Every exercise ranking in this video is extrapolated from long-muscle-length research done on hamstrings, calves and triceps. He states this repeatedly and rates his own rankings as speculation.
- **If you do one forearm exercise, make it a seated wrist curl** with the forearm resting on a surface — it trains the most forearm tissue, because essentially every anterior-compartment muscle has good leverage for wrist flexion.
- His top pick is the **finger-flexion wrist curl** (let the load roll down to the finger bases, then flex fingers and wrist together). Rationale: the **flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus are the two largest forearm muscles**, and they lengthen with both wrist *and* finger extension, so this variation reaches the longest length available.
- **The brachioradialis section overturns the standard advice.** The EMG literature does **not** support neutral or pronated grips: 2 studies favoured pronated, **3 found no difference, and 4 favoured supinated**. He calls this a controversial take and invites contradiction.
- **Grip training, radial/ulnar deviation, and pronation/supination work are all classified as non-essential** — the muscles are either already covered by wrist curls or too small to matter for physique. He does not pretend otherwise to fill runtime.

## Do you need direct forearm work?

Partly no. Vertical pulls, horizontal pulls and deadlifts all demand sustained static finger flexion, which trains the flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus — sometimes to the point where grip becomes the limiting factor, in which case straps are the right answer if the goal is to stimulate the target muscle. Curls load the wrist flexors isometrically (the weight would pull you into wrist extension); lateral raises load the wrist extensors the same way.

But the argument for direct work is clean and follows from tension-primacy: maximising growth requires high fibre recruitment, which requires training **to or close to failure**. In compound lifts the forearms are usually not the limiting factor, so they are nowhere near their own failure point. In direct forearm work they always are. If you want accelerated forearm development, direct training is required.

## Which muscles are worth the sets

The **anterior compartment is larger** than the posterior, so it is the priority. Within it, everything except the pronator teres and pronator quadratus contributes to wrist flexion, and **all of them have good moment arms for pure wrist flexion** ([Gonzalez 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9239550/)). That single fact carries the whole exercise-selection argument: one movement pattern trains nearly all the mass.

Two anatomical details he uses later: the **flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus are the two largest forearm muscles** ([Holzbaur 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17241636/)) and they cross the fingers as well as the wrist. And the **palmaris longus is absent in up to 26% of people** — sometimes in one arm only ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539784/); [Shenoy 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6440361/) for the tendon-visibility tests).

## The wrist curl, and why finger flexion improves it

The reasoning chain is explicit and worth following because it is the model for everything else in the video.

**Premise:** training muscles at longer lengths produces more growth. Two literatures support this. Exercise comparisons — the seated leg curl grew the three hip-crossing hamstring heads more than the lying leg curl, while the biceps femoris short head (which crosses only the knee and so sits at the same length in both) grew equally ([Maeo 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/)) — and range-of-motion work, where a **calf raise partial at long lengths beat both the short-length partial and the full range of motion** ([Kassiano 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37015016/)).

He then does something most exercise-selection videos skip: he lists what this literature **cannot** yet tell him. Is a long-length partial always better than a full ROM? Is there a threshold beyond which more length adds nothing? And critically, **all of the long-length research has been done on untrained subjects**, with at least two trained-subject studies in progress at the time of recording. He rejects the mechanistic arguments circulating that trained lifters wouldn't benefit, but concedes the studies simply don't exist.

**Application:** wrist flexors are longest with the wrist extended. **Standing** wrist curls only travel neutral-to-flexed; **seated** wrist curls with the forearm on a bench or thigh reach genuine wrist extension. So seated ranks above standing.

**Refinement:** the two flexor digitorum muscles also lengthen with finger extension. Letting the load roll down to the base knuckles (extending at the metacarpophalangeal joints while keeping some bend in the interphalangeal joints so it doesn't drop), then flexing fingers and wrist together, reaches the longest position available for the two biggest forearm muscles. He credits the idea to a Stronger by Science article by Cameron Gill, who also advised on the video.

**Objection he raises against himself:** does adding finger flexion compromise the other wrist flexors? His best guess is no, on the seated-leg-curl precedent — that exercise lengthened three hamstrings more without being *worse* for the fourth. He is explicit that this is not an identical comparison.

**The load objection:** you cannot go as heavy on finger-flexion wrist curls. He answers with the triceps evidence — overhead extensions grew the long head more than pushdowns **despite subjects lifting heavier on pushdowns throughout** ([Maeo 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/)). Heavier does not mean more stimulus.

**Fat grips** get a partial endorsement: they force more finger extension than a normal bar, so they lengthen the flexor digitorum more than a plain wrist curl, but generally less than a finger-flexion wrist curl.

The practical caveat he attaches is one this library should note: **if you dislike the top-ranked variation, the lower-ranked ones still work.** Meaningful hypertrophy is documented at shorter muscle lengths; taking a movement you'll actually do to or near failure is not a wasted set. He also suggests **supersetting** wrist curls with something gripless (leg extensions, say) for time efficiency, citing current superset evidence that pairing exercises for different muscles is fine for growth.

## Extensors

The posterior compartment is smaller but "isn't terribly small," and most of its muscles have good leverage for wrist extension. Same logic inverted: extensors are longest with the wrist **flexed**, so standing wrist extensions (neutral-to-extended) rank below a seated version with the forearm on a surface and knuckles up, which reaches genuine wrist flexion. Gripping the load already flexes the fingers, which is fine — several extensors attach to the fingers and are longer when the fingers are flexed.

## Grip and finger-flexion training: optional, not useless

He opens the section by undercutting it: **isometric grip training is probably not essential**, because it trains finger flexion, and the flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus are already well covered by wrist curl variations (plus flexor pollicis longus if the thumb contacts the load). It gets covered because it's popular.

The ranking follows the same length logic. **Normal grip work** — holding a dumbbell, barbell or pull-up bar to near failure, or farmer's walks — puts the wrist near neutral with fingers and thumb flexed, i.e. **moderately short lengths**. Isometric training at short lengths does produce hypertrophy but less than at long lengths ([Oranchuk 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30580468/)). **Fat-grip holds and plate/dumbbell pinch training** extend the fingers and thumb further and so rank above.

On whether isometric training works at all, only two comparisons exist, and he reports both with their flaws. A **1957** study compared an isometric biceps exercise to dumbbell curling and found better upper-arm circumference gains with curling — but circumference is a poor proxy for growth, and the isometric was not performed at long lengths ([Rasch & Morehouse 1957](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13462914/)). The second compared isometric to concentric-only and eccentric-only leg extension, with **no significant difference** in quadriceps hypertrophy between the three; he notes he'd have preferred a normal concentric-plus-eccentric group, and that it is one study ([Jones & Rutherford](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1192197/)). His conclusion — isometric training at long lengths *might* match dynamic training, and if it doesn't, the gap is probably small — is appropriately hedged.

For a dynamic alternative: **pure finger curls with the wrist held extended** rank top (longest length), then hand grippers and standing finger curls (wrist neutral). A neat integration: perform finger-flexion wrist curls until wrist flexion fails, then squeeze out pure finger-flexion reps. Or hang on the bar after your last pull-up set; or add a few finger curls after your last curl set.

## The parts you can skip

**Radial and ulnar deviation:** five of the seven relevant muscles are already well trained by wrist curls and extensions. The two exceptions — abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis — are small enough that growing them will have a "trivial impact on your physique." Not essential.

**Pronation and supination:** direct work is the only way to train pronator teres, pronator quadratus and supinator (the biceps also supinates). These are a small fraction of forearm mass, so again not essential, though he notes elite arm wrestler Devon Larratt's highly developed pronator teres as a case that direct pronation work does build the muscle. Method: forearm on a bench or thigh, uneven dumbbell, rotate in each direction.

This is the section that most distinguishes the video from typical exercise-selection content — roughly five minutes spent telling you which exercises not to bother with.

## The brachioradialis: the received wisdom fails

Standard advice says neutral or pronated grips (hammer curls, reverse curls) grow the brachioradialis better, on the reasoning that the biceps has its best elbow-flexion leverage when supinated, so pronating shifts work to the brachioradialis. He tests this against the EMG literature and finds it **unsupported**: **2 studies** found higher brachioradialis activity with the forearm pronated, **3 found no difference**, and **4 found higher activity with the forearm supinated**. The most recent had **10 competitive bodybuilders** perform cable curls with supinated, pronated and neutral grips; **both biceps and brachioradialis activity tended to be higher supinated**.

His defence of taking EMG seriously here is careful but is also the weakest link in the video. He argues EMG may be more predictive **when comparing near-identical variations of one exercise** than when comparing very different exercises — citing calf work where EMG across slight calf-raise variations did predict growth ([Nunes 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32735428/)), against cases like squats versus hip thrusts where EMG did not track growth ([Plotkin 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37461495/)). That is a plausible principle but it is his own generalisation from two data points, and the conclusion it supports is an EMG conclusion about growth.

The supporting growth evidence is thinner than the section's confidence implies: one study of previously untrained subjects doing dumbbell and machine **preacher curls** (normally supinated) found the biceps, brachialis **and brachioradialis all grew significantly and similarly** ([Erskine 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24610245/)). He states plainly that since it compared nothing, it cannot prove a supinated grip is *sufficient* — only that substantial brachioradialis growth happens with one.

His explanation for why lifters feel the opposite is genuinely interesting: during pronated curls the load tries to flex the wrist, so the **wrist extensors** work hard to resist — and they sit right next to the brachioradialis, so the sensation may be misattributed. He labels this "a controversial take," says he is "completely open to being utterly wrong," and gives the sensible practical out: if vertical pulls, horizontal pulls and at least one curl haven't given you the brachioradialis you want, add curl volume, or experiment with neutral/pronated grips — **"even though the present evidence doesn't support it"** — since there's little to lose.

## Which curl for the brachioradialis?

Two competing arguments, and no research to settle them:
1. **Harder at the top.** Moment-arm analyses suggest the brachioradialis's leverage advantage over the other elbow flexors is largest at high elbow flexion angles ([Murray 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)), so exercises hardest near the top — free curls, or banded curls — may favour it.
2. **Harder at the bottom.** Brachioradialis leverage is not bad at low flexion angles either, and long-length training is favoured generally, so cable curls and preacher curls (hardest at the bottom) may be better. He adds a precise distinction: the biceps crosses the shoulder and so is longer in a **cable** curl with the shoulder extended than in a preacher curl, whereas the **brachioradialis doesn't cross the shoulder**, so its length is the same in both.

His answer is "no published research can settle this, one study is in the works, don't overthink it, pick the curl you prefer." That is the correct answer and most channels wouldn't give it.

## Caveats & flags
- **The entire exercise ranking is inference, not evidence.** No study has compared wrist curl variations for hypertrophy. The chain runs: long-length training is better (in hamstrings, calves, triceps, in untrained subjects) → therefore the wrist-curl variation reaching the longest length is best. He flags this at every step, but the video's conclusions are one link weaker than its detail suggests.
- **The long-length literature is entirely untrained-subject research**, and he says so — which means the top-ranked exercises are recommended for trained lifters on untested grounds.
- **The brachioradialis conclusion rests on EMG plus one uncontrolled growth study.** He defends the EMG use with a novel and unvalidated principle about within-exercise comparisons. He flags it himself, which is the right handling, but the resulting advice contradicts widespread practice on weak outcome data.
- The isometric-versus-dynamic comparison is **two studies, one from 1957** (PubMed dates the second to 1987, not 1986 as stated), with acknowledged design gaps.
- Reference-list note: the description's "Ackland et al." entry resolves to a systematic review of **glenohumeral** joint moment arms, which does not obviously match anything in a forearm video — possibly a mis-paste, and it is listed below as given.
