# Post-Failure Partials vs Lengthened Partials: A First Head-to-Head in Trained Lifters

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Training BEYOND Failure - This NEW Study is Epic" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=13LiLwBZfa8)) · 2025-04-28 · 12 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- On exercises hardest at the **shortened** position (calf raises, rows, pulldowns, lateral raises, many leg machines), you can go past failure with partials, or skip full ROM and do **lengthened partials** throughout. A new preprint is the first to test the two against each other in trained lifters.
- Growth **tended** to favour lengthened partials — but the statistical analysis gave nowhere near strong support and measurement error was high. A signal, not a finding.
- Calves: lengthened partials are a good default. Everything else: the evidence is genuinely split, and he says so.
- Two trials in trained lifters — 25 and 297 subjects — found **no** difference between full ROM and lengthened partials for arm and thigh growth. That is the strongest counterweight in the video.
- Nothing has yet found lengthened partials *inferior*, which makes "experiment on some sets" defensible rather than a hedge.

## The two ways past a shortened-position sticking point
A shortened-position-challenged exercise fails when you can no longer reach the top. Two responses: keep squeezing out partial reps in the bottom half until you fail there too (**post-failure partials**, previously called "lengthened supersets" on this channel), or skip the top half from the start and train only the lengthened half (**lengthened partials**).

Both had prior support in untrained subjects — one calf-raise trial found post-failure partials beat full ROM for medial gastrocnemius growth, another found lengthened partials beat both full ROM and shortened partials. Nobody had put the two techniques in the same study.

## The new study
23 trained individuals, average **7.3 years** of training experience. Within-subject unilateral design: four sets of Smith machine calf raises, twice weekly, for **8 weeks**.

- One leg: lengthened partials to failure in the 10–20 rep range, stopping when the heel could no longer be raised to 0°.
- Other leg: full ROM to failure, then partials past it, stopping at the same 0° criterion. Load was set so each set produced 5–10 full reps plus 5–10 partials — deliberately matching total reps to the lengthened-partial leg.

Loads progressed as subjects got stronger. Medial gastrocnemius thickness was the outcome.

Growth tended to be greater with lengthened partials. The interesting implication is that hitting failure **twice** in a single set bought nothing extra.

Three caveats he raises himself, which is worth crediting: n=23; the statistical analysis "failed to provide anywhere close to strong support"; and measurement error was relatively high. He also notes that despite 7.3 years of lifting, these subjects generally trained calves with low volume — so they were not calf-trained in the way that figure implies.

## The cross-study comparison, and why it is weak
He then compares effect sizes across the two earlier untrained trials: post-failure partials beat full ROM by 43.3%, lengthened partials by 126.9%, offered as "indirect evidence" for lengthened partials.

**Flag:** that is a between-study comparison of relative differences from different samples, protocols and measurement setups. He hedges it, but it should carry almost no weight beside the direct comparison above.

The mechanistic case is stronger: the gastrocnemius generates less contractile tension at short lengths, so post-failure partials spend a larger proportion of their reps in the muscle's weak zone.

## Does this generalise? Three live possibilities
**1. Lengthened partials are simply the better range of motion.** Supported by untrained-subject work: 42 women on calf raises (greater medial and lateral gastrocnemius growth), 45 women on leg extensions (greater quad growth, biggest at the distal regions near the knee), and an unpublished abstract in 34 untrained subjects showing greater glute and hamstring volume gains on a machine hip extension.

Against it: **Wolf et al.**, 25 well-trained subjects on an upper-body programme, full ROM vs lengthened partials on everything — biceps and triceps growth comparable. And a second trial with **297 trained subjects** on a full-body programme found arm and thigh gains clearly not different.

He does not simply accept the null. The Wolf paper has an open dispute about how the lengthened partials were standardised; the 297-subject trial used quite low volumes; and both measured growth at the **mid-region** of the muscle, when the positive data hint the advantage sits distally.

**2. It only works on shortened-position-challenged exercises.** The two null trials used Bayesian curls, overhead triceps extensions and incline curls — exercises already hardest at moderate-to-long lengths, where a lengthened partial adds nothing. The three positive trials used shortened-position-challenged movements. Plausible, currently unfalsified either way.

**3. It only works on specific muscles.** Calves are the clean case, because their force-generating capacity drops off sharply at short lengths in a way other muscles' may not. He speculates traps behave similarly (so lengthened partials on rows may build traps better), and that pecs may not need it since most chest exercises already load the lengthened position. He labels this a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

## Practical verdict
- Calves: use lengthened partials. This is the one recommendation he makes with confidence.
- Everything else: full ROM as the default is fine; or experiment with lengthened partials on selected sets, since nothing has shown them to be worse.
- Post-failure partials remain reasonable and convenient on a final set of rows, pulldowns or lateral raises — untested outside calves, so neither endorsed nor ruled out.
