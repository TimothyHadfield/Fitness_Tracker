# Are Isolation Exercises Useless? What the "Compounds Are Enough" Studies Actually Show

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Are Isolation Exercises USELESS for Muscle Hypertrophy? (19 STUDIES)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qtm1yCpjiPM)) · 2022-05-30 · 11 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- Three studies found adding isolation work to a compound program produced **no extra arm growth**, and a 2017 review concluded as much. Those studies are real, but their outcome measures are narrow.
- Every one of them measured only **elbow flexors or arm circumference, at a single mid-muscle site**. Muscles do not grow uniformly, so a single measurement site can hide real differences.
- The four post-2017 studies that would have strengthened the "isolation is useless" case are **Barbalho papers containing improbable and atypical data**, and he refuses to use them.
- A varied-exercise study (Costa et al., 22 men, 8 weeks) found more regional growth when different compound *and* isolation exercises were rotated across the week.
- Several muscles and regions are barely trained by compounds at all: **lateral delt, calves, knee-flexion hamstring function, rectus femoris, triceps long head**. Those are the strongest arguments for isolation work, and they are mostly mechanistic rather than growth-outcome arguments.

## The case that compounds are enough
[01:01] **Gentil et al. (2015)** compared lat pulldowns to barbell curls for 10 weeks. Elbow flexor thickness (biceps plus brachialis) increased similarly — an isolation exercise produced no more growth than a compound.

[01:33] That design cannot answer whether *adding* curls helps, so **Gentil et al. (2013)** tested it: bench press plus lat pulldown, versus the same two plus a biceps curl and a triceps extension. Both groups did 3 sets of 8–12 to failure per exercise, so the second group accumulated **more total volume**, not less. After 10 weeks, elbow flexor thickness increases were similar. Triceps growth was not assessed at all.

[02:36] **França et al. (2015)** repeated the pattern in trained men: compounds only versus the same compounds plus two isolation exercises per session, 3 sets of 10–15 per exercise, again unequated volume favouring the isolation group. After 8 weeks, **arm circumference** increases were statistically similar.

[03:08] All three fed into the **Gentil et al. (2017) review**, which stated that adding single-joint exercises to a multi-joint program does not seem to increase gains in size or strength.

[03:39] Four further studies by Barbalho and colleagues pointed the same way. He discards them: it has been established that much of this group's work contains **improbable and atypical data** that cannot be trusted, possibly because it is illegitimate. Worth noting for the reader — this decision removes evidence for the position he is arguing against, but it is the correct call on data-integrity grounds and he states his reasoning openly rather than quietly omitting the papers.

## Why the null results may not mean what they appear to
[04:40] Ribeiro et al.'s published comment on the Gentil review supplies the core objection: growth was measured **only in elbow flexors and arms, and only at roughly the midpoint of those muscles**.

[05:11] Regional hypertrophy is well enough established to make that a serious limitation. **Kawakami et al.** found 16 weeks of overhead extensions grew triceps cross-sectional area mainly in the middle-to-slightly-lower regions, with little change at the most upper and lower regions. **Ema et al.** found 12 weeks of leg extensions produced growth that differed between quadriceps heads *and* between the upper and lower portions within each head.

If exercises that differ biomechanically grow different regions preferentially, then using a range of them should maximise growth across a muscle — and isolation exercises are essential to that range.

[06:43] **Costa et al. (2021)** tests it directly. 22 men, 3 days/week for 8 weeks. Both groups used compound *and* isolation exercises; the non-varied group did the identical exercises on all three days, the varied group did different ones each day. Thickness was measured at upper, middle and lower regions of rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, elbow flexors and triceps. Some regions were similar; **other regions favoured the varied group**, giving it more regional growth overall.

## The muscles compounds barely touch
[08:13] His strongest section, and the one that does not depend on the arm studies at all:
- **Lateral deltoid** — abducts the shoulder; no compound significantly involves that motion, so lateral raises are likely required.
- **Calves** — plantar flex the ankle; no compound significantly involves that motion.
- **Hamstrings** — compounds train them via hip extension, but different parts appear to be recruited during knee flexion, so leg curls are probably needed for full regional development.
- **Quadriceps** — compounds grow the vasti well but not the **rectus femoris**; leg extensions and reverse Nordics develop it.
- **Triceps long head** — two studies indicate the bench press develops the lateral and medial heads reasonably but the long head minimally. The long head crosses the shoulder as well as the elbow, and bench presses, push-ups and dips all involve shoulder extension on the lowering phase, shortening the long head and limiting its force contribution. Skull crushers, overhead extensions and rope pushdowns all develop it.

[10:49] Conclusion: substantial hypertrophy is achievable with compounds alone, but "compounds are sufficient" is likely wrong, because many muscles and regions are not optimally trained by them.

**Overreach flag:** the muscle-by-muscle section leans on activation, architecture and joint-mechanics reasoning rather than growth outcomes, and only Costa et al. — 22 men, 8 weeks, ultrasound thickness — directly tests the variety claim. That single small study is carrying more weight than its size warrants.
