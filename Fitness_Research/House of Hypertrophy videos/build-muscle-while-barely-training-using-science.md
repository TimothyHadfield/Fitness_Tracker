# Minimum Effective Training: How Little Can You Do And Still Grow?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "You CAN Build Muscle While BARELY Training (Using Science)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DMDuwCqlgiY)) · 2024-01-22 · 17 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- His headline number is **2–4 working sets per muscle per week**, run at 6–12 reps, taken to or very near failure. That is the floor he thinks is defensible, not the optimum — he still puts the optimum at 12–20 weekly sets.
- He corrects a number the rest of the internet is quoting wrong. The minimum-dose meta-analysis everyone cites **contains a counting error**; using the fixed numbers from one of the paper's own authors, fewer than five weekly sets produced **53% of the growth** seen with more than ten weekly sets — not the higher figures in circulation.
- Frequency is free. When weekly sets are matched, splitting them across one, two or three days produces largely the same growth, so the minimalist should pick whatever fits the week.
- With very few sets he thinks **failure probably does matter**, which reverses his usual position. The one study close to the question (about two weekly sets per muscle) favoured true failure over just-shy-of-failure.
- Rest 2.5–3 minutes, not less — which means a time-efficient session is mostly spent waiting. His fix is not shorter rests but **structural**: antagonist supersets, drop sets, rest-pause and the 3/7 method, each of which he says can roughly halve session time.

## The corrected minimum-dose number
This is the most useful thing in the video and the easiest to miss. The widely quoted meta-analysis on low weekly volumes was published with a set-counting error. He deliberately reports the fixed values instead, obtained from an article written by one of the paper's own authors: **fewer than five weekly sets per muscle yielded 53% of the hypertrophy achieved with more than ten weekly sets**. He flags explicitly that when viewers see other numbers from this analysis in other videos, those numbers are not right.

"Fewer than five" is vague, so he unpacks it: most of the pooled studies actually used **two to four weekly sets**, and he leans toward four as the practical floor, citing a review on time-efficient hypertrophy.

He then does the thing that makes this channel worth reading. He states the limitation immediately: **most of those studies used untrained subjects**. He offers the one counterweight he has — a paper finding three weekly sets per muscle produced measurable gains in trained men — and then the counterweight to that, a recent paper where some previously untrained older adults **barely grew at all on two weekly quad sets but grew much better on eight**. His conclusion is appropriately hedged: 2–4 sets is a starting recommendation and an average, and a meaningful fraction of people will need more.

> **Library note.** This is a direct numerical conflict with the minimalist-training material we already hold, which puts 1–4 weekly sets at roughly 64% of gains and 5–9 sets at roughly 84%. Those are the *uncorrected* figures from the same underlying analysis. His 53%-for-under-five figure comes from the author-issued correction. The shared caveat holds in both versions — the evidence base is overwhelmingly untrained subjects.

## Reps, tempo and failure
Reps between **6 and 35** produce similar growth provided you get to or near failure — his standing channel position. Below six reps still builds muscle but appears to need extra sets to get there: he cites one paper where seven sets of three matched three sets of ten, and a second where seven sets of four matched three sets of twelve. For a time-minimiser the implication is to stay inside 6–35, and lean to **6–12** because fewer total reps means less time — though he notes honestly that the heavier loading can eat the saving in setup and warm-ups.

On tempo he cites a recent review concluding **2–8 second rep durations are likely comparably effective**, so a minimalist should use the fast end. He mentions the fast-concentric/slow-eccentric combination has "some evidence" behind it but calls it an inconsistent finding and files it under personal preference rather than recommendation.

Failure is where he breaks from his own default. Normally he holds that stopping 1–3 reps short matches training to failure — but he points out those studies all used **six or more weekly sets per muscle**. At two to four sets there may be nothing left to spare. The closest evidence is a study in which subjects performed roughly two weekly sets per muscle, one group to complete failure and one just short; **muscle gain favoured the failure group**. He immediately downgrades it — single study, imprecise hypertrophy measure, "most definitely not conclusive" — but treats it as enough to recommend getting to failure or as close as possible when set counts are this low.

## Exercise selection on a tiny budget
Two tiers. The **simple** version is one push, one pull, one squat pattern and one hip-hinge, which he says collectively stimulate nearly every muscle; add isolation for biceps, triceps, side delts or legs if time allows. Two sessions a week covers 2–4 weekly sets per muscle.

The **complete** version spends the same sets across more variety to cover more of each muscle: squat one day and leg extension the other (rectus femoris, which squats hit poorly); RDL one day and seated leg curl the other; flat bench one day and incline the other; pulldown one day and row the other. He notes machines are fine — the literature finds machines and free weights comparably effective.

A deliberate bias runs through the selection: **exercises that load the muscle at long lengths**. Seated leg curls out-grow lying leg curls; overhead extensions out-grow pushdowns. His stated reasoning is that if you only get a handful of sets, you want each one to be the most productive version available. He also flags — without resolving it — that there is contention over whether the stretch advantage persists long-term, and defers it to a future video.

## The time-saving techniques, ranked by how much he trusts them
- **Antagonist and non-competing supersets** — best supported for *not costing you anything*. Research shows performance is largely maintained, with documented cases of improvement (leg curls before leg extensions increased leg-extension reps). Two hypertrophy studies (biceps/triceps, and leg curl/leg extension) found non-significant differences from normal sets. Roughly halves session time.
- **Drop sets** — meta-analytic data says roughly equivalent to normal sets for growth. His time-efficient protocol: reps to or near failure, cut the load 20%, repeat, 2–4 drops total. Research suggests three or four drops can be equivalent in stimulus to three normal sets at less than half the time. Best on isolation work, which is where the highest-quality studies did it.
- **Rest-pause** — two studies found *superior* hypertrophy versus normal sets, one reporting a 38% time saving. He immediately guts the finding: **the normal-set comparison groups were training much further from failure**, so the comparison cannot show rest-pause is superior. He also mentions an unpublished paper on myo-reps finding parity. Verdict: it can produce time-efficient gains; it is not proven better.
- **The 3/7 method** — from a 2005 French fitness book. 3, 4, 5, 6, then 7 reps with 15 seconds between, using a ~12RM load. The single published study found two bouts beat eight sets of six with the same load for biceps growth — but he calls the comparison "odd", since the normal group would have been far from failure on most sets. Not proof of anything; offered as an option people might enjoy.

## Where the argument outruns the evidence
The minimalist prescription rests almost entirely on untrained-subject data, and he says so — but the recommendation is still delivered as a general one. The honest reading is that 2–4 weekly sets is a **demonstrated floor in beginners** and a **plausible-but-thin floor in trained lifters**, resting on one three-set study. His own cited counter-example, in which two weekly quad sets produced almost nothing in older untrained adults, shows how wide the individual spread is around that average.
