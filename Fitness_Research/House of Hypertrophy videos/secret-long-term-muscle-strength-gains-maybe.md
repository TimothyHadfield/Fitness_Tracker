# Do Training Breaks Improve Long-Term Gains?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The SECRET to Long-Term Muscle and Strength Gains (Maybe!)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cBzaFBVVs50)) · 2022-07-18 · 13 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- A 24-week Japanese trial found a group that trained **6 weeks on, 3 weeks off** matched a group that trained continuously — despite doing **25% less total training**.
- The mechanism driving the difference: the continuous group's gains **decelerated** across the study, while the periodic group's gains **stayed at first-block rates** every time they came back.
- Candidate explanation is **desensitisation of the anabolic signalling pathway**. p70S6K activation falls after roughly **7 sessions in humans**, and a **10-day break restores it**.
- Ten days may therefore be the better break length than three weeks — long enough to resensitise, short enough that little or nothing is lost. He is explicit that **this specific combination has never been tested**.
- He will not claim deloads do the same job, and says so: reduced volume still stimulates the pathway, which may prevent the reset. That is speculation and he labels it as such.

## The 24-week study
**14 previously untrained men** were split into a continuous group (24 straight weeks) and a periodic group (**6 weeks training, 3 weeks complete rest, repeated across 24 weeks**). During training weeks both did the same thing: **barbell bench press, 3 sets of 10 at 75% 1RM, three times a week, 2–3 minutes rest**, with the 75% load recalculated every three weeks.

At week 24, **pectoralis major growth, triceps growth and bench 1RM were all similar** between groups. The interesting part is the shape of the curves. The continuous group's pec growth ran at **~15% in the first six weeks and only ~5% in the last six**. The periodic group lost some size and strength during each rest block, then regained and exceeded it faster on return, running at **~15% in its first six training weeks and ~15–20% in its last six**. Their gains never slowed.

His point is the extrapolation, not the result: if the trend lines held, the periodic group would eventually overtake. He states plainly that the study did not run long enough to show this.

## Why gains might decelerate
Myofibrillar protein synthesis is driven by signalling pathways, of which the PI3K–Akt–mTOR pathway is the best characterised; researchers commonly measure **phosphorylation of p70S6K** as a readout.

A rat study from the same lead author found p70S6K phosphorylation **fell progressively across 12–18 consecutive every-other-day sessions**, and that a **12-day break restored it**. A later human study corroborated this: **only 7 sessions** of leg press and leg extension were enough to significantly blunt post-training p70S6K phosphorylation, and a **10-day break restored responsiveness**.

This is a mechanism argument, and he treats it as one — the signalling data is the proposed explanation for the 24-week result, not independent proof of it.

## Ten days instead of three weeks?
The periodic group took three weeks off, well past the 10 days that appear sufficient to resensitise. Detraining timelines are messy — he cites an analysis noting the literature is not clear on the time course — but several studies show strength and size losses inside **3–4 weeks**, while one found **fibre cross-sectional area was not significantly reduced after 8 days**. So a 10-day break may buy the resensitisation with far less loss. He calls this speculation and does not pretend otherwise.

He then does something worth noting: he interrogates his own outcome measure. Some of the size lost during a break may be **glycogen and its associated water**, not contractile tissue. Doubling relative quadriceps glycogen concentration raised vastus cross-sectional area by **3.5%** — a real change, but it took a doubling to get it. And in a training study, glycogen rose with training while **relative** concentration stayed flat, meaning glycogen scaled with fibre size rather than driving the measurement. His conclusion is that glycogen fluctuations probably contribute little here, with more research needed.

## Deloads and periodisation
He is sceptical that deloads resensitise the pathway, on the grounds that reduced volume still stimulates it. He flags this as pure speculation and calls for research.

On periodisation as an alternative route to sustained gains, the evidence he presents does not support it strongly: a meta-analysis found **periodised training does not produce more hypertrophy** than non-periodised, and while a strength meta-analysis favours periodisation, he notes the periodised groups trained with **heavier average relative loads** — equate those and strength gains are similar. His decisive limitation is that essentially all periodisation studies run **6–16 weeks**, which is exactly the window in which this video's question cannot be answered.

## Takeaways
Short breaks of around **10 days** might resensitise the growth pathways and, over years, produce more muscle and strength. He states directly that it would be misleading to present this as fact. The robust takeaway is the defensive one: **stop worrying about breaks**. Whether for a nagging injury or ordinary life, worst case your size and strength hold or dip and come back faster via muscle memory; best case you reset the pathway and gain more on return.

## Where the conclusion outruns the evidence
The whole thesis rests on **one 14-subject untrained bench-press study**, plus a rat signalling study and acute human signalling data — no trial has ever tested the 10-day protocol he ends up recommending. To his credit, he never claims otherwise and repeatedly labels the speculative steps.
