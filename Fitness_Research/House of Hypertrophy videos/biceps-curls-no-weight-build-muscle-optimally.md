# No-Load Curls Matched Weighted Curls — In One Small Study

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Biceps Curls With NO WEIGHT Build Muscle Optimally (Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ipNBCdopegM)) · 2021-09-01 · 9 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Counts and colleagues had untrained subjects train one arm with **no weight at all** — just maximally squeezing the biceps through a curling motion — and the other arm with conventional 70% 1RM dumbbell curls. Biceps thickness gains were **similar at all three measured regions**.
- The mechanistic explanation is coherent: mechanical tension is the driver, and you can self-generate high active tension by maximally contracting through a range of motion without any external load.
- It is **one study, n=13, untrained, 6 weeks**, thickness only. He says so repeatedly and does not overclaim.
- The useful takeaway isn't "throw away your dumbbells" — it's that external load is a convenient way to force high tension, not the only way.

## The study
Five men and eight women, all previously untrained, trained both arms 3×/week on non-consecutive days for 6 weeks.

- **No-load arm:** curling motion with no weight, aiming to maximally contract and squeeze the biceps throughout. 4 sets of 20 reps, 30s rest. Electrodes were placed on the biceps to give activation feedback and encourage harder contraction.
- **High-load arm:** dumbbell curls at 70% 1RM, 4 sets of 8–12, 90s rest.

Biceps thickness was measured at roughly 50%, 60% and 70% of upper arm length. Growth was similar between conditions at every site.

## The limitations he works through
This section is the strongest part of the video — he raises the objections himself rather than waiting for the comments.

**Cross-education.** Could the no-load arm have grown from training the other arm? He cites Hubal and colleagues: 243 men and 342 women trained one biceps with unilateral preacher and concentration curls. The trained arm gained **18.9% cross-sectional area**; the untrained arm gained **0.2%**. Cross-education moves strength, not size — and Counts noted the effect is minimal or absent when the two limbs use different protocols anyway.

**Unequal rest intervals.** The no-load arm rested 30s, the loaded arm 90s. He concedes this could matter, but notes the rest-interval literature is conflicting for isolation work (longer rest looks better mainly for compounds), so it is hard to say the shorter rest helped.

**Untrained subjects.** Flagged plainly: the result may not transfer to trained lifters.

## The mechanism
Mechanical tension is the best-characterised hypertrophy stimulus. Its active component comes from two things: how many fibres are recruited, and how much force those recruited fibres produce. Training close to failure with weights raises both — which is also why heavy and light loads produce similar growth.

His argument is that a maximal voluntary squeeze through a range of motion can drive both variables directly, without an external load standing in as the forcing function.

## Flag
The closing speculation — that external resistance may not be needed for optimal growth at all — runs well ahead of a single 6-week study in untrained people using muscle thickness. He labels it speculation and asks for more research, but the video title does not.

One practical note he adds from the paper: subjects often found the no-load sets **harder and more uncomfortable** than the dumbbell sets. That is the difficulty — sustaining a genuinely maximal voluntary contraction is not something most people will do consistently, which is precisely what external load solves.
