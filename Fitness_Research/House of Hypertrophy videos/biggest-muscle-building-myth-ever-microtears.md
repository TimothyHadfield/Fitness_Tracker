# Muscle Damage Is Not the Driver of Hypertrophy

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The BIGGEST Muscle Building Myth Ever (Microtears)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Nzzp3DbAHwA)) · 2022-08-08 · 10 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- The "microtears" story — damage the fibre, repair it bigger — has no outcome data behind it. Every argument for it is mechanistic, and the mechanisms cut both ways.
- Eccentric-only training does produce far more damage, but the post-2010 body of evidence fails to show it produces more hypertrophy when load or work is matched. That kills the strongest indirect argument for damage.
- Two lines of direct evidence go the other way: an acclimatisation study where the low-damage group grew as much as the high-damage group, and Damas 2016, where the post-workout myofibrillar protein synthesis spike correlated with hypertrophy only *after* damage had subsided.
- A 1999 study of severe eccentric overload found muscle volume ended up **10% below baseline** and stayed there for weeks. Excessive damage can be net-negative.
- Indirect check: three training variables known to raise damage (higher reps, short rest, bro splits) do not produce more growth — and short rest produces less.
- His verdict: **mechanical tension is the primary stimulus**, achieved by recruiting as many fibres as possible and holding decent force on them — i.e. train to within roughly three reps of failure, at which point rep range and tempo matter little.

## Why anyone believed it
He gives the pro-damage case fairly before dismantling it. Neutrophils clearing debris also produce reactive oxygen species, theorised to raise MAPK signalling and therefore protein synthesis. Macrophages produce myokines. Damage causes fluid and protein accumulation, so the fibre swells, and cell swelling is thought to be read as a threat to membrane integrity, raising synthesis and suppressing breakdown. Separately, eccentric-only training (a dynamometer resisting only the lowering phase) causes much more damage than normal or concentric-only work, and was once thought to produce more growth.

His rebuttals are proportionate. The immune-cell and swelling arguments are mechanism, not outcome — they establish a possible route, not that more damaging training grows more muscle. And reactive oxygen species are also believed to have anti-growth effects, so their net role is unclear. This is the channel at its best: he does not claim the mechanism is wrong, only that it cannot carry the conclusion.

## The eccentric argument collapses
The idea that eccentric-only training grows more muscle comes from work published before 2010. He quotes a review concluding that when matched for maximum load or for work, eccentric and concentric resistance training produce similar increases in muscle size. He dates this quote to 2017; the description's reference list instead carries Roig 2009, so the attribution is worth treating as loose. He flags in passing that eccentric-only work may produce a *different distribution* of growth, and defers that to another video rather than folding it into the argument.

## The direct evidence
**Acclimatisation (2011, 14 untrained men).** One group did eight weeks of eccentric leg-cycle training cold; the other had a three-week ramp-in first and therefore experienced far less damage across the eight weeks. Quadriceps growth was comparable. The naive group averaged slightly more, and he explicitly says this was not statistically significant and may be chance. This study is not in the description's reference list, so the author name is not verifiable and is omitted here.

**Damas 2016 (untrained, Brazil).** After a first session, myofibrillar protein synthesis rose but did not correlate with subsequent hypertrophy — it appears to have been spent repairing damage. Only after several weeks, once damage had attenuated, did the protein synthesis response correlate with growth. The implication runs against damage rather than for it.

**Foley 1999 (6 men).** Five sets of ten eccentric curls at 110% of concentric 1RM. Swelling and damage markers persisted seven days; once they subsided, muscle volume was 10% below pre-study and remained smaller for weeks — plausibly from destruction of subpopulations of fibres.

## Where this sits against the library
This is the mechanisms question again, and his verdict matches the library's position: mechanical tension survives, muscle damage does not. Two things sharpen it. First, his evidence base is the one the library already leans on (Damas, Wackerhage, Schoenfeld's own 2012 review posing the question). Second, Schoenfeld himself was notably more agnostic in a 2024 interview than this 2022 video is — the video treats the question as settled, and Schoenfeld, whose review is cited here as a source, does not. Dhim's confidence outruns the primary literature's own author.

**Overreach flag:** the closing prescription ("at least three reps from failure ensures maximal fibre recruitment") is asserted from motor-unit recruitment theory, not from the studies just reviewed — the same mechanism-over-outcome move he criticises earlier in the video.
