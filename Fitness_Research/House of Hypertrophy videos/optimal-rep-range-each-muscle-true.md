# No, You Don't Need to Match Rep Ranges to Fibre Type

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Optimal Rep Range for Each Muscle (Is it TRUE?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bAwIy03_tSg)) · 2023-05-04 · 6 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- The claim under test: slow-twitch-dominant muscles (calves, especially soleus) need **high reps**, fast-twitch-leaning muscles (biceps, triceps, pecs) need **heavy loads**. The research does not support it.
- A 10-week unilateral calf study found **soleus, medial and lateral gastrocnemius grew equally** on 4x20–30 and 4x6–10, both to failure. The soleus is the strongest possible test case, and it failed.
- A meta-analysis of five studies found **slow-twitch and fast-twitch fibre growth were both comparable** between light and heavy loads when sets go to or near failure.
- Working range is roughly **6–35 reps**, provided proximity to failure is matched. Fibre composition doesn't change that.
- The mechanism is Henneman's size principle: light loads taken to failure end up recruiting fast-twitch fibres anyway.

## The premise
[00:04] Muscles differ in average fibre composition. Most sit near an even split; the **soleus is overwhelmingly slow twitch**; pectoralis major, triceps and biceps skew **slightly** fast twitch. Since slow fibres are low-force and fatigue-resistant while fast fibres are high-force and fatigable, the folk inference is to train each with the matching rep range. Dhim notes these figures are **averages across multiple studies**.

## The calf test
[01:36] A 2020 study (Schoenfeld et al.) trained 26 **untrained** men unilaterally, 2x/week for 10 weeks, on seated and standing calf raises. One leg did **4x20–30 to momentary failure**, the other **4x6–10 to momentary failure**, both with 90 s rest. Thickness of soleus, medial gastrocnemius and lateral gastrocnemius was measured before and after.

**All three muscles grew similarly** under both loading schemes. This is the cleanest available refutation: within-subject design, and the soleus is the muscle the hypothesis most confidently predicts should favour high reps.

## The fast-twitch side and the fibre-level data
[03:07] For biceps, triceps and pecs, the evidence is indirect but consistent — across a range of studies, reps anywhere from roughly **6 to 35** produce similar growth when taken to or very near failure. The same pattern already holds for the quads in work covered elsewhere on the channel.

[03:37] The underlying premise fails too. **Grgic's meta-analysis** of five studies found slow-twitch fibre growth comparable between heavy and light loads, and fast-twitch fibre growth comparable as well, given reps to or near failure. Morton et al. supply the activation half: with sets taken to task failure, recruitment of both fibre types is comparable across loads. [04:38] That is exactly what the size principle predicts — fast fibres aren't needed at the start of a light set, but get recruited as fatigue accumulates and the set approaches failure.

## The individual-variation caveat
[05:08] Dhim closes by flagging, unprompted, that everything presented is **group averages**, and that newer data suggests **some individuals may genuinely respond better to one rep range than another** — for reasons that appear unrelated to fibre type. He explicitly says he doesn't know the mechanism and defers it to a future video. This is the kind of caveat most channels omit, and it is what keeps the conclusion honest: fibre-type-based programming is unsupported, but "6–35 reps are equivalent" is an average, not a personal guarantee.

## Where the evidence is thin
The calf study is untrained subjects, and the biceps/triceps/chest case is assembled from studies that were not designed to test fibre-type matching — so the conclusion is well-supported as a rejection of the claim, weaker as a positive prescription.
