# Push-Ups Match the Bench Press for Chest and Triceps Growth

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Bench Press vs Push Ups | Which is BEST? (Two Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_VWQMGdCU54)) · 2020-06-03 · 8 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- Two longitudinal trials found **no significant difference in pectoralis major or triceps thickness** between push-up training and bench press training.
- Bench press 1RM rose similarly in both, though raw percentages favoured the bench group — almost certainly specificity, not a superior stimulus.
- The push-up is progressively overloadable: harder variations shift the percentage of body weight you press, so it is not stuck at one load.
- Neither study measured anterior deltoid growth. Dhim fills that gap with EMG and flags the inference as weak.
- This is an "either is fine" conclusion, not a "push-ups are better" one — he ends by saying you can programme both.

## The two trials
**Kikuchi & Nakazato (2017)** split 18 men with at least a year of training into bench press or push-up groups. The bench group used **40% 1RM**; the push-up group adjusted hand and foot position to approximate that resistance (regular push-ups for some, kneeling for others). Both did **3 sets to failure, twice a week for 8 weeks**. Ultrasound pec major and triceps thickness increased similarly. Bench 1RM rose similarly too, with the raw percentage favouring the bench group.

Dhim pre-empts the obvious objection about the light load: Schoenfeld's meta-analysis shows **30–85% 1RM produces similar hypertrophy when sets go to failure**. He is careful to separate that from strength, where heavier loads are preferred — so he grants that the bench press is the better strength tool because it is trivially easy to load heavy.

**Kotarsky et al. (2018)** ran the harder test: 23 men with 2–6 months' experience, **3x/week for 4 weeks**, with both groups genuinely progressing. The bench group went 3x6 → 3x8, then added 4.5 kg and reset. The push-up group climbed a ladder of harder variations, progressing when they hit 3x8 on a double-arm variation or 3x4 on a single-arm one. Pec major thickness gains were statistically similar, with raw percentages **slightly favouring the push-up group**. Bench 1RM again favoured the bench group on raw numbers. Push-up level advanced **2.78 levels** in the push-up group versus **0.78** in the bench group — specificity running in the other direction.

## Loading the push-up
Ebben et al. (2011) measured what fraction of body weight each variation presses: **~64% for a standard push-up**, more with feet elevated, less with hands elevated, **~49% kneeling**. That gives a usable progression scheme — add reps, then move to a harder variation — alongside banded and weighted push-ups.

## Where the evidence thins
The Kotarsky trial ran **only four weeks**, which he says outright. The anterior deltoid claim rests on EMG showing weighted push-ups matching the bench press at four load-equated conditions; he names the limitation of inferring growth from activation, then makes the inference anyway on biomechanical similarity. That is the one place here where mechanism outruns outcome data.
