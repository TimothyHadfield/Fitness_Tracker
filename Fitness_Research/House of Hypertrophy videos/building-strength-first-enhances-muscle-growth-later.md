# Does a Strength Block First Make the Hypertrophy Block Work Better?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Building Strength First ENHANCES Muscle Growth Later?! (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w9mAq4TrF3I)) · 2021-03-23 · 11 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- One new study found that **three weeks of 1-3 rep training before five weeks of 8-12 rep training produced more quad growth than eight weeks of 8-12 alone** — despite the strength phase producing *less* growth while it was running.
- He **rejects the authors' own explanation.** They attributed it to heavier loads meaning more mechanical tension; he argues that at matched proximity to failure the relative stimulus is the same regardless of absolute load.
- His two alternative explanations — **neural adaptations carried forward**, and **resensitisation to the 8-12 rep range** — are both offered as speculation, and he says he has no strong evidence for the second.
- **Indirect evidence points the other way**: a linear vs reverse-linear periodisation study favoured linear; alternating rep ranges does not beat a fixed rep range; a meta-analysis found linear and daily undulating periodisation equivalent for hypertrophy.
- **His verdict: insufficient data.** He does not adopt the practice, and does not recommend it.

## The study

Carvalho et al. recruited **26 men averaging 4.5 years of training experience** who could squat at least 1.5× bodyweight. Subjects were **pair-matched on vastus lateralis thickness** and split into a hypertrophy group and a strength-plus-hypertrophy group. Both trained back squat and leg press twice weekly for 8 weeks, after a 3-week identical lead-in.

- **Hypertrophy group:** 4 sets of 8-12 to failure, 1 min rest, for all 8 weeks.
- **Strength + hypertrophy group:** weeks 1-3 at 4 sets of 1-3 to failure with 3 min rest; weeks 4-8 identical to the hypertrophy group.

Measurements at baseline, week 3, and week 8: back squat and leg press 1RM, and **vastus lateralis thickness by ultrasound** at 60% of thigh length.

**Results.** 1RM gains were significantly greater for the strength-plus-hypertrophy group at both week 3 and week 8. VL thickness at week 3 **favoured the hypertrophy group** — the strength phase did not itself produce much growth. But **by week 8 the strength-plus-hypertrophy group had grown more overall**, meaning the growth during their five-week hypertrophy phase outpaced the other group's.

The design is decent for its size: pair-matching on the outcome variable, ultrasound rather than calipers, both exercises measured for strength. The pooled 26 subjects across two groups is still small.

## Why he rejects the authors' explanation

The authors proposed that because the strength group could use heavier loads during the shared hypertrophy phase, they experienced greater mechanical tension and therefore more growth. He calls this "unlikely and slightly misleading," and the argument is the substantive part of the video.

Mechanical tension's largest component is **active tension** — force produced by recruited fibres. Recruitment and force output scale with **effort**, not with absolute load. In weeks 4-8 both groups did 8-12 reps to failure, so both were at the same relative effort and both would have reached high active tension. That the strength group's bar was heavier is irrelevant.

His illustration is clean: a beginner and a three-year lifter both take a set of 8 to failure on the squat. The trained lifter uses far more weight. Does he grow more *because* of the weight? Obviously not — if anything the beginner grows more, from novelty.

**This is a good correction of a common category error** — conflating load with tension — and it is applied against the study he is presenting favourably, not against a straw man.

## His two alternatives

**Neural adaptations.** Some research suggests heavier-load training produces neural adaptations that increase fibre recruitment and force per recruited fibre. If so, the strength group entered their hypertrophy phase able to generate more active tension at the *same* relative effort. He then supplies the counter himself: neural adaptations are generally thought to occur mostly in the early months of training, and these subjects averaged 4.5 years. He adds that he has seen evidence trained individuals can still adapt neurally, so leaves it unresolved.

**Resensitisation.** Given how popular 8-12 reps are, the subjects had probably been training in that range for years. Three weeks at 1-3 reps might restore sensitivity to it. He labels this a hypothesis and states plainly: **"I'm not aware of any strong evidence to support this claim."** He also notes the authors did not report prior training, so the premise is itself an assumption.

## The evidence pointing the other way

He assembles three indirect lines, each with its limitations stated:

- **Prestes et al.** split 20 trained women (6+ months) into **linear** (12-14 reps progressing to 4-6 over 12 weeks) and **reverse linear** (4-6 progressing to 12-14), training 3×/week, with recovery weeks at weeks 4, 8 and 12. The reverse-linear group is loosely analogous to strength-then-hypertrophy. **Fat-free mass gains favoured the linear group.** He immediately discounts it partway: fat-free mass was measured by **skinfold calipers**, which he calls a poor tool next to the ultrasound used in Carvalho.
- **Alternating rep ranges.** If low-rep work inside a hypertrophy programme helps, mixing rep ranges should beat a fixed one. The research finds **no difference**, at least over 12 weeks or less. Carvalho ran 8 weeks, so it sits inside that window.
- **A Grgic meta-analysis** of linear vs daily undulating periodisation found **no significant difference** for measures of muscle growth — so the *organisation* of rep ranges appears not to matter over short timeframes.

## His verdict

He does not have enough to conclude that getting strong first builds more muscle later. He also does not pretend the opposing evidence is decisive — he calls it indirect, and points out that the **resensitisation hypothesis specifically survives all of it**, since none of the three lines tests a deliberate break from a habitual rep range. He wants a direct replication and says he will make an updated video if one appears.

**Where the evidence is thinnest:** the entire discussion of mechanisms rests on one 26-person, 8-week study with a single hypertrophy measurement site. His refusal to convert it into a recommendation is the correct call, and worth noting given the video's clickbait title argues the opposite.
