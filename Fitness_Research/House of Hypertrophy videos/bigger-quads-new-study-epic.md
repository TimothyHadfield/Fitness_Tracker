# Leaning-Back Leg Extensions and the Rectus Femoris Problem

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Bigger Quads: This NEW Study is Epic" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ao50iV9ENdc)) · 2024-05-20 · 10 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- New trial: **22 untrained men**, one leg trained a normal leg extension (hip flexed 90°), the other a leaning-back leg extension (hip flexed 40°), knee moving 110°→0° in both, 15–20 rep range with load raised whenever 20 reps were hit on set one.
- **Rectus femoris growth was 1.4 to 2.7× greater with the 40° variation**, at both the upper and lower regions measured. Vastus lateralis growth was similar between variations — exactly what you would predict, since it crosses only the knee and both legs moved through the same knee range.
- Mechanism: rectus femoris crosses hip and knee, so leaning back extends the hip and puts it at a longer length. This is the biarticular case for long-length training, and this study is one of its cleanest demonstrations.
- Mitsuya 2023 (9 bodybuilders, MRI-based activity, 0° / 40° / 80° hip flexion) suggests the finding may extend to trained lifters: both 0° and 40° beat 80° for upper and middle rectus femoris. No advantage for 0° over 40° — if anything the raw numbers favoured 40°.
- Squats, leg presses and lunges **minimally grow the rectus femoris** but develop the vastii well, because the rectus femoris is a hip flexor and a strong contraction would fight the hip extension the lift requires.
- His practical conclusion: run **both** an isolated knee extension and a combined knee-and-hip extension exercise. That is the recommendation, not a single best exercise.

## The new study
The within-subject design is the strength: the same 22 men trained both variations, so genetics, nutrition and lifestyle cannot confound the contrast. Only one variable differed — hip angle. Rectus femoris and vastus lateralis were each measured at an upper and lower region. The rectus femoris result is large by the standards of this literature; the vastus lateralis null is the internal control that makes it credible.

## Does it hold in trained lifters?
Mitsuya 2023 is the bridge. Nine bodybuilders performed leg extensions at 0°, 40° and 80° hip flexion, with muscle activation measured indirectly by MRI. Upper and middle rectus femoris activity was higher at 0° and 40° than at 80°; lower rectus femoris did not differ statistically, though values trended the same way.

He handles this carefully. He notes that this activation measure has been associated with actual growth in prior analyses, but that **none of those papers verified it predicts growth when comparing exercises at different muscle lengths**, and that it may fail to capture passive forces generated at long lengths. His conclusion is therefore that the MRI data may *underestimate* the 0° variation, and that a direct 0° vs 40° growth comparison is still needed. That is an unusually disciplined use of a surrogate measure.

## Alternatives to the machine
He names two: the reverse Nordic curl, and a squat variation the auto-captions render only as "squat" (from his description — virtually no hip flexion, deep knee flexion — most likely the sissy squat). Both are done at essentially zero hip flexion, like the 0° leg extension, but allow greater knee flexion — so all four quad heads reach longer lengths than any leg extension. He hypothesises they could grow the vastii even more, then immediately flags the counter-hypothesis: there may be a threshold beyond which additional lengthening stops helping. He calls both "excellent alternatives" as a hypothesis, not a finding, and says training normal leg extensions is perfectly fine if preferred.

## Compounds versus isolation for the vastii
Most EMG comparisons he found showed squats, lunges and leg presses producing higher vastii activity than leg extensions. He restates the limitations of EMG rather than leaning on it, and reports two growth studies instead. Brad Schoenfeld had flagged an upcoming paper finding leg presses grew vastus lateralis better while leg extensions targeted rectus femoris. And a study comparing Smith machine squats to leg extensions in trained subjects found leg extensions grew rectus femoris more (especially the lower region), while vastus lateralis growth favoured Smith squats at the upper and middle regions and — interestingly — favoured leg extensions at the lower region. He notes limitations with that study and wants it replicated.

His inference: the vastii may grow better in at least some regions from combined knee-and-hip extension work, so a program should contain both movement classes.

## Where this sits against the library
The library holds the **hack squat** as the best-supported quad pick and the rectus femoris as a known gap in squat patterns. This video confirms the gap in strong terms — he generalises it to all combined knee-and-hip extension exercises, with a clean mechanistic reason (rectus femoris hip-flexion function opposing the lift), and treats it as established across multiple studies.

It does not support the hack squat as the single best pick. He explicitly argues against a best-exercise framing here: the hack squat is a combined knee-and-hip extension movement and so falls in the class he says will not meaningfully grow the rectus femoris. His recommendation pairs a compound with an isolated knee extension, preferably a leaning-back leg extension. He also defers all squat/leg press/lunge comparisons to a future video, so this video contains no ranking among compounds at all.

**Overreach flag:** the reverse Nordic and squat-variation recommendations rest entirely on muscle-length reasoning with no growth data comparing them to leg extensions — he labels them a hypothesis, but the video's summary framing still lands them as recommendations.
