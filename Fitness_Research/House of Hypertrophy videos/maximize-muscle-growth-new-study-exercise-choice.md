# Two Principles for Picking Exercises: Biarticular Muscles Lose Out in Compounds, and Longer Is Better

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Maximize Muscle Growth: This NEW Study On Exercise Choice is Epic" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IoaYrIZM0BM)) · 2024-07-29 · 19 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **The new study** (Burke et al. 2024): **28 trained subjects**, within-subject design — one leg trained the leg press while the other trained the leg extension; one leg trained straight-leg calf raises while the other trained bent-leg. All to failure at 8–12 reps.
- **Rectus femoris grew better with the leg extension; vastus lateralis grew better with the leg press.** So the quads split, and neither exercise alone covers them.
- **Medial gastrocnemius clearly favoured straight-leg calf raises**, lateral gastrocnemius favoured them less clearly, and **soleus mildly favoured bent-leg** — a difference he says many would just call similar.
- **Principle 1:** a biarticular muscle grows suboptimally when one of its actions *opposes* what the compound exercise requires at the other joint. Rectus femoris flexes the hip while the leg press extends it.
- **Principle 2:** positioning one of the two joints a biarticular muscle crosses so the muscle is **longer** produces more growth — straight knee lengthens the gastrocnemius and avoids active insufficiency.
- **Practical upshot for quads:** run both an isolated knee-extension exercise (for rectus femoris) and a quad-focused compound (for the vasti). For calves, straight-leg raises may be all you need.
- **He refuses to resolve the biceps case**, and the reasoning behind that refusal is the most valuable part of the video.

## The study design, and why it matters
The within-subject unilateral design is the strength here: because all 28 subjects trained all four exercises, genetics, nutrition and lifestyle are controlled by construction. He puts it plainly — your left leg doesn't sleep more or eat more protein than your right. Quadriceps growth was measured at the rectus femoris and vastus lateralis, summed across three regions; calf growth at lateral gastrocnemius, medial gastrocnemius and soleus, **each at a single region only**. That asymmetry in measurement density is worth carrying forward: the calf conclusions rest on thinner data than the quad ones.

## Principle 1: opposing actions suppress involvement
All four quadriceps heads cross the knee and extend it, but the **rectus femoris also crosses the hip and flexes it**. Any compound that trains quads well — leg press, squat — requires simultaneous knee *and* hip extension. A strong rectus femoris contraction would partly work against the hip extension, so the nervous system plausibly minimises its contribution. Hence: compounds underdevelop the rectus femoris, and isolated knee extension is needed.

He extends the principle to the **triceps long head**, which crosses the shoulder and contributes to shoulder extension and adduction — the opposite of the shoulder flexion and abduction in pressing. Direct growth data support this: pressing grows the lateral and medial heads better than the long head. Isolated elbow extension does the job for the long head. He speculates that a pullover-to-extension hybrid, combining elbow extension with shoulder extension, might work well, and then immediately says there is **no direct data on it** and that he is sceptical it beats isolation. That restraint is characteristic.

## The biceps case, and why he won't call it
This is where the video earns its keep. The biceps cross the shoulder (contributing to flexion and abduction), so by Principle 1 pulling exercises — rows, chin-ups, pull-downs — should underdevelop them, since those involve shoulder extension and adduction. He has direct support: dumbbell rows with a supinated grip produced **much lower elbow flexor hypertrophy than dumbbell curls**.

Then he lays out three counterarguments *against his own principle*, calls them "pretty good", and answers each:

1. **Biceps involvement at the shoulder is minimal** (per a review of biceps shoulder actions), so the nervous system needn't suppress it. His counter: minimal isn't zero, and it still partially shortens during pulling.
2. **A pull-down vs curl study found similar elbow flexor growth.** Two counters. The measurement was *elbow flexors*, which includes the **brachialis** — a monoarticular muscle unaffected by the principle — so biceps-specific growth can't be isolated from it. And, stated transparently, **that study's author has been involved in multiple studies with statistical anomalies**, so validity is questionable. (Worth cross-referencing: this library already carries a note on retracted work from the same research group.)
3. **EMG shows high biceps activity in chin-ups and pull-ups.** His counter is the cleanest EMG critique in the channel: the triceps long head shows *high* EMG during bench press yet *minimally grows* from it, which proves EMG can mislead in exactly this class of question.

He ends **on the fence** — his own words — and asks for a study directly comparing chin-ups to curls. He offers a fallback hypothesis (in pulling exercises the back is usually the limiting factor, so the biceps may never reach maximal activation and tension) but labels it as hypothesis. The practical recommendation, direct biceps isolation, survives either way.

**Flagged:** Principle 1's causal claim — that the nervous system down-regulates the opposing biarticular muscle — is not measured anywhere in the new study. The study measured growth only. The neural account is inference layered onto an outcome, and while the outcome pattern is real and replicated across three muscles, the mechanism is asserted rather than demonstrated.

## Principle 2: train the biarticular muscle at longer lengths
Both gastrocnemius heads cross the knee as well as the ankle. In a bent-leg calf raise they start already shortened at the knee, and shorten further at the ankle at the top of each rep — **active insufficiency**, where a biarticular muscle shortened at both joints loses force capacity. Straightening the knee lengthens them and avoids it.

This is not a single-study finding. A **December 2023 study in untrained subjects**, also unilateral, compared bent-leg to standing straight-leg calf raises and likewise found **greater lateral and medial gastrocnemius volume increases with straight-leg raises and similar soleus growth**. Two independent designs, same direction.

He then shows the principle already holds across three other muscles from prior research:
- **Hamstrings** — lengthened by hip flexion; **seated leg curls beat lying leg curls** for hamstring hypertrophy.
- **Rectus femoris** — lengthened by leaning back on the leg extension; a preprint found this beat normal leg extensions.
- **Triceps long head** — lengthened by shoulder flexion; **overhead extensions beat pushdowns** for long-head growth, and unexpectedly also produced greater combined medial and lateral head growth.

He is careful to connect this to the wider long-length literature without overclaiming the mechanism: active insufficiency is "one potential mechanism", and others are "almost certainly in play too". His practical line on triceps is characteristically moderate — keep pushdowns if you like them, but include at least one exercise with the shoulder flexed.

## The two results the principles don't explain
He does not skip the awkward findings, which is the mark of the channel.

**Why did the vastus lateralis grow better with the leg press?** The tempting story — that with rectus femoris suppressed, the other quad heads pick up the slack and grow more — he rejects, because the parallel prediction for the soleus fails (see below). His preferred explanation is Principle 2 again: the leg press involves **more knee flexion** than a standard leg extension, so it trains the vasti at longer lengths.

**Why didn't the soleus grow much better with bent-leg raises?** The compensation logic predicts it should: reduce gastrocnemius involvement and the soleus takes over. The data don't show it, at least not significantly. He accepts the result over the reasoning, and concludes straight-leg raises may be sufficient, with bent-leg useful only if you specifically want to isolate the soleus.

**Overall recommendation:** for maximal quadriceps development, use both an isolated knee-extension movement and a quad-focused compound. The two exercises grow different heads and are not substitutes.
