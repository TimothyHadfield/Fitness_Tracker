# Combining Rep Ranges: The Fibre-Type Reason Is Wrong, The Other Reasons Might Not Be

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The BENEFITS of Combining Rep Ranges" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C1YUvQaIErM)) · 2022-09-26 · 12 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- The standard justification for mixing rep ranges — that heavy reps grow fast-twitch fibres and light reps grow slow-twitch fibres — **is not supported**. A meta-analysis by [Grgic](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33312275/) pooling five studies (high reps ~20–40 at 30–50% 1RM, low reps 3–10 at 75–90% 1RM, all to or near failure) found **both loads grew slow and fast-twitch fibres similarly**.
- The defensible case for mixing is **concurrent development of other attributes**: low reps favour 1RM strength and probably bone density; high reps favour local endurance, mitochondrial protein content and likely capillarisation.
- **Direct hypertrophy evidence shows no difference.** Most trials comparing a single rep range to varied rep ranges find equal growth. The one outlier — a 2003 study in previously untrained women training nine months — favoured varied rep ranges on **fat-free mass**, which he immediately discounts as a weak measure from a single study.
- His hypertrophy argument for mixing is therefore **entirely mechanistic**: better neural recruitment from heavy work, capillarisation and aerobic capacity from light work, and pain tolerance. None of these has been shown to translate into more muscle in a mixed-rep design.
- **Strength results favour varied rep ranges, but the comparison is confounded** — the varied groups also trained more often with heavy loads, which alone could explain the gap.

## Why the fibre-type story fails
The intuition is that slow-twitch fibres (fatigue-resistant, low force) would respond to long light sets and fast-twitch fibres (high force, fatigable) to heavy sets, so a complete programme needs both. Grgic's meta-analysis tested this directly at the fibre level and found no such split: provided sets reach or approach failure, both loading zones grow both fibre types to a similar degree. This kills the most commonly cited reason for periodising rep ranges, and he says so plainly before offering any alternative — a good example of him dismantling the popular version of his own conclusion first.

## What varied rep ranges do buy
**Low reps.** Better 1RM gains, via skill with heavy loads and neural adaptations that improve the ability to recruit fibres and expose them to tension. He connects this to health rather than sport: cohort and meta-analytic data associate low muscular strength with elevated all-cause mortality independent of muscle mass ([Metter 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12242311/), [García-Hermoso 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29425700/)). Heavy loading also appears more consistently effective for bone mineral density, though light-load work can raise it too.

**High reps.** Better local endurance — more reps at a given %1RM — plausibly mediated by mitochondrial and vascular adaptations. A 2019 Canadian study ([Lim 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31306302/)) found 30% and 80% 1RM to failure produced similar slow and fast-twitch fibre growth, but **mitochondrial protein content increased more with 30% 1RM**. On capillarisation, the one study he cites found **no statistical difference** between 20–25 reps and 8–12 reps, with percentages favouring the higher reps — he states the null result rather than reporting only the direction.

## The speculative hypertrophy case
Three proposed routes, all indirect:
1. **Neural.** Not everyone can fully recruit a muscle voluntarily; he cites data putting roughly 15.5% of quadriceps activation potential out of reach versus about 5% for elbow flexors and calves. Heavy work could unlock some of that.
2. **Capillarisation.** Correlational work in older men ([Snijders 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27897408/), [Moro 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31518665/)) and a 2022 study where prior aerobic conditioning preceded greater fast-twitch growth ([Thomas 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35971745/)) suggest capillary density may gate hypertrophy. He also notes lifting is more aerobic than assumed — even a 200 m sprint draws roughly 29% from aerobic metabolism — so aerobic capacity supports between-set and between-session recovery.
3. **Pain tolerance.** Habituating to a set of 25 makes a set of 8 feel manageable. He concedes dedicated cardio beats high reps for central aerobic adaptations, but keeps high reps because mitochondrial and capillary adaptations are local to the trained muscle.

**Flagged:** the whole "combining is better for hypertrophy" section runs on mechanism, correlation and acute markers. The only outcome data he has says no difference, and he says so — but the video's framing ("the benefits") still promises more than the evidence delivers. The strength conclusion is explicitly withheld pending better-controlled trials.
