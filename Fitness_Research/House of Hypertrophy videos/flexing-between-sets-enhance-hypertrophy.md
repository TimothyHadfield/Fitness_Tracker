# Flexing Between Sets: No Effect Anywhere Except the One Muscle the Programme Missed

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Does Flexing Between Sets Enhance Hypertrophy?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qWiY8sb7FZc)) · 2020-07-15 · 5 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Schoenfeld 2020: **27 men with at least one year of training experience**, **8 weeks, 3×/week**, six exercises (bench, overhead press, wide-grip pulldown, seated cable row, back squat, leg press), **3 sets to failure at an 8–12RM load, 2 min rest**.
- The isometric group spent the **first 30 seconds of every 2-minute rest** holding a maximal contraction of the muscle relevant to that exercise pair — triceps between the presses, elbow flexors between the pulls, quads between squat and leg press.
- Ultrasound thickness: **elbow flexors, triceps and lateral thigh showed no benefit** from the holds. **Mid-thigh (rectus femoris + vastus intermedius) slightly favoured the isometric group.**
- The explanation is exercise selection, not flexing: **squats and leg presses train the rectus femoris poorly**, and the quad hold is effectively an isometric leg extension filling that gap.
- So the honest reading is not "flexing builds muscle" — it is **"an isometric leg extension between squat sets partly substitutes for a leg extension."**

## The result, and how it was reached
Results were presented as confidence intervals on the difference (isometric minus passive). For elbow flexors, triceps and lateral thigh the intervals straddled zero. For mid-thigh, the interval leaned toward the isometric group.

Then the part worth watching. Two data points were doing a lot of work: one passive-rest subject who grew the mid-thigh substantially, and one isometric subject who barely grew at all. Removing them shifted the interval so that **every value favoured the isometric group**. Dhim reports this openly rather than quietly presenting the adjusted result, which is the right way to handle it — but it remains a **post-hoc removal on the study's one positive outcome**, in 27 subjects split two ways. Treat the mid-thigh finding as a hypothesis, not a result.

## Why the rectus femoris was the one that responded
The rectus femoris crosses the hip as well as the knee, and it is poorly stimulated by compound leg work. [Kubo 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-019-04181-y) found back squats at both 140 and 90 degrees of knee flexion **failed to grow the rectus femoris at all**. Leg extensions, by contrast, do grow it ([Ema 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-013-2700-1)). The isometric quad hold — seated, extending the knee, squeezing maximally — is a low-grade leg extension, so it added tension to the one quadriceps head the programme was neglecting.

That also explains the three null results: the elbow flexors, triceps and lateral thigh were already well stimulated by the exercises both groups performed, so there was no gap for the holds to fill.

## The two readings he gives
1. **If your programme already covers the muscle properly, isometric holds add nothing.** He extends this by prediction: had both groups also done leg extensions, the mid-thigh benefit would likely have vanished too.
2. **If you cannot or will not add leg extensions**, holds between squat or leg press sets are a workable way to get some rectus femoris stimulus out of rest time otherwise spent doing nothing.

He frames these as equally valid, which is the correct level of confidence given the data.

**Flagged:** mid-thigh ultrasound is a composite of rectus femoris and vastus intermedius, so attributing the effect to the rectus femoris is inference from the Kubo and Ema studies, not a direct measurement in this trial. It is a good inference; it is still an inference.
