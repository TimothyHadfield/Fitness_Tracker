# Long-Length Partials vs Full Range: The First Trial in Trained Lifters

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "It's Finally Here! This NEW Study on Muscle Growth is Epic" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6i7wHWITWeY)) · 2024-09-30 · 9 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- **25 trained individuals** (average **4.9 years** experience) trained twice weekly for **8 weeks**, one side of the body using full range of motion and the other long-length partials on every exercise, all to momentary failure. Elbow flexor and triceps thickness at 45%, 50% and 55% of humerus length grew **similarly**.
- Partials weren't better — but they weren't worse. That still matters: it suggests the **peak squeeze at the shortened position is dispensable**, contradicting the old assumption that a full range is needed for both the stretch and the contraction.
- The three prior studies favouring long-length partials were all in **untrained** subjects. This is the first in trained lifters.
- Crucially, this is about **range of motion within an exercise**, not exercise selection. Choosing exercises that reach longer muscle lengths still looks better.
- Distal regions were not measured. Prior work suggests that's exactly where long-length partials show their advantage.

## The study
[01:06] The within-subject design is the strength: the same person supplies both conditions, so genetics, nutrition and lifestyle can't confound the comparison — as Dhim puts it, one side of your body doesn't sleep more or eat more protein than the other. Both sides trained to momentary failure within their defined range.

Result: similar growth at all three measured regions.

## Three caveats he raises himself
[03:09] **One study proves nothing definitively**, and the result is specific to the exercises and techniques used.

[03:40] **Only the 45–55% regions were measured.** Muscles don't grow uniformly along their length, and long-length partials appear especially effective **distally**. In untrained subjects, a preacher curl study comparing short- to long-length partials found similar growth at 50% but much better growth at 70% for the long-length condition. A leg extension study comparing long-length partials to full range found vastus lateralis growth similar at 40–50% but better for partials at 70%, and rectus femoris growth better for partials across regions with the largest gap at 70%. So distal growth of the elbow flexors and triceps in the new study is simply unknown.

[04:42] **Exercise-specific effects.** Long-length partials may work best on exercises hardest at the **shortened** position — calf raises and leg extensions being the cases where untrained studies found partials superior. Several exercises in the new trial are hardest around mid-range, which could explain the null.

## The distinction that matters most
[05:12] Do not read this as "training at longer muscle lengths doesn't matter for trained lifters." In this study **both conditions reached the same maximum muscle length**; the partials simply omitted the shortened half. Exercise comparisons are a different question — seated versus lying leg curls, overhead extensions versus pushdowns, leaning-back versus normal leg extensions, incline versus preacher curls — where one option reaches a *longer* maximum length. Those still favour the longer-length option.

[06:44] He then adds the counterweight: there may be a **threshold**, beyond which longer isn't better. Lying down on the leg extension stretches rectus femoris more than leaning back and produced better activity than a normal leg extension, but was **not** better than the leaning-back version. And a forthcoming study apparently found cable curls with the shoulder neutral similarly effective to shoulder-extended when tension was highest at the start.

## Recommendations
[07:14] Basics first — hard training, sufficient volume, consistency. Still prefer exercises that train muscles at longer lengths. On range of motion, you now have flexibility: full range and long-length partials are both on the table, so experiment.

**Overreach flag:** the exercise-hardest-at-short-lengths hypothesis is a post-hoc pattern fitted to two untrained studies, and he labels it a possibility rather than a finding.
