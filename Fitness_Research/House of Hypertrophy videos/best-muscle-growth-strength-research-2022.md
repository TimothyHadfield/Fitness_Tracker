# The 2022 Round-Up: Stretch, Training Breaks, and Size-Strength

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The BEST Muscle Growth & Strength Research in 2022" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d5_j8DFkGUs)) · 2022-12-17 · 7 min
**Topic:** Evidence & method

## Bottom line
- **Stretch-position partials** beat full ROM and shortened partials for calf growth in a Brazilian trial, replicating a 2021 leg extension result — but both were in **untrained women**, and two studies is not a body of evidence.
- **Overhead triceps extensions** produced greater long-head growth than pushdowns, and unexpectedly greater lateral/medial head growth too, which he flags as **mechanistically unexplained**.
- **Inter-set stretching** appears to work only when the stretch is taken **immediately after the eccentric**; studies where subjects walked to another machine found no benefit and no harm.
- **Training breaks** may resensitise anabolic signalling: 7 consecutive sessions blunted post-training p70S6K activation, and a **10-day break restored it**. Molecular data only.
- Hypertrophy measures **correlate strongly** with bench press 1RM, including in elite powerlifters — but every study here is correlational.

## Part I: the stretch research
**Kassiano et al.** had untrained women train calves for **3 sets of 15–20 reps to failure, 3x/week for 8 weeks**, comparing full ROM, stretch-position partials and shortened-position partials. Medial and lateral gastrocnemius growth tended to favour the stretch partials. **Pedrosa et al. (2022)** had found the same pattern the previous year on leg extensions. He immediately limits it: both were untrained women, and two studies is not enough. His recommendation is to experiment, not to switch.

**Maeo et al. (2023)** compared overhead extensions to pushdowns. Long head growth was greater overhead — consistent with the stretched position. But the **combined medial and lateral heads also grew more**, which shouldn't follow from the stretch explanation, and he says so rather than smoothing it over.

A 2022 US review (Schoenfeld et al.) covered whether stretch signals growth. Static stretching in humans doesn't appear useful for hypertrophy, though **extreme** protocols might: a **German study (Warneke et al.) found one hour of daily calf stretching produced significant gastrocnemius growth**. The review's mechanistic point is that the stretch should follow the lowering phase immediately, because the elastic elements are stiffer then. Evidence:
- **Silva et al.** — 30 s calf stretch between calf press sets, greater gastrocnemius growth. **Conference abstract only, never published or peer-reviewed** — he says this explicitly.
- **Van Every et al. (2022)** — 20 s calf stretch between calf presses, **greater soleus growth but not gastrocnemius**.
- **Wadhi et al.** — walking to cable-fly machines to stretch the chest between bench sets: no difference.
- A Japanese paper on flywheel squats with a quad stretch at a table: no difference.
- **Evangelista et al. (2019)** — 30 s stretches between sets across a range of exercises did enhance hypertrophy in untrained subjects.

His own added caveat, beyond the review's: the soleus effect was **slight**, not large.

## Part II: training breaks
A German study found **seven consecutive training sessions significantly reduced post-training p70S6K activation** — the pathway becoming less responsive — and a **10-day break restored it**. He is explicit that this is molecular data with the outcome research "very much in its infancy," and that the honest use of it is reassurance that breaks are not something to fear, not a prescription to schedule them.

## Part III: does size drive strength?
A 2022 study found hypertrophy measures correlated strongly with bench 1RM in subjects benching **~91 kg on average**. In elite powerlifters, lean mass correlates strongly with bench, squat and deadlift; **Reya et al. (2021)** found hypertrophy measures were the best predictors of bench strength, ahead of elbow or shoulder flexion strength and technical factors. He states these are correlations, then argues from mechanism — more force-generating units in parallel means more force — which is plausible but is exactly the mechanism-over-outcome move to watch for.
