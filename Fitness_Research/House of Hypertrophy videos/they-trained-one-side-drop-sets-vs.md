# Drop Sets vs Normal Sets: A New Within-Subject Trial in Trained Lifters

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "They Trained One Side DROP SETS vs NORMAL Sets" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DB7DDt_Suo)) · 2026-02-02 · 15 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- New within-subject trial: **29 trained lifters** (average **7.6 years** experience), one arm on drop sets, the other on normal sets, preacher curls and kneeling lat pulldowns, 2–3×/week for **10 weeks**. Elbow flexor thickness gains **numerically favoured normal sets**, but the difference fell inside the authors' smallest effect size of interest and was called **practically equivalent**.
- Drop set sessions were **10 minutes shorter — 60% less time**. Per training minute, drop sets win clearly.
- This matches the wider literature: **two meta-analyses**, each pooling five studies, found similar hypertrophy between drop sets and normal sets, with drop sets cutting training time by **30–70%**.
- **Strength is the exception.** One meta-analysis found no significant strength difference, but he argues normal sets probably retain an edge — some normal-set conditions in those studies used lighter peak loads, and within-subject designs are confounded for strength by the **cross-education effect** (there is no such transfer for hypertrophy, so the design is fine for growth).
- Practical position: use drop sets on a **few** movements — ideally isolation or machine exercises — or as a final load-reduction set on an exercise you otherwise train normally. Doing them for every exercise in a program is questionable purely on how brutal they are.

## The new study
Training used an electromagnetic cable machine (Tonal). The normal-set arm did **4 sets of 8–12 reps to momentary failure** on preacher curls and **2 sets of 8–12 to failure** on pulldowns, loads adjusted to keep failure inside the rep range. Every rep used maximal intended concentric velocity with a controlled eccentric.

The drop-set arm did **2 drop series** on preacher curls and **1** on pulldowns. Each began at an 8–12RM load, and the device dropped the load automatically. The design detail worth keeping: the researchers deliberately built the protocol so drop sets contained a **greater proportion of reps close to failure** than normal sets — after the first drop, they wanted every rep at ≤2 RIR. Pilot testing settled on **dropping load 8% whenever concentric velocity fell 20% below the fastest rep of that segment**. In practice that produced roughly **1–3 reps between load reductions**, around four drops, and **16–24 total reps per drop series**. The second preacher-curl drop series started 15% lighter. Subjects alternated which limb went first each session.

Outcome: elbow flexor thickness at the mid-region increased slightly more with normal sets, but within measurement-error-informed bounds, so the authors read it as equivalence.

**Limitations he raises himself** — and this is characteristic of him:
- Training was only **semi-supervised** (logs plus frequent contact). Subjects reported discomfort during drop sets. Submaximal effort cannot be ruled out, and might explain the small numerical gap.
- The study was **funded by Tonal Systems**, whose device was used, and subjects kept the devices. He states he does not think it affected the results but discloses it anyway.
- He also notes the authors' track record on volume, frequency and proximity-to-failure meta-analyses, and that two of them run Data Driven Strength.

## What the rest of the literature says
Two meta-analyses, both pooling five studies, found similar hypertrophy between drop sets and normal sets. Crucially, **all five underlying studies used conventional equipment**, which makes them more transferable to a normal gym than the Tonal trial. One analysis reported drop sets cutting session time by **30–70%**.

The most time-efficient variant is a **single set with multiple load reductions**:

- **[Fink 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28474868/)** — 16 previously untrained men, triceps pushdowns. Normal: 3 sets to failure at a 12RM load. Drop: to failure at 12RM, immediately drop 20% and continue to failure, then repeat once more. Triceps CSA increases were not significantly different but **numerically favoured drop sets**, in far less time. Both groups did three "sets."
- **[Ozaki 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28532248/)** — 9 previously untrained subjects, dumbbell curls, arms assigned to heavy, light, or drop sets. Heavy: 3 sets to failure at 80% 1RM, 3 min rest. Light: 3 sets to failure at 30% 1RM, 90 s rest. Drop: to failure at 80%, then drop 15% and continue to failure, three more times. Elbow flexor CSA increases were similar across all three; drop sets took far less time.

A **less** time-efficient variant appears in **[Enes 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34260860/)**: trained subjects, three lower-body exercises; normal group 4 sets of 12 at 70% 1RM, drop group 3 sets of 10 at 75% 1RM with a single drop to 55% for 6 more reps within each set. Lateral thigh thickness changes were not significantly different — but this is multiple sets with one drop each, not one set with many drops, so it saves less time.

## Strength and endurance
The new study measured neither. On **strength**, one meta-analysis found no significant difference, but two of the pooled papers had normal-set conditions training at slightly lower peak loads, and heavier loads favour 1RM strength. Add the **cross-education effect** in within-subject designs and he concludes drop sets are clearly effective for strength but normal sets likely have the edge.

On **endurance** there is no meta-analytic data. The two studies that measured it generally favoured drop sets — except Ozaki, where **normal sets with lighter loads** produced larger endurance gains. One small study, so he does not lean on it.

## The rest-interval tension
This is the most interesting section. His own channel has covered evidence that **resting longer than 60 s between sets** tends to produce more hypertrophy than resting less. A single-set-with-multiple-drops protocol is effectively **zero rest**. So why does it not lose?

He gives two candidate explanations without committing to either:
1. Most of the "longer rest is better" evidence used exercises training **large amounts of muscle mass**. Shorter rest may be fine on small-muscle isolation work — and Fink and Ozaki used arm isolation. But we lack evidence that drop sets fail on large-muscle movements.
2. Some drop-set conditions accumulate **more hard reps**. The new study was designed that way explicitly. In Ozaki, the drop condition could be counted as five sets against the normal groups' three. But this does not explain Fink, where both did three.

His hedge if you want one: use the **Ozaki style**, where the drop protocol effectively gives you more sets than you would otherwise do.

**Overreach flag:** the "practically equivalent" framing rests on the authors' pre-declared smallest effect of interest, and the raw direction favoured normal sets. Combined with semi-supervised drop-set training and industry funding, calling this a clean equivalence is slightly stronger than the data alone supports — though he does surface every one of those concerns.
