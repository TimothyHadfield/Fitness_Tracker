# Training at Long Muscle Lengths: The 2022 Case

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "TRIPLE Hypertrophy by Training a Muscle Like This (New Research)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hQLGlATCw94)) · 2022-04-11 · 13 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- A 5-week preacher curl study found the group training only the **stretched half** of the range gained roughly **three times** the average elbow flexor thickness of the group training only the contracted half.
- The advantage is **regional**. Across three studies, upper and middle portions of a muscle grow similarly at long and short lengths; the **distal region** is where long-length training pulls away.
- One leg extension study suggests a **lengthened partial may beat a full range of motion**. He is careful that this is a single study and refuses to generalise from it.
- Even with an identical joint range, the exercise that puts the muscle under more stretch wins: **seated leg curls beat lying leg curls** for the three biarticular hamstrings, with no difference in the biceps femoris short head.
- He explicitly doubts this transfers to **back and traps**, and flags that all the data is limbs-only.

## The preacher curl study
**32 people untrained for at least six months** were assigned to a long-length group, a short-length group, or a non-training control. Both training groups did **dumbbell preacher curls twice weekly for 5 weeks, 3 sets of 10, 3 minutes rest**. The long-length group worked **0–50° of elbow flexion** (stretched); the short-length group **80–130°** (contracted).

Load prescription was unusual and worth recording. Each group's dumbbell load was derived from their **maximal isometric force at the mid-point of their own range** — 50° for the long group, 90° for the short group — starting at **30% of that value in session one and progressing to 100% by session ten**.

Elbow flexor thickness (biceps plus brachialis) was measured at **50%, 60% and 70% of upper arm length**. At 50% and 60% the groups were similar. At **70%, the long-length group grew significantly more**. Averaging the three sites, the long-length group's gains were **approximately three times greater**. He immediately notes the missing arm of the study: there was **no full-range-of-motion group**.

## Does a lengthened partial beat full range?
Most range-of-motion research compares full range against a **short-length** partial, and full range wins. He identifies the one study that instead compares full range against a **long-length** partial: previously untrained women trained the leg extension in one of four ways — long-length (**100–65° knee flexion**), short-length (**65–30°**), full range (**100–30°**), or a varied group alternating long and short each session.

Vastus lateralis and rectus femoris cross-sectional area were measured at **40%, 50%, 60% and 70% of femur length**:
- **40%** — all groups statistically similar.
- **50% and 60%** — long-length and varied similar to each other, both greater than full range and short.
- **70%** — long-length greatest of all four.

So the long-length group came first overall, with the varied group a close second. He treats this as **evidence that a lengthened partial might beat a full range of motion**, and then says directly that one study cannot establish it as universally true.

## The supporting evidence
Three further lines:
- A **meta-analysis of nine isometric studies** comparing long versus short muscle lengths found greater hypertrophy at long lengths.
- A leg extension study (long: 90–40° knee flexion; short: 50–0°) found VL cross-sectional area **not different at 25% and 50%** of femur length, but **significantly greater for the long-length group at 75%**.
- A hamstring study compared **lying versus seated leg curls**, both through **0–90° of knee flexion** — the same joint range. Because three of the four hamstrings cross the hip, the hip flexion of the seated position stretches them more. Volume increases in **those three muscles were significantly greater with the seated leg curl**, while the **biceps femoris short head**, which crosses only the knee and is therefore at a similar length in both, grew **similarly**. This is the cleanest result in the video: same joint excursion, different muscle length, different growth, with the internal control behaving exactly as predicted.

## The regional pattern, and where it may not hold
Pulling the three regional studies together: upper and middle portions of a muscle grow **similarly, or slightly better at long lengths**; lower regions grow **substantially more** at long lengths. That pattern is consistent enough to be the video's most transferable claim.

His main self-imposed limit is muscle group. All the data covers **biceps, quadriceps and hamstrings**. He thinks limbs and probably chest will follow the pattern, but says he has "a hard time believing" partial rows in the bottom position beat full-range rows, or that partial shrugs in the low position beat full shrugs — and adds that he could be completely wrong. Registering an explicit prediction he might lose is characteristic of this channel.

## Practical application
- Prefer exercises and ranges that **meaningfully load the muscle in a stretched position** for limb muscles and likely the chest.
- **Seated leg curls over lying leg curls.**
- **Squat as deep as you comfortably can.**
- For hamstring work from deadlifts, avoid excessive knee bend and pick a variation allowing significant hip flexion, such as a stiff-legged deadlift.
- Lengthened partials will produce robust growth if you want to experiment with them, but he does not claim they beat full range.

## Where the conclusion outruns the evidence
The "triple hypertrophy" headline comes from **5 weeks of training in untrained people on an isolation exercise with no full-range comparison**, and the load-prescription method was non-standard. The full-range-versus-lengthened-partial claim rests on a **single study**, which he says. Worth flagging for the library: **later trained-subject research (2024) found lengthened partials roughly equal to full range**, so the tentative framing here aged better than the title.
