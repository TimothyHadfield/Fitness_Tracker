# A Week Off Mid-Block Cost Nothing in Size and a Little in Strength

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Train LESS For More Muscle? (New Training Breaks Data)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vl1Q3dzW0h0)) · 2023-06-26 · 7 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **39 trained individuals** (avg 3.5 years' experience), 9 weeks: a group that took week 5 completely off matched the continuous group on **every** lower-body growth measure.
- Strength gains were **slightly greater** in the continuous group — isometric leg extension and Smith machine squat 1RM.
- The break group achieved equal growth on **40 fewer lower-body sets, about 11% less work**.
- This does **not** support the anabolic-resensitisation hypothesis, but 9 weeks may be too short to test it.
- Practical read: breaks are not something to fear, and there is no case for scheduling them on a fixed calendar rather than when you want one.

## The hypothesis being tested
Molecular data suggests the anabolic pathways regulating growth become desensitised over consecutive weeks of training, and that a break may resensitise them — implying scheduled breaks could yield more muscle long term. Dhim is clear that this was **only molecular data** before this trial, which is the first to measure hypertrophy and strength outcomes directly.

## The trial
Both groups trained upper and lower body twice weekly; all measurements were on the lower body, so the analysis covers only the Smith machine back squat, leg extension, calf press and seated calf raise. The **normal group** trained all nine weeks. The **break group** trained weeks 1–4, rested completely in week 5, and trained weeks 6–9.

Strength: maximum isometric leg extension and Smith machine back squat 1RM. Growth: rectus femoris and vastus lateralis thickness at 30%, 50% and 70% of lower leg length, plus medial gastrocnemius, lateral gastrocnemius and soleus at 25%.

All growth measures increased **similarly** between groups. Both groups increased strength, but gains were slightly greater in the normal group. He also notes something rarely reported: the break group **reported feeling lethargic after the break rather than refreshed**.

## Reconciling it with Ogasawara
An earlier study by Ogasawara ran 24 weeks, with one group training continuously and another cycling 6 weeks on, 3 weeks off. Chest and triceps growth matched despite the break group training **25% less** — and bench press strength gains matched too, which conflicts with the new study's small strength decrement.

He then makes the most interesting point in the video, and labels it speculation. In the Ogasawara data the **continuous group appeared to be approaching a plateau**, while the break group's gains rebounded quickly after each break. Over a longer horizon the break group might genuinely have come out ahead. The same could apply to the new 9-week study: if growth in the continuous group had plateaued and the break group had even a small resensitisation effect, the ranking could invert. He is careful to say this is speculative and unresolved.

## What he draws from it
Two defensible interpretations are offered rather than one. If you like breaks, the good news is that a week off cost nothing in size. If you dislike them, the good news is they are not necessary — and strength gains were slightly worse with one. His recommendation is to take breaks or deloads **when your body or mind wants one**, not on a schedule.

One genuine gap he names: **deloads (reducing volume or intensity) are not the same as complete breaks**, and this study says nothing about them. Prior research found the molecular responses largely similar, but that is mechanism standing in for outcome data, and he says outcome research on deloads is still needed.
