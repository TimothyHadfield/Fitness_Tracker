# Adding Load or Adding Reps: Which Progression Builds More?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The BEST Progressive Overload Way (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l-IOxNL81O4)) · 2024-03-11 · 13 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- A 2024 within-subject trial found progressing by **adding reps at a fixed load** grew the quads as well as progressing by **adding load in a fixed rep range**. A 2022 study in trained lifters found the same.
- His reframe is the useful part: progressive overload is **not "training harder than last time"** — it is what keeps each session **as hard as usual**. Successful overload is **evidence that adaptation happened**, not the cause of it.
- Because 6–35 reps produce similar growth when taken to or near failure, rep progression has a ceiling. Past ~35 reps, add load. Over years **most people will use both**, with rep progression dominating on small-muscle exercises and as training age rises.
- He argues **adding sets or sessions is not progressive overload** — it adds work rather than maintaining hard work per set. This is a definitional claim, and one of his own citations cuts against the practical version of it.
- For **strength**, adding load is likely better than adding reps, and low-rep work needs load progression by definition.

## The new study
**39 previously untrained individuals** trained a **unilateral leg extension, 4 sets, 2–3 times per week for 10 weeks**. One leg progressed by **load** — adjusting weight to keep reaching failure in the **9–12 rep range**. The other leg progressed by **reps** — keeping the session-one load and going to failure each set, moving from an average of **9 reps to 15 reps** by the end.

The design deserves credit and he says so: because each subject was in both conditions, genetics, nutrition and lifestyle cannot confound the comparison. Dominant legs were split equally across conditions and the order of training was alternated each session.

**Vastus lateralis growth was similar between conditions. Leg extension strength gains were also similar.**

## The trained-subject replication
A **2022 study on 38 trained individuals** ran the same contrast across lower body exercises: a load group increasing weight within **8–12 reps**, a rep group holding the session-one load and adding reps. **Quadriceps and calf regional growth was similar between groups**, as were squat strength gains. So the finding is not a beginner artefact.

## Why the two are equivalent
His mechanistic account is simple and, unusually, doesn't overreach: high muscle fibre recruitment and tension are what stimulate growth, and you get them by training **to or close to failure**. Hard training produces adaptation; adaptation means you can now lift more weight for the same reps **or** more reps with the same weight; doing either **restores the same proximity to failure**. Both routes accomplish the identical thing.

Hence the reframe: **progressive overload keeps you training as hard as usual**, rather than digging into progressively deeper effort. It is proof gains are happening, not the mechanism by which they happen.

## The rep-range constraint
Between roughly **6 and 35 reps**, taken to or near failure, hypertrophy is similar. Below 6 there is probably too little time under high tension; above 35, fatigue likely impairs recruitment and tension. So there is only so far rep progression can run — beyond 35 reps, add weight.

Two practical consequences. On **small-muscle exercises**, load jumps are often unavailable without microplates, so adding a rep here and there is the realistic route. And as **training age increases** and the size of each adaptation shrinks, rep additions become far more common than load additions.

## Different paths within a session
Load progression across sessions is not the only lever. He covers two alternatives that both worked:
- One study had all subjects curl to failure starting each exercise at a **10RM load**. One group held that load across sets; a second dropped it **5%** on the final two sets; a third dropped it **10%**. 10RM was retested every two weeks to update loads. **Biceps hypertrophy was similar in all three** — and the group dropping 10% rated their sessions as **slightly easier** despite still training to failure.
- Another study compared increasing load across sets (fewer reps each set) against a more typical scheme, and again found **similar growth**.

## Sets and frequency
By his definition, adding a set changes nothing about how hard the earlier sets are — assuming the first session stimulated adaptation, those sets are now *easier*, and the extra set is just more total work. The same logic applies to adding a weekly session. He adds the recovery ceiling as a second constraint.

He then, characteristically, presents the study that complicates his own position: a paper he had covered previously found that **adding sets across weeks to very high numbers produced greater growth** than performing the same number of sets each week — though all groups also progressed load, and he says the study is not proof you must add sets. His summary is that keeping sets and frequency constant week to week, with changes over months or years, is fine and more common.

## Strength
Strength gains were similar between load and rep progression in both trials, but he does not take that at face value. He argues that since strength is defined as maximum load, **load progression is likely better**, and points to a study of trained men in which a group training to failure at **2–4 reps** beat a group at **8–12 reps** for bench press and squat strength — and holding a low rep range necessarily requires adding load. Two technical objections to the strength findings were deferred to a pinned comment, which is not retrievable here.

## Where the conclusion outruns the evidence
The claim that adding sets or frequency "is not progressive overload" is a **definitional move, not an empirical finding**, and he leans on it while his own cited set-progression study showed more growth from adding sets. Readers should take the video's real result — load and rep progression are interchangeable for hypertrophy — and treat the sets/frequency section as framing.
