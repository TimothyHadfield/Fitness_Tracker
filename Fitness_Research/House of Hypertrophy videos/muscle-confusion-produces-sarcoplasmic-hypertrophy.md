# Muscle Confusion Doesn't Add Growth — But It May Change What Kind

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Muscle Confusion Produces Sarcoplasmic Hypertrophy (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LYYXlYKT9e8)) · 2022-03-14 · 16 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Fox et al. ran a **within-subject design** — 20 trained men, one leg on a constant programme, the other randomly varying its training variables, twice weekly for 8 weeks. Whole-muscle CSA and fibre CSA increased **equally in both legs**. Muscle confusion adds nothing.
- It does not subtract anything either. The variation leg was not worse, so variety is a preference, not a mistake.
- The interesting result is *inside* the fibre. Both legs started at ~**70% myofibrillar space**. The constant leg stayed at ~70% — proportional growth of myofibrils and sarcoplasm, i.e. conventional myofibrillar hypertrophy. The variation leg dropped to **~61%** — the sarcoplasm grew disproportionately, i.e. **sarcoplasmic hypertrophy**.
- He does not sell the finding. He raises two reasons it may not be real: **internal measurement disagreement** in the paper itself, and the possibility that the variation leg was simply **swollen** at the time of measurement because it never got a full repeated bout effect.
- The generalisable point he draws is the useful one: **two protocols producing the same whole-muscle growth may not be producing the same thing inside the fibre**, and virtually the entire hypertrophy literature is blind to this.

## The study
Twenty men, average age 26, average 2.5 years training experience. Each subject had **one leg assigned to a constant condition and the other to a variation condition** — which eliminates genetics, nutrition, sleep and every other between-subject confounder. Both legs trained twice a week for 8 weeks. Results were reported across two papers (Fox et al. and Damas et al.), and he says explicitly that he is pooling from both.

**Constant leg, every session:** 4 sets of 9–12 reps to failure on unilateral leg press and 4 sets of 9–12 reps to failure on unilateral leg extension, 2 min rest, loads adjusted each set and across sessions to keep reps in range.

**Variation leg:** one of four protocols per session, randomised and balanced —
1. **Reps varied** — 4×25–30 to failure on both exercises, 2 min rest.
2. **Sets varied** — 8×9–12 to failure on both exercises, 2 min rest.
3. **Contraction type varied** — 4×10 eccentric-only contractions on both, at 110% of normal training load, 2 min rest.
4. **Rest varied** — 4×9–12 to failure on both, 4 min rest.

**Outcome:** vastus lateralis cross-sectional area increased similarly in both legs. Muscle fibre CSA from vastus lateralis biopsies also increased similarly. **Muscle confusion is not supported.**

He opens the video with the folk theory in the bodybuilders' own words — including an Arnold clip about the "shocking principle" — which makes clear exactly which claim is being tested.

## The myofibril packing result
This is what the video is actually about. Muscle fibres contain **myofibrils** (chains of sarcomeres — the force-producing units); everything else inside the fibre is **sarcoplasm**, largely water but also glycogen, sarcoplasmic proteins and non-contractile organelles.

Before training, both legs' fibres were about **70% myofibril by space**. After 8 weeks:
- **Constant leg: still ~70%.** Since fibres got bigger and the proportion held, myofibrils and sarcoplasm must have grown at the same pace — conventional or **myofibrillar hypertrophy**.
- **Variation leg: ~61%.** Same fibre growth, smaller myofibrillar share, so the sarcoplasm must have grown disproportionately — **sarcoplasmic hypertrophy**.

He inserts a clarification most coverage of this topic omits: sarcoplasmic hypertrophy **does not mean the myofibrils shrank**. They may have grown somewhat or not at all; the defining feature is only that the sarcoplasm grew relatively more.

## His speculation, labelled as speculation
Why would this happen? His honest answer is that nobody knows — this research area is "very much in its infancy." He then offers a hypothesis and tags it four separate times as speculation:

- Myofibrillar hypertrophy, involving proportional growth of the force-producing elements, should be more useful for strength. The constant leg progressed largely by adding load, which may be what drives it.
- The variation leg accumulated **more volume load** (sets × reps × load) by the end. More total work might favour sarcoplasmic expansion, perhaps via increases in energy-generating components within the fibre.

He then **argues against his own hypothesis** with the paper's own data: the researchers measured a range of energy-generating components in the sarcoplasm and found them **unchanged by either condition**, while total sarcoplasmic protein concentration — paradoxically — increased **only in the constant leg**. So the mechanism he proposed is not supported by the measurements available. Reporting this is the mark of the channel.

## Two reasons the result may not be real

**1. The paper disagrees with itself.** Myofibrillar space was measured by **phalloidin staining**. The researchers separately used **SDS-PAGE** to quantify actin and myosin heavy chain per milligram of wet tissue — both myofibrillar proteins, so a decrease implies myofibrils occupy less relative space (sarcoplasmic hypertrophy) and no change implies they do not (myofibrillar hypertrophy).

- **Actin** decreased in the variation leg and was unchanged in the constant leg — **agrees** with the phalloidin result.
- **Myosin heavy chain** decreased in **both** legs — which would imply both conditions produced sarcoplasmic hypertrophy, **contradicting** it.

He states he cannot reconcile these, guesses that different tissue samples were used for the different assays, and repeatedly hedges with "unless I'm mistaken" / "unless I've misinterpreted something." This is a self-taught creator reading methods sections closely enough to catch an internal inconsistency and honest enough to say he might be wrong about it.

**2. It may just be swelling.** Fibres swell for hours to days after damaging training, and swelling is water accumulating inside the fibre — which is **technically indistinguishable from sarcoplasmic hypertrophy** on these measures. The **repeated bout effect** normally reduces damage over weeks of training, but it is likely strongest when training variables stay consistent. The variation leg — which included **eccentric-only sets at 110% of normal load** — would have had less opportunity to develop it. So the variation leg may simply have been swollen at measurement rather than genuinely sarcoplasmically hypertrophied.

Measurements were taken **three days after the final session**, and he says plainly it is unclear whether that is long enough for swelling to dissipate under these circumstances. The researchers **did not measure swelling**, so the question is unresolvable. He closes: "nothing definitive can be concluded."

## The broader implication
The point he wants readers to carry away is methodological. Across the hypertrophy literature it is routine to find two protocols producing similar whole-muscle or fibre growth — light loads at 30% 1RM versus heavy at 80%, different frequencies, different volumes. Every one of those null results is a statement about **fibre size**, not about **fibre composition**. If Fox's finding holds, protocols that look equivalent at the ultrasound may be building structurally different tissue, with potentially different functional consequences.

That is a genuinely useful frame, and he is careful to present it as a question worth watching rather than a conclusion: "I wonder if growth within the fibres is different between these various things."

## Where the evidence is thin
The whole sarcoplasmic finding rests on **one study**, with an unresolved internal contradiction, an unmeasured confounder (swelling) that could fully explain it, and no functional outcome attached — nobody tested whether the two legs differed in strength or endurance. He acknowledges all of this. The one thing the video is unambiguously entitled to claim is the null: **randomly varying training variables produced no more and no less whole-muscle or fibre growth than a constant programme in trained men over 8 weeks**. Everything past that is an interesting hypothesis.

Note also the title. "Muscle Confusion Produces Sarcoplasmic Hypertrophy" states as fact what the video spends its last four minutes questioning — a clickbait framing at odds with the content's own caution.
