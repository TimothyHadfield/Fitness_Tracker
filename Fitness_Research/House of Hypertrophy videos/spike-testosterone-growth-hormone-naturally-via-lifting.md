# Chasing Post-Workout Hormone Spikes: Why the Mechanism Doesn't Cash Out

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Spike Testosterone & Growth Hormone (Naturally via Lifting Weights) For Enhanced Muscle Hypertrophy?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jXGiDFbyiqQ)) · 2021-12-31 · 23 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Training to maximise the **acute** post-workout spike in testosterone, growth hormone or IGF-1 is unnecessary. The mechanism is plausible on paper; the outcome data does not follow it.
- **Six correlation studies**: two found a relationship between hormone spikes and growth (Mangine, testosterone; McCall, growth hormone), four found nothing or near-nothing (West & Phillips, Fink, Mitchell, Morton). The two positives are internally messy.
- **Two "low-hormone arm vs high-hormone arm" trials** disagree: Ronnestad (n=11) found more distal elbow-flexor growth in the high-hormone arm; West (n=12) found no difference. The designs differ in whether the leg work came *before* or *after* the arm work.
- Two independent lines cut against the hypothesis: **free weights spike free testosterone more than Smith-machine equivalents but grow the same amount** (Schwanbeck, 8 weeks), and **short rest intervals spike growth hormone more but grow less** than 2.5+ minutes.
- His conclusion: for naturals inside the normal physiological range, **local factors inside the muscle** — recruitment, tension, then calories and protein — dominate over transient systemic hormone concentrations.

## What actually spikes the hormones, and why anyone thought it mattered
[00:00] The training profile that best produces acute spikes is: exercises involving a lot of muscle mass, moderate loads and reps, a good number of sets, short rest. The worked example he gives is **10 sets of 10 at 70% 1RM on the back squat**, load adjusted set-to-set to keep hitting 10 reps, which produced significant temporary elevations in total testosterone, free testosterone and growth hormone. Spikes peak somewhere between immediately post-session and 60 minutes after, then fall.

[01:30] The mechanistic case is genuinely reasonable, and he lays it out fairly before demolishing it. Free testosterone binds androgen receptors inside the fibre and raises muscle protein synthesis; it also binds a membrane-bound receptor that activates PI3K–Akt–mTOR. Growth hormone works mostly indirectly, driving hepatic IGF-1, which also feeds PI3K–Akt–mTOR. There is even a non-genomic route: testosterone raises intracellular calcium, calcium is central to force production, more force means more tension, and mechanosensors convert tension into a growth signal. If any of that scaled with circulating concentration, chasing the spike would be rational.

## Test one: do bigger spikes go with more growth?
[04:31] **Positive results (2).** Mangine and colleagues trained 33 men averaging 5.7 years of experience four times a week for 8 weeks, four sets per exercise, 3–12 reps at 70–90% 1RM, 1–3 min rest. Blood draws in weeks 1 and 8 confirmed acute spikes in testosterone, IGF-1 and growth hormone. Rectus femoris and vastus lateralis thickness and cross-sectional area (measured at ~50% femur length) rose, and testosterone spikes predicted that growth. Growth hormone spikes did not.

McCall and colleagues trained 11 experienced men three times a week for 12 weeks, three sets per exercise at a 10RM load with 1 min rest, sampling blood in weeks 4 and 8. Here training spiked growth hormone but barely touched testosterone or IGF-1, and the growth hormone spikes correlated with slow- and fast-twitch fibre CSA gains in the biceps. He is careful to flag two things that don't reconcile: **the growth hormone spikes correlated with fibre-level growth but not with whole-biceps CSA**, and IGF-1 barely moved despite growth hormone being supposed to act through it.

[08:35] **Null results (4).**
- *West & Phillips*: 56 men untrained for at least 8 months, 2–4 sets of 6–12 reps, five sessions a week, 12 weeks. No correlation for free testosterone or IGF-1 with vastus lateralis fibre growth; a *weak* correlation for growth hormone. There was also a weak correlation between cortisol spikes and fast-twitch growth — cortisol being catabolic, he reads that as noise, which is the honest reading.
- *Fink*: 20 men untrained for 2+ years, arm exercises, three sets of 8–20 reps, 30 s–3 min rest. No correlation between growth hormone spikes (week 2) and arm CSA. No other hormones measured.
- *Mitchell*: 23 men untrained for a year, 2–4 sets of 6–12 reps, 60–120 s rest, four times a week for 16 weeks. No relationship for any hormone with fibre growth.
- *Morton*: 49 men with 2+ years of training, three sets of 8–25 reps to failure, four times a week for 12 weeks. No relationship for any hormone.

Four of six null, and the two positives disagree with each other about *which* hormone matters.

## Test two: programmes engineered to spike hormones
[11:39] Correlation is not the design that answers the question, so he moves to the within-subject arm trials.

**Ronnestad** (n=11, untrained, 12 weeks, twice weekly): one arm did biceps curl, hammer curl and reverse curl for two sets each at 6–10RM — too little muscle mass to spike anything. The other arm did the identical curl work, but **first** performed leg press, leg extension and leg curl for three sets each with 60–90 s rest, which spiked testosterone and growth hormone through the subsequent curls. Elbow-flexor CSA gains were the same at two proximal sites but **greater in the high-hormone arm at two distal sites**.

**West** (n=12, untrained, 15 weeks, 1–2 sessions weekly): the same design with one change — the leg work came **after** the arm curls. Spikes in testosterone, growth hormone and IGF-1 all occurred. Elbow-flexor CSA gains were identical at every region, and so were slow- and fast-twitch fibre CSA gains.

[15:43] His candidate explanation for the discrepancy is exercise order: doing the hormone-spiking work first means the arm work happens under elevated concentrations, and Spiering's data shows that a compound upper-body protocol (four sets at 10RM) performed before knee extension/flexion work raised vastus lateralis **androgen receptor content** 180 minutes later, where the isolated protocol alone did not.

He then takes that hypothesis apart himself, which is the strongest section of the video:
1. Other studies find androgen receptor content **down-regulates** in the hours after a testosterone-spiking session (Ratamess).
2. If pre-loading spikes helped, compounds-before-isolation should beat isolation-first for arms. **Three exercise-order studies** (Simao; Spineti ×2) found biceps and triceps growth similar regardless of order — and if anything the numbers slightly favour doing the isolation work first.

He concludes the Ronnestad result may simply be small-sample noise. That is a creator arguing against a finding that supports the more interesting story, and it should be counted in his favour.

## Two more lines of evidence, from the other direction
[18:45] **Free weights vs machines.** Free-weight squats and bench presses spiked free testosterone more than the Smith-machine versions, yet after 8 weeks quadriceps and biceps thickness gains were similar (Schwanbeck).

[19:15] **Rest intervals.** Rest of ≤90 s spikes hormones — particularly growth hormone — more than ≥2.5 min. But on compound exercises with everything else matched, ≥2.5 min produces *more* hypertrophy. The variable that maximises the spike is the one that grows less muscle. This is the cleanest single disconfirmation in the video.

## Why the mechanism fails to cash out
[20:17] He offers three candidate explanations and refuses to pick between them:
1. **Receptor down-regulation** cancels the ligand increase.
2. **Magnitude** — training-induced spikes are transient and never approach the supraphysiological range that unambiguously does build muscle. He is explicit that steroids work; the argument is about the size and duration of natural fluctuations, not about whether testosterone is anabolic.
3. **Purpose** — the spikes may be a response to metabolite accumulation, or oriented towards mobilising fuel for contraction, rather than towards post-exercise protein synthesis.

## Where this lands against the library
This directly supports the library position that **acute post-training testosterone spikes do not build muscle**, and it does so with a wider evidence base than the wood-chopping worked example: six correlation studies, two hormone-manipulation trials, plus the free-weight/machine and rest-interval literature. It also keeps the acute and chronic questions properly separated — he concedes supraphysiological chronic elevation is powerfully anabolic while arguing the transient natural version is not.

**Overreach flag:** the exercise-order/androgen-receptor speculation at 15:43 is presented as a possible mechanism for a single small (n=11) result, and while he does dismantle it himself, several minutes are spent building a story on one acute receptor-content measurement. He also treats "no correlation between spikes and growth" as evidence the spikes are causally inert, which is the right direction but weaker than the interventional data he presents later.
