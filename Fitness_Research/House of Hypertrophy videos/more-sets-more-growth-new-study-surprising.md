# The 2024 Volume Meta-Regression: No Plateau Found, But Sharp Diminishing Returns

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "More Sets, More Growth: This NEW Study is Surprising (& Epic)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UIMuCckQefs)) · 2024-11-18 · 17 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- A new meta-regression pooling **35 studies and over 1,000 subjects** found no plateau and no inverted U. The best-fitting model was a **square-root curve** — more sets kept producing more growth all the way to **43 weekly sets per muscle**, with heavily diminishing returns.
- The authors estimate later sets are about **one third as stimulative** as the first ones. That, not the 43 figure, is the practical finding.
- The paper does something new: it tested how to *count* sets. The **fractional method** — indirect sets count as half a direct set — explained the data better than counting all sets equally or counting only direct sets, with the evidence "strongly or very strongly" favouring it.
- He refuses the face-value conclusion. Above **25 weekly sets** the credible intervals widen sharply because few studies go there, he knows of **two unpublished studies failing to find benefit at very high volumes**, and the rest-interval moderator is exploratory at best.
- His own recommendation is unchanged and deliberately conservative: fix training quality first, then **10–20 weekly sets per muscle** for most people, progressed gradually, with very high volumes reserved for one or two specialised muscle groups.

## What the analysis actually did
Volume here means working sets — not warm-ups — with most included studies using around 10 reps per set and most sets taken to failure or within about two reps of it. He notes the definition of failure varies across the pooled studies, which is a real source of noise.

The genuinely novel contribution is the set-counting comparison. A **direct set** is one where the muscle is the primary force generator (biceps in a curl, chest in most bench presses); an **indirect set** is one where the muscle contributes but is not primary (biceps in a row, triceps in a wide-grip bench). Three counting schemes were tested against the data:

- **Total** — direct and indirect count equally. Three sets of rows plus three sets of curls = 6 biceps sets.
- **Fractional** — indirect counts as half. Same example = 4.5 biceps sets.
- **Direct only** — indirect counts as zero. Same example = 3 biceps sets.

Fractional won, and he offers a sensible interpretation: indirect sets are clearly worth *something*, which sinks the direct-only method, but clearly worth *less than a direct set*, which sinks the total method. He also flags that the 0.5 weighting is a modelling convenience rather than an objective truth, and that no two people will agree on the direct/indirect classification for every exercise.

**This matters for reading every other volume study.** Almost all prior research counted volume by the total method, so historical set-count recommendations and this paper's numbers are not on the same scale.

## The result, and the four reasons he won't take it at face value
Growth increased monotonically to 43 weekly sets per muscle. He illustrates what 40 weekly biceps sets would actually look like to make clear how absurd a prescription that would be.

**1. Diminishing returns.** His orange-squeezing analogy: the first sets give a large return, the later ones much less. The authors put the later sets at roughly one third the stimulus of the initial ones. He frames this as the reason low-volume trainees should not despair — a respectable chunk of the available growth comes from the first few sets.

**2. Thin data above 25 sets.** The credible intervals widen conspicuously past this point because few studies have gone there. He states plainly that treating the model as the final word would be "a big mistake", and that a plateau may yet be found. He also volunteers that he is aware of **two not-yet-published studies that failed to find more growth at very high volumes** — disclosing evidence that cuts against the exciting result, which is characteristic of this channel.

**3. Swelling.** The most-argued objection online: is high-volume "growth" just fluid? He handles it in both directions. Yes, swelling can persist up to 72 hours, and most studies measured 48–72 hours after the last session. But the studies showing multi-day swelling did so after a **single novel bout**, and the **repeated bout effect** should blunt it over a training programme. His evidence is the strongest repeated-bout study available: previously untrained subjects doing five sets of 15 maximal eccentric-only leg extensions weekly for 10 weeks. Damage and fatigue markers persisted at least five days after session one; by the final week subjects recovered in a day or two. He notes the study did not measure swelling itself, and that **no study has compared swelling between different volumes** — so it is also possible high volumes cause no more swelling than moderate ones. Verdict: not convinced swelling confounds the data, but not dismissive either.

**4. Rest intervals.** Longer rests (~3 min) can beat shorter ones at matched sets — a study found greater quad hypertrophy from three leg-press sets with 3 minutes' rest than 3 sets with 1 minute — but **five sets with one minute matched three sets with three minutes**. That implies volume and rest trade off, and high volumes might only pay when rests are short. The meta-regression's moderator analysis did find growth plateauing earlier with 3-minute rests than 1-minute rests, but he refuses to run with it for two stated reasons: the authors themselves call the moderator exploratory and weakly powered, and accepting it would force you to also accept that very high volumes with one-minute rests are optimal overall, which is hard to justify. He adds that the longer-rest evidence is built mostly on compound exercises; short rests may be fine on isolation work.

Other moderators — trained vs untrained, ages 21–35, shorter vs longer study durations — showed a similar volume-growth relationship throughout, but he applies the same caution flag to all of them.

## What he tells you to do
1. **Maximise training quality before adding sets** — exercise selection, proximity to failure, rest intervals. He is explicit that volume is the last lever, not the first.
2. Then perform **as much volume as you can personally and practically handle consistently**. His guess for most people: **10–20 weekly sets per muscle**.
3. Adjust by feedback. Burnt out with minimal progress → do less. Recovering well → push past 20.
4. Set counts need not be uniform across muscles. **Muscle group specialisation** is the sane way to experiment with very high volumes — and he notes the one recent study finding a benefit to super-high volumes even with longer rest intervals did so in subjects **training only the quads**.
5. **Progress into high volumes gradually** rather than jumping — his own speculation, flagged as such.

## Where he is unusually careful, and where it strains
The video's whole structure is a headline result followed by four reasons not to believe the headline, including unpublished data he could easily have omitted. That is the rigorous part.

The soft spot is the recommendation itself. His 10–20 sets figure is offered as a guess ("if I had to guess"), and the analysis he just spent fifteen minutes on does not actually locate that range — the curve does not bend there. The number is his prior, sensibly held, not an output of the paper; the paper's honest output is "more is more, with a third the return, and we don't know where it stops."

Note also the counting mismatch: 10–20 sets under the **fractional** method is meaningfully more total work than 10–20 sets counted the traditional total way, and he does not spell that out when giving the recommendation.
