# Why "The Best Exercise" Is the Wrong Question

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Best Exercise for Muscle Hypertrophy (What Science Says)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JVyLnLWpbxE)) · 2022-01-10 · 10 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- Three separate arguments against a single best exercise: **modality doesn't matter**, **regional growth needs variety**, and **individuals recruit differently on the same lift**.
- Free weights vs machines: a meta-analysis (Heidel 2022) found them comparably effective, and Schwanbeck 2020 found similar biceps and quadriceps thickness gains after 8 weeks of an all-free-weight vs an all-machine program.
- Bodyweight is not inferior either: Kikuchi & Nakazato found push-up variations and bench press at 40% 1RM produced similar pec and triceps thickness gains over 8 weeks — consistent with the broader finding that **30–85% 1RM grows muscle similarly when sets go to volitional failure**.
- Costa 2021 (22 detrained men, 8 weeks): a group rotating three different exercises per muscle group across the week matched a non-varied group at some measurement regions and beat it at others. Variety buys regional coverage, not more total growth at any one site.
- Individual variation is real: on the bench press, some lifters preferentially recruit pecs, some front delts, some triceps (Stronska); hamstring recruitment on Nordic curls and stiff-legged deadlifts likewise differs between people (Boyer).
- He is not saying every exercise is equal. Three things do make an exercise worse: the target muscle not being the limiting factor, no stretch under load, and personal discomfort or pain.

## The mechanistic case for modality-agnosticism
This is where the video leans hardest on mechanism. Mechanical tension is the primary stimulus; mechanosensors within the fibre detect force and transduce it into a signalling cascade. Maximising growth means recruiting as many fibres as possible and holding decent force on each for a reasonable duration — and, as he puts it, the body has no mechanism to detect whether that force came from a barbell, a machine or bodyweight. His operational rule: roughly **5–35 reps at 3 or fewer reps in reserve** satisfies those conditions on essentially any modality.

He then adds passive tension. When a fibre is sufficiently stretched, titin generates passive force, analogous to a stretched rubber band, and mechanosensors — possibly within titin itself — may detect it. Exercises reaching a good stretch of the working muscle involve high passive tension, and free weights, machines and bodyweight movements can all achieve that (his examples for quads and glutes: barbell back squat, hex bar squat, pistol squat).

On the EMG evidence, he is careful in the way that makes this channel usable: he notes the "notable limitations" of muscle excitation studies before citing them, and only uses them for a corroborating point — that load-equated push-ups and bench presses generate similar pec, triceps and front delt excitation.

## Variety and regional growth
Costa 2021 is the load-bearing study. Twenty-two men detrained for at least six months, 8 weeks, 3 sets of 8–12 with 90–120 s rest, three sessions a week. The non-varied group repeated identical exercises; the varied group used different exercises on each of the three days. Thickness was measured at upper, middle and lower regions of rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, elbow flexors and triceps. Results were similar at some regions and favoured the varied group at others.

His explanation is that regions of a muscle, and muscles within a group, do not act in unison. Nunes 2020 found calf presses with feet rotated outward preferentially grew medial gastrocnemius and feet rotated inward preferentially grew lateral gastrocnemius. At the group level, back squats develop the vastii well but fail to meaningfully target rectus femoris, whereas leg extensions and reverse Nordic curls — isolated knee motion with little hip motion — preferentially grow it. The conclusion: roughly three varied exercises per muscle group per week grows more of the muscle, so asking for one best exercise misses that different exercises are complementary rather than competing.

## Individual differences
Stronska's data show relative recruitment of pecs, triceps and front delts on the bench press differs between individuals — some are pec-dominant, some delt-dominant, some triceps-dominant. Boyer observed the same for the hamstring heads on Nordic curls and stiff-legged deadlifts. His inference is appropriately narrow: that someone else grew a particular way from an exercise does not guarantee you will.

## What does make an exercise worse
He closes with the qualifiers, and this is the practically useful part.

- **Stability.** If your balance limits the set before the target muscle does, the exercise is suboptimal. A BOSU-ball pistol squat is his example; back squats, hack squats or assisted pistol squats provide a more stable environment.
- **Stretch.** The evidence is "increasingly leaning" towards exercises that reach a good stretch being a more powerful stimulus than those training only at short lengths.
- **Preference.** Anatomy, injury history and pain matter; and if mind-muscle connection works better for you on a dumbbell curl than a barbell curl, outcomes could be better there.

**Overreach flag:** the "5–35 reps at 3 RIR on any modality achieves maximal fibre recruitment" claim is inferred from recruitment theory, not measured in the studies cited — it is the mechanistic spine of the video and it is doing more work than the outcome data underneath it. The stretch qualifier is also hedged ("increasingly leaning") and then used as a firm selection criterion.
