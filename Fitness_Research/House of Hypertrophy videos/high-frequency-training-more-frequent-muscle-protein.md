# The Muscle Protein Synthesis Argument for High Frequency Doesn't Work

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "'High Frequency Training = More Frequent Muscle Protein Synthesis = More Gains'" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gYHqoiazXFU)) · 2022-05-02 · 12 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- The popular argument — train a muscle more often, spike muscle protein synthesis (MPS) more often, stay anabolic, grow more — **is not supported by the MPS literature it claims to rest on**. That is his conclusion, and he reaches it by dismantling the evidence step by step rather than by asserting it.
- The time-course data everyone quotes (trained lifters back to baseline by ~10 h, untrained still elevated at 40–50 h) is **mixed** muscle protein synthesis, which includes proteins that have nothing to do with hypertrophy. Mixed and **myofibrillar** MPS do not track each other.
- Myofibrillar MPS elevations only correlate with hypertrophy **once muscle damage has been attenuated** ([Damas 2016](https://doi.org/10.1113/jp272472)). Early in a programme, most of the signal is repair, not growth.
- When you restrict yourself to low-damage data, myofibrillar MPS stays elevated for **at least 48–72 hours** — which, taken at face value, argues for *lower* frequency, not higher. Neither study measured far enough out to find the endpoint.
- He is explicit that this leaves the pro-frequency case standing only on a **volume-distribution** argument: if there is a per-session ceiling on how much MPS a muscle can be driven to, spreading sets across days avoids wasted sets. He says there is no data verifying that either.

## The naive derivation, and why it fails twice

[02:34] A session raises MPS above muscle protein breakdown, and the review by [Damas 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25739559/) pooled the time-course data. In trained individuals MPS peaks a few hours post-session and has fallen sharply by 10 hours; in untrained individuals it peaks nearer 20 hours, has a larger area under the curve, and is still meaningfully elevated at 40–50 hours. Read straight off, that says trained lifters should hit a muscle every other day and untrained every third day. This is exactly the derivation used to justify high-frequency training online.

[04:36] **First failure: wrong protein pool.** All of that is *mixed* muscle protein synthesis, which sums every protein inside the fibre, most of which is irrelevant to size. Hypertrophy is conventionally an increase in **myofibrillar** (contractile) proteins. The two measures do not correlate: [Burd 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21289204/) had subjects do four sets of leg extension to failure at either 90% or 30% 1RM, and 24 hours later **mixed MPS was unchanged while myofibrillar MPS was significantly elevated**. Deriving frequency recommendations from mixed MPS is therefore reading the wrong number.

The myofibrillar time course, from two studies pooled in the same Damas review, looks different again: **similar shape in trained and untrained**, peaking around 5 hours, with a lower magnitude in trained subjects, and still elevated at 16 hours in both. Neither study followed subjects long enough to see it return to baseline.

[06:39] **Second failure: damage contaminates the signal.** [Damas 2016](https://doi.org/10.1113/jp272472) tracked 10 weeks of training and found that in the early sessions, myofibrillar MPS elevations were largely directed at **repairing damage** and did *not* relate to eventual growth. Only once the repeated-bout adaptations had blunted damage did post-session myofibrillar MPS correlate with hypertrophy. So the elevation you measure in an unaccustomed subject is partly, sometimes mostly, not a growth signal at all.

[08:12] Two datasets come close to a damage-free reading. In the Damas study, a session in **week 10** — when damage was low — left myofibrillar MPS still elevated at **48 hours**, with no later measurement taken. [Miller 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002437/) used an hour of one-legged kicking at 67% of maximum workload, a largely concentric protocol producing no signs of damage, and found MPS still elevated at **72 hours**, again with no later measurement. Both are floors, not endpoints.

That is the quiet sting of the video: the cleanest available numbers point at **48–72 hours or more** of elevation. Run the original argument on them and you land on training a muscle every second or third day at most — the opposite of what the argument is usually deployed to support.

## The assumptions nobody states

[09:13] The whole framework assumes you must wait for MPS to subside before re-stimulating it. He notes there is **no evidence that training into an elevated MPS response is harmful, and none that it is useful** — the question has simply not been asked.

[09:44] It also ignores volume. Set count per session presumably scales the size of the MPS response. Nine weekly biceps sets done as three sets on three days produces a steadier, smaller series of elevations; nine sets in one session produces a single large one. **The total area under the curve might be identical**, in which case frequency is irrelevant. He offers no data because none exists.

[11:17] He then gives the strongest surviving version of the pro-frequency case, and it is a volume argument rather than an MPS one: **if there is a per-session ceiling on stimulatable MPS**, nine sets in a day might push some sets past it into junk, and splitting the volume would raise net weekly MPS by avoiding waste. Speculative, and labelled as such.

## Where this sits against the library

It agrees with the library on both counts. Our position is that **acute MPS does not predict hypertrophy** — he demonstrates it directly via the Damas damage findings, which is stronger evidence than the usual hand-waving. And our position that the honest case for frequency is **volume spread rather than MPS theory** is exactly where he ends up: he discards the MPS mechanism entirely and says the real answer must come from studies that measured growth under different frequencies, which he defers to the following video.

**No overreach to flag.** This is a video whose entire content is refusing to draw a conclusion the data cannot support, including refusing the conclusion that low frequency is better — which the same data would license if he were arguing rather than reasoning.
