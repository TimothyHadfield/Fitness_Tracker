# Squat Depth: Deep Wins for Size, Specificity Rules Strength, and Athleticism Depends on Training Age

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Partial Squats Are Superior to Deep Squats for Size, Strength, and Speed (True or False?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L1vVOFtDUWQ)) · 2022-01-24 · 10 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- **Hypertrophy: deeper is better.** Two studies, both pointing the same way. Squatting to 60° of knee flexion left most of the front thigh ungrown; 90° was enough for the quads but not for glute max or adductors.
- **Strength: pure specificity.** You get better at the depth you train. Deep training beats partial training for deep 1RM; partial training beats deep training for partial 1RM. Four studies agree.
- **One study suggests combining both is better than deep alone** — a combination group beat a deep-only group on *both* full and partial 1RM. Single study, trained subjects, one specific partial depth.
- **Athleticism depends on training experience.** In untrained or lightly trained subjects, deeper squats produced better jump gains. In college athletes, shallow 55–65° squats produced the biggest vertical jump and 40-yard sprint improvements.
- **Injury risk: no support for the "deep squats are dangerous" claim.** A comprehensive review concluded deep squats do not carry greater risk, and suggested partials may carry *more* risk because they require heavier loads.

## Hypertrophy
**Bloomquist et al.** took 17 men untrained for at least the six months before the study and assigned them to a **0–60°** or **0–120°** knee flexion squat, three times per week for 12 weeks. Front thigh cross-sectional area (quadriceps, sartorius and adductors) was measured across **six regions along the thigh**. The deep group grew at all of them. The partial group grew only at the **two most proximal regions**. Region-by-region measurement is what makes this study valuable — a single mid-thigh slice would have missed the pattern entirely.

**Kubo et al.** took 17 men untrained for at least a year and compared **0–90°** to **0–140°**, three sets at roughly an 8RM load, twice per week for 10 weeks. Quadriceps volume increased **similarly** in both groups — so 90° may be deep enough for the quads specifically. But **adductor and gluteus maximus volume increases were significantly greater in the deep group**.

Read together: 60° is clearly insufficient; 90° is adequate for quads but leaves glute and adductor growth on the table.

## Strength
Bloomquist, Kubo, **Rhea et al.** and **Hartmann et al.** all show the same specificity pattern. Train partials, get better at partials. Train deep, get better at deep. Nothing surprising, and nothing that supports partials as a general strength superior.

The interesting exception is **Bazyler et al.**, on 17 men with an average full squat 1RM of **146.9 kg**. One group did full squats only; the other did **both full and partial squats**, partials to 100° of knee flexion, in a block-periodised design with heavy and light days. Weekly total volume — here defined as load × range of motion × reps — was matched. After 12 weeks, the **combination group had greater increases in both full squat 1RM and 100° partial squat 1RM**.

He flags the limits himself, and they are the right ones: the partial depth tested was specifically 100° (would a 60° partial do the same?), the subjects were already strong, and there is no replication.

## Athleticism
Three studies, and they split by training status.

- **Bloomquist** (untrained ≥6 months): 120° squats produced greater squat jump and countermovement jump height increases than 60° squats.
- **Hartmann** (men and women with low training experience): full squats beat 60° Smith machine partials for both jump measures.
- **Rhea** (college athletes, squatting 120–130 kg, vertical jump 73–75 cm, 40-yard dash 4.68–4.76 s): compared **55–65°**, **85–95°** and **>110°** squats. The **55–65° partials produced the greatest vertical jump and 40-yard sprint improvements**, and 85–95° beat >110°.

So the "partials are better for athletes" claim has real support — but from exactly one study in trained athletes.

## Injury risk
He caveats that he is not an injury expert, then points at Hartmann's extensive review, which concluded deep squats do not carry greater injury risk. Supporting observations from that review: professional weightlifters, who spend substantial time in deep positions, have **significantly thicker knee joint cartilage** than untrained individuals, and elite weightlifters show **133% higher lumbar bone mineral density**. The reviewers further argued that partial squats may involve **greater** injury risk, since they require much heavier loads to provide an effective stimulus.

## What to actually do
- For quad, glute and adductor size, squat deep. 60° is not enough for anything; 90° is a quad-only compromise.
- Train the depth you want to be strong at. If you compete, that settles it.
- Adding partials alongside full squats is a plausible strength enhancer for already-strong lifters, on one study's evidence.
- If jumping and sprinting are the goal and you are a trained athlete, shallow squats may be the better tool — but this rests on Rhea alone.
- The deep-squats-are-dangerous belief has no support in the review literature he cites.

**Flagged:** the athleticism conclusion is the weakest link — one study in trained subjects, against two in untrained, and he says so outright. The 133% bone mineral density and cartilage thickness figures are cross-sectional observations of weightlifters reported secondhand through a review; they show adaptability, not that deep squatting caused it. He is otherwise careful throughout, consistently naming sample sizes, training status and study count before drawing conclusions.
