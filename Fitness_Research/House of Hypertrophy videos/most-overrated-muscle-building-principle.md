# Time Under Tension: Why the 30-60 Second Rule Doesn't Survive Contact With the Evidence

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Most OVERRATED Muscle Building Principle" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XB9477odyBw)) · 2022-08-01 · 11 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- **Time under tension (TUT) is not a training variable you need to manage.** Provided reps are taken to or close to failure and volume is adequate, a wide span of set durations produces equivalent growth.
- Every study that appears to support TUT **fails to equate proximity to failure**. Slower tempos at matched reps and load simply mean harder sets, so the tempo group was training closer to failure.
- When proximity to failure *is* equated — two Brazilian within-subject studies using one leg per condition — 1s/1s and 3s/3s tempos grow the quads the same, and self-selected ~0.9s reps match a deliberate 2s/2s.
- A **2016 Schoenfeld meta-analysis** pooling four studies found rep durations from **0.5 to 8 seconds** comparably effective when sets go to or near failure. The 30-60 second window has no evidential basis.
- His proposed thresholds: a floor somewhere near **9 seconds** per set (sets of three still grow muscle) and a ceiling somewhere around **84-140 seconds** (super-slow training underperformed). Both are inferences from single studies, not established numbers.
- On slowing **only the eccentric**, the literature is genuinely conflicting and he refuses to conclude.

## The case for TUT, and why it collapses

Three lines of evidence are usually offered. A Canadian myofibrillar protein synthesis study found 3 sets of leg extension at 30% 1RM produced greater MPS at 24-30 hours with a 6s/6s tempo than a 1s/1s tempo. Three Japanese training studies found greater long-term growth with slower tempos — in one, quads and hamstrings grew more with 3s/3s than 1s/1s. And a Brazilian study equating TUT between 12 reps at 3s each and 6 reps at 6s each (36 s per set both ways) found equal chest and triceps growth.

He dismantles all three:

- **The TUT-equated Brazilian study proves nothing about TUT.** Only one duration was tested. A study with a single value of the independent variable cannot show that variable matters.
- **The Canadian MPS study did not equate effort.** The 6s/6s condition went to failure; the 1s/1s condition merely matched the *rep count* of the slow condition, so it stopped nowhere near failure. That is an effort comparison dressed as a tempo comparison.
- **The Japanese studies have the same flaw.** In the one he details, both conditions did 3 sets of 8 at 50% 1RM. At that load, 8 reps at 3s/3s is a hard set; 8 reps at 1s/1s is barely stimulating. The slow group was closer to failure by construction.

This is the recurring methodological point of the video, and it is well made: **when load and reps are fixed, changing tempo changes effort, not just time.**

## What happens when effort is controlled

Two within-subject Brazilian studies do control it. In the first, subjects trained unilateral leg extension for 3-4 sets to failure at 50-60% 1RM, one leg at 1s/1s and the other at 3s/3s. Rectus femoris and vastus lateralis growth were similar — and notably, several of the fast-leg sets fell **below the supposedly essential 30-second threshold**. In the second, 3 sets to failure at 70% 1RM compared a deliberate 2s/2s leg against a self-selected tempo averaging **0.9s/0.9s**; vastus lateralis growth was again similar despite substantially different TUT.

He then adds an argument from a different literature entirely, which is the strongest part of his case. Numerous studies show loads from **30% to 80% 1RM** produce similar hypertrophy when taken to or near failure. But 30% 1RM yields roughly 30-40 reps and 80% yields roughly 7-12 — a huge TUT gap with no growth difference. If TUT drove hypertrophy, light-load training should win outright. It doesn't.

## Where the floor and ceiling might sit

For a **minimum**, he cites Schoenfeld's comparison of 7 sets of 3 against 3 sets of 10, both near failure, which found similar hypertrophy. The author noted on Instagram that the 7×3 group accumulated roughly **9 seconds of TUT per set**. So sets that short can still build muscle *provided total volume is sufficient* — the qualifier matters, since that group did more than twice the sets.

For a **maximum**, he offers the super-slow study: untrained women trained leg press, squat and leg extension, a normal group at 80-85% 1RM with 1-2s tempos versus a super-slow group at 40-60% 1RM with a 10s concentric and 4s eccentric, both near failure. Slow- and fast-twitch fibre growth in the vastus lateralis favoured the normal group. Back-calculated, the super-slow sets ran **84-140 seconds**. He offers endurance exercise as the reductio: enormous TUT, minimal hypertrophy.

**Where the confidence outruns the evidence:** the 9-second floor rests on one study plus an author's social-media remark, and the 84-second ceiling on one study in untrained women with load confounded against tempo. He labels both as needing more research, but they still end up in the summary as numbers, which is more precision than a single study each can carry.

## Eccentric-only slowing

Five studies, no consensus. Two preacher-curl studies favoured a 4s over a 1s eccentric for biceps growth, but **neither difference was statistically significant**. A Japanese back-squat study found 2s and 4s equivalent for thigh size. A Brazilian leg-extension study found rectus femoris and vastus lateralis equivalent but vastus medialis favouring 4s. A US leg-extension study found one anterior thigh region equivalent between 1s and 2s and another favouring the **faster** 1s eccentric. He concludes, correctly, that nothing can be concluded.
