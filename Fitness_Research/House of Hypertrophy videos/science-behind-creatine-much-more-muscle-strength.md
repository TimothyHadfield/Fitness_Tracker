# Creatine: About a Third Faster Growth, Small-to-Moderate Strength Gains, and No Hair-Loss Evidence

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Science Behind Creatine: How Much More Muscle & Strength?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sm9P5kYUGtM)) · 2024-01-08 · 20 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Lean mass:** five meta-analyses since 2014 put creatine's added lean-mass gain at **1–1.4 kg over roughly 7–14 weeks** of training. But lean mass is all non-fat tissue, not muscle specifically.
- **Direct muscle measures:** a 10-study meta-analysis using ultrasound/MRI found only **0.10–0.16 cm extra thickness** across triceps, elbow flexors, quads and hamstrings — overall **effect size 0.11**, which the study authors called trivial. He argues they used the wrong yardstick.
- His reframing: applying **hypertrophy-specific effect-size thresholds** (Greg Nuckols' work) makes 0.11 small-to-medium, and benchmarked against typical training effects it means **creatine builds muscle about one-third faster**.
- **Strength:** small-to-moderate meta-analytic effects for bench and squat; a classic review puts it at **+8 percentage points strength and +14 percentage points muscular endurance**.
- **20–30% are non-responders** — they tend to start with higher intramuscular creatine, smaller fibres and lower fat-free mass. Vegetarians and vegans look like high responders.
- **Hair loss:** one unreplicated 2009 study, 20 rugby players, DHT rise within normal range, measuring the wrong DHT. There is **zero evidence linking creatine to actual hair loss** — which is not the same as evidence it doesn't.
- **Monohydrate, 3–5 g/day, whenever you like.** No loading needed, no cycling needed.

## What creatine does, and why the obvious mechanism may not be the answer
Most creatine sits in muscle, averaging about **120 mmol/kg** with an apparent ceiling around **160 mmol/kg**. You turn over 1–2% per day, so **1–3 g daily** is needed just to hold stores; endogenous synthesis plus animal foods normally covers it, which is why vegetarians and vegans run lower. The performance mechanism is the phosphagen system: creatine donates a phosphate to ADP to regenerate ATP, so higher stores mean faster ATP replenishment during intense effort.

The standard explanation for the hypertrophy benefit is that this extra work capacity drives more growth. **He is openly sceptical of it** — his reasoning is that other supplements demonstrably raise exercise capacity without producing long-term hypertrophy, so capacity alone can't be the whole story. He offers alternatives (direct effects on intracellular pro-hypertrophy signalling, or suppression of inhibitory pathways) without overselling them. This is a good instance of him refusing a tidy mechanism that the outcome data doesn't require.

## How much muscle, actually
The five lean-mass meta-analyses converge on **1–1.4 kg** extra over ~7–14 weeks. He immediately flags the measurement problem: lean mass includes water, connective tissue and organ mass, not just muscle.

The more informative analysis is the recent one pooling 10 studies with direct site-specific measurement. Creatine added **0.10–0.16 cm of muscle thickness** at triceps, elbow flexors, quadriceps and hamstrings, **effect size 0.11**. The original authors concluded the practical significance may be minimal.

His objection is specific and, in my view, correct: those effect-size thresholds were **derived from strength and power research**, and applying them to hypertrophy data isn't sensible. Under thresholds derived from the hypertrophy literature the same 0.11 sits in the small-to-medium band. Benchmarking it against the typical hypertrophy effects seen in training studies yields his headline: **about one-third faster muscle gain**.

**Flagged:** this is the one place where the presentation runs ahead of the data. The "one-third faster" figure is a *reinterpretation* of an existing effect size against a different (blog-published) threshold set, plus a ratio against a literature average — not a new measurement. The underlying number is still 0.10–0.16 cm. The reframing is defensible and he explains exactly what he did, but the headline is doing more work than the effect size can carry.

**What kind of growth?** Creatine is an osmolyte, and one study found supplementation increased intracellular water, so some of the size gain is sarcoplasmic fluid. He treats it as probably both fluid and contractile protein, with proportions unknown. Two honest consequences he draws out: once stores saturate, no further water-driven size accrues, and if you stop, the water-driven portion presumably goes. He does not conclude creatine is therefore a short-term trick — he says future research is required.

## Who responds
The classic responder study describes **non-responders, partial responders and high responders**, with roughly **20–30% non-responders**. Non-responders have higher baseline intramuscular creatine, smaller fibres, and lower fat-free mass; high responders the reverse — which fits the saturation logic.

The vegetarian question has essentially one relevant trial: **18 vegetarians (3 vegan) and 24 non-vegetarians**, creatine or placebo across 8 weeks of lifting. The vegetarian creatine group saw the largest increases in muscle creatine, lean mass and endurance; **strength gains were similar**. He concludes vegetarians and vegans are "generally" higher responders — reasonable, but it rests on a single study, and he says so.

**Age:** in the direct-measurement meta-analysis, younger adults showed an effect size of **0.23** and older adults **0.06**. He explicitly declines to conclude older adults get little benefit, noting that older adults often build less muscle overall (so the denominator changes) and that individual variability persists — one trial in older adults found meaningful extra hypertrophy.

## Strength
Two meta-analyses (upper- and lower-limb) establish creatine boosts bench press and squat strength with small-to-moderate effects. The classic review figure is **+8 percentage points of strength and +14 percentage points of muscular endurance**.

He then does something worth noting: rather than quoting the average and moving on, he shows the spread. One 12-week study had the creatine group add **22.6 kg** to bench 1RM against **15 kg** for the control. Another 8-week study had **6.8 kg vs 5.7 kg** — a difference barely worth naming. Same supplement, wildly different returns. Older adults do reap respectable strength gains, per the older-adult-specific meta-analysis (chest press and leg press, small-to-moderate).

## The peripheral questions
**Recovery.** Evidence exists for reduced damage markers after a single session, but he calls the long-term picture ambiguous and notes that the supporting signal beyond that is athlete survey data — i.e. subjective.

**Hair loss.** The whole concern traces to one 2009 study in **20 rugby players** finding a DHT increase. His takedown is four-part: never replicated; the increase stayed within normal physiological range; there's a case it was a false positive; and — the crucial point — the researchers measured **serum** DHT, which is not strongly related to hair loss, whereas **scalp** DHT is. Other data show no increase in free testosterone. His conclusion is carefully worded: **"there is zero scientific evidence associating creatine with actual hair loss"**, and he explicitly refuses the stronger claim that creatine definitely does not cause it, because absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. He also explains why the anecdotes don't help — no measurement, heavy confounding, and a large overlap between men predisposed to male-pattern baldness and men who lift and try creatine.

**Forms.** Monohydrate is the most studied. A review of the marketplace found alternative forms all cost more, none has been proven superior, and some don't consistently beat placebo.

**Dosing.** Two routes. Load with **20–25 g/day split into 4–5 doses for 5–7 days**, then 3–5 g to maintain; or skip loading and take **3–5 g/day**, saturating in roughly **28 days at 3 g** or **~14 days at 5 g**. Long-term comparison finds similar strength adaptations at 3 vs 5 g, and a classic paper found **2 g** maintains saturation post-loading — so lower doses likely suffice for most, with larger individuals scaling to about **0.04 g/kg/day**. Skipping the load is cheaper and less likely to cause the mild GI discomfort some get at high doses.

**Safety.** No need to cycle. Kidney concerns come from misreading creatinine metabolism plus a 1998 case study with multiple shortcomings; large reviews find no adverse effects once confounders are accounted for.

**Timing.** A meta-analysis found better lean-mass gains post-training than pre — but on **three studies only**, with no strength difference, and a subsequent paper found no difference either way. His verdict: not a make-or-break factor, especially once stores are saturated.
