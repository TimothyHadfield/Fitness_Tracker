# Sets per Session Show No Plateau, But the Uncertainty Starts at 11

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How Many Sets per Workout? - This NEW Study Is Epic" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vZT0uiHp6kE)) · 2025-04-14 · 12 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- A new meta-analysis of **35 studies, 1,000+ subjects** finds sets per muscle group per *session* relate to hypertrophy on a rising curve with **no clear plateau**, out past 20 sets — but with **strongly diminishing returns**, each added set buying less than the last.
- The **fractional counting method** (direct set = 1, indirect set = 0.5) fit the data better than counting indirect sets fully or ignoring them. All set numbers in the video use it.
- The researchers' own statistical criterion put the point of **greater uncertainty at 11 sets per session** (and 31 sets per week in the companion weekly-volume analysis). Above that, credible intervals widen and study counts thin out.
- He does **not** accept the surface reading. He reviewed the individual papers and reports that many used **incomplete programs** (one or a few muscle groups trained), that definitions of failure varied, and that the average study ran **~10 weeks** — so he is "not truly confident that super high volumes are superior".
- Practical line: **10–20 weekly sets per muscle group** for most people, at least **2x/week frequency**, after training quality is maximised.
- Aside worth flagging: the same analysis found **strength gains essentially maximised at ~2 sets per muscle group per session**.

## What the new data actually shows
The analysis covered working sets taken to or near failure, not warm-ups; the studies averaged about **10 reps per set** with most sets to failure. Plotting sets per muscle group per session against hypertrophy gives a curve that keeps climbing to 20+ sets. The shape matters more than the endpoint: the curve is concave, so the marginal return per set shrinks steadily.

Moderator analyses held the relationship in **untrained and trained** subgroups and across **shorter and longer inter-set rest**. He calls out the rest finding specifically because he has previously argued on this channel that set number and rest interval might interact — and says the current evidence does not support that interaction. That is him retiring one of his own prior positions, which is worth noting.

## Why the session, week, and frequency data cohere
He cross-checks the new per-session analysis against two companion analyses in the same preprint family. **Weekly sets** also show more growth with more sets, with no low plateau. **Frequency**, with weekly volume held constant, shows **no meaningful effect** — spreading 12 weekly biceps sets across one, two or three days gave similar growth, with once-weekly numerically lower but inside typical error.

The three fit together logically: if growth plateaued at a low per-session set count, higher frequency would have to be superior in order for higher weekly volume to help. It isn't, and there is no low plateau — so the picture is internally consistent. This is a good example of him checking a new result against the existing evidence base rather than reporting it in isolation.

## The caveats he raises himself
This is the most valuable part of the video, and unusually candid:

- **Incomplete programs.** A fair number of included studies trained only one or a handful of muscle groups. Systemic fatigue and recovery demand in those studies do not resemble a full program. He says explicitly he "probably should have done a better job at emphasizing" this in previous videos.
- **Failure definitions varied**, so he cannot rule out that some subjects trained insufficiently hard.
- **Short durations.** ~10 weeks average. A long-duration moderator analysis kept the relationship, but the researchers themselves flag it as low-data and to be read with caution. He adds the alternative he cannot exclude: over a long enough horizon, **moderate volume may reach the same endpoint as high volume**.
- **Thin data at the top.** Few studies examined very high set numbers, which is why the credible intervals widen there.

His conclusion is calibrated to this: he is confident that **moderate beats low** volume even in a complete program, and not confident that very high beats moderate.

## What he recommends
Order of operations: exercise selection that actually loads the target muscle, then sufficient effort per set, then **as much volume as you can sustain practically and consistently** — for most, 10–20 weekly sets per muscle group, expressly as a broad range rather than a cutoff, with variation expected between individuals and between muscles within an individual. Higher volumes are fine to experiment with, most feasibly via **muscle-group specialisation** (cutting sets elsewhere to fund one or two muscles).

On frequency, any split is defensible on the data, and splits need not fit a 7-day week. But because returns diminish sharply above 11 sets in a session, he predicts once-weekly will look worse as better studies with higher weekly volumes appear — the mean weekly volume in the frequency data was only 13 sets — and he therefore recommends **at least twice weekly**, while noting once-weekly clearly still builds muscle.

**Flagged:** the 10–20 set recommendation is not what this analysis shows; the analysis shows more is better up to 20+. He is overriding the point estimate with his own judgement about study quality and practicality, and he says so, but a viewer skimming will hear "the data says 10–20" when the data does not say that. Both source analyses are also **preprints**, unpublished at the time of the video.
