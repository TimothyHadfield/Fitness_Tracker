# Rest Between Sets: What the First Meta-Analysis Actually Shows

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Rest Periods: NEW Study Changes Things (Or DOES It?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hyQHdr8as3U)) · 2024-05-06 · 15 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The **first meta-analysis on inter-set rest and hypertrophy** finds **longer rest (>60 s) beats shorter rest (≤60 s)** for both arm and thigh growth. Beyond that, precision collapses.
- Splitting rest into four bands, the **intermediate** band showed the largest effect — but it also had the **widest 95% credible interval**, all bands overlapped substantially, and the network meta-analysis showed **uncertainty about any benefit of resting longer than intermediate durations**. The paper floats **~90 seconds** as possibly enough; he explicitly says do not treat that as a strong conclusion.
- Viewed from another angle, **every** rest duration produced good hypertrophy. Rest time is not among the important variables.
- The single most relevant well-designed trial is still [Schoenfeld 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/): in trained men, **3 minutes beat 1 minute**.
- Practical advice: **rest as long as you feel you need** — likely longer on compounds, shorter on isolation work. If time-constrained, either add sets or use antagonist supersets, which preserve per-muscle rest while cutting clock time.

## How the field got here
The short-rest orthodoxy came from hormones. A **1990** paper found shorter rest produced larger acute growth hormone elevations ([Kraemer 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2262468/)), and a **1987** survey found most bodybuilders rested **10–90 seconds** between sets ([Kraemer 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3667019/)) — Arnold recommended a minute or less. Reviews through the late 2000s carried the assumption forward.

From 2009 researchers started measuring actual growth instead of hormones:
- **2009** ([Buresh](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19077743/)): untrained men, **2.5 min tended to beat 1 min** for arm and thigh growth — despite the 1-minute condition showing larger early testosterone and GH spikes.
- **2010** ([de Souza](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20543741/)) and **2011** ([Souza-Junior](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22032491/)): trained subjects, 2 min constant rest vs a group whose rest was **progressively decreased across weeks down to 30 s**. Arm and thigh growth similar.
- A **2014 review** ([Henselmans & Schoenfeld](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25047853/)) noted no study had shown greater hypertrophy with shorter rest — except one published the same year.
- That **2014 study** ([Villanueva](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25294666/)) found in **older men** that 1 min beat 4 min. He dismantles it in one move: **both groups did the same reps with the same load**, so the short-rest group necessarily trained closer to failure. That is a proximity-to-failure finding, not a rest finding.
- **2016** ([Schoenfeld](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/)), the most-cited rest study: trained subjects, 3×/week for 8 weeks, growth overall better with **3 min vs 1 min**.
- A **2017 review** ([Grgic](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28641044/)) summarised a possible advantage for longer rest in trained subjects, and noted a study where **muscle protein synthesis was higher with 5 min than 1 min** after leg extensions and presses to failure ([McKendry 2016](https://doi.org/10.1113/EP085647)) — again despite larger acute testosterone with 1 min. Acute hormone elevations are now understood to play essentially no role in hypertrophy.

## What the meta-analysis adds — and what it doesn't
The analysis used a method allowing comparison across **multiple** rest durations rather than the pairwise comparisons every individual study offers.

Headline: shorter (≤60 s) vs longer (>60 s) favoured longer rest for arms and thighs. Four-band split: intermediate showed the greatest point effect, but with the widest credible interval and heavy overlap with every other band. The network analysis simply showed uncertainty about anything beyond intermediate durations.

His caution about the inputs is the most valuable part of the section:
- It included the **2010 and 2011 progressive-reduction** studies, coding that group as resting **80 s** — its average. But resting 80 s every session is not the same intervention as tapering rest across weeks.
- It included the confounded **2014 older-men** study.
- It included a **2007** study on untrained women comparing 20 s to 80 s.

So the comparisons the method enables are only as good as the heterogeneous studies feeding it. He concludes that the best-designed, most relevant trial still says 3 min > 1 min in trained people.

## Compounds vs isolation
There is no direct evidence that ideal rest differs by exercise type, but the pattern is suggestive. Buresh 2009 (2.5 > 1 min) used a mix of compound and isolation work; Schoenfeld 2016 (3 > 1 min) was predominantly compound. Against that, a **2017 study not included in the meta-analysis** ([Fink](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27984843/)) used predominantly arm isolation exercises and found **30 s tended to beat 3 min** for arm growth. It was excluded because the 30 s group used lighter loads — but since 6–35 reps to or near failure produce similar hypertrophy, he argues that may not be a real confounder. He still wants replication before asserting anything.

## If you want short rest anyway
Two things:

1. **Add sets.** A 2020 paper ([Longo](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35622106/)) found in untrained subjects that 3 leg press sets with 3 min rest beat 3 sets with 1 min — but **4–5 sets with 1 min rest matched 3 sets with 3 min**.
2. **Possibly adapt.** Every study here ran 8–12 weeks. Three indirect strands hint that you might adapt to short rest over the long term: in the 1987 survey, bodybuilders (habitual short resters) maintained intensity across sets with 10 s rest better than powerlifters did; the 2010 and 2011 studies showed progressively reduced rest matched 2 min; and a **2021 study** ([Davids](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00464.2021)) found trained subjects doing blood-flow-restricted lower-body work with lighter loads, higher reps and **45 s rest** matched a normal group at 8 reps with 2 min rest for quad and both slow- and fast-twitch fibre growth over 9 weeks.

He labels the adaptation hypothesis "very much hypothetical" and "possibly wrong," and closes by telling viewers that simply taking longer rest is perfectly fine.

**Overreach flag:** the adaptation argument leans on three indirect strands, none of which was designed to test it — the BFR study in particular confounds rest with load, rep range and occlusion. He says so, which is why it reads as speculation rather than advice.
