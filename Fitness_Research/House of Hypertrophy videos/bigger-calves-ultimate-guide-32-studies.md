# Calf Training: What 32 Studies Say About Knee Angle, Foot Position, Range and Volume

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Bigger Calves: The ULTIMATE Guide (32 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y8YNP1S0Z48)) · 2025-03-31 · 26 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **Straight-leg (standing) calf raises are non-negotiable for the gastrocnemius** — every growth study agrees, and the effect is large. The open question is the soleus.
- Dhim **partially reverses his own earlier position** that straight-leg raises are all you need. Three growth studies all show the soleus growing slightly-to-clearly better with the bent leg, and a swelling study points the same way. He now says bent-leg work is a defensible addition — while still holding that training entirely straight-legged is fine.
- **Lengthened partials beat full range of motion for the gastrocnemius by a wide margin** — 126.9% greater medial gastrocnemius growth in the one study that compared them. Lengthened supersets (full ROM to failure, then past-failure partials) produced 43.3% greater growth in a separate study, so partials may be the stronger of the two.
- **Rep range does not matter for the calves.** Training one leg at 6–10 reps to failure and the other at 20–30 grew all three calf muscles similarly. This directly contradicts the common claim that the soleus, being predominantly slow-twitch, requires high reps.
- **More sets grew more calf**, with diminishing returns: 12 > 9 > 6 weekly sets in 61 untrained women, with the 6→9 jump bigger than 9→12.
- Foot position biases the heads: **toes out favours the medial head, toes in the lateral head**, in one small study supported by EMG.
- Squats and leg presses train the soleus somewhat and the gastrocnemius barely; leg curls train the gastrocnemius a little. Neither substitutes for calf raises.

## Decision 1: straight leg, bent leg, or both?

The calf comprises the gastrocnemius (medial and lateral heads) and the soleus. The gastrocnemius crosses the knee, so bending the knee shortens it, which is believed to impair its contribution and shift load to the soleus. The evidence, in the order it accumulated:

- **Kinoshita 2023** (untrained; within-subject, one leg standing, one seated). Both lateral and medial gastrocnemius growth substantially greater with the straight leg. **Soleus growth fairly similar.**
- **Burke 2024** (trained; within-subject). Medial gastrocnemius clearly greater with the straight leg. Statistics were equivocal for the lateral gastrocnemius and soleus, though the raw numbers favoured the straight leg for the lateral head and **slightly favoured the bent leg for the soleus**.
- **Unpublished conference paper, Brazil** (52 untrained women; between-group, straight-leg group vs bent-leg group). Lateral and medial gastrocnemius growth greater with the straight leg, but **soleus growth clearly and meaningfully better with the bent leg**.
- **Kassiano 2023** (17 young women; acute muscle swelling after each variation, i.e. the pump). Gastrocnemius swelling greater after the straight leg; **soleus swelling notably greater after the bent leg.**
- Several EMG papers report greater soleus activity from bent-leg raises, though not unanimously.

His reason for changing his mind is methodological, not a new headline result: the soleus signal points the same direction in every study — slightly in two, clearly in the third — and science is cumulative rather than decided by two papers. He is careful about the pump study, noting the pump is not a strong driver of hypertrophy but arguing it can indirectly reflect the tension a muscle experienced, and flagging that as inference.

**Recommendation as given:** straight-leg raises are clearly needed. Training entirely straight-legged is perfectly acceptable, since it still contributes to soleus growth; including some bent-leg work is also reasonable, depending on how you read the soleus evidence. All straight-leg variations (standing machine, Smith, leg press toe press) are fundamentally the same thing — pick whichever, or mix.

**A finer point on the two gastrocnemius heads.** Several papers suggest the medial head is impaired more than the lateral head when the knee is bent, and in both the unpublished and swelling studies the medial head saw minimal stimulation from the bent leg while the lateral head still saw some. So bent-leg raises may still respectably grow the lateral gastrocnemius. Dhim volunteers that this has little practical use — a rare and useful bit of self-labelling.

## Decision 2: where do you point your feet?

**Nunes 2020** had 22 men perform three sets of straight-leg calf raises under three conditions — feet forward, feet outwards, feet inwards.

- Medial gastrocnemius growth tended to be best with **feet outwards**. The difference reached statistical significance only against the feet-inwards condition, though the raw numbers also favoured it over feet forward.
- Lateral gastrocnemius growth tended to be best with **feet inwards**, again significant only against feet outwards, with raw numbers favouring it over feet forward.
- Soleus growth was not measured.

One small study, supported by EMG work pointing the same way. Dhim adds that you may feel the difference, then immediately supplies the counter-example: subjects in a hip thrust vs squat study felt their glutes more with hip thrusts, yet glute growth was similar. He argues feel probably tracks growth here, without pretending that is established.

**Practical use:** bias a head if you want to. Combining feet-out and feet-in sets *might* grow both heads more than feet-forward alone, but he notes no study has tested that and calls it a harmless thing to try — correctly labelled as speculation.

## Decision 3: how far should the ankle travel?

This is where the largest effects in the video appear.

**Kassiano 2023 (range of motion).** Previously untrained women, straight-leg calf raises, three sets, three times a week for eight weeks, all groups to failure at 15–20 reps. Three conditions: full range of motion; **lengthened partials** (the bottom half only, calves at longer length); and shortened partials (the top half). Both medial and lateral gastrocnemius growth tended to be best for lengthened partials, then full ROM, then shortened partials. Soleus was not measured. Dhim later quantifies the gap: **medial gastrocnemius growth was 126.9% greater for lengthened partials than full ROM.**

Does this transfer to trained lifters? A 2025 paper found biceps and triceps grew similarly between full ROM and lengthened partials in trained individuals, which is the obvious objection. Dhim's case for the calf being special is anatomical: the gastrocnemius produces its greatest active tension at longer lengths, while a full-ROM calf raise is hardest at the *top* — the shortened position where it can generate least tension. Lengthened partials avoid that mismatch. He recommends them on the basis that there is no major drawback while the evidence develops.

**Lengthened supersets** are the alternative: take a full-ROM set to failure at the top, then continue with partial reps into the stretched position. **Larsen 2025** had untrained men train Smith-machine standing calf raises, one leg to full-ROM failure and the other to full-ROM failure plus as many past-failure partials as possible, aiming to fail near the lengthened position. Only medial gastrocnemius growth was examined; it was **43.3% greater** with the lengthened supersets.

Comparing the two figures across separate studies, Dhim suggests lengthened partials may beat lengthened supersets, and notes a direct comparison in trained individuals is due. Treat that as a hint only — different subjects, comparators and protocols.

## Decision 4: reps and sets

**Reps.** The theory under test is that slow-twitch fibres grow better from high reps, which would matter because the soleus is well established as predominantly slow-twitch while the gastrocnemius has a more even split.

- **Fujiwara 2010:** older women performing 100 reps of standing calf raises grew the soleus more than the gastrocnemius. Dhim lists the problems himself — subjects were not training close to failure, there was no comparison condition using different reps, and 100 reps is not what most people want to do.
- **Schoenfeld 2020:** previously untrained men trained standing and seated calf raises, with one leg randomly assigned to **20–30 reps to failure** on every set and the other to **6–10 reps to failure**. **All three calf muscles grew similarly.**

That second result opposes the slow-twitch/high-rep rationale directly. It fits the broader literature — training to or near failure, roughly 6–35 reps produces similar hypertrophy — and other evidence that low and high reps produce comparable slow- and fast-twitch fibre growth. Dhim asks for more comparisons in trained subjects and at very high reps, but concludes that if you train hard enough, rep range is yours to choose.

**Sets.** Anecdotally the calves tolerate more volume, and **Chen 2019** found they experienced lower fatigue and soreness than many other muscle groups after a full-body session. Dhim immediately qualifies this with the repeated bout effect, which narrows fatigue differences between muscles over time.

Does more volume actually grow more calf? **Kassiano 2024** had **61 previously untrained women** train the straight-leg calf raise three times a week for six weeks, all sets to failure at 15–20 reps:

- 2 sets/session = **6 weekly sets**
- 3 sets/session = **9 weekly sets**
- 4 sets/session = **12 weekly sets**

Not all differences reached significance, but growth in all three muscles tended to be best at 12, then 9, then 6. **Diminishing returns were visible: the 6→9 increase was larger than the 9→12 increase.** Higher set numbers were not tested. Dhim suspects the calves grow more still at higher volumes based on the general sets literature — a reasonable extrapolation, clearly marked as suspicion.

## Decision 5: is stretching worth adding?

Two distinct interventions here, and the practical one is the second.

**Long-duration static stretching.** Short, low-intensity stretching does not increase muscle size. But **Warneke 2022** had subjects hold an intense calf stretch for **one hour a day for six weeks**, and the lateral gastrocnemius increased in size by **15%**. A follow-up compared one hour of daily stretching against straight-leg calf raises for five sets three times a week over six weeks; both groups grew the medial and lateral gastrocnemius with no statistically significant difference, though the numbers favoured lifting for the lateral head. A case study on a competitive natural bodybuilder with 18 years of training combined four sets of calf raises five times a week with an hour of intense stretching six days a week and showed signs of further hypertrophy — but with measurement variability and no lifting-only condition, so the stretching's contribution cannot be isolated. Dhim's own assessment is that this is interesting but impractical, since almost nobody will stretch their calves for an hour a day. Agreed.

**Stretching between sets.** A 2016-published conference abstract reported that holding the stretched position of a straight-leg calf raise for 30 seconds after each set produced greater gastrocnemius growth than passive rest. **Van Every 2022** was the published test: untrained men trained seated and standing calf raises, one leg descending immediately into a loaded stretch for 20 seconds after each set then resting 100 seconds, the other resting passively for the full 120. Medial gastrocnemius growth was similar; lateral gastrocnemius slightly favoured stretching (some would read this as no difference); **soleus growth more convincingly favoured stretching between sets.** He declines to call it superior, noting research on other muscles that finds no benefit, but points out it costs no extra time and no study has found it to impair hypertrophy. That is a fair basis for "try it".

## Decision 6: do squats and leg curls count?

**Squats and leg presses.** Plantar flexion occurs during these lifts even with the heel on the floor, so the calves are involved — but how much? EMG shows the soleus more active than the gastrocnemius at deeper squat positions, and during the leg press medial gastrocnemius activity is much lower than soleus activity (lateral gastrocnemius less so).

Dhim argues this is a case where EMG probably does track growth, and the reasoning is good: the gastrocnemius is a knee flexor, and knee *extension* is what these lifts require, so a strongly contracting gastrocnemius would fight the movement. He draws the parallel to the rectus femoris, which multiple studies find grows minimally from squats because it is a hip flexor working against the hip extension the lift demands.

So the gastrocnemius will not meaningfully grow from squats. The soleus has never been measured directly for growth from these lifts; his estimate is some growth but far less than calf raises get, based on the far lower demands at the ankle than at the knee and hip, and on soleus activity sitting well below its maximum. One study found the calves hardly grew after six weeks of Smith-machine squatting, but Dhim declines to lead with it because that protocol also included calf raises — mostly far from failure and through a short partial range — which makes the null hard to interpret. (A parallel group doing the same work with whole-body vibration did see some calf growth.) Handling an inconvenient study by explaining its confounds rather than ignoring or over-claiming it is what makes this channel usable.

**Leg curls and Nordic curls.** The gastrocnemius crosses the knee and assists knee flexion. **Van Vossel 2024** had untrained subjects train seated leg curls: the lateral gastrocnemius showed small, non-significant growth, the medial head virtually none, while hamstring growth was much larger.

Two design points might change that. First, **ankle position**: dorsiflexion lengthens the gastrocnemius, and its knee-flexion force output is greatest when dorsiflexed. Ankle position was not reported in that study, and Dhim suspects most subjects were not dorsiflexed. Some EMG papers find similar activity dorsiflexed and plantarflexed, but he argues EMG is unreliable specifically when comparing conditions differing in muscle length. Second, **where the exercise is hardest**: the gastrocnemius is more lengthened and stronger in knee flexion when the knee is straighter, yet many leg curl machines are hardest when the knee is most bent. Machines or movements loaded toward knee extension — some leg curls, Nordic curls — could increase the stimulus. Does dorsiflexing cost the hamstrings anything? One study measuring biceps femoris long head found similar growth either way; only one head was measured. Even with both adjustments, he expects knee flexion work to pale next to calf raises for the gastrocnemius.

## Where this lands against what the library already holds

1. **Standing/straight-leg primacy is confirmed** for the gastrocnemius, strongly and consistently.
2. **The "seated vs standing rationale looks outdated" walk-back is partly re-opened.** This video moves the other way: on four separate data points the soleus responds at least as well, and possibly better, to the bent-leg (seated) variation. The rationale is not dead; it is under-tested.
3. **The claim that the soleus needs 15–25 rep sets because it is ~90% type I is contradicted** by Schoenfeld 2020, where 6–10 and 20–30 reps to failure grew all three calf muscles similarly. The fibre-type premise is not in dispute; the training inference drawn from it is.
4. By omission: the **mandatory bottom pause** is never discussed. The nearest relative here is the lengthened-partial finding, which concerns where in the range you spend reps rather than pausing in the stretch — compatible, but no evidence for a pause specifically.

**Overreach flag:** placing the 126.9% (lengthened partials) and 43.3% (lengthened supersets) figures side by side to rank the two techniques compares across studies with different subjects, comparators and protocols — a hint, not a ranking.
