# Is the Overhead Press Worth Training for Size?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Should You Overhead Press for Muscle Growth?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o6k8hMa1Z7w)) · 2025-04-21 · 13 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- His verdict: overhead presses are **among the best front delt builders**, but **not essential** — nothing they do cannot be got elsewhere. Their real merit is **efficiency**: front delts, side delts and two triceps heads in one movement.
- **Lateral raises beat overhead presses for side delts.** The one paper suggesting parity is outweighed by a second paper favouring lateral raises, plus mechanical reasoning about external rotation and high shoulder angles.
- **Plane of motion is the real variable.** Frontal/scapular plane (abduction) favours the side delts; sagittal plane (flexion) favours the front delts and minimises the side delts.
- The **triceps long head is likely poorly trained by any overhead press**, because its shoulder actions oppose the lift. The **lateral and medial heads should do well** — with direct evidence that the bench press grows the lateral head **more than skull crushers do**.
- **Upper pec involvement is minimal.** Incline pressing remains the pick there.
- Almost the entire case is built on **moment arms and EMG, not growth outcomes**, and he says so repeatedly.

## Frontal, scapular and sagittal planes
Pressing with the arms out to the sides — wide barbell grip, behind-the-neck, dumbbells beside the head, machines with lateral handles — puts you in the **frontal plane**. Machines and front barbell presses that keep the upper arms slightly forward of the head put you in the **scapular plane**. Both perform **shoulder abduction**, and the two behave similarly.

By moment arm: **side delts have good leverage throughout the range**; **front delts are respectable and, above 100° of abduction, match or exceed the side delts**; **rear delts are poor abductors and sometimes act as adductors**. He defines moment arm properly (perpendicular distance from the muscle's line of force to the joint centre), notes the evidence that moment arm relates to activation, and then flags that **the usefulness of leverage data for inferring growth is debated** — deferring his full justification to a pinned comment that is not retrievable.

Two things push involvement toward the front delts: overhead pressing has the arms **externally rotated**, which increases front delt abduction leverage while barely affecting side and rear delts; and these presses are **hardest at high shoulder angles**, exactly where front delt leverage is proportionally best.

Pressing with the arms in front — narrow barbell grip, dumbbells travelling forward — is the **sagittal plane** and performs **shoulder flexion**. Here the side delts retain some leverage but the **front delts have far greater leverage**, making this the variation for maximising front delt while minimising side delt.

## Modality, stability and hormones — dismissed
He rejects two common arguments outright. The claim that **machine stability produces greater mechanical tension** rests on evidence he thinks does not apply to typical hypertrophy exercises, and there is evidence in trained lifters that **seated barbell overhead pressing did not elicit greater deltoid activity than standing**. The claim that **barbells and dumbbells are superior via acute anabolic hormone elevations** fails because the literature does not link transient hormone spikes to growth. Provided you train to or close to failure, **all modalities can produce similar growth** — it is preference and convenience.

Worth registering against the library's position that the **machine shoulder press** is the best-supported front delt pick: he sees no modality advantage at all, so a machine press is fine but not privileged. Bringing the hands together at the top (available on dumbbells and some machines) adds nothing — reaching shorter muscle lengths generally does not confer extra growth.

## Behind the neck
An EMG paper found the side delts **slightly more activated during behind-the-neck barbell pressing** than front barbell pressing, with front delt activity similar. He immediately notes that EMG research is controversial and that he is **critical of it** — there are clear cases where it fails to predict growth and others where it tracks it. The mechanical rationale is that the side delts have relatively more leverage than the front delts in the frontal plane, and there is evidence of slightly greater side delt activity abducting in the **frontal versus scapular** plane. So frontal-plane variations, behind-the-neck included, may be marginally better for side delts.

On safety, he acknowledges the evidence people cite against behind-the-neck work, then quotes a paper concluding that for **participants with normal trunk stability and adequate shoulder range of motion, overhead pressing is safe for shoulder and spine whether in front of or behind the head**. His practical line is to use your judgement — dumbbells or the scapular plane are solid alternatives if it is uncomfortable.

## Triceps
His argument here is the most mechanically satisfying part of the video. The **long head crosses the shoulder** and contributes to shoulder extension and adduction — the **opposite** of what an overhead press requires. A strong long head contraction would fight the lift, so it is likely poorly trained by any overhead press variation. The same logic applies to the bench press, and there the prediction has been tested directly: bench pressing produced **minimal long head hypertrophy**, while the **medial and especially lateral heads grew well** — the **lateral head grew more from the bench press than from skull crushers**, with the medial head somewhat less.

He extrapolates that to overhead pressing, supported by EMG showing **lateral head activity fairly high across barbell and machine overhead presses, front and behind**, and medial head activity constant across five bench incline angles (the fully upright position was not tested, but he can see no reason it would drop). Two further points: sagittal-plane variations may elicit slightly more triceps activity, though **no study has tested this**; and two papers found **dumbbell overhead pressing in the frontal or scapular plane produces lower triceps activity than barbell** — plausibly because your capacity to produce lateral forces is lower with dumbbells. He is explicit that this does **not** apply to sagittal-plane dumbbell pressing.

## Upper pecs
Upper pec activity is maximised at a **30° incline**; higher angles, which approach overhead positions, reduce upper pec and increase front delt activity. The upper pecs have little leverage for **abduction**, so frontal and scapular plane presses involve them minimally. They do have decent **shoulder flexion** leverage, so sagittal-plane presses may reach them a little — but he thinks the growth from that is far short of incline pressing.

## Overhead press versus bench press and lateral raises
As the torso becomes more upright, **chest involvement falls and front delt involvement rises**, so scapular-plane overhead pressing produces greater front delt activity than the bench press. There is also data showing greater front delt activity than dips, from a paper he says has shortcomings. On whether the greater front delt **stretch** available in high incline pressing and dips should matter, he is **generally sceptical** that longer lengths help in this particular case — which is why he concludes overhead presses are among the best front delt options.

For side delts, one paper found **no significant difference** between a front barbell overhead press and a dumbbell lateral raise — the source of the "lateral raises are overrated" claim. Another found **better side delt activity with both dumbbell and cable lateral raises**. Combined with the external rotation and high-angle factors that shift work to the front delts, he concludes **lateral raises are better for the side delts, with overhead presses still respectable**.

## The verdict on necessity
Front raises combined with horizontal pressing, incline pressing or dips cover the front delts well; lateral raises deliver a better side delt stimulus; triceps options are abundant. So the overhead press is **not required**. But since programmes are not limited to one exercise per muscle, its efficiency makes it easy to justify — and he adds that strength goals or simple enjoyment are legitimate reasons on their own.

Note against the library position that **front delts already get enough stimulus from pressing**: he broadly agrees that other pressing covers them, while still rating overhead presses at the top for that head.

## Where the conclusion outruns the evidence
This video is **almost entirely moment-arm and EMG data**, with only the bench press triceps study offering direct hypertrophy outcomes. He flags this three separate times and refers justification to a pinned comment — so the caveat is his, but it still means the plane-of-motion recommendations are predictions rather than findings.
