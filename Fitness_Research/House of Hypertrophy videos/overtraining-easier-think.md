# How Hard Is It Actually to Overtrain?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Is Overtraining Easier Than We Think?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mB9znHdMvIg)) · 2025-06-02 · 13 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The title is a question he answers **no**. A systematic review of **22 studies that deliberately tried to induce overreaching or overtraining found 10 failed to produce any sustained performance decrease at all**.
- The protocols that did work are absurd by normal standards: **60 one-rep-max squat attempts a week for two weeks**. Even **40+ weekly quad sets for two weeks** left leg press strength *improved*.
- The one place he thinks overtraining-like states arrive easily is **too much too soon** — specifically unaccustomed heavy eccentric work, where a **single session** produced months-long strength loss and, in one documented case, **rhabdomyolysis**.
- Markers are unreliable. In the study that did induce overtraining, **testosterone was unchanged** and rep capacity at 70% 1RM was **not reduced** even as maximal strength fell. This is why the literature defines overtraining by **recovery time**, not symptoms.
- Two 12-week trials progressing trained lifters up to **38–52 weekly quad sets** produced the *best* strength and size outcomes, with **no injury-related dropouts**.

## The definitions he uses
He works down a continuum with the literature's definitions rather than gym usage:
- **Undertraining** — insufficient even to maintain size and strength.
- **Maintaining** — enough to hold current size and strength.
- **Effective training** — enough to increase them.
- **Functional overreaching** — performance drops for **days to a few weeks**, then improves.
- **Non-functional overreaching** — performance drops for **weeks**, and no improvement follows recovery.
- **Overtraining** — performance drops and recovery takes **months**, with no improvement once recovered.

This distinction is the load-bearing part of the video. Everything short of the last two categories is ordinary fatigue.

## What failed to cause overtraining
The striking negative case: **18 trained individuals** trained normally for 4 weeks, then ran a **2-week intensification**. Upper body stayed the same, but the quads got **hack squats and leg presses for six days and front and back squats on the seventh, each 3 sets of 6RM** — over **40 sets per week for the quads**. There were signs of fatigue, testosterone dipped slightly, and one subject developed patellar tendon pain. Yet **leg press strength improved four days after the final session**, with non-significant increases on leg curl and bench press, and testosterone returned to normal quickly. Neither overtraining nor non-functional overreaching occurred.

## What did cause it
A 1990s paper: **11 trained men performed 10 sets of one-rep maxes, six days a week, for two weeks** on a squat machine — **60 1RM attempts per week**. Strength fell, and follow-up surveys found it took subjects **2–8 weeks to return to normal loads**. Two subjects were **medically diagnosed with knee overuse syndrome**.

The instructive detail is what *didn't* change: **maximum reps at 70% 1RM were not reduced, and testosterone was unchanged**. He uses this to explain why the field defines overtraining by the time course of performance recovery rather than by any marker or symptom — the candidate markers are simply inconsistent.

A borderline case: a study with four three-week blocks, the most intense involving **6 sets per exercise, six days a week — 36 sets per exercise per week**. Power clean strength fell and failed to recover after 3 weeks of reduced training plus 3 weeks of complete rest. He is **sceptical this counts**, since detraining could explain part of it, and notes that **another paper using the identical protocol reported squat strength back to baseline after the reduced-training block**. Every other performance decrease in the review recovered inside a week.

## Too much too soon
This is where he thinks the "easier than we think" framing has some purchase, and it is a different mechanism from cumulative overtraining.

**Six untrained men** performed **5 sets of 10 eccentric-only biceps curls at 110% of 1RM**. He explains why this is heavier than it sounds: eccentric-only training at that intensity is highly damaging when you are unaccustomed to it. Damage, soreness and swelling lasted about a week — and **elbow flexor size fell below baseline and stayed smaller for numerous weeks**, possibly from partial or total destruction of some fibres.

A series of case reports found that after **two sets of 25 maximal eccentric curls**, a small number of subjects had **strength loss lasting longer than 43–47 days** with extreme arm swelling for weeks. **One subject was clinically diagnosed with rhabdomyolysis.** He describes this as non-functional overreaching or borderline overtraining reached in a **single session**.

He is careful to bound it: the muscle-loss study had **six subjects**, the case report paper recorded a **3% incidence rate over the past year**, and many studies using similar or more intense eccentric protocols in untrained subjects found neither muscle loss nor prolonged fatigue. His conclusion is that the **repeated bout effect** would likely have prevented all of it had the subjects ramped up gradually — which is his actual recommendation.

## What gradual progression looks like
Two companion studies, one on **trained men** and one on **trained women**, both trained back squat, leg press and leg extension **twice weekly for 12 weeks** after an acclimation phase, at **2 RIR on all sets except the final set of each exercise per session**, which went to failure.

- **Men:** control held **22 weekly quad sets**; a +4 group added four sets every two weeks reaching **42 by the final week (average 32)**; a +6 group reached **52 (average 37)**.
- **Women:** control held **22**; a +2 group went from 16 to **26 (average 21)**; a +4 group went from 18 to **38 (average 28)**.

**Strength gains were best in the highest-set groups, and size gains tended to follow.** There were **no injury-related dropouts and no joint problems**, though the highest-volume men reported excessive perceived fatigue in the final weeks.

His caveats are the right ones: only the quads were escalated, hamstrings were held at 8 weekly sets, and other muscles were untracked — so this is evidence for **muscle-group specialisation phases**, not for high volume everywhere.

## Individual and situational differences
Recovery capacity varies by **age**; endurance research suggests those with a greater proportion of **fast-twitch fibres** are more prone to overreaching; recovery improves as you become accustomed to a programme. On the "no such thing as overtraining, only undereating" claim, he grants partial truth — endurance data suggests some overtraining cases are confounded by **low energy intake** — but points out that the resistance-training papers producing borderline overtraining had **sufficient energy and protein**. Higher **psychological stress** slows recovery, and a placebo study in which subjects believed they were taking steroids reported greater strength *and* increased vigour, suggesting mindset shapes perceived energy.

## Verdict
Overtraining is possible but **improbable for most**. Once you have training experience, you will likely only reach it by actively pushing to extremes, and joint or tendon issues may arrive first. Shorter bouts of fatigue are common and resolve with a few rest days or a couple of easier sessions.

## Where the conclusion outruns the evidence
Nothing much — this is one of his more disciplined videos. The one soft spot is that the review's failure to induce overtraining partly reflects **ethical limits on what researchers may impose**, so "hard to induce in a study" is not quite the same as "hard to reach in life", and he does not raise this.
