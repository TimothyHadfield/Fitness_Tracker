# Regional Hypertrophy: Muscles Do Not Grow Evenly, and Range of Motion Decides Where

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Optimize Muscle Growth: Most People Don't Know This" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BifpjmlDRfc)) · 2024-08-26 · 14 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- Muscles do **not** grow uniformly across their proximal, middle and distal regions. Two literatures show this: range-of-motion comparisons and exercise comparisons.
- **Short-length partials** grow the proximal and middle regions decently — sometimes as well as full ROM — but **fail at the distal region**, and in one squat study the distal thigh **lost** size.
- **Long-length partials and full ROM** both grow distal regions well. Across three studies comparing them, long-length partials produced more overall hypertrophy, though all three used untrained subjects.
- For **biarticular** muscles, training at longer muscle lengths beats shorter lengths, with the **largest differences at the proximal region**. Based on three studies, so not established fact.
- What this does **not** give you: a way to change muscle shape (peak vs long biceps), or a menu of exercises each targeting a specific region. The practical takeaway is boringly simple — use full ROM or long-length partials, and include exercises that train biarticular muscles at long lengths.

## What regional hypertrophy is and isn't
People hear "regional hypertrophy" and think of muscle shape — a high biceps peak versus a longer biceps. No compelling evidence suggests you can change that. What the evidence does show is that the **proximal** (closer to origin), **middle** and **distal** (further from origin) regions of a muscle do not always grow uniformly. That technically changes shape; whether it changes it enough to be visually meaningful is the question he returns to at the end.

## Range of motion
Three ROM categories: **full** (joints moved through their fullest potential — full elbow extension and flexion in a curl), **partial at short length** (the top part of a curl), **partial at long length** (the bottom part).

**Full vs short-length partials:**
- Leg extension ([McMahon 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23629583/) / [McMahon 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23625461/)): vastus lateralis proximal growth leaned to full ROM, middle similar, **distal considerably better with full ROM**.
- Squats ([Bloomquist 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23604798/)): 120° of knee flexion vs 60°. The short-length partial grew **only the proximal** regions and **lost size at the distal** regions. The 120° squats grew all regions fairly evenly. Biggest gap again at the distal region.

On the size loss, he flags there is evidence certain muscle parts can decrease in size even while training, and promises a future video rather than speculating.

**Long-length vs short-length partials:**
- Leg extension ([Pedrosa 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33977835/)): growth across all three vastus lateralis regions favoured long-length partials, but **only statistically significant and considerably different at the distal region**.
- Preacher curl ([Pedrosa 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36828324/)): biceps middle-region growth similar; **distal region better with long-length partials**.

**Long-length partials vs full ROM:** three studies have compared them and all found more overall hypertrophy with long-length partials, but only one measured regions. That one trained the leg extension with full ROM, long-length partial and short-length partial, measuring four regions of both vastus lateralis and rectus femoris:
- **Vastus lateralis:** short-length partials produced notably lower growth at the 60% and 70% regions but were fairly decent at the 40% and 50% regions. Long-length partials beat full ROM mainly at the **60% and especially 70%** regions.
- **Rectus femoris:** same pattern for short-length partials. Long-length partials favoured in **all** regions, biggest differences at 50% and 70%.

He does not overclaim: the evidence is not strong enough to say long-length partials always beat full ROM, all three studies used **previously untrained** subjects, and most exercises are unexplored. "Promising early evidence — experiment if you like."

## Exercise selection and biarticular muscles
The second literature compares exercises, mostly on **biarticular** muscles (crossing two joints).

- **Leaning-back vs normal leg extensions** ([preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/407/853)): the rectus femoris is the only biarticular quad head (it crosses knee and hip). Leaning back places it at a longer length, and produced more proximal and distal growth than normal leg extensions — with the **biggest difference at the proximal region**.
- **Seated vs lying leg curls** ([Maeo 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/)): three of four hamstrings are biarticular and sit at longer lengths in the seated version. The researchers measured proximal and distal growth of two biarticular hamstrings; both showed better growth at both regions with seated curls, again with the **biggest difference proximally**.
- **Incline vs preacher curls** (conference abstract, unpublished at time of recording): incline curls start the biceps at a longer length. Overall biceps hypertrophy was greater with incline curls, with the authors noting the biggest difference at the **proximal** regions. He explicitly says he will hold his full commentary until publication — an appropriate handling of an abstract.

He then argues against his own pattern. It rests on **three studies**, so he refuses to call it established. And a threshold may exist: lying leg extensions lengthen the rectus femoris **more** than leaning back, but a study found lying leg extensions were **not more effective** than leaning back despite producing more activation, especially proximally — and their activation was actually slightly lower than leaning back.

**Overreach flag:** the extension of the ROM findings to pecs and lats — that short-length partials will develop proximal and middle regions but underperform for distal regions there too — is explicitly labelled speculation with no direct research. Reasonable, but it is inference from quads and biceps to entirely different muscle architectures.

## The honest limitations
All these studies lasted a few months. What happens over years is unknown. His best guess is that a lifter using long-length partials for years would end up with visibly more pronounced development, especially distally — but he says plainly that claiming certainty would be lying.

## Practical takeaways
- To grow all regions maximally: **full ROM, or long-length partials**.
- To *avoid* distal growth in a muscle (for whatever reason): short-length partials will do it.
- Include exercises that train **biarticular muscles at long lengths** — seated leg curls over lying, leaning-back or incline positions where available.
- There is no exercise-per-region menu. That is what most viewers wanted and it does not exist.
