# Muscle Hyperplasia: Does Training Add Fibres, or Only Enlarge Them?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Do You Gain MORE Muscle Fibers With Training? (Research Overview)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5fPsnaCMnw8)) · 2021-08-28 · 26 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- **Hyperplasia** means an increase in the *number* of muscle fibres, either by manufacturing new fibres or by existing fibres splitting in two. It is distinct from hypertrophy, which is existing fibres getting bigger.
- **In animals, the evidence is reasonably good.** A meta-analysis of animal work found fibre number up ~11.6% under weight-training protocols, ~21% under stretch protocols and ~5.4% under synergist ablation. The weight-training subset was five studies, *all five* positive, including both studies that used the more reliable direct-count method.
- **In humans, there is no clean answer and probably cannot be one with current methods.** Direct fibre counting requires removing the whole muscle; a human biceps is estimated at 198,000–419,000 fibres. Nobody has counted them, and cross-sectional counting has not been done either.
- The human evidence is a stack of **indirect cross-sectional comparisons pointing both ways**, and every study on both sides is weak. Four studies found bodybuilders/powerlifters with bigger whole muscles but *same-sized* fibres (implying more fibres); at least two found bigger whole muscles *and* bigger fibres (implying no extra fibres).
- **Practical upshot for a lifter: none.** Even if hyperplasia happens, no training variable in the video is shown to control it. This is a mechanism note, not a programming note.
- Dhim's own conclusion is that several different positions are defensible on this evidence, and he declines to pick one — an unusually honest ending for a 26-minute video.

## What would count as evidence, and why humans are the hard case

The video is at its best on method, and the method section is what makes the rest interpretable.

**Training the animal.** Three protocols appear in the literature. *Stretching* hangs a weight from a limb — the classic preparation stretches the anterior latissimus dorsi of Japanese quail, either chronically (30 straight days of continuous stretch) or intermittently (one day on, two or three off, repeated). *Synergist ablation* surgically removes one muscle of a group so the remainder is overloaded during ordinary movement; the standard version removes the gastrocnemius so the soleus carries the load. *Weight training* has the animal actually lift — one rat protocol had animals squat for 15 sets of 15 reps at 65–75% of 1RM in a custom rig.

Dhim is explicit that only the third is remotely analogous to human training, and he weights the results accordingly rather than quoting the biggest number. That is the right call, and it is worth noting because the stretch protocols produce the largest effects and are the ones usually quoted by people arguing that hyperplasia is settled.

**Counting the fibres.** Two methods. *Direct counting* dissects the whole muscle and counts every fibre — reliable, but destructive and therefore only possible once. So the comparison must be either trained limb versus untrained limb in the same animal, or trained animal versus untrained animal, and both rest on the assumption that the two sides or two animals started with comparable fibre numbers. *Cross-sectional counting* takes one slice and counts the fibres in it. Far more practical, but it has a failure mode that matters: the count in a slice can rise without any new fibres existing at all. Two ways this happens — an increase in **pennation angle** that outpaces fibre growth will bring more fibres through the plane of the slice, and an increase in **fibre length** can push a fibre that previously ended short of the slice into it. Both pennation angle and fibre length are known to change with training, so a cross-sectional count is systematically biased toward false positives.

This is why the animal result carries weight: of the five weight-training studies, two used direct counting and both were positive.

**Why humans are different.** The direct method needs the whole muscle out. Setting aside willing subjects and ethics clearance, the counting itself is impractical at human scale — hence the 198,000–419,000 figure for the biceps. Cross-sectional counting has not been done in humans either, which Dhim attributes to the difficulty of getting a biopsy sample large enough to be representative. So every human study in this area is measuring something other than fibre number and inferring backwards.

## The human evidence, both directions

**Pointing toward hyperplasia.** Four cross-sectional studies compared trained bodybuilders or powerlifters against untrained controls and found the expected larger whole-muscle cross-sectional area but *no difference* in the cross-sectional area of the individual fibres sampled. If the muscle is bigger and the fibres are not, more fibres is one explanation. Dhim immediately gives the competing one, and it is fatal: a biopsy samples a limited number of fibres from one site, and fibres elsewhere in the muscle — never sampled — could well have been larger in the trained group. The inference only works if the biopsy is representative, and there is no way to know that it is.

A fifth cross-sectional study found signs of **fibre splitting** in the vastus lateralis and trapezius of trained powerlifters, with some splits running deep enough to divide the fibre completely, and none in untrained controls. That is the most direct human observation on offer.

**Pointing away from it.** One study compared five bodybuilders to 18 untrained subjects and found both larger whole muscles *and* larger individual fibres — the ordinary hypertrophy explanation. Another, on the biceps, found the same pattern and went further: it divided each subject's biceps cross-sectional area (corrected for connective tissue) by their mean fibre cross-sectional area to estimate fibre number. Estimated fibre numbers in bodybuilders came out similar to untrained controls.

The only longitudinal human attempt used that same equation. Eight trained men trained a mix of free-weight and machine exercises, three sets to failure with a 10RM load, three times a week for 12 weeks. Biceps cross-sectional area rose, fibre cross-sectional area rose, estimated fibre number did not.

Then the wrinkle Dhim does not hide: two subjects had unusually large increases in fast-twitch fibre size but only ordinary whole-muscle growth. Removing those two outliers, the remaining **six** subjects did show an increase in estimated fibre number. He reports this and then immediately guts it — six subjects is a tiny sample, and the estimating equation is crude and can be wrong in many ways. Presenting a result that supports the interesting conclusion and then arguing against it is exactly the behaviour that makes this channel usable.

**Net position.** Conflicting results where the studies on both sides are weak, because the measurement that would settle it is impossible. He lays out three defensible reader positions — trust the animal data, trust animal plus indirect human data, or discount both — and endorses none.

## Anabolic steroids

Three of the four "same fibre size, bigger muscle" cross-sectional studies included users of anabolic steroids, which suggests users might be the population where hyperplasia is most likely. Two further studies from the fibre-splitting group found that powerlifters using steroids had a higher percentage of trapezius and vastus lateralis fibres expressing proteins associated with new fibre formation than natural powerlifters, and a third found more steroid-using powerlifters showing signs of fibre splitting.

Against that: in the biceps study using the estimating equation, **all** the bodybuilders were on steroids, and their estimated fibre numbers matched untrained controls. Dhim's summary — plausible that steroids make hyperplasia more likely, not verifiable or falsifiable on current human evidence — is appropriately weak.

## Why a fibre might split at all

Two hypotheses, both offered as speculation.

**Abnormal regeneration after damage.** Damaging or novel training injures fibres; the repair process may misfire into a split. Two proposed routes: a damaged fibre is degraded and satellite cells fuse irregularly to it, differentiate into an immature fibre and produce a split; or damage occurs on one side of the fibre's outer wall, satellite cells fuse at that spot and develop into a second fibre there. The circumstantial support is that the two most damaging animal protocols (stretch and ablation) are the ones producing the largest fibre-number increases, and that the powerlifter splitting study also found dead fibre segments and fibres in the middle of regenerating alongside the splits.

**A fibre size threshold.** A fibre grows to some ceiling and then divides. The supporting study is on mice doing progressive weighted wheel running: at eight weeks, soleus fibre cross-sectional area had increased with no change in fibre number; by twelve weeks, fibre cross-sectional area had *decreased* while fibre number had increased — the signature you would expect if fibres had grown, then split into two smaller ones.

Dhim's proposed reason for the ceiling is oxygen diffusion. Mitochondria sit inside the fibre and are supplied with oxygen diffusing from capillaries; mitochondrial density does not appear to rise with resistance training and may fall. As the fibre enlarges, the diffusion distance to the innermost mitochondria grows until it becomes limiting, and splitting into two smaller fibres restores it. He notes supporting evidence from rats and crabs. This is a mechanism argument with no human outcome data behind it — reasonable, unproven, and labelled as such.

## Where this leaves the question

The honest conclusion is the one he gives: no firm answer is likely soon, because the limiting factor is not funding or interest but the impossibility of counting fibres in a living human. A non-invasive fibre-counting technology would change that; nothing else will.

One caution on the animal-to-human step. Even the weight-training animal protocols — 15 sets of 15 in a rat squat rig — are not obviously comparable to human training in volume or in the degree of novelty and damage involved, and if the abnormal-regeneration hypothesis is right, damage is precisely the variable that matters. Dhim discounts stretch and ablation for being extreme but does not apply the same scepticism as forcefully to the rodent weight-training protocols, which is the one place the argument stretches slightly past its evidence.
