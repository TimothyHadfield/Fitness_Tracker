# Cheat Reps vs Strict Reps: The First Trial

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Cheat Reps vs Strict Reps (NEW Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZhOW9ql9x0Y)) · 2025-01-21 · 10 min
**Topic:** Effort & failure

## Bottom line
- The first ever hypertrophy trial on cheating found **no difference**. 25 untrained subjects, within-subject design, unilateral dumbbell curls and pushdowns, 2×/week for 8 weeks, 4 sets of 8–12 to failure, one arm cheating on every rep and the other strict on every rep.
- Elbow flexor and triceps thickness were measured at 55% and 65% regions; increases were similar in every region. The authors' phrasing, which he repeats: external momentum "neither helped nor hindered" hypertrophy of the target muscles.
- He does **not** treat this as the end of strict form. His stated conclusion is that stricter technique still makes sense as the default, but the study is a reason to relax about a bit of momentum creeping into a set.
- The two remaining objections to cheating are safety and progress tracking. Safety is untested — 5 of 30 recruits dropped out, none for injury, which he correctly calls absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence.
- The tracking objection is the real one: cheating makes it hard to know whether a heavier load reflects getting stronger or just cheating harder.
- The study tested cheating on *every* rep, not cheating as an intensity technique past failure — a distinct question that remains untested.

## The design, and why he defends it
Both conditions were trained by the same subjects, one arm each, randomly assigned. Cheating on the curl meant swinging the weight up with external momentum to failure; on the pushdown, momentum plus elbow flare and leg drive. Strict meant a stationary torso with movement only at the elbow. The supplementary material included instructional videos shown every session — a detail he notes as a standardisation strength.

He pre-empts the standard objections to within-subject designs. Arm dominance does not matter because the comparison is between condition averages, and the number of dominant arms in each condition is typically similar. Cross-education is real but affects **strength**, not size — so this design is inappropriate for strength outcomes and acceptable for hypertrophy. The upside: 25 subjects training both conditions is worth more than 50 subjects split between them, because genetics, nutrition and lifestyle cannot confound a within-subject contrast.

## The caveats he raises himself
This is the channel's characteristic move — he lists the reasons his own headline might be wrong.

- The authors reported that cheat form was hard to standardise; subjects did not cheat identically, and some cheating styles could plausibly be worse than others.
- The design tested all-cheat versus all-strict, not strict reps that turn into cheat reps near failure. He notes cheat reps are the only way to push past ordinary failure, and that this use case is untested.
- Untrained subjects, biceps and triceps only, 8 weeks. He rejects the reflexive dismissal that untrained people grow from anything — the literature contains many examples of untrained subjects growing more from one thing than another — but accepts the finding may not transfer.
- Both conditions progressed, and the cheat condition unsurprisingly used heavier loads.

## The progression problem
His most practically useful point. With strict technique, lifting more for the same reps is fairly clean evidence you got stronger. With cheating, you cannot untangle load increases from cheating increases. He then steelmans the other side: a cheat-rep advocate might not care about the attribution if growth is no worse, since long-term visual change plus the fact that they now cheat with loads they could not have cheated with before is assurance enough. He calls that a fair position while noting many lifters prefer the reassurance strict form gives.

## The muscle-length reading, rejected
Someone could read this as a long-muscle-length study in disguise: swinging a curl shifts tension earlier in the range where the biceps is longer, and growth was equal, so making an exercise harder at long lengths may not help. He declines that reading — too many other variables changed, and nobody measured how much tension actually differed at long lengths. He instead files it under his "resistance challenge" category of the muscle-length literature (changing *where* in the range the exercise is hardest, as opposed to range of motion or biarticular joint positioning), noting that category has few studies and none showing a real benefit.

## Where this sits against the library
The library's position is that controlled cheating is defensible only when the muscle's strength curve, rather than its fatigue, ends the set. This video does not test that case at all — every rep was cheated, so fatigue ended these sets too — which means the trial is not evidence for or against the library's framing. What it does supply is a null result for wholesale cheating in untrained lifters.

It also directly confirms the library's note about a prominent creator relaxing his strict-form stance: Dhim states that **Jeff Nippard** has said he will be involved with a cheat-rep study on trained individuals. That is the study the library records him co-authoring.

**Overreach flag:** none of substance — if anything he under-claims. The one soft spot is treating a single 8-week untrained trial as sufficient to loosen a training recommendation at all, which he half-acknowledges by keeping strict form as the default.
