# Sarcoplasmic Hypertrophy: What It Claims, What Has Been Measured

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Science of Sarcoplasmic Hypertrophy (What, How, and Why)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LU4iIemeN9M)) · 2021-08-22 · 26 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- **The claim is a ratio, not a substance.** Sarcoplasmic hypertrophy is where the sarcoplasm expands *faster than* the myofibrils, so sarcoplasm disproportionately accounts for the fibre's increase in cross-sectional area. Myofibrillar hypertrophy is both expanding at the same rate; myofibrillar packing is myofibrils expanding faster.
- **Evidence for it exists but is thin**: five long-term studies plus two cross-sectional comparisons. Three of the five long-term studies used high volumes (Dhim defines high as **21+ weekly sets per muscle**), which is the basis for his one training-relevant claim.
- Of the candidate drivers, **mitochondria can be ruled out**, sarcoplasmic reticulum and t-tubules have **never been measured** after resistance training, **glycogen is contradictory**, and **creatine phosphate and sarcoplasmic proteins look most promising** — each supported by a single study.
- The "it's just swelling" objection is the strongest challenge and Dhim answers it credibly: one study's sarcoplasmic protein increase is independent of swelling, another ran five months with measurements 4–6 days post-session, and cross-sectional evidence exists in long-trained bodybuilders.
- **Effect size, direction and desirability are never established.** The video never asks whether a lifter should want sarcoplasmic hypertrophy — the same size with proportionally less contractile material. That gap matters more than anything settled here.
- He labels his central mechanistic explanation as **his own hypothesis**, twice, and says future research could render it obsolete. Take the whole note as mechanism, not prescription.

## What the concept actually claims

Muscle is nested: whole muscle → fascicles → muscle fibres → myofibrils. Myofibrils are arrays of sarcomeres and are where force is produced, when the myosin head extends from the myosin filament and pulls actin toward the M line. Everything inside the fibre that is *not* myofibril is sarcoplasm — mostly water, plus glycogen, sarcoplasmic proteins, and non-contractile organelles.

So the three growth patterns are defined by which compartment grows faster, not by whether one grows at all. This matters for reading the literature: nobody claims the sarcoplasm grows and the myofibrils do not. The claim is only about proportion.

**What would count as evidence.** Two measurement routes appear. The first is electron microscopy of a biopsy, estimating the *relative space* occupied by myofibrils versus sarcoplasm (and, within sarcoplasm, by glycogen, mitochondria and so on) before and after training. The second is **specific tension** — force produced per unit of fibre cross-sectional area. If a fibre is bigger but its specific tension has fallen, proportionally less of it is contractile, which is what sarcoplasmic hypertrophy predicts. Higher specific tension implies the opposite, myofibrillar packing.

Both routes inherit the same weakness that dogs every fibre-level question: they rest on a tiny biopsy sample taken from one site and assumed to represent the muscle.

## Can you train for it?

The training-variable evidence is a post-hoc classification of a handful of studies, and Dhim says so.

Load and frequency do not separate the three patterns — the overwhelming majority of studies used moderate loads and moderate frequencies, and moderate loads produced all three outcomes. He states plainly that the current evidence cannot tell you whether light or heavy loads preferentially produce one pattern.

**Volume is the only candidate differentiator.** Low-to-moderate volumes were associated with myofibrillar hypertrophy or myofibrillar packing; high volumes with sarcoplasmic hypertrophy. The support is that three of the five long-term sarcoplasmic-hypertrophy studies used high volumes. He calls this "by no means overwhelming or definitive" and says future research will be needed — which is right, because three out of five, classified after the fact, from five studies total, is close to the weakest quantitative claim a person can make.

## What might drive the disproportionate growth

Six candidates, evaluated one at a time.

**Sarcoplasmic reticulum and transverse tubules.** Both are organelles central to the events preceding force production, and both would plausibly expand with training. Dhim is aware of no research measuring their volume after resistance training at all. Unknown, and honestly labelled as such.

**Mitochondria.** Probably ruled out. The majority of evidence indicates the relative space occupied by mitochondria does not increase — and may decrease — with resistance training.

**Glycogen.** Mechanistically attractive: each gram of glycogen is stored with at least three grams of water, so more glycogen means more intracellular water and a larger sarcoplasm. One study had subjects train the triceps for **five months** with high volumes and found the relative space taken by glycogen in triceps fibres increased. Against it, a second study found no increase in glycogen space in the vastus lateralis after **six weeks** progressing to high volumes. Two further confounds cut across both: high-carbohydrate diets meaningfully raise intramuscular glycogen space, and glycogen space is meaningfully *depressed* immediately after hard training. If glycogen is the driver, this form of growth is transient and diet-dependent — which is a substantive qualification, and Dhim draws it himself. His verdict is "not completely clear", which is fair.

**Creatine phosphate.** The same five-month triceps study found the relative space occupied by creatine phosphate increased. Creatine is thought to draw water into the fibre, so this would also expand the sarcoplasm.

The creatine supplementation question gets a careful answer. More intracellular water does not guarantee sarcoplasmic hypertrophy: the water increase has to outpace myofibril growth to change the ratio. Four studies in which subjects supplemented creatine monohydrate while training with **low volumes for four to ten weeks** found evidence of **myofibrillar packing** — the opposite pattern — meaning any creatine-driven water gain was insufficient to shift the ratio. Whether creatine combined with high-volume training would accentuate sarcoplasmic hypertrophy has never been studied, and he says so rather than extrapolating.

**Sarcoplasmic proteins.** In the six-week study that progressed to high volumes, the relative space taken by sarcoplasmic proteins tended to increase, and notably many of the proteins that increased were involved in anaerobic glycolysis. A third study found sarcoplasmic hypertrophy *without* an increase in sarcoplasmic protein space — but it used only 12–16 weekly sets for the quadriceps, below his high-volume threshold, and it was one of only two of the five studies that did not use high volumes. The other of those two he had previously set aside for good reason: no statistical analysis was performed and no fibre or whole-muscle growth measurement was reported. So the discrepant study is treated as a probable outlier, and the magnitude of sarcoplasmic hypertrophy was in any case much larger in the high-volume study.

**Verdict:** creatine phosphate and sarcoplasmic proteins are the most promising drivers. Each rests on a single supporting study, and the two studies are not the same study.

## Why the body might do it

Four scenarios, in descending order of how much Dhim believes them.

**1. A response to high-volume training.** This is where his own hypothesis sits, and he flags it as such. A typical set lasts well under two minutes, drawing on the creatine phosphate system (roughly the first five to ten seconds) and anaerobic glycolysis (up to about two minutes). More sets means both systems are used and replenished to a greater extent. It is therefore logical that high-volume training would provoke increases in creatine phosphate and in the glycolytic sarcoplasmic proteins — exactly the two candidates the previous section identified. The reasoning is clean and it connects the volume association to a plausible mechanism. It is also entirely inferential; no study has manipulated volume and measured these compartments as the outcome.

**2. It is merely swelling.** The serious objection. Damaging exercise causes fibres to swell with water, which technically satisfies the definition. The illustrative study had sedentary men run down ten flights of stairs, take the lift back up, and repeat fifteen times — a heavily eccentric, highly damaging protocol for unaccustomed subjects. Soleus fibre cross-sectional area was inflated by **30%** seven to eight days later, which the authors and Dhim both attribute to fluid.

Three counters. First, the six-week study's increase in sarcoplasmic protein space is not a swelling phenomenon. Second, the five-month triceps study ran long enough that swelling responses would have attenuated, and its measurements were taken four to six days after the final session. Third, cross-sectional studies find the pattern in bodybuilders with many years of training behind them, where an acute swelling explanation does not apply. His conclusion — "very questionable and I'd argue unlikely" — is proportionate.

**3. Spatial and energetic priming for later myofibril growth.** The elegant one. Growing myofibrils takes space and is energetically costly; expanding the energy-producing machinery first creates both the room and the metabolic capacity to do it. If true, high-volume training would ultimately cause myofibrillar hypertrophy or packing, with sarcoplasmic hypertrophy as an intermediate stage. Dhim states there is no strong evidence for or against it. It is a hypothesis with no test attached, and should be read that way.

**4. A myofibril size threshold.** Myofibrils grow to a ceiling, after which the fibre expands its energy-producing components instead. Two of the five long-term studies were on trained individuals, who might plausibly have reached such a ceiling. But the other three were on untrained men, who would not have. He concludes the evidence leans against this one — the correct reading of his own table.

## Anabolic steroids

Two cross-sectional studies found evidence of sarcoplasmic hypertrophy: one comparing seven well-trained lifters to five untrained men, where the trained group had more relative space taken by sarcoplasm; and one finding that twelve bodybuilders had lower specific tension than six power athletes and fourteen untrained men. In the first, six of seven trained lifters were using or had used anabolic steroids; in the second, nine of twelve bodybuilders were reported to be using.

Two things cut against a steroid explanation. The three non-using bodybuilders in the specific-tension study also had lower specific tension than the power athletes and untrained men. And a separate study found two steroid-using bodybuilders with *higher* specific tension than untrained men — the signature of myofibrillar packing, the opposite pattern. His conclusion is that the role of steroids, if any, is unknown. Note the sample sizes throughout: seven, twelve, two.

## The question the video does not ask

Everything above concerns whether sarcoplasmic hypertrophy occurs and what causes it. Nowhere does the video ask whether a lifter should want it. A fibre that has grown disproportionately in non-contractile volume is a bigger fibre with proportionally less force-producing material — good if the goal is size, less good if the goal is strength, and potentially just a transient glycogen-and-water state if glycogen turns out to be the driver. The takeaway is framed as "you could potentially train for sarcoplasmic hypertrophy", with high volume as the lever, without establishing that this is a desirable target. That is the clearest place where the framing outruns the evidence.

Two smaller cautions. The volume threshold of 21+ weekly sets is a classification boundary applied retrospectively to five studies, not a value any study tested. And the entire construct is built on electron-microscopy estimates of relative compartment space in small biopsies — a measurement whose representativeness is assumed rather than demonstrated.
