# Time Under Tension: The 30–60 Second Rule Has No Basis

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Time Under Tension Is OVERRATED...or Is It? (10 studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_RVjlfyyjow)) · 2021-05-14 · 16 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- The three studies that appear to show slow tempo builds more muscle **all share the same fatal design flaw**: reps and load were fixed, so the slow group was necessarily training closer to failure. They are studies of proximity to failure, not of tempo.
- Once you look only at studies where **both groups trained to failure**, the tempo effect disappears. Lacerda (2s vs 6s per rep, within-subject, 14 weeks) and Chaves (4s vs self-selected ~1.8s, within-subject, 8 weeks) both found equal growth.
- Schoenfeld's meta-analysis pooling four such studies found **no difference between rep durations of 0.5 and 8 seconds**, provided reps go to failure.
- The one study suggesting a limit is Schuenke: **10s up / 4s down at 40–60% 1RM was inferior** to 1–2s reps at 80–85% 1RM for fibre CSA. One study, six weeks, untrained women — he explicitly declines to conclude that tempos beyond 10 seconds are sub-optimal.
- The commonly circulated **30–60 second per set recommendation is not substantiated**. In Lacerda's study, sets in the fast condition ran 25–38 seconds — below the supposed threshold — and grew the same as sets of 37–52 seconds.

## Where the library lands, and where he lands
Our library holds that **tempo is the lowest-priority variable, that anything from 0.5 to 8 seconds per rep works**, and that one creator publicly retracted slow negatives in December 2024.

He lands in exactly that place, three and a half years earlier, and the 0.5–8 second range in our material is **the range from the Schoenfeld meta-analysis he cites here**. Two useful additions from this video:

1. He supplies the *reason* the older pro-tempo studies were wrong, which is a specific and checkable design critique rather than a general dismissal.
2. He adds a **lower bound** the library does not currently state. There is likely a minimum time under tension per set — 1RM training builds no muscle, so some reps must accumulate. He puts the plausible floor at the TUT of about **five normal-speed reps**, while noting that reps as low as three may build muscle effectively, so it could be lower still.

He does **not** endorse slow negatives. The nearest he comes is noting that slowing tempo is one legitimate way to apply progressive overload when you cannot add load, and that varying tempo might produce more balanced regional growth — both offered as options, not recommendations.

## The three studies that look supportive, and why they aren't
**Usui et al.** — 16 untrained young men, back squat to ~90° knee flexion, 3×10 at 50% 1RM, 3×/week for 10 weeks. Slow group: 3s down, 3s up. Normal group: 1s down, 1s up. The normal group saw **virtually no growth at any measured site**; the slow group gained significantly at rectus femoris (70% thigh length) and vastus intermedius (50% and 70%).

**Watanabe et al. (2013)** — untrained men, 3×8 at 50% 1RM, knee extension and leg curl, 2×/week for 12 weeks, same 3s/3s vs 1s/1s split. Quadriceps and hamstring thickness increases significantly greater for the slow group.

**Watanabe et al. (2014)** — untrained men, knee extension, 3×12 at 30% 1RM, 2×/week for 12 weeks, same tempo split. Quadriceps CSA favoured the slow group.

**The shared flaw.** In all three, **load and rep count were held constant across groups**. Slower reps mean fewer reps are possible with a given load, so at a fixed 10 reps the slow group was closer to failure than the fast group — and at 50% 1RM with 1-second reps, the fast group would have been a very long way from failure. The measured effect is confounded with effort, and he says there is no way to separate them from these designs. That the normal-speed groups grew almost nothing in Usui's study is itself the tell.

## The studies that isolate tempo properly
**Lacerda et al.** — the best available, in his judgement. Ten untrained men, **within-subject**: one leg on a 2-second condition (1s up, 1s down), the other on a 6-second condition (3s up, 3s down). Unilateral knee extension, 3–4 sets **to failure**, 50–60% 1RM, 2–3×/week for **14 weeks**. Time under tension per set: **37–52 s** for the 6-second leg, **25–38 s** for the 2-second leg. Rectus femoris and vastus lateralis CSA increased **similarly**. Note the significance he draws out: some of the fast-condition sets fell **below the 30-second threshold that the popular recommendation claims is necessary**, and grew just as much.

**Chaves et al.** — untrained men, unilateral knee extension, 3 sets **to failure** at 70% 1RM, 2×/week for 8 weeks. One leg on a controlled 4-second rep (2s up, 2s down), the other on **self-selected** tempo, which averaged 0.9s up and 0.9s down. Vastus lateralis CSA increases similar between legs.

**Schoenfeld et al. meta-analysis** — pooled four studies where reps were performed to failure, classifying rep durations of **0.5–4 s as fast** and **4–8 s as medium**. No difference in muscle growth. This is the source of the 0.5–8 second range.

**The exception: Schuenke et al.** — untrained women, leg press, squat and knee extension, 3 sets to failure, 2–3×/week for 6 weeks. Traditional strength group: 1–2s up, 1–2s down at 80–85% 1RM. Super-slow group: **10s up, 4s down** at 40–60% 1RM. Vastus lateralis slow-twitch and fast-twitch fibre CSA increases were significantly **greater for the traditional group**. He presents this as the one piece of counter-evidence to his own thesis, then declines to build on it: one study, so no confidence that tempos over 10 seconds are sub-optimal, and more research is needed.

## The mechanistic argument
He is upfront that **every study above used untrained subjects**, including all four in the meta-analysis, and that no trained-subject data exists on this question. His reason for expecting the result to transfer is mechanical tension.

Mechanical tension is the best-characterised hypertrophy mechanism, and tension tracks force, which tracks the number of fibres recruited. Slow-twitch fibres are recruited at low force demands, fast-twitch as demand rises. Training to or very near failure should therefore drive high recruitment of both **regardless of tempo**.

His evidence is **Morton et al. (2019)**, in men with at least two years of training experience — the one trained-subject dataset in the video. Four conditions on the leg extension, all to failure:

| Condition | Load | Tempo | TUT/set |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 80% 1RM | 1s up / 1s pause / 1s down | 26 s |
| 2 | 80% 1RM | 3s up / 1s pause / 3s down | 31 s |
| 3 | 30% 1RM | 1s up / 1s pause / 1s down | 54 s |
| 4 | 30% 1RM | 3s up / 1s pause / 3s down | 76 s |

Recruitment of slow- and fast-twitch fibres from vastus lateralis, measured by **glycogen depletion**, was similar across all four — a threefold spread in time under tension producing no recruitment difference. This is an acute mechanistic measure rather than a growth outcome, and he does not overstate it; it functions as support for a conclusion the outcome data already delivered.

The rep-range literature supplies the final corroboration. Two meta-analyses find **5–35 reps produce similar growth** when taken to or near failure, which necessarily means very different TUTs are equivalent. And Morton et al. (2016), in men with 2+ years of training, found no difference in vastus lateralis fast- or slow-twitch fibre growth between **8–12 reps to failure and 20–25 reps to failure** — a large TUT difference in trained subjects with a null result.

## Practical upshot
- Rep durations from roughly **0.5 to 8 seconds** are interchangeable for hypertrophy, provided sets reach or approach failure.
- **Ignore the 30–60 second per set rule.** It is unsupported, and sets below 30 seconds grew as well as sets above it in the best-controlled study.
- Very slow tempos (10 s concentrics) are the one thing with any evidence against them, but it rests on a single six-week study.
- Slowing tempo is a legitimate **progressive overload lever** when load cannot be increased — same load, same sets, same reps, longer reps next session.
- Varying tempo may promote more balanced regional growth; he flags this as covered elsewhere and does not develop it here.

## Where the argument depends on inference
Two places. First, the entire tempo literature is **untrained subjects**, and the bridge to trained lifters is built from mechanism (recruitment at failure) plus two rep-range studies in trained men — good reasoning, but not a direct test. Second, the minimum-TUT floor is genuinely speculative: he derives it from the fact that 1RM training does not build muscle and from the low end of the rep-range literature, and he says he would like to see research on it. Neither is presented as more solid than it is.
