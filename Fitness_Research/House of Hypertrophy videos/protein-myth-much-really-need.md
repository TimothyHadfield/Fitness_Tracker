# How Much Protein You Actually Need — and Why the Number Is Fuzzier Than It Looks

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Protein Myth: How Much You REALLY Need" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a18y3JaRUOQ)) · 2024-03-25 · 12 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Three meta-analyses in six years put the plateau at roughly **1.5–1.6 g/kg total bodyweight/day**, and nitrogen-balance and indicator-amino-acid-oxidation work lands in the same **1.5–2.0 g/kg** window.
- His central point is not the number but the **scatter behind it**. Above 1.25 g/kg in the 2018 dataset, protein intake explains **1% of the variance** in fat-free mass gains (r² = 0.011). The line of best fit is real; the precision people read into "1.6" is not.
- So he refuses a single figure and gives a **spectrum**: 1.2–1.59 g/kg *possibly* enough, 1.6–2.0 *almost certainly* enough, >2.0 *very likely more than enough* — compatible with maximal gains but not required.
- **Training harder or being more advanced does not reliably raise the requirement.** Four studies, including two on elite bodybuilders, give estimates from 1.2 to 1.7 g/kg with no upward trend for experience.
- **Cutting is the one place he allows an increase**, and he is explicit that the supporting evidence is weak: the two key trials used 40% deficits and 1.0–1.2 g/kg control arms, which is not the comparison anyone actually faces.
- **Bulking needs no elevation.** And the framing that matters: in the 2018 meta-analysis, training accounted for a substantially larger share of the variance in gains than protein did.

## What the meta-analyses show, and what they don't

[00:30] All three recent meta-analyses agree that protein relates to gains, and all three point at diminishing returns. [Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/) found a plateau at **1.6 g/kg/day**; [Tagawa 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33300582/) at about **1.5 g/kg/day**; [Nunes 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35187864/), because it modelled the data differently, looks at first glance like more is always better — but its **confidence intervals widen as intake rises**, which is uncertainty, not benefit. Nitrogen balance and IAAO studies stop in the same place, at roughly 1.5–2.0 g/kg.

[02:02] Then the good part, which he credits to Eric Trexler, formerly of Stronger By Science. Strip the fitted line off the 2018 plot and look at the raw points. There is an association, but no visible step. Restrict the analysis to intakes at or above **1.25 g/kg** and the **r² is 0.011** — one percent of the variance in fat-free mass change. The same visual argument applies to the 2022 dataset. This is a properly honest move: he is undermining the precision of the very number his own recommendation is built on.

[03:03] Hence the spectrum rather than a target. Where you sit in it is a preference about risk, not a fact about physiology: aim high if you want to leave nothing on the table, and if you cannot hit 1.6 g/kg, "you may indeed be leaving very little to nothing on the table." He allows two modifiers toward the higher end — **lower protein quality** in the diet, and **older age**.

## Does training hard, or being advanced, raise the requirement?

[04:38] No, on the available data. [Lemon 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1400008/) had novice bodybuilders train intensely 1.5 hours a day, six days a week for four weeks: strength and muscle gains were **similar at 1.35 and 2.62 g/kg**, with the nitrogen-balance data pointing at 1.6–1.7 as sufficient. An IAAO study of **eight male bodybuilders near their maximum muscular potential** estimated the requirement at **1.7 g/kg** (this is [Bandegan 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28179492/), which he lists under his IAAO heading). [Tarnopolsky 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3356636/), also on elite bodybuilders, estimated only **1.2 g/kg**. And [Roberts 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29200983/) found recovery measures after a hard session **similar at 1.8 and 2.9 g/kg**.

## Cutting

[06:40] Three lines of evidence could support raising protein in a deficit. [Mettler 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19927027/): trained men, calories cut 40% for two weeks, lean body mass better maintained at **2.3 vs 1.0 g/kg**. [Longland 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/): untrained men, calories cut 40% for four weeks, greater fat loss and lean mass gain at **2.4 vs 1.2 g/kg**. And [Helms 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/), a review concluding higher intakes may better protect muscle in trained people.

[07:41] He then dismantles his own case. Both trials used a **40% deficit** — far more aggressive than most dieters run — and both low arms sat at **1.0–1.2 g/kg**, well below the plateau. Nobody has tested the comparison that matters: 1.6 g/kg against something higher, at a moderate deficit. Other evidence, itself limited, finds higher intakes are not always better. His conclusion is flagged as hypothetical: **the leaner you are and the harder you are dieting, the more reason to sit high** as insurance.

## Bulking, and the size of the protein lever

[08:11] He does not think higher-than-recommended protein is needed on a bulk, and defers the [Antonio](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24834017/) high-protein overfeeding studies to a future video on the grounds that they have limitations he wants space for. Fair, but it means the bulking section is an assertion here rather than an argument.

[08:41] The framing is the most useful thing in the video. In the 2018 meta-analysis, **resistance training accounted for a substantially greater portion of the variance** in muscle and strength than protein intake did. He adds a study in which, across three days of single-leg immobilisation, higher protein did **not** preserve muscle or strength better than lower protein — I cannot map that one to a listed reference, so it is unattributed here. You cannot eat your way to hypertrophy; protein lets you realise the gains training has already earned.

## Two footnotes

[11:19] **Body fat.** All these figures are per kilogram of *total* bodyweight, which overestimates needs at high body fat. Use fat-free mass, or estimate what you would weigh lean and apply the numbers to that.

[11:49] **Kidneys.** Citing the [ISSN protein position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/): the concern traces to data on renal-failure patients and to teaching that was never revised against contrary evidence. In otherwise healthy people there is no indication of harm, including in trials well above 2.2 g/kg.

## Where he lands against the library

- Against **Nippard's 1.6–2.2 g/kg bulking range**: he agrees on the floor and treats the top of it as optional insurance rather than a requirement.
- Against the **ISSN's 1.4–2.0 g/kg**: near-identical numerically, but the framing inverts. The ISSN calls 1.4–2.0 a *minimum*; he calls 1.6–2.0 *almost certainly enough*.
- Against **Menno Henselmans's 1.8 g/kg with no increase when cutting**: his central estimate is effectively the same figure, and both arrive at it as a safety margin over a ~1.6 plateau. They split on cutting — Henselmans holds there is no good evidence for elevating protein in a deficit; Dhim thinks Mettler, Longland and Helms are suggestive enough to justify going higher when lean or dieting hard, while conceding the comparison has never been run properly.
- He is closer to **Nippard's 1.8–2.7 g/kg cutting figure** in direction than Henselmans is, but he would not endorse 2.7 as necessary.

**Where the confidence outruns the evidence:** only the cutting recommendation, and he says so himself in the same breath — the practical advice rests on trials whose deficit and control-arm intakes make them a poor model for the decision a real dieter faces.
