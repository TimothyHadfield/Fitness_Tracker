# Three Ways a Muscle Fibre Can Get Bigger, and What 33 Studies Say About Each

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Sarcoplasmic vs Myofibrillar Hypertrophy/Packing (33 STUDIES)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u4jxTekn6eE)) · 2021-08-09 · 21 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- A muscle fibre can enlarge in three ways, defined by **relative** space, not absolute: **myofibrillar hypertrophy** (myofibrils and sarcoplasm grow at the same rate), **sarcoplasmic hypertrophy** (sarcoplasm outpaces myofibrils), **myofibrillar packing** (myofibrils outpace sarcoplasm). In none of them does anything shrink.
- Across the 33 studies he counts, **myofibrillar hypertrophy is the best-supported outcome (15 studies)**, then packing (11), then sarcoplasmic (7).
- The only training variable that separates the three is **volume**. Studies finding myofibrillar hypertrophy or packing used low-to-moderate volume; the studies finding sarcoplasmic hypertrophy mostly used high volume.
- **Load tells us nothing here**, because almost every study in the whole literature used moderate loads — and moderate loads produced all three outcomes. There is no direct evidence that heavy or light loading preferentially drives any of them.
- The volume→sarcoplasmic conclusion rests on a slight majority of five studies, one of which reported no statistics at all. He says so himself, and one cross-sectional study points the opposite way.

## The definitions, and why "relative" is the whole argument
Muscle → fascicles → fibres → myofibrils → sarcomeres. The myofibrils are the contractile machinery; everything else inside the fibre is sarcoplasm, which is mostly water but also holds glycogen, sarcoplasmic proteins and non-contractile organelles.

The distinction people usually get wrong is that sarcoplasmic hypertrophy does **not** mean the myofibrils shrank. It means the sarcoplasm grew faster. The myofibrils may have grown substantially and still lost relative share. Likewise, packing does not mean the sarcoplasm shrank.

He also flags a wrinkle rarely mentioned: **myofibrillar packing can occur without the fibre getting bigger at all.** For packing to increase fibre cross-sectional area, either the myofibrils grow enough to push the fibre's outer wall outwards, or the sarcoplasm grows too — just more slowly.

## The three ways researchers can tell them apart
1. **Long-term study with direct assessment.** Biopsy before and after training; measure fibre cross-sectional area and estimate the relative space occupied by myofibrils and sarcoplasm.
2. **Long-term study measuring specific tension.** Specific tension = force per unit of fibre cross-sectional area. Since only myofibrils generate force, specific tension is a proxy for myofibrillar share. Up = packing, down = sarcoplasmic, unchanged = myofibrillar hypertrophy.
3. **Cross-sectional study.** Compare bodybuilders, powerlifters and untrained people at one time point.

He states the limitations plainly rather than burying them. Both longitudinal methods sample **different fibres** before and after — the pre-training fibres are physically removed from the subject — so the method rests on the assumption that fibres from the same muscle region share characteristics. Cross-sectional designs cannot establish causation: the bodybuilders may have had lower specific tension before they ever trained. This is the kind of caveat-stating that makes the channel worth reading despite the missing credentials.

## What the 33 studies actually found
His tally is clean and the arithmetic checks out — each study is counted once:

| | Direct assessment (15) | Specific tension (12) | Cross-sectional (6) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Myofibrillar hypertrophy | 5 | 8 | 2 | **15** |
| Myofibrillar packing | 5 | 4 | 2 | **11** |
| Sarcoplasmic hypertrophy | 5 | 0 | 2 | **7** |

The striking cell is the zero: **no specific-tension study has found a decrease**, which is the signature of sarcoplasmic hypertrophy. All the sarcoplasmic evidence comes from direct assessment and cross-sectional comparison.

## Volume is the only variable that separates them
He colour-codes each supporting study by load, frequency and volume.

- **Myofibrillar hypertrophy:** moderate loads, low-to-moderate frequency, low-to-moderate volume — the same picture from both longitudinal methods. The two cross-sectional studies involved bodybuilders, powerlifters and recreationally trained men; one (auto-captioned as "Shoepe", unverified) described moderate-to-heavy loads and moderate frequency, with no volume detail.
- **Myofibrillar packing:** moderate loads, moderate frequency, low-to-moderate volume. Effectively **identical variables to myofibrillar hypertrophy**, which is why he concludes you cannot currently train for one over the other.
- **Sarcoplasmic hypertrophy:** moderate loads, moderate frequency, and — the one point of difference — **high volume**, in a slight majority of the direct-assessment studies.

Two honest qualifications he raises against his own conclusion:
- One supporting low-volume study (Penman) **ran no statistical analyses and reported no fibre or whole-muscle growth measurement**. Discounting it strengthens the volume argument, and he says so — but it also means the argument rests on four usable studies.
- The cross-sectional study he names as D'Antona (unverified) found **packing** in bodybuilders doing four to five sets per exercise, i.e. plausibly high volume. That cuts directly against the volume hypothesis, and he presents it rather than skipping it.

The volume inference from cross-sectional data is doubly indirect: it depends on assuming bodybuilders performing four to five sets per exercise also performed multiple exercises per muscle, which is never actually recorded.

## What this leaves you with
Nothing actionable, and he does not pretend otherwise. Myofibrillar hypertrophy is the most commonly observed outcome of ordinary training; myofibrillar hypertrophy and packing appear at low-to-moderate volume and cannot be told apart by any training variable we can currently see; sarcoplasmic hypertrophy is the rarest finding and *may* be more likely at high volume.

**Where a conclusion outruns the evidence:** the "high volume causes sarcoplasmic hypertrophy" claim is drawn from a slight majority of five studies, at least one of them statistically empty, with one cross-sectional finding pointing the other way. He labels it tentative — the label is correct and should be kept when the claim is repeated elsewhere.

The obvious gap he identifies is real and still largely open: nearly every study used moderate loads, so **whether heavy-only or light-only training preferentially produces one of the three outcomes has simply never been tested directly.**
