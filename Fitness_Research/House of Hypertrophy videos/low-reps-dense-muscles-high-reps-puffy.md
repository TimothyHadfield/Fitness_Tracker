# Do Rep Ranges Decide Whether Muscle Grows "Dense" or "Puffy"?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Low Reps = DENSE Muscles, High Reps = PUFFY Muscles" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wHibrCW6XvU)) · 2022-09-19 · 18 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- The evidence people cite *for* rep ranges determining growth type is all weak: two cross-sectional athlete comparisons with an anabolic-steroid confound, correlational powerlifter data, and one acute protein-synthesis measurement that reverses direction depending on when you take it.
- The stronger evidence points the other way. **Every long-term study directly finding myofibrillar hypertrophy used roughly 7–12 reps** — bodybuilding rep ranges — and isometric strength gains are similar between 8–12 and 20–35 reps, implying contractile content increases similarly across the range.
- The 1RM gap between 8–12 and 25–35 reps **is not evidence of different growth type**, because a 1RM is a skill and heavy training develops that skill specifically.
- What *may* drive sarcoplasmic hypertrophy is **volume, not reps**: three of the five long-term studies finding it used 21+ weekly sets per muscle, and none of the myofibrillar-hypertrophy studies used high volume.
- The magnitude point is the one worth carrying: in **MacDougall's** study, triceps fibre cross-sectional area rose 23% while myofibrillar relative space fell **2%**. He is openly sceptical that a 2% relative shift produces a visibly puffier muscle.

## The claim being tested
He opens by restating his own position on rep ranges: 6–35 reps to or near failure produce similar hypertrophy; 3–5 reps are likely less efficient per set, needing extra sets to match. Given equal *magnitude*, does the *type* of growth differ?

The framing comes from two folk arguments. One: low reps produce myofibrillar growth ("dense"), high reps produce sarcoplasmic growth ("puffy"). Two: elite weightlifters get myofibrillar hypertrophy while bodybuilders get sarcoplasmic — a claim made in *The Science and Practice of Strength Training*. Weightlifters and powerlifters generally train at five reps or fewer, bodybuilders at 8–15, so the two arguments collapse into one.

For this video he merges myofibrillar hypertrophy and myofibrillar packing under one label, since both involve substantial myofibril growth.

## Part I: the case for
- **Meijer 2015** compared vastus lateralis fibres from 12 bodybuilders, 6 power athletes (footballers, track and field athletes, weightlifters) and 14 untrained subjects. Bodybuilder training diaries showed mostly 8–15 reps. Bodybuilder fibre **specific tension was 62% lower than the power athletes and 41% lower than untrained subjects**. Specific tension is force per unit of fibre cross-sectional area — effectively a proxy for how much of the fibre is myofibril — so lower values indirectly imply more sarcoplasm.
- **Ikegawa 2008** compared elite bodybuilders to elite weightlifters. Bodybuilders had larger triceps cross-sectional area and greater absolute isometric force, but a **lower ratio of cross-sectional area to force** — per unit of size, weaker. The correlation between triceps size and force was also slightly stronger in the weightlifters, consistent with their size containing proportionally more contractile material.
- **Powerlifter correlation studies** (Reya, Keogh, Brechue, Ye) find strong relationships between muscle mass measures and bench, squat and deadlift performance, again suggesting size that is heavily myofibrillar.
- **Burd 2010** compared four sets to failure at 90% versus 30% 1RM. At 24 hours post-session, sarcoplasmic protein synthesis was greater in the 30% condition — indirect support, since more sarcoplasmic protein could draw water in.

## Part II: why none of that holds up
He takes each apart rather than leaving them standing:

- **Steroids.** Both athlete comparisons used high-level competitive bodybuilders, and **nine of the twelve in the Meijer study admitted anabolic steroid use.** Steroids may increase sarcoplasmic expansion independent of training. He notes there is no science that can validate this, but quotes Greg Nuckols: people gain a lot of weight very quickly on steroids and lose it very quickly coming off — more than plain myofibrillar hypertrophy explains, likely water.
- **Causation.** Both are single-time-point comparisons. The bodybuilders may have had lower specific tension before they ever trained.
- **The Burd study reverses.** At **4 hours** post-session, sarcoplasmic protein synthesis was greater in the **90%** condition. And an acute synthesis measurement is not evidence of long-term sarcoplasmic expansion regardless of direction.

## Part III: the stronger evidence against
**The long-term direct studies.** He tables all the studies that longitudinally found myofibrillar hypertrophy and points out that **all of them trained at roughly 7–12 reps** — the exact rep range the "puffy" argument blames. He is careful to say this does not mean fewer reps fail to produce it; no research has looked. But it does mean classic bodybuilding rep ranges do not preferentially produce sarcoplasmic hypertrophy.

**The 1RM objection, and the answer.** People point to **Schoenfeld 2015**: trained men, 3 sets per exercise, 8–12 versus 25–35 reps to failure, similar hypertrophy but greater bench and squat 1RM gains for 8–12. If size gains matched but strength did not, the argument goes, the high-rep group must have added less myofibril.

His counter is that a 1RM is a skill. Technique and recruitment patterns differ between light and heavy loads on the same exercise (Król 2017 on the bench press, Larsen 2022 on the squat), so heavier training develops the specific skill and neural adaptations the test measures.

**The better test.** Isometric strength has a much lower skill component. A **2017 meta-analysis** found isometric strength gains were **similar between heavier (mostly 8–12 reps to failure) and lighter (20–35 reps to failure) training**. Combined with the finding that 7–12 reps produce myofibrillar hypertrophy, he infers 20–35 reps must be adding myofibrillar content just as effectively.

## Part IV: volume, not reps
He tables the five long-term studies finding sarcoplasmic hypertrophy. **Three of the five used high volume**, defined as 21+ weekly sets per muscle — one (MacDougall) with a minimum of 27 triceps sets per week, another with 36 quadriceps sets per week. **None** of the myofibrillar-hypertrophy studies used high volume.

He advises caution: three studies is not much. But if it holds, it also offers a cleaner explanation for the bodybuilder comparisons in Part I — those bodybuilders may have been high-volume trainers, and volume rather than rep range explains their profile.

## The magnitude point, which matters most
Except in one of the five studies, the **relative** sarcoplasmic expansion was small. In MacDougall's study, triceps fibre cross-sectional area rose **23%** while myofibrillar relative space fell **2%** — indirectly, the sarcoplasm took up 2% more relative space. He is straightforwardly sceptical that this manifests as a puffier-looking muscle, and concludes that for natural lifters sarcoplasmic hypertrophy is probably not an aesthetic concern at all.

**Where a conclusion outruns the evidence:** the volume→sarcoplasmic association rests on three studies and he labels it as such; the inference that 20–35 reps produce myofibrillar hypertrophy is an extrapolation from isometric strength data rather than a direct ultrastructural finding, and he says so. Both labels should survive any repetition of the claims.
