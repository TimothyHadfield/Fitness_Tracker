# Training Frequency: A 35-Study Meta-Regression Finds Almost Nothing

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How Often Should You Train a Muscle? - This NEW Study Is Epic" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Em6bweyQCZo)) · 2024-12-02 · 13 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- A new meta-regression of **35 studies, 1,000+ subjects** (the same Pelland et al. analysis that produced the volume results) finds **no consistent or large effect of weekly frequency on hypertrophy once weekly sets are equated**.
- Going from 1× to 2× per week produced a **slight numerical increase, but with credible intervals compatible with negligible effects**. Beyond 2×, no clear benefit at all. No meaningful difference between trained and untrained subjects.
- **This is the note's headline for our library**: it does not support the "2× beats 1× at matched volume" position held elsewhere in the collection, and it finds nothing for 3× either. Dhim's verdict is that frequency is not the make-or-break variable — pick it by preference, schedule and recovery.
- The "high frequency only wins at recoverable volumes" defence was tested directly and failed: splitting studies into recoverable versus unrecoverable volumes still showed no clear benefit of higher frequency in either bin.
- Best method for *counting* frequency: the **fractional method**, where indirect sets count as half a session. Evidence was strongly-to-very-strongly in its favour over counting all sets equally or counting direct sets only.
- He says outright that he expected higher frequency to win at high volumes and is **"genuinely surprised"** it isn't clearly supported. That candour is the most useful thing in the video.

## What the analysis actually equated
[01:01] Weekly sets were held constant. Nine biceps sets in one session versus three sets on each of three days. Most included studies had subjects working to or very close to failure within a rep range, with loads adjusted to stay in that range. This matters mechanically: nine sets in one session means the later sets use much lighter loads to reach failure, whereas splitting them means fresher, heavier sets. The question is whether that translates into growth. It does not, clearly.

## The direct/indirect counting problem
[02:33] A **direct set** is one where the muscle is the primary force generator (biceps in a curl, triceps in an extension, chest in most bench pressing). An **indirect set** is one where the muscle is trained but is not primary (biceps in a row, triceps in a wide-grip bench). Three counting methods were compared:
- **Total** — direct and indirect count equally. Row Monday + curl Thursday = 2×/week for biceps.
- **Fractional** — indirect counts as half. Same example = 1.5×/week.
- **Direct** — indirect ignored. Same example = 1×/week.

The fractional method explained the data best. Dhim's reading is that this is logical from both sides: indirect sets are not as effective as direct ones (so total overcounts), but they do provide stimulus (so direct-only undercounts). He notes the authors' direct/indirect classification table is a judgement call not everyone will agree with — a limitation he flags rather than hides.

## The recoverability test
[05:39] The standard rebuttal to null frequency findings is that the studies used too many sets per session, so higher-frequency groups never recovered. The researchers split studies by whether volumes were plausibly recoverable, defined as: **≤3 sets per session at 3×/week, ≤5 sets per session at 2×/week, ≤12 sets in the session at 1×/week**. Neither the recoverable nor the unrecoverable subset showed a clear, large benefit of higher frequency. He notes these sub-analyses are less rigorous than the main model, but the implication is that higher frequency cannot be rescued either below or above a set threshold.

[07:10] He states the intuition he holds and the evidence contradicts: 15 weekly biceps sets in one session should be excessively fatiguing, and splitting it across days should produce higher-quality sets and more growth. "This is something that I would think to be true and I am genuinely surprised it's not clearly supported by the literature."

## Those recoverability thresholds are probably too low
[07:40] He pushes back on the ≤3/≤5/≤12 numbers, which circulate on social media as if they were recovery limits. Five studies against them:
- Trained individuals training **daily for four days**, four sets each for chest and quads per session, maintained bench and squat performance — with a slight squat increase on the final day. Recovery from four sets in **24 hours** (House et al.).
- After **seven sets per muscle**, the majority of trained subjects recovered by **72 hours** (McLester et al.).
- After **12 sets for chest**, trained subjects could on average replicate the session's work **72 hours** later (Miranda et al.).
- Untrained subjects doing **six sets for quads 3× per week** progressed loads across the study — no serious recovery problem from six sets **48 hours** apart (Rønnestad et al.).
- Well-trained subjects doing **11 sets for quads 2× per week** progressed loads across the weeks (Enes et al.).

Note the evidence type: performance recovery and load progression, not growth. He is arguing the thresholds are too conservative, not that more frequent training grows more muscle.

## His explanation for the null: individual variation
[09:43] Frequency has to matter at some extreme — 70 triceps sets in one monthly session will not be optimal — but within a seven-day window the averages converge. His hypothesis is that frequency is highly individualised: people differ in between-session recovery and in how well they dissipate fatigue *within* a session, so some benefit from splitting and others do not, and pooling across studies washes it out. He immediately marks this as speculation and links a paper explaining why the existing data is not designed to detect individual differences at all. That is the correct caveat, and it also means the hypothesis is currently untestable with this literature.

## Practical verdict
[10:45] Choose frequency by preference, schedule and recovery. If you are running high volume for a muscle, splitting it across more days is probably practical for most people — there was a numerical, uncertain 1×→2× increase, and at worst higher frequency is neutral on average. Experiment across your training career.
