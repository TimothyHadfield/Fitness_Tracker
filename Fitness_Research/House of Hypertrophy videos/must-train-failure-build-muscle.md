# The Failure Meta-Regression, and Why It Doesn't Prove What It Was Said to Prove

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "\"You MUST Train to Failure to Build Muscle\" (NEW Research)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Inr630aZFbo)) · 2023-06-12 · 11 min
**Topic:** Effort & failure

## Bottom line
- The 2023 meta-regression finds hypertrophy improving **non-linearly as you approach failure**. That headline is real. It was also, at the time of the video, a **preprint** — not peer-reviewed, still open to revision.
- The parts nobody quoted: the benefit of nearing failure **shrank with heavier loads, higher frequency, more sets per muscle, longer study duration, and stricter definitions of failure** — and in those moderator analyses the null sat inside the confidence interval.
- The reps-in-reserve figures the regression runs on are **estimates**, and he works through one case (Martorelli) where the estimate is demonstrably wrong in a known direction.
- His pick for the best single study — Santanielo, within-subject, trained men — found **no significant difference**, with raw gains leaning slightly toward the *non*-failure leg.
- Landing position: nearing failure is a genuine variable; **≤3 reps from failure, failure included if you want it**. He does not endorse mandatory failure.

## What came before

Three meta-analyses in the preceding three years ([Grgic 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33497853/), [Refalo 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/), [Vieira 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33555822/)) all found no significant hypertrophy difference between failure and non-failure training. He accepts the standard objection to them: "non-failure" is a bucket that contains one rep in reserve and ten. A meta-regression across proximity-to-failure is the right fix.

## The three things the headline left out

**Load interaction.** With heavier loads there was *less* benefit to nearing failure. He gives the mechanistic reading: nearing failure matters because it forces full fibre recruitment, and heavy loads already force it, so the marginal value of the last reps falls.

**Moderators.** Higher training frequency per muscle, higher weekly sets per muscle, and longer study durations each reduced the failure benefit — and in each case the null point estimate sat within the confidence interval. In plain terms, once volume and frequency are decent, the data leave open the possibility that there is **no benefit to failure at all**.

**The definition moderator, which cuts the wrong way.** Studies using a strict definition — momentary muscular failure or concentric failure — showed *less* benefit than studies using vaguer "volitional failure". If failure were the driver, the studies that measured it properly should show the effect most clearly. They show it least.

## The reps-in-reserve problem

Most included studies never reported how far from failure the non-failure groups stopped, so the authors estimated it with equations. He credits them for doing as well as could be done, then shows the ceiling on how well that can be.

His worked case is [Martorelli et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28713535/): two barbell curl groups at 70% 1RM, one doing three sets to momentary failure, the other three sets of seven. Baseline maximum at that load averaged **10.37 reps**, so the estimate is **3.37 RIR**. Two errors follow. First, cumulative fatigue — the estimate might fit set one, but sets two and three were closer to failure than 3.37. Second, the researchers did not update the load until **week five**, so as subjects got stronger their true rep maximum at that fixed load rose, and 3.37 RIR became an increasing underestimate across the first five weeks.

He also notes the regression included cluster sets and rest-pause protocols, where the RIR equations are least applicable. He would have excluded them, but says the moderator data suggest their inclusion did not swing the result — a fair-minded concession against his own objection.

## The best single study

He argues that with limitations like these, high-quality individual studies still deserve weight, and nominates [Santanielo et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33343066/) as the strongest to date.

**14 men, average 5.1 years of training.** Unilateral leg press and unilateral leg extension, twice a week for **10 weeks**. Two design strengths he calls out. First, sets were **individualised**: each subject's pre-study weekly quad set count was raised by 20% (a 15-set lifter did 18), split between the two exercises. Second, it was **within-subject** — one leg took every set to momentary muscular failure, the other stopped at perceived just-shy of failure, with dominant legs counterbalanced. That removes genetics, nutrition and lifestyle as confounders.

The non-failure leg averaged **1.6 fewer reps** than the failure leg, which he reads as roughly 1–3 RIR given the standard deviations, while noting the estimate is broad because legs are not equally strong. **Vastus lateralis cross-sectional area increases were not significantly different**, and the raw numbers leaned slightly toward the non-failure leg. Two further reasonable studies, both on untrained subjects ([Lacerda 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31809457/), [Nóbrega 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29189407/)), agree.

## What he actually recommends

Train **within three reps of failure, failure included if you prefer it**. Mix both. Get real experience with failure at some point, because otherwise you cannot calibrate what two reps in reserve feels like. And let the interaction with other variables decide: at higher volumes and frequencies it is easier to leave reps in reserve; at lower volumes and frequencies going to failure is more straightforward.

The compound-versus-isolation split — reps in reserve on squats and benches, failure on curls and lateral raises — he presents explicitly as **what many people find practical, not as a research finding**. The current literature cannot separate the two.

**Overreach flag:** none in the direction the title implies. If anything the pressure runs the other way — Santanielo's n=14 does a lot of work as "the single best study to date", and the raw gains favouring the non-failure leg are within noise he would not credit if they pointed the other way.

**Rigour note:** he refused to overclaim from a paper that supports the position he is arguing against being oversold, named its preprint status, reported the moderators that weaken the case *for* his own conclusion as well as against it, and conceded the point about alternative set structures. This is the channel at its best.
