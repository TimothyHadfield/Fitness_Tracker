## How to read this source

House of Hypertrophy is the most densely cited channel in this library and the narrowest. The
median video is twelve minutes on a single training variable. Across the channel he cites 1,557
references, 977 of them distinct.

The creator is **self-taught and has no formal credentials**, which he says himself. Judge him
on the work, and the work is unusually good in a specific way: he presents the study that
contradicts him, states sample sizes and training status, distinguishes a statistically null
result from a demonstrated absence, and **publicly retires his own positions when the evidence
moves**. This summary records several of those reversals, because the reversals are the strongest
evidence that the rest is worth trusting.

Three cautions apply throughout, and they are the reason the domain summaries keep repeating the
same three questions.

**Was growth measured, or something that stands in for it?** Enormous parts of this channel —
almost every exercise ranking, every "best exercise for the side delts" tier list — rest on EMG,
moment-arm modelling or acute signalling rather than on measured hypertrophy. He usually says so.
Readers usually don't notice.

**Were the subjects trained, and was the program complete?** Much of the volume literature ran
on programs training one or two muscle groups for around ten weeks. That is not a program, and
he now says so about work he previously cited without the caveat.

**Is there a reference list at all?** **Twenty-six videos have none**, and they are
disproportionately his longest and most confident: the 73-study biceps guide, the 87-study
mechanisms video, the 63-study triceps guide, the 36-study lats guide. Those cite on screen
only. Where a claim traces back to one of them it is unverified, and the domain summaries mark it.

## The short version

- **Mechanical tension is the driver**, and the case for it is an elimination argument built on
  signalling studies rather than a direct measurement. The negative findings are better evidenced
  than the positive one: microtears, the pump, lactate and post-workout hormone spikes each fail
  the same way — the thing rises and growth does not follow.
- **More volume keeps working further than most people think.** The best current meta-regression
  finds no plateau out to 43 weekly sets per muscle. He still recommends 10–20, and is candid
  that this is his prior overriding the curve.
- **Rep range barely matters between about 6 and 35 reps** when sets are taken near failure — and
  his sharpest single argument is why the studies claiming otherwise are broken: they gave the
  low-rep groups more sets and longer rest.
- **Effort matters more than tinkering.** Training to failure and stopping 1–3 reps short grow
  much the same at normal volumes; drop sets, rest-pause, supersets and tempo tricks are time
  levers, not growth levers.
- **Lengthened partials are at least as good as full range of motion and probably not better**
  in trained lifters — a position he built aggressively and then largely walked back in 2024.
- **Exercise selection has one usable predictor**, and it is not activation: a two-joint muscle
  grows poorly when one of its actions opposes the compound movement, and better when the other
  joint holds it at length.
- **Almost everything else is a rounding error** — time of day, free weights versus machines,
  cardio at ordinary volumes, exercise variation, pre-sleep protein, and creatine's effect on
  lean mass once you measure it directly rather than through body water.

## What each domain adds, in one paragraph

**[Volume, frequency and effort](SUMMARY-volume-frequency-and-effort.md)** is the highest-traffic
material here and the least settled. Six trials disagree about whether high volume keeps paying,
and the domain file lays the disagreement out unsmoothed rather than averaging it into a number:
the rest-interval hypothesis that might explain it, the muscle-swelling objection to short-trial
results, the fractional set-counting change, and the Barbalho retraction that removed one leg of
the dispute entirely. Frequency, once sets are equated, does nothing that anyone still defends.

**[Mechanisms](SUMMARY-mechanisms.md)** is the channel's intellectual centre and the place to be
most careful, because mechanism claims get quoted as if they were training findings. The domain
file labels the species and design of every load-bearing study, which matters more here than
anywhere: the four tension studies carrying the 87-study video are never given a species in the
note, and at least one is a rat mTORC1 dose-response experiment. Most mechanisms have no practical
payoff at all, and the file says so for each.

**[Programming](SUMMARY-programming.md)** contains the sleeper variable of the whole library. Rest
intervals may explain several of the volume trials that appear to contradict each other — 4.5 sets
at one minute matching 3 sets at three minutes — which means a set is not a fixed unit of stimulus
and set-counting across studies is shakier than it looks. Advanced techniques mostly trade
within-session performance for time saved, not for growth.

**[Exercise selection, upper](SUMMARY-exercise-selection-upper.md)** and
**[lower](SUMMARY-exercise-selection-lower.md)** carry the rankings, and both files keep the tiers
of evidence separate: measured hypertrophy, EMG, and moment-arm modelling. Several of the rankings
have no hypertrophy study in them at all, which he states himself. The lower-body file also holds
the transferable selection principles — biarticular conflict, muscle length, and why "the best
exercise" is the wrong question.

**[Range of motion and tempo](SUMMARY-range-of-motion-and-tempo.md)** is where the evidence moved
fastest and where he changed his mind most visibly, so the file is organised with dates attached:
the 2021 enthusiasm for lengthened partials, and the 2024 trained-subject nulls that retired it.

**[Load and reps](SUMMARY-load-and-reps.md)** turns on one argument worth carrying everywhere —
nearly every study claiming low reps build muscle handed the low-rep group extra sets and longer
rest, and it still only tied. It also keeps strength and size rigidly separate; maxing out builds
one and not the other.

**[Nutrition and reading the evidence](SUMMARY-nutrition-and-evidence.md)** is misnamed by half.
Four nutrition notes cannot answer a nutrition question — go to the ISSN position stands or Jeff
Nippard for that. The other ten notes are the most transferable material on the channel: sampling
variance, why individual response is usually measurement noise, what confounds a training study,
and the four growth proxies that fail.

## Where he departs from the rest of this library

This library holds five sources. Where they disagree, the disagreement is the useful part, and
this channel disagrees more often than its narrow subject range suggests. Full adjudication is in
[WHAT-TO-BELIEVE.md](../WHAT-TO-BELIEVE.md).

**On volume ceilings.** Jeff Nippard's [junk volume](../Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/junk-volume.md)
position treats roughly six hard sets per muscle per session as the point of diminishing returns,
resting on a Krieger meta-analysis. The meta-regression this channel reports finds growth still
climbing past 20 sets per session and 43 per week, with no inverted U anywhere. The two are not
strictly incompatible — per-session and per-week are different questions, and the high-volume
studies mostly trained one muscle group — but they pull hard in opposite directions, and a reader
following Nippard's number is training well below what the newer analysis supports. Menno
Henselmans sits closer to this channel, doubting that a ceiling has been demonstrated at all.

**On the Barbalho papers.** Both this channel and Menno Henselmans report that the Barbalho volume
trials were **retracted for implausible data**. Those trials were one of the three legs of the
volume-ceiling dispute this library records, and the Nippard material predates the retraction and
does not mention it — a correction has since been applied to the Nippard summaries. This channel
handles it best: it removes the papers from an argument they would have supported.

**On rep-range floors.** This library's older material puts the equivalent band at 5–35 reps.
This channel argues for a floor of 6 rather than 5, on the grounds that the trials defending very
low reps systematically gave the low-rep group more sets and longer rest. That is a better
argument than the number it produces, and it applies to any source repeating the 5–35 figure.

**On lengthened partials.** Nippard's material and this channel's own earlier videos lean toward
lengthened partials beating full range of motion. The 2024 trained-subject studies found no
advantage — and one of the null studies was **funded by Nippard**, which this channel discloses.
Where the library still asserts a partials advantage in trained lifters, it is behind the
evidence.

**On EMG.** This channel produced the library's hardest anti-EMG argument — the triceps long head
shows high activation in the bench press and grows minimally from it, which is exactly the
inference EMG is used to make. He then softened that stance in later videos and built whole
exercise rankings on activation data. Both positions are in this folder. The earlier one is
better reasoned.

**On fibre type and the soleus.** This channel's position is that fibre type should not drive rep
selection, resting on a calf trial that found no fibre-type effect. Nippard agrees in general but
carves out the soleus, prescribing 15–25-rep calf sets — a fibre-type-driven rep prescription
that runs against the study this channel relies on. Neither source engages the other.

**On creatine.** The headline "one third faster muscle growth" is a reinterpretation, not a
measurement. The direct-measurement meta-analysis found an effect size of about **0.11**, which
its own authors called trivial; the reclassification to "small-to-medium" uses hypertrophy-specific
thresholds taken from a blog post. The methodological objection is reasonable. The headline
outruns it, and he does not say so.

## Known problems in this material

Recorded here rather than quietly fixed, because a reader needs to know where the floor is thin.
Each domain summary carries its own Weak points section with the detail.

- **Twenty-six videos have no retrievable reference list**, including the four most confident
  guides on the channel. Every claim traced to them is unverified. They are marked in their own
  notes and in the domain summaries, but they are also the videos most likely to be quoted.
- **Reference lists are occasionally wrong.** One video describes a strength *frequency*
  meta-analysis and links the *set volume* meta-analysis instead. Flagged in the note rather than
  corrected.
- **The same study is reported with different numbers in different notes.** Enes 2021 appears as
  28 trained men and as 18; the Schoenfeld calf trial as 8 weeks and 10; a Schoenfeld
  repetition-duration meta-analysis as 2015 and 2016; Costa 2021 four different ways, including a
  trained/detrained conflict that no note flags. Ahtiainen 2005 carries two different PMIDs across
  three notes. None changes a conclusion; all are reference hygiene.
- **He reads the same paper two ways across years.** Gentil 2015 is used at face value in 2022 to
  argue isolation adds nothing, then discounted in 2024 because its author has been involved in
  studies with statistical anomalies. Both notes are in this folder and neither mentions the other.
- **Titles inflate study counts.** A video titled "11 Studies" rests on two chronic trials and
  nine acute or mechanistic papers; a video titled "18 Studies" is carried by three. The notes say
  so where it matters.
- **187 reference links could not be resolved to a citation.** Most are congress abstracts,
  strength-and-conditioning journals that are not machine-indexed, and PDFs rehosted on private
  domains. They appear in the bibliography as the bare link he gave, which is honest but not
  followable in every case.
- **Caption transcription mangles researcher names**, so every name in these notes was taken from
  the description's reference list rather than the audio. Where a video has no reference list and
  a name was only spoken aloud, the note describes the study without naming an author. Some of
  these are recoverable from adjacent verified lists and have not yet been cross-linked.
