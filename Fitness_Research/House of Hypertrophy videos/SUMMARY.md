## How to read this source

House of Hypertrophy is the most densely cited channel in this library and the narrowest. The
median video is twelve minutes on a single training variable. Across the channel he cites 1,557
references, 977 of them distinct.

The creator is **self-taught and has no formal credentials**, which he says himself. Judge him
on the work, and the work is unusually good in a specific way: he presents the study that
contradicts him, states sample sizes and training status, distinguishes a statistically null
result from a demonstrated absence, and **publicly retires his own positions when the evidence
moves**. This summary records several of those reversals, because the reversals are the strongest
evidence that the rest is worth trusting.

Three cautions apply throughout, and they are the reason this summary keeps repeating the same
three questions.

**Was growth measured, or something that stands in for it?** Enormous parts of this channel —
almost every exercise ranking, every "best exercise for the side delts" tier list — rest on EMG,
moment-arm modelling or acute signalling rather than on measured hypertrophy. He usually says so.
Readers usually don't notice.

**Were the subjects trained, and was the program complete?** Much of the volume literature ran
on programs training one or two muscle groups for around ten weeks. That is not a program, and
he now says so about work he previously cited without the caveat.

**Is there a reference list at all?** **Twenty-six videos have none**, and they are
disproportionately his longest and most confident: the 73-study biceps guide, the 87-study
mechanisms video, the 63-study triceps guide, the 36-study lats guide. Those cite on screen
only. Where a claim in this summary traces back to one of them, it is unverified, and it is
marked.

## The short version

- **Mechanical tension is the driver**, and the case for it is an elimination argument built on
  signalling studies rather than a direct measurement. The negative findings are better evidenced
  than the positive one: microtears, the pump, lactate and post-workout hormone spikes each fail
  the same way — the thing rises and growth does not follow.
- **More volume keeps working further than most people think.** The best current meta-regression
  finds no plateau out to 43 weekly sets per muscle. He still recommends 10–20, and is candid
  that this is his prior overriding the curve.
- **Rep range barely matters between about 5 and 35 reps** when sets are taken near failure — and
  his sharpest single argument is why the studies claiming otherwise are broken: they gave the
  low-rep groups more sets and longer rest.
- **Effort matters more than tinkering.** Training to failure and stopping 1–3 reps short grow
  much the same at normal volumes; drop sets, rest-pause, supersets and tempo tricks are time
  levers, not growth levers.
- **Lengthened partials are at least as good as full range of motion and probably not better**
  in trained lifters — a position he built aggressively and then largely walked back in 2024.
- **Exercise selection has one usable predictor**, and it is not activation: a two-joint muscle
  grows poorly when one of its actions opposes the compound movement, and better when the other
  joint holds it at length.
- **Almost everything else is a rounding error** — time of day, free weights versus machines,
  cardio at ordinary volumes, exercise variation, pre-sleep protein, and creatine's effect on
  lean mass once you measure it directly rather than through body water.

## What actually makes a muscle grow

Twenty-six videos cover this, the channel's home ground and where its method is most visible: set an evidential bar, apply it to your own preferred stimulus first, and say which findings are measured and which are inferred. They agree on the headline and disagree on how confident it should be.

### The one stimulus left standing

Mechanical tension is his answer, reached by elimination rather than demonstration. In [the 87-studies mechanisms video](most-important-muscle-building-stimulus-87-studies.md) he sets three criteria — a plausible mechanism, protocols raising the stimulus producing more growth, and the ability to isolate it — then discards the classic animal evidence *for tension* because it fails the third: tenotomy, stretched-limb immobilisation and weighted chicken wings all confound tension with electrical signalling, metabolites and damage. Throwing out his own side's evidence first is the most credible move in the domain.

What replaces it is four newer studies, and their type matters enormously. A 2019 Danish experiment blocked active force production while leaving electrical input intact: activation of the key growth-signalling complex did not occur; the same group equated peak tension between eccentric contractions and pure passive stretch and found equal activation, so total tension registers rather than its origin. A 2008 US study raised active force at constant electrical input and saw signalling rise; a 2001 Canadian study found peak tension linearly related to signalling protein activation. **All four measure signalling over hours, not size over months.** He says so. The human-level support — protocols "presumed" to equate or elevate tension producing equal or greater growth — is inference, because tension cannot be measured in a training human. That is the load-bearing distinction in the whole channel: mechanism is measured in cells, outcome in people, and nothing measures both at once.

Two studies push at the definition. [Counts's no-load curls](biceps-curls-no-weight-build-muscle-optimally.md) — 13 untrained subjects, 6 weeks, one arm doing 4×20 maximal squeezes with no weight, the other 4×8–12 at 70% 1RM — produced similar biceps thickness gains at all three sites: external load is a convenient forcing function, not the stimulus. [Warneke 2023](stretching-builds-muscle-similar-lifting-weights.md), 69 subjects, found six weeks of **one hour of daily static calf stretching at 7–8/10 pain** matched 5×10–12 calf presses three times weekly for gastrocnemius thickness. Passive tension is still tension. Both videos are far more careful than their titles. [The mind-muscle note](mind-muscle-connection-help-build-more-muscle.md) is adjacent: Schoenfeld's 27 untrained men gained significantly more elbow flexor thickness with an internal focus over 10 weeks, but showed **no difference for the quads**.

His definition ends on the two levels most people miss: tension must last a decent duration and fall within one time window — ruling out 1RM work and 50-rep sets. [The 2024 myths video](90-still-believe-builds-more-muscle.md) turns that into his cleanest correction: **time under tension is not mechanical tension.** A long slow set spends most of its time far from failure; what matters is time under *high* tension, reachable anywhere in roughly 6–35 reps.

### The mechanisms that turn out not to drive growth

**Microtears.** The repair-it-bigger story is carried by two videos with 40 million combined views and no outcome data. [The microtears video](biggest-muscle-building-myth-ever-microtears.md) dismantles it using Schoenfeld's own 2012 review as the case's strongest statement. Direct evidence: a 2011 study of **14 untrained men** where a three-week ramp-in group had little damage or soreness across an eight-week eccentric cycle-ergometer programme and grew comparably to the naive high-damage group; **Damas 2016**, where the myofibrillar protein synthesis spike correlated with hypertrophy only *after* damage attenuated; and **Foley 1999**, six men, five sets of ten eccentric curls at 110% of concentric 1RM, where muscle volume finished roughly **10% below baseline** for weeks. [The repeated bout effect note](most-unappreciated-muscle-building-principle.md) closes it from the other side: maximal eccentric quad work, 5×15 weekly for 10 weeks in untrained men, saw DOMS, creatine kinase and C-reactive protein fall to near-nothing by weeks 8–10 while training continued. Damage disappears; growth does not.

**The pump.** [The pump video](pump-best-indicator-hypertrophy-true-false.md) separates measured from inferred exactly: the supportive evidence — drop sets, short rest on isolation work, constant-tension partials — comes from studies where **the pump was never measured**, and he flags that each time. The one direct test (Hirono) correlated acute quadriceps swelling with six-week hypertrophy at rho of only **0.4–0.6**. Against it: 30% 1RM gives a far bigger pump than 80% with similar growth; BFR at 20–40% 1RM gives an enormous pump and matches 70–90% loading (Lixandrão); 2.5+ minutes rest beats 90 seconds on compounds despite a smaller pump.

**Metabolic stress.** [The lactate video](huge-muscle-building-myth-22-studies.md) supplies the strongest disconfirmations. Infusing lactate into training humans raised blood and muscle lactate without raising anabolic signalling above saline — and he volunteers the design flaw favouring him (infused into the arm, not the working leg). The dissociations need no mechanism: 400 m running and sprint cycling match five sets of lower-body work or three sets of curls to failure for muscle lactate, and build almost nothing. Cuffing 3–5 minutes *after* sets added nothing and **reduced** growth in women (Dankel 2016).

**Hormones.** Four notes converge. [Acute spikes](spike-testosterone-growth-hormone-naturally-via-lifting.md): six correlation studies, four null, the positives disagreeing about which hormone matters; the arm-trials conflict (Ronnestad n=11 positive distally, West n=12 null); short rest spikes GH more and grows less. [Resting levels](lifting-weights-not-increase-testosterone.md): two meta-analyses and six trials find no chronic rise, even in men aged 60–78 starting at bioavailable testosterone around **129 ng/dL**. [Between people](myth-testosterone-muscle-hypertrophy.md): across McCall (n=11), Morton (49 trained men) and Mobley (67 untrained men), resting and intramuscular free testosterone fail to separate high from low responders. [Within a person](science-increasing-testosterone-muscle-strength.md): raising it works, but **additively** — Bhasin's four-group trial gave 6.7% quadriceps CSA for testosterone alone, 5.4% for training alone, 13.7% combined against a 12.1% sum; Finkelstein puts a realistic natural-range rise at **2–4%**.

### The open questions

The taxonomy comes from [the definitional video](science-muscle-growth-actually.md): five routes — myofibrillar hypertrophy, sarcoplasmic hypertrophy, myofibrillar packing, hyperplasia, increased length — the first three being one continuum defined by the ratio of myofibril to sarcoplasm growth. [The myofibril video](stronger-bigger-muscles-myofibrillar-packing-hypertrophy.md) is blunt that **no human studies examine the myofibril level at all**; thickness and splitting rest on chicken and mouse ultrastructure. Only fascicle length transfers, responding to long-length ROM and eccentric overload.

**Sarcoplasmic hypertrophy** is where his own position moved. The [33-studies tally](sarcoplasmic-vs-myofibrillar-hypertrophy-packing-33-studies.md) counts 15 studies finding myofibrillar hypertrophy, 11 packing, 7 sarcoplasmic — the striking cell being **zero of 12 specific-tension studies finding the decrease sarcoplasmic hypertrophy predicts**. [The dedicated video](science-sarcoplasmic-hypertrophy.md) frames high volume (21+ weekly sets) as a lever, on three of five studies classified after the fact, without asking whether a lifter should want proportionally less contractile tissue. A year later [the dense-vs-puffy video](low-reps-dense-muscles-high-reps-puffy.md) deflates it: in MacDougall's data triceps fibre CSA rose **23%** while myofibrillar relative space fell **2%**, which he doubts is visible. That is a genuine reversal of emphasis, and the later note is right. [The muscle-confusion study](muscle-confusion-produces-sarcoplasmic-hypertrophy.md) — 20 trained men, within-subject — found myofibrillar space holding at ~70% in the constant leg and falling to ~61% in the varied leg, before he catches an internal contradiction in the paper and notes unmeasured swelling could explain all of it.

**Hyperplasia.** [The fibre-number video](gain-more-muscle-fibers-training.md): animal meta-analysis gives +11.6% under weight training (five studies, all positive), +21% stretch, +5.4% ablation. A human biceps holds an estimated **198,000–419,000** fibres, nobody has counted them, and every human study infers backwards from biopsies. He lays out three defensible positions and picks none.

**Deceleration.** [The 16-studies video](muscle-growth-slows-down-fast-16-studies.md) rejects Counts's three-month-plateau claim: four studies show quads levelling at 6–8 weeks, but a 1970 study found no biceps plateau across 100 days and Narici found quads rising across six months. His reinterpretation — that untrained people grow for three months while ignoring nutrition and variation — is untested. [The newest video](much-muscle-gain-causes-growth.md) puts lifetime natural gain at roughly **5 / 12 / 21 kg** fat-free mass for low, average and high responders, and warns against building programmes on speculation about what maximises tension when outcome data says the variable doesn't matter — him correcting his own framework.

### Rigour, and where to hold the line

Rigorous: leading with the study contradicting the popular one ([androgen receptors](hidden-key-building-muscle-legit.md), where Morton's 49 trained men and Mobley's 67 untrained men disagree and he picks no winner); killing the systemic-hormone explanation for [cross-education](cross-education-effect-training-one-limb-increases.md) with his own hormone evidence; demolishing surface EMG normalisation in [the activation video](change-muscle-activation-any-compound-exercise-maybe.md) before conceding what survives it.

Where it outruns evidence: his damage position hardened then softened — the 2022 microtears video treats it as settled while the 87-studies video from the same year, the 2024 myths video and the 2025 review all allow a threshold role, and Schoenfeld, whose reviews supply the demolished case, stays more agnostic than any. [The calf video](calf-training-worth.md) rests entirely on between-study comparisons.

The citation problem is worst where the stakes are highest. **The 87-studies video — the intellectual centre of the channel — cites on screen only; its description carries no reference list, so all eighteen studies underpinning the tension case are unverified.** The same holds for the sarcoplasmic, hyperplasia, myofibrillar-packing, 2024-myths and calf videos. Those claims may be sound; they are not checkable.

## How much, how often, and how hard

### What he actually concludes about weekly sets

There are two answers and he knows it: the **analysis** says more is more, the **recommendation** says 10–20.

[Schoenfeld/Ogborn/Krieger 2017](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md) pooled 15 studies for roughly **0.37% more growth per added weekly set** — but mostly untrained subjects and almost no arm above 9–12 sets, so he narrows it to "9–12 beats less, in beginners." [The 2024 meta-regression](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md) (Pelland et al., **35 studies, 1,000+ subjects**) then found a square-root curve with **no plateau and no inverted U**, climbing to **43 weekly sets per muscle**, later sets worth about **one third** of the first. [The companion per-session analysis](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md) found the same shape per session, still rising past 20, with the researchers' uncertainty threshold at **11 sets per session and 31 per week**. And [Enes 2024](2-2x-more-muscle-new-study-surprising.md) — 31 trained men, 12 weeks, progressing from 22 to **42 or 52 weekly quad sets** — beat a fixed 22 on cross-sectional area and squat 1RM.

Against that he recommends **10–20 weekly sets per muscle**, "if I had to guess," after training quality is fixed. He is candid that this is his prior, not the paper's output — [the curve does not bend at 20](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md) — but a skimming viewer hears "the data says 10–20" when it does not. Two mismatches sit underneath: the meta-regression's winning counting scheme was **fractional** (indirect sets count 0.5), so 10–20 fractional sets is more work than 10–20 counted traditionally, which he does not say; and his 2021 position of **12–18** with long rests and **30–45** with short ones was hedged as "probably not practical or sustainable" — [a dose only defensible under conditions he advises against](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md).

His reason for overriding the curve is the strongest thing in this domain: **a fair number of the pooled studies used incomplete programs**, training one or a handful of muscle groups, so systemic fatigue and recovery demand do not resemble a real program. He says he "probably should have done a better job at emphasizing" this earlier. It lands hardest on the headline results — Enes trained **quads only**, and [that is the very study he cites as finding benefit at high volume with long rests](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md). His calibrated position: **confident moderate beats low even in a complete program, not confident very high beats moderate.** Durations averaging **~10 weeks** carry the rest.

### Rest interval as the hidden variable — and the position he retired

His sharpest original contribution — and the arc ends with him abandoning it.

In [the 2021 ultimate guide](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md) he counted **exactly six trials** in trained lifters testing above 9–12 weekly sets, splitting three-three. **Brigatto** (27 men, 16/24/32 sets, 8 wk, **60 s rest**), **Radaelli** (48 men, up to 45 triceps sets, **6 months**, **90–120 s**) and **Schoenfeld 2019** (34 men, ~4.4 yr, up to 45 quad sets, 8 wk, **90 s**) favoured the highest volume. **Heaselgrave** (49 men, 9/18/27 biceps sets, 6 wk, **3 min**), **Ostrowski** (35 men, 7/14/28 triceps sets, 10 wk, **3 min**) and **Aube** (35 men, 12/18/24 quad sets, 8 wk, **2 min**) found a plateau at 12–18. The correlation is perfect: every high-volume win used short rest, every plateau long rest. The mechanism is **Longo** — 3 leg-press sets with 3 min beat 3 with 1 min, but **4–5 sets with 1 min matched 3 with 3 min** — so the six trials become one finding in two currencies. He used it to attack the [2022 Baz-Valle review for pooling short- and long-rest studies](many-sets-per-muscle-examine-science.md), and to insist any per-session ceiling carry its rest interval: **15–16 sets was productive with short rests**, against a mechanistic **10–12** with long rests drawn only from acute protein synthesis. [In the splits video he pushed that to 6–10](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md), labelled it speculation, and supplied the counterexample — Bartolomei's bro-split group did **20 quadriceps sets in one session** with 2 min rest and still won.

Then the 2025 per-session meta-analysis ran rest interval as a moderator and the relationship **held across both shorter and longer rests**. [He calls this out and retires his own position](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md), having already declined to bank the 2024 moderator that pointed his way [because the authors called it exploratory and accepting it would force him to conclude that very high volumes with one-minute rests are optimal](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md). Correct in both directions — and the honest summary is that the hypothesis was always a **post-hoc regrouping of six trials**, never a tested comparison, and the one analysis that tested it did not support it.

### Where the Barbalho retraction bears

The "less is more" leg of this dispute was largely the Barbalho papers: twin trials in trained men and untrained women, every muscle trained **once weekly** at 5, 10, 15 or 20 sets — so weekly sets equalled per-session sets — concluding **5–10 sufficed and 15–20 were slightly worse**. [He states plainly that both are retracted, probably because the data were not legitimate](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md), citing Vigotsky and Stronger by Science, and **removes them from an argument they would have supported** — he was at that moment building the case for a per-session ceiling. The clearest instance of the channel's core virtue.

The "5 beat 20" position therefore loses its primary evidence. What remains against high volume is the three long-rest plateau trials (**8–10 weeks**, n=35–49), two **German Volume Training** trials (n=19 and 12) in which [5 sets beat or tied 10 but **neither group showed significant ultrasound hypertrophy**](german-volume-training-actually-build-muscle-fast.md) — detection failure more than ranking — plus two unpublished nulls he volunteers. The strongest surviving objection was **swelling** — that studies measuring 48–72 h post-session read residual fluid as growth. [Alvarez tested it directly](many-sets-muscle-growth.md) (13 trained men, ~5.1 yr, crossover, 7 vs 14 vs 21 sets, measured to 72 h): **no differences between set numbers, swelling resolved by 24 hours**. He reports **no effect sizes, confidence intervals or power discussion** for that null: the hedge does more work than the evidence.

### Frequency: the variable that keeps coming back empty

With weekly sets equated, frequency does almost nothing. The Pelland analysis found [**no consistent or large effect** — 1×→2× a slight increase compatible with nothing, nothing beyond 2×, and the "high frequency only wins at recoverable volumes" defence failing in both bins](often-should-train-muscle-new-study-epic.md); he is **"genuinely surprised"**. Below ~12 weekly sets the picture is clean ([Schoenfeld 2019, 13 studies, 11 at ≤12 sets](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md)) and bro splits are not inferior; above 12, five studies give four answers. The mechanism collapses too: [the MPS argument runs on *mixed* protein synthesis, which does not track myofibrillar MPS](high-frequency-training-more-frequent-muscle-protein.md), whose damage-free readings stay elevated 48–72 h — arguing for *lower* frequency, which he also refuses. [Overtraining is likewise hard to induce: 10 of 22 studies that tried produced nothing qualifying](scientifically-dismantling-mike-mentzer-claims.md), and [24-hour spacing tested fine](long-should-wait-train-muscle-again.md). Frequency is a **container for volume**, [possibly an individual-response variable](best-training-frequency.md); **2×/week** is a hedge, not a finding.

The clearest illustration of why frequency keeps coming back empty is [a trial that could not separate it from volume in the first place](training-muscle-2x-vs-3x-week-growth.md): 21 untrained subjects trained one side 2×/week at 6–8 weekly sets and the other 3×/week at 9–12, so the two variables moved together by design. Every muscle tended to grow more on the 3× side — by **1–3%** for quads, hamstrings and biceps and **4–5%** for triceps — and he says plainly that the extra sets were probably the driver. The same study killed the fibre-type hypothesis in passing: subjects with more fast-twitch fibres did not do better on lower frequency, and grew no more overall than the slow-twitch group.

### Effort is a separate question, and the answer is narrower

Failure is not required, and the claim is small: **3–1 RIR matches failure for hypertrophy.** [Four longitudinal studies say so](training-failure-must-hypertrophy.md) — Lacerda (n=10), Nóbrega (n=32) and Sampson (n=28), all untrained over 12–14 weeks, plus **Santanielo** (14 men, ~5.1 yr, within-subject, 10 wk), the only trained entry. The best test since is [an 18-subject within-subject trial averaging **7.7 years' experience**](hard-should-workout-new-study-epic.md): failure vs 1–2 RIR, 8 weeks, 10–17 weekly quad sets, similar growth, with **verified RIR accuracy** and individualised set counts.

The bounds matter more than the headline. The null holds at **6–19 weekly sets**; at **~2 weekly sets** Giessing found failure better, and [the minimum-dose video reverses his usual position on that ground](build-muscle-while-barely-training-using-science.md). Beyond 3 RIR is **unresolved, not permitted**: [four studies, three directions, one confounded by unequal rest (Karsten: 2 vs 1 min), one by concurrent sprint training (Carroll)](leaving-more-3-reps-reserve-vs-training.md). Where higher RIR survives, **extra sets compensate** — Lasevicius needed 2–3 extra sets for 5 RIR to match failure, and 14 RIR could not be rescued at all. He also retracted his own claim that velocity data licensed >3 RIR, binning a 6,000-word script. And [the 2023 meta-regression that appeared to favour failure is dismantled from the inside](must-train-failure-build-muscle.md): the benefit shrank with heavier loads, higher frequency, more weekly sets and longer durations, the null inside the interval each time — and studies using a **strict** failure definition showed *less* benefit than those using vague "volitional failure" — the wrong way round if failure is the driver.

### How well can anyone actually judge RIR?

Better under instruction than assumed, badly in habitual practice. [Halperin's pooled 12 studies found an average **1-rep underestimation** — but individuals out by **10–13 reps**](become-laser-accurate-leaving-reps-reserve.md). Accuracy improves with heavier loads (past ~12 reps underestimation grows), with estimates made *during* rather than before a set, and on upper body; training years barely helped. Against that, [Hackett 2017 had 81 adults predict 5–6 more reps when they could do 10](should-train-failure-muscle-growth.md), while [the 2025 varied-RIR trial measured a worst average error of **−1.5 reps**, improving to −1 on retest](train-easier-size-strength-new-study-interesting.md). The reconciliation: people judge proximity reasonably when *trying to*, and poorly when just training — which makes occasional failure sets **calibration, not stimulus**, and means "3 RIR" silently degrades into junk volume for anyone who never checks.

Two things sit alongside effort without being it. **Form is looser than the orthodoxy claims**: [the first hypertrophy trial on cheat reps](cheat-reps-vs-strict-reps.md) — 25 untrained subjects, within-subject, curls and pushdowns, 4 sets of 8–12 to failure, 2×/week for 8 weeks, one arm swinging every rep and the other strict — found momentum "neither helped nor hindered" growth at either measured region. He does not use it to abandon strict form: the untested safety question remains, and the real cost is that cheating makes a heavier load ambiguous between getting stronger and cheating harder. And **belief measurably changes what you can lift**: [deceived into thinking they had taken Dianabol, nationally ranked powerlifters added 9.5 kg to the bench, 12.2 kg to the squat and 10.9 kg to the deadlift](gain-strength-muscle-underrated.md), the effect scaling with what the subject believed the substance did, and a sham-fatigue nocebo running the other way. Almost all of it is acute strength rather than hypertrophy, and the one 10-week trial where both groups ran an identical program but only one was told it was individualised is a pilot he declines to lean on.

Two points close the domain. **Volume load is the wrong currency** — [matched volume load produced very unequal growth in Lasevicius](volume-load-not-drive-muscle-hypertrophy.md) — so count **hard sets of 6–35 reps within ~3 of failure** (his own settled band — see Load and rep ranges below for why the floor moved off 5). And effort cannot be bought structurally: [rest-pause, drop sets and clusters only reach parity](normal-sets-vs-rest-pause-vs-drop.md), [buying time rather than stimulus](science-drop-sets-vs-normal-sets-hypertrophy.md).

### Rigour and reach

He is rigorous three ways: discarding retracted data that helps him, retiring his own rest-interval and >3 RIR claims, and volunteering unpublished nulls against his own findings. Where he outruns: the 30–45 set figure was never usable; the swelling null rests on n=13 with no effect sizes; the individual-differences case is built almost entirely on **untrained** subjects and its transfer asserted rather than shown. Two claims rest on videos with **no retrievable reference list** — [the 27-study HIT video](1-set-failure-hit-vs-high-volume.md), and [the minimum-dose video, including its correction that fewer than five weekly sets yield **53%** of the growth of more than ten](build-muscle-while-barely-training-using-science.md), a figure conflicting with the uncorrected 64%/84% numbers held elsewhere in this library and unverifiable from the source.

## Load and rep ranges

### The band, and where it moved

The channel's most repeated number moved twice. The 2020 version: [The hypertrophy rep range is roughly 5 to 35](best-rep-range-build-muscle.md) reads Schoenfeld's 2017 meta-analysis as spanning 30–85% of 1RM — "roughly 5 to 35 reps" — provided sets reach failure. It held two years.

In April 2022 he raised the floor on camera. [Are sets of 5 reps enough](5-reps-maximize-hypertrophy.md) walks back his own position on Vann et al.: 15 trained men (average squat 167 kg), within-subject, 3×/week for 6 weeks, one leg at 5 reps and 82.5→95% 1RM near failure, the other at 10 reps and a fixed 60% with ~10 reps in reserve. The light leg grew more vastus lateralis CSA; the heavy leg averaged **no gain at all** — while doing *more* weekly sets (18 vs 15) and training *closer* to failure. New advice: **8–35 reps**.

Five months later it came back down and stopped. [4 vs 8 vs 12 reps](4-vs-8-vs-12-reps-strength.md) states the position he has held since: **6–35 reps are similarly effective per set; 3–5 reps work but are less efficient per set and need extra sets; 1–2 reps may not build muscle at all.** [The whole rep-range literature in one place](many-reps-muscle-growth-40-studies.md) (2024, five meta-analyses) defends 6–35 against the 5–30 others quote, arguing it better describes the reps actually performed.

**He landed on 6–35.** [Can sets of 3–5 reps maximise growth](3-5-reps-truly-maximize-growth.md) (August 2025) covers a bioRxiv preprint — n=14, 2+ years trained, within-subject, 3–5 vs 20–25 reps to failure, 3 sets at 2 min rest, 9 weeks — finding similar vastus lateralis growth (7.4% and 8.5% at two regions). He will not lower the minimum on two supporting studies, says he *hopes* it comes down, and tells anyone already progressing below 6 reps to change nothing.

### Why he rejects the low-rep studies

This is the sharpest argument in the domain, and it pins the floor. [Can reps below 5 maximise growth](reps-below-5-maximize-muscle-growth.md) tallies six studies comparing 2–5 reps against moderate or high reps; five found parity. Four of those five gave the low-rep group more sets, longer rest, or both — usually because researchers equated *volume load*. Mangine (33 men, 2+ years trained), 3 min rest against 1 min; Schoenfeld 2014 (17 trained), **7 sets of 3 against 3 sets of 10** at 3 min against 90 s; Campos (27 untrained), 4 sets/3 min against 2 sets/1 min; Chestnut & Docherty (19 untrained), 6 sets/3 min against 3 sets/2 min.

Two clean comparisons remain, and they contradict. Weiss 2000 — 44 untrained men, 4 sets of squat, 6 min rest throughout, 7 weeks — found 3–5 reps as good as 13–15 and 23–25. Schoenfeld 2016 — 19 trained men, 3 sets, 2 min rest, 8 weeks — found 2–4 reps grew *less* vastus lateralis and elbow flexor than 8–12.

The inference, put plainly in [Can sets of 3–5 reps maximise growth](3-5-reps-truly-maximize-growth.md), is that those confounds run *toward* the low-rep group, so they cut against the low-rep case rather than for it: handed extra sets and longer rest, ≤5 reps should have *beaten* higher reps if it were equally potent per set, and it only tied. He also dismantles the volume-load equating behind the confound — 6–35 reps grow similarly at matched sets despite very different volume loads, so equating it introduced a confound rather than removing one — the same move he runs in [Adding load vs adding reps](adding-more-load-repetitions-better-hypertrophy-strength.md) against a pooled n=14-vs-10 re-analysis.

Below the floor it is firmer: in [Maxing out](maxing-out-effective-building-muscle-one-rep.md), Dankel (n=5, 21 consecutive days) and Mattocks (38 untrained, 8 weeks) both had 1RM-only training match full training for strength with near-zero thickness change — singles build the skill, not the tissue. The ceiling is softer — Lasevicius (untrained, 12 weeks) found 40/60/80% 1RM similar and all better than 20% (60+ reps, half the growth), yet a curl trial averaging **100 reps on set one** matched 80%, and [How light is too light](light-too-light-maximizing-muscle-growth.md) leaves sub-30% at two studies for and two against, refusing to resolve it. **No reference list** there.

### Fibre type, and whether rep range varies by muscle

It does not. [The optimal rep range for each muscle](optimal-rep-range-each-muscle-true.md) and [Training by fibre type](should-train-muscles-according-fiber-type.md) both centre on Schoenfeld 2020: 26 untrained men, within-subject, one leg 4×20–30 and the other 4×6–10 to momentary failure on calf raises, 2×/week for 10 weeks. Soleus and both gastrocnemius heads grew equally — and the soleus is where the hypothesis predicts most strongly. Grgic's five-study meta-analysis adds the fibre level: type I and type II growth comparable across loads, confidence intervals wide and flagged. Sixteen histochemical surveys show most muscles sit near 50/50 anyway, and the rep-count self-test fails (Terzis 2008, r < 0.2 against biopsies). His closing line that fibre type "is not a consideration" in programming outruns one 8-week untrained calf study: it shows *load prescription* need not track fibre type, nothing more.

The regional version is thinner: [Heavy and light loads and regional growth](heavy-light-weights-produce-different-regional-muscle.md) rests on Hisaeda 1996, **11 untrained women**, whose groups differed in reps, sets, rest *and* tempo at once — he breaks his own fibre-type explanation on the data and still recommends load variety. **No reference list.**

### Combining and alternating

Permitted, not prescribed. [Alternating rep ranges](alternating-rep-ranges-improves-muscle-growth-18.md) is titled "18 studies" and rests on three with MRI or ultrasound: Schoenfeld (19 trained men, 8 weeks, constant 8–12 vs 2–4/8–12/20–30 weekly), De Souza (26 untrained, 6 weeks), Fink (21 untrained, within-arm, 8 weeks). All null. He goes past "not significant" to Krieger's individual-data reanalysis, then calls the residual triceps signal noise. **No retrievable reference list; studies listed unverified.** The contrary result — Kraemer 2003, 27 untrained women, nine months — is by **skinfold calipers**, discounted on measurement, duration aside. [The benefits of combining rep ranges](benefits-combining-rep-ranges.md) is where framing outruns evidence: he kills the fibre-type rationale, concedes the hypertrophy data show no difference, then argues from neural recruitment, capillarisation and pain tolerance. The defensible part is concurrent development of *other* attributes — 1RM and bone density from heavy work, local endurance and mitochondrial content from light (Lim 2019).

And the average hides people. [The best rep range isn't what you think](best-rep-range-muscle-growth-isn-t.md) — 24 untrained women, mean age 58, 24 weeks, 12 at 8–12 reps and 12 at 27–31 — found comparable means over individuals who diverged wildly (one −5% then +10%, another +13% then +3%). He rejects the authors' plateau-delay reading with a worked two-subject example: rotation is insurance for the uncertain, not a plateau-breaker. The design cannot separate response from measurement error, which he concedes in 2024 via the Robinson critique.

## Range of motion, stretch and tempo

### The arc, 2021 to 2025

This is the domain where the channel's own position visibly moved, and the notes date every step.

**June 2021 — the opening move.** [Full range of motion is NOT optimal](full-range-motion-not-optimal-building-muscle.md) is the founding argument. Six prior studies had compared full to partial ROM; four favoured full, and in all four the partial trained the muscle at a **short or neutral** length. His conclusion is narrow and correct: those test short-length partials, not partials as a category. Pedrosa — **45 untrained women, 12 weeks, knee extension**, five groups, each group's 60% 1RM retested fortnightly against its *own* range — found the long-length partial (100°–65°) grew best overall, beating full ROM at 50%, 60% and especially 70% of femur length. He proposed mechanical tension (voluntary activation rising from **93.6% at 50° knee flexion to 98.5% at 100°**, plus passive titin force), explicitly rejecting metabolic stress and damage, then spent three minutes hedging: one study, untrained women, two of four quad heads, and a knee extension whose resistance profile flatters the result.

**April 2022 — peak optimism.** [TRIPLE hypertrophy](triple-hypertrophy-training-muscle-like.md) is the high-water mark. Sato's preacher curl trial — **32 untrained subjects, 5 weeks**, 0–50° elbow flexion versus 80–130° — gave the long group roughly **three times** the average elbow flexor thickness gain, driven almost entirely by the 70% site. He flagged immediately that there was **no full-ROM group**, and that the full-versus-lengthened-partial claim still rested on Pedrosa alone.

**October 2022 — the sweep.** [Stretch-mediated hypertrophy](key-more-muscle-stretch-mediated-hypertrophy.md) generalised the pattern to quads, glutes, adductors, hamstrings, biceps and triceps, largest **distally**, while stating plainly there was then **no direct research at all** on back, shoulders, calves, abs or chest. He still refused to say lengthened partials beat full ROM.

**August 2024 — the regional framing.** [Optimize muscle growth](optimize-muscle-growth-most-people-don-t.md) recasts ROM as a regional question. Short-length partials grow proximal and middle regions decently but **fail distally** — in Bloomquist's squat trial the distal thigh actually *lost* size. All three studies then favouring lengthened partials used **untrained** subjects, and he says so.

**September 2024 — the turn.** [It's finally here](finally-here-new-study-muscle-growth-epic.md) covers Wolf et al.: **25 trained individuals, 4.9 years average experience, 8 weeks**, within-subject, one side full ROM and one lengthened partials on every exercise. Elbow flexor and triceps thickness at 45%, 50% and 55% of humerus length grew **similarly** — the first trained-lifter trial, and null. He still reads it as informative (the peak squeeze at the shortened position is *dispensable*) and raises the sharpest caveat himself: **only the 45–55% regions were measured**, precisely where the effect is smallest. Worth recording — the study was **funded by Jeff Nippard**, another creator in this library, disclosed in [Most important studies of 2024](most-important-muscle-building-studies-2024.md).

**April 2025 — the counterweight consolidates.** [Training beyond failure](training-beyond-failure-new-study-epic.md) puts both trained-subject nulls side by side: **n=25** and a full-body trial with **n=297**, neither finding an arm or thigh difference. He does not simply accept them — Wolf has an open dispute over how the partials were standardised, the 297-subject trial used low volumes, and **both measured only mid-region** — but he stops recommending lengthened partials generally. Only calves survive as a confident recommendation.

**September 2025 — the reconciliation.** [Faster muscle growth](faster-muscle-growth-new-study-epic.md) tests the distal hypothesis directly: **13 trained lifters, ~6 years experience, 8 weeks**, unilateral preacher curls, full ROM (0–140°) versus lengthened partial (0–70°). The 50% region was a dead heat; the 70% favoured partials at **Bayes factor 4.87** — moderate evidence, not a win. Of six studies, all three in untrained favour partials and all three in trained show similar growth — but two of those three could not have seen a distal effect.

### Where it stands

Lengthened partials are **at least as good as full ROM, occasionally slightly better distally**; nothing has found them inferior; and the untrained/trained split plus the mid-region measurement problem explains most of the contradiction. Full ROM remains a defensible default. Separately — and he is emphatic on the distinction — **exercise selection reaching longer maximum lengths still wins**: seated over lying leg curls (Maeo 2021, with biceps femoris short head as internal control), overhead over neutral triceps extensions, leaning-back over normal leg extensions. He flags a possible **threshold**: lying leg extensions lengthen rectus femoris more still, and were *not* better.

The related results hold up independently. [Partial squats](partial-squats-superior-deep-squats-size-strength.md): 60° knee flexion grows only the proximal thigh; 90° suffices for quads but leaves **glute max and adductors** on the table (Kubo, 17 men, 10 weeks). [Half bench press reps](half-bench-press-reps-more-strength-size.md): Martínez-Cava, **49 men, 10 weeks**, range-specific 1RMs — full-range training beat the partials at full-, two-thirds- *and* one-third-range 1RM, inverting specificity. [Isometrics](they-said-worst-way-build-muscle.md) fits the same logic: **23 trained subjects, 6 weeks**, long-length (~125°) isometrics matched full-range knee extensions.

### Tempo and time under tension: a separate question, cleaner answer

Stretch is about *where* in the range you load, tempo about *how fast*. Unlike ROM, the tempo answer has not moved since 2021.

[Time under tension is overrated](time-under-tension-overrated-10-studies.md) supplies the decisive methodological point. The three studies that appear to show slow tempo builds more muscle (Usui, Watanabe 2013, Watanabe 2014) **fixed load and rep count**, so the slow group was necessarily closer to failure: they measure effort, not tempo. When effort is controlled — Lacerda, **within-subject, 14 weeks**, 2s versus 6s reps to failure; Chaves, 4s versus self-selected ~1.8s — growth is equal, and Schoenfeld's meta-analysis puts **0.5–8 seconds per rep** at parity. Morton 2019 backs it mechanistically: a **threefold TUT spread** across four conditions to failure produced no difference in glycogen-depletion fibre recruitment. [The most overrated principle](most-overrated-muscle-building-principle.md) kills the **30–60 second rule** — Lacerda's fast sets ran 25–38 s and grew the same.

[The best rep speed](best-rep-speed-size.md), October 2025, closes it: a meta-analysis of **14 studies, 278 subjects**, restricted to trials manipulating one phase in isolation, found **no clear advantage either way** — eccentric SMD **0.06**, about 0.9 percentage points, credible interval spanning zero. Both conditions grew **5–6%**, the literature-wide norm. The single exception across every tempo note is **super-slow**: Schuenke's 10s/4s protocol underperformed for fibre CSA, and [SuperSlow training](superslow-training-produce-super-fast-gains.md) dismantles the force-velocity argument for it — with a fixed load, moving slower *reduces* acceleration and therefore force. [Regional tempo effects](repetition-tempo-impact-regional-muscle-growth.md) is the weakest note here: three studies, one significant regional difference, and the flagship Earp comparison confounds tempo with load and effort.

### Rigour and overreach

He is unusually rigorous in three recurring ways: he explains statistics before using them (forest plots, credible intervals, converting SMD 0.06 into a usable percentage); he discards evidence that would help him, throwing out the squat-versus-hip-thrust glute paper over data-validity concerns in [Stretch-mediated hypertrophy](key-more-muscle-stretch-mediated-hypertrophy.md); and he dismantles the study that best supports his own premise, as with the Japanese concentric-versus-eccentric trial in [Mentzer](fastest-way-build-muscle-says-mike-mentzer.md).

Where conclusions outrun evidence: the chest section of [Stretch-mediated hypertrophy](key-more-muscle-stretch-mediated-hypertrophy.md) builds a hypertrophy conclusion entirely on a **strength** study with no growth measurement, and [Half bench press reps](half-bench-press-reps-more-strength-size.md) repeats it — "almost certain" full-range benching grows more, on zero bench hypertrophy data. [Constant tension](constant-tension-key-building-more-muscle.md) reclassifies Pedrosa post hoc to keep alive a technique his own data (two null Tanimoto trials, a Goto/Pinto contradiction) suggests is inert. [Biceps growth](have-talk-about-biceps-growth.md) leans on an arm-circumference result from **n=7** in the same breath as calling circumference the crudest measure available. And the 9-second TUT floor and 84-second ceiling in [The most overrated principle](most-overrated-muscle-building-principle.md) rest on one study plus an author's Instagram comment.

### Unretrievable references

[The most overrated principle](most-overrated-muscle-building-principle.md) has **no reference list at all** — the description holds only timestamps and music credits, and every study is reconstructed from auto-captions. [Mentzer](fastest-way-build-muscle-says-mike-mentzer.md) is the same, on-screen citations only, so the **11 accentuated-eccentric studies**, the n=53 Japanese trial and the 84.5% voluntary-activation figure are all unverified. [Best rep speed](best-rep-speed-size.md) cites a **meta-analysis of 111 studies** absent from its own reference list.

## Back, legs, and how to choose exercises at all

### First decision: does the equipment matter?

No — the best-supported claim here. [Free weights vs machines](free-weights-vs-machines-muscle-hypertrophy.md) covers Schwanbeck 2020: 36 subjects averaging 2 years training, 8 weeks, every set to failure, matched movement for movement, similar biceps and quadriceps gains. [These 3 exercises](3-exercises-don-t-build-more-muscle.md) adds Hernández-Belmonte 2023, the strongest: 36 trained men, 8 weeks, similar quadriceps growth at two sites plus pec major and rectus abdominis. Push-up trials (Kikuchi 2017, n=18 trained; Kotarsky 2018, n=23) agree. [The best training mode](best-training-mode-muscle-growth-most-get.md) closes the escape routes: free weights produce larger acute hormone spikes, but those do not predict growth; variable resistance is null across four trials; eccentric overload won in only **4 of 11 studies**. Recruitment follows the *movement*, not the equipment.

Two caveats: Schwanbeck measured **only two muscles by ultrasound over 8 weeks**, and a null in an underpowered trial is thin ground for "equivalent"; and his band scepticism is **entirely mechanistic** — no band hypertrophy trial exists, and he states the takeaway more firmly than that supports.

Where modality bites is progressive overload. [Bodyweight training](bodyweight-training-bad-muscle-growth-true-false.md) locates it: upstairs is fine, but there is **no proper bodyweight hip-extension exercise** for the hamstrings, and calf raises and hip thrusts blow past 35 reps for novices. "You probably cannot maximise lower-body hypertrophy with calisthenics alone" is hedged as speculation; that note's regional section is EMG, which it flags.

### Second decision: compounds only, or isolation too?

[Are isolation exercises useless](isolation-exercises-useless-muscle-hypertrophy-19-studies.md) is the reference note. Three trials (Gentil 2013, Gentil 2015, França 2015; 8–10 weeks) found adding single-joint work produced no extra arm growth despite the isolation groups doing *more* volume. His rebuttal: every one measured **only elbow flexors or arm circumference at a single mid-muscle site**, which cannot detect regional differences. Four further Barbalho studies pointed the same way and he discards them for improbable data — throwing out evidence that helped the side he opposes. The affirmative case is mostly mechanistic and the note says so: lateral delt, calves, knee-flexion hamstrings, rectus femoris and triceps long head are argued from joint mechanics, with **only Costa 2021 (22 detrained men, 8 weeks)** testing variety directly.

### Third decision: how often do you change exercises?

Never randomly. [Switch exercises every session](switch-exercises-every-session-more-muscle-hypertrophy.md) covers the only direct test: Baz-Valle, **19 men with 2+ years training**, 8 weeks, an 80-exercise app randomising selection each session versus a fixed list, 3×6–12 to failure. Quad growth was statistically indistinguishable, some raw percentages favouring the *fixed* group. His case against switching — skill, repeated bout effect, tracking — is entirely mechanistic, and its recovery limb is contradicted by the study's own null. Variation-group motivation did rise while the fixed group's declined.

That sits in tension with [Underrated selection principles](underrated-exercise-selection-principles-muscle-growth.md), which endorses Rauch 2017 — **17 men, 5.6 years training, 9 weeks** — where an autoregulated group picking its own exercises beat a fixed programme on lean mass. That note and [The best exercise](best-exercise-muscle-hypertrophy-science-says.md) both argue individuals recruit differently on the same lift (Boyer 2021, hamstring heads; Stronska, bench press) — **all surface EMG**, which he elsewhere says cannot predict growth. He even defends Stronska's normalisation seven months after demolishing it, and his "isolate a muscle to raise its compound involvement" principle has **no growth data at all**.

### The principle that actually predicts things: biarticular muscles and length

[Maximize muscle growth](maximize-muscle-growth-new-study-exercise-choice.md) is the best video here. Burke 2024, **28 trained subjects, within-subject** — one leg leg-pressed, the other did leg extensions; one did straight-leg calf raises, the other bent-leg, all to failure at 8–12. Rectus femoris grew better with the leg extension, vastus lateralis with the leg press. Two principles: a biarticular muscle grows poorly when one of its actions *opposes* the compound (rectus femoris flexes the hip; the leg press extends it), and positioning the other joint so the muscle is **longer** grows it more.

The within-subject design is the rigour — your left leg does not sleep more than your right — and his refusal to resolve the parallel biceps case, laying out three counterarguments to his own principle and ending on the fence, is him at his most honest. Two flags: the neural down-regulation story is **inference layered on an outcome**, the study having measured growth only; and calf outcomes came from a **single region per muscle** versus three for the quads.

### Quads

[Is the squat enough](squat-enough-leg-growth.md) found the gap early — Fonseca (49 untrained men, 12 weeks): squat-only groups failed to grow rectus femoris significantly; varied groups grew all four heads. Kubo (17 untrained men, 10 weeks): **neither** 90° nor 140° depth grew it. [Bigger quads](bigger-quads-new-study-epic.md) supplies the fix — **22 untrained men**, within-subject, leaning-back leg extension (40° hip flexion) versus normal (90°), same knee range, **1.4–2.7× greater rectus femoris growth**, vastus lateralis similar — the internal control that makes it credible. [Unilateral vs bilateral](unilateral-vs-bilateral-training-muscle-growth.md) replicates it in **63 untrained women, 8 weeks**: leg extensions won rectus femoris at all three regions, squats won vastus lateralis distally. Run both classes. Overreach: the reverse-Nordic and sissy-squat picks rest on length reasoning with **no comparative growth data**, and Mitsuya 2023 (9 bodybuilders) is MRI *activity*, not growth.

### Glutes, hamstrings and deadlifts

[Squats vs hip thrusts](squats-vs-hip-thrusts-glute-growth.md): 34 untrained subjects, 8–12 reps to volitional failure — **no difference** in gluteus maximus growth at upper, middle or lower regions. Squats additionally grew quads and adductors; neither grew hamstrings. The Barbalho result claiming a 2.5× squat advantage is discarded. Limitations stated: untrained subjects, vague failure criterion. Hamstrings are the hole — Kubo found no significant growth at either squat depth, Bloomquist some decreases.

[Trap bar vs conventional](trap-bar-deadlift-vs-conventional-deadlift-depth.md) is **entirely biomechanical modelling and acute data**, and he says so. Its best move: subjects lift **8.4% more** on the trap bar, so scaling Swinton's moments by that erases the hip and spinal differences and leaves the knee difference intact — quads more, glutes and erectors similar. The hamstring claim has **no data behind it at all**.

### Calves

[Bigger calves](bigger-calves-ultimate-guide-32-studies.md) is where he **reverses himself** — the clearest disagreement in this set. [Maximize muscle growth](maximize-muscle-growth-new-study-exercise-choice.md) concluded straight-leg raises may be all you need; the 2025 guide, on three growth studies plus a swelling study all pointing the same way for the soleus, calls bent-leg work a defensible addition. Straight-leg primacy for the gastrocnemius is unchallenged. Lengthened partials produced **126.9% greater medial gastrocnemius growth** than full ROM (Kassiano 2023, untrained women, 8 weeks, 15–20 reps to failure); lengthened supersets 43.3% greater (Larsen 2025) — ranking the two by those figures compares across studies, a hint not a ranking, as he flags. Rep range does not matter: Schoenfeld 2020 found 6–10 versus 20–30 reps to failure grew all three calf muscles similarly, killing the slow-twitch/high-reps argument. Volume: 12 > 9 > 6 weekly sets in **61 untrained women**, diminishing.

### Back: the domain with no growth data

[Build your lats](build-lats-ultimate-guide-36-studies.md) is titled "36 studies" and contains **zero direct measurements of lat growth** — he states this twice, unprompted. Everything is moment arms, isometric sEMG, and analogy from quad, glute and triceps trials. **The video has no retrievable reference list**: sources appear on screen only, unnamed. Same for [Pullovers](pullovers-not-train-lats-uh-oh.md), a clip of it; and [Is the squat enough](squat-enough-leg-growth.md), whose seven sources are dead `bit.ly` links.

His organising move is good — classify by joint action, so close-grip vertical and horizontal pulls are both shoulder extension and should develop the lats comparably, while wide-grip rows are horizontal extension where lat leverage is poor. The regional-bias claims are the weak link: a moment-arm model chained to isometric sEMG at an angle **not actually reached in the exercise**, then to unmeasured regional growth. The pullover ranking (machine > cable > free weight) is a resistance-profile argument, not a result. And here he argues *more* range is not automatically better, which sits awkwardly beside the long-length principle driving his quad and calf sections.

## Chest, shoulders and arms

### Which pressing variation should I use?

Whichever you like. The bench press has the best growth data of any upper-body exercise here: [Is the Barbell Bench Press Enough for the Chest?](bench-press-enough-chest-growth-24-studies.md) assembles five longitudinal trials and benchmarks them against Wernbom's pooled elbow-flexor rate (0.20%/day). Ogasawara's 24-week study (untrained men, 3×10 at 75% 1RM, 3×/week) produced +43% pec thickness, 0.26%/day; Yasuda's six-week trial hit 0.42%/day; Davies' trained lifters (n=10, 4×5 at 85%, 8 weeks) reached 0.18%/day at the mid pec. He flags every time that thickness and cross-sectional area are being compared against a CSA benchmark.

Everything else is a tie. One direct hypertrophy comparison found machine chest press ≈ flat barbell bench. Push-ups match too, in [Bench Press vs Push Ups](bench-press-vs-push-ups-which-best.md) and [Push-Ups vs the Bench Press](push-ups-vs-bench-press-muscle-hypertrophy.md): Kikuchi (18 trained men, 40% 1RM, 3 sets to failure, 2×/week, 8 weeks — similar pec and triceps thickness) and Kotarsky (23 novices, 4 weeks — neither group significant). The real constraint is load availability, not stimulus: growth holds down to ~30% 1RM, roughly a 35-rep set, and a standard push-up loads ~64% of body weight. Smith machine rests on **five EMG studies and zero hypertrophy data**, which he labels a "tentative hypothesis" in [The BEST Chest Exercises](best-chest-exercises-building-muscle-33-studies.md). Dips have neither.

### Do I need incline work? Yes — but the reason is arithmetic

[How Effective Is the Flat Bench Press for Upper, Middle, and Lower Pec](effective-flat-bench-press-upper-middle-lower.md) is his cleanest piece of reasoning in the domain. Ogasawara's regional CSA gains were upper 36.3%, middle 37.3%, lower 40.0% — near-identical percentages. But baseline upper pec CSA was ~15 cm² against 25–27 cm² for middle and lower, so the same percentage bought **5.5 cm² versus ~10 cm²**. Flat pressing doesn't skip the upper chest; it grows a smaller muscle by the same proportion. Lower-pec work is correspondingly unnecessary — that region has no arithmetic problem.

Which upper-pec exercise is a different matter, and here everything is **EMG**: 5 of 7 studies favour incline for upper-pec activity, 4 of 8 favour a closer grip, 1 of 2 a reverse grip, and the two head-to-head comparisons he leans on are non-significant results from a single paper ([Blow up Your Upper Chest](blow-upper-chest-exercises.md)). Angle is unresolved — 30°, 44°, or no difference.

**Where his position changed.** Chavez et al. (47 untrained men, 1×/week for 8 weeks, incline vs flat vs both) is treated in 2021 as "the one longitudinal study" and the basis for recommending incline. By [Grow Your Chest](grow-chest-most-detailed-guide-internet-72.md) in 2024 he dismantles it: 54–62% thickness gains across **eight training sessions** is ~4.6% growth per workout against a literature averaging 33 sessions, and only 30 of the 47 completers appear in the hypertrophy data, unexplained. Throwing out the study that best supports his own recommendation is the most creditable ninety seconds in the domain.

### If I bench, do I still need direct triceps work?

Yes, and this is the best-evidenced claim in the domain. Brandão 2020 (43 untrained men, four groups, 80% 1RM to failure, 3 min rest, 2×/week for 10 weeks, MRI by head) is covered in [Is the Bench Press Enough for Triceps Hypertrophy?](bench-press-enough-triceps-hypertrophy.md) and [Build Your Triceps Faster](build-triceps-faster-science-simplified.md): bench grew the **lateral head** well and the **long head** barely at all; skull crushers did the reverse; the groups doing both grew all three. The mechanism is clean: the long head alone crosses the shoulder, and shortens there during the press eccentric.

Two cautions: he twice overrides non-significant whole-triceps and medial-head results by calling them type II error, in a study with ~10–11 subjects per cell; and the combined-exercise groups did double the set volume, so only bench-only vs skullcrusher-only is volume-matched.

### Which triceps isolation exercise?

Overhead extension. Maeo (21 untrained adults, within-subject, 12 weeks, 5×10, ROM matched 0–90°, alternating which arm went first) found whole-triceps volume **1.4×**, long head **1.5×**, and medial+lateral combined **1.4×** favouring overhead — despite heavier absolute loads on the pushdown ([The BEST Triceps Hypertrophy Exercise](best-triceps-hypertrophy-exercise.md)). The medial/lateral result has no accepted explanation. Stasinaki (n=9, 6 weeks) was null and always ran pushdowns first each session, a confound he identifies himself.

**Two position changes converge here.** [The ULTIMATE Guide to Building Triceps](ultimate-guide-building-triceps-63-studies.md) (2021) had only the n=9 null and hedged correctly; he adopted Maeo by 2023. That same guide treats the pushdown's hardest-at-lockout profile as a *virtue*, complementary to the skull crusher; by the 2024 chest guide he argues that tension near the shortened position adds little. Both readings cannot stand.

**Reference gap.** The 63-study triceps guide has **no retrievable reference list**. Its 30–45 weekly-set recommendation, its five-study frequency table pointing four different ways, and the fibre-subpopulation argument for 2–3 isolation exercises are all unverifiable.

### Do rows and pulldowns cover my biceps? His answer moved

In 2021, [Rows and Lat Pulldowns Are Not Enough](rows-lat-pulldowns-enough-biceps-growth.md) reports Mannarino (10 untrained men, within-subject, 8 weeks, 4–6 sets of 8–12 to failure, thickness averaged at 25/50/75%): curl arm ~11%, row arm ~5%. In 2025, [Compound Exercises Are Enough for the Biceps](compound-exercises-enough-biceps.md) reports the same team's follow-up (9 untrained men, 8 weeks) splitting it regionally: **curls won at 75%, rows matched them at 25%**. He emailed the authors and confirmed subject overlap between the two papers. His conclusion softens from "rows are not enough" to "back work is not a null stimulus, but isolation is still needed to maximise". The honest reading is his own: nine untrained subjects, and non-significance is not equivalence. One inconsistency — the Gentil 2015 pulldown-vs-curl trial (29 untrained men, 10 weeks, no difference at a single site) is used without comment in 2021 and 2025, while [The ULTIMATE Guide to Biceps Growth](ultimate-guide-biceps-growth-73-studies.md) raises a research-integrity concern about that author.

### Which curl?

His 2023 hypothesis — a curl with the shoulder extended, for the long-head stretch — rested on quadriceps isometrics, preacher-curl partials (Sato and Pedrosa: initial-position partials beat final-position, concentrated at the 70% region) and the Maeo triceps analogy ([The Most EFFECTIVE Biceps Exercise](most-effective-biceps-exercise-not-think.md)). No direct test existed.

Four now do, and he handled the sequence unusually well. [Incline vs Preacher Curls (2023)](incline-vs-preacher-curls-biceps-growth-2.md) opens by saying the first trial **raises the possibility he was wrong** — Zabaleta-Korta's 31 women trended toward preacher at all three regions, though nothing was significant, including the incline group's own gains. Vendruscolo (21 trained men, BFR, **3 weeks**) was null. Kassiano 2025 (63 untrained women, 3×8–12 to failure, 2×/week, 8 weeks) split it: 50% favoured incline, 60% similar, 70% favoured preacher ([2025 note](incline-vs-preacher-curls-biceps-growth.md)). Attarieh (15 untrained men, within-subject, 10 weeks, **resistance profiles matched at ~60° elbow flexion**, biceps and brachialis measured separately) found no significant difference anywhere ([Bigger Biceps: This NEW Study](bigger-biceps-new-study-interesting.md)).

His muscle-by-muscle resolution — incline for biceps, preacher for brachialis and brachioradialis — rests substantially on **Kobayashi, a conference abstract with no full text**. He flags it, but he also rejects the obvious alternative reading (the two studies favouring incline used untrained subjects, the two nulls used trained) in favour of weighing study quality. That is a judgement, and the confound remains available.

Two things dwarf curl choice, both from the 73-study guide, which — like the triceps guide — has **no retrievable reference list at all**. Effort: Martorelli (89 untrained women, barbell curl only, 70% 1RM, 10 weeks) found failure beat stopping 5–6 reps short by a large margin, and a fourth non-failure set barely closed it. Measurement: the biceps is only **46%** of elbow flexor volume (brachialis 37%, brachioradialis 17%), so most of this literature measures something half made of another muscle.

### Side delts, rear delts and forearms: rankings without growth data

This is where confidence and evidence diverge most. [How to Build 3D Shoulders](build-3d-shoulders-25-studies.md), [Bigger Side Delts](bigger-side-delts-ranking-best-exercises-using.md), [How to Build Your Rear Delts](build-rear-delts-28-studies.md) and both forearm notes ([guide](build-forearms-faster-most-detailed-guide-web.md), [ranking](bigger-forearms-ranking-best-exercises-using-science.md)) contain **not one hypertrophy outcome study on the muscle being ranked**. Everything is moment-arm modelling, EMG, and the long-muscle-length literature transplanted from hamstrings, calves and quads, mostly in untrained subjects. He says so in dedicated limitations sections, and his heuristic — EMG is more trustworthy comparing *similar* exercises than dissimilar ones — is applied consistently. The tier-list format still implies more certainty than the method supports.

**The prediction that failed is the most instructive item here.** The 2024 side delt ranking put cable lateral raises in S tier and dumbbells in A on resistance-profile logic, while disclosing he was running the trial that would test it. [We Have to Talk About Lateral Raises](have-talk-about-lateral-raises.md) reports it: 24 trained lifters averaging 7.1 years, one arm cable and one dumbbell, 5 sets to failure at 12–16 reps, 2×/week for 8 weeks — **similar growth proximally and distally**. He leads with the failure, notes growth in both arms sat near measurement error, and reorganises the length literature into three categories — resistance challenge, range of motion, biarticular position — with long-length advantages surviving best in the last.

The best-sourced section in the domain is the impingement defence of upright rows — the only place resting on **outcome data**, notably subacromial decompression failing to beat placebo surgery, backed by a Cochrane review. The weakest is the biceps guide's supination argument, built on fine-wire EMG in a video that spends three minutes rejecting EMG for exercise selection, and contradicted elsewhere in the library on the grounds that supination *shortens* the biceps. [Should You Overhead Press](should-overhead-press-muscle-growth.md) is likewise moment arms and EMG throughout, and [This Arm Muscle is Underrated](arm-muscle-underrated-don-t-ignore.md) ranks brachialis exercises on **one 1995 study** plus a pump measure. His forearm work does something rarer: the received wisdom that neutral or pronated grips favour the brachioradialis fails the EMG count (2 pronated, 3 no difference, **4 supinated**), and he reports that against his audience's expectations.

## Programming: rest, progression, cardio and time

### How long to rest between sets

Four videos across five years; the direction is stable, the precision keeps loosening. The [2020 deep dive](short-vs-long-rest-intervals-muscle-hypertrophy.md) sets the frame: on compounds, 2.5–3 minutes beat 1 minute or less in four of five training studies (12–28 subjects each, untrained through trained, 8–10 weeks). The fifth, Villanueva (22 men, mean age 68), favoured 1 minute; he disqualifies it because volume load was equated, so the short-rest group necessarily trained closer to failure — a proximity-to-failure result, not a rest result. The [2022 guide](optimal-rest-time-muscle-strength-12-studies.md) adds the better organising axis — rest tracks **how much muscle mass the exercise recruits**, not size versus strength — and sinks the hormonal case for short rest: 1-minute rest produced higher acute testosterone but *lower* protein synthesis than 5 minutes.

The [2024 meta-analysis](rest-periods-new-study-changes-things.md) — the first on rest and hypertrophy — he uses to soften the number, not harden it. Longer rest (>60 s) beats shorter (≤60 s) for arm and thigh growth, but split four ways, every band overlaps heavily. The paper floats ~90 seconds as possibly enough; he says explicitly not to treat that as a conclusion. **Current state: rest as long as you feel you need — longer on compounds, shorter on isolation**, with nothing yet comparing 2.5–3 minutes to 2, and four isolation studies pointing four ways.

The escape hatch matters more than the number: Longo's third arm ran **4.5 sets at 1 minute** and matched 3 sets at 3 minutes in equal session time. Short rest lowers yield per set; it does not forbid growth — which is also why [tapering rest can serve as progression](decrease-rest-between-sets-overtime-more-hypertrophy.md), matching load progression in two small trials (~11 per group, 8 weeks).

### How to progress

Two trials converge. The [2022 RCT](best-progressive-overload-method.md) randomised trained lifters to load or rep progression: similar growth across quads and calves, a 2 kg edge to load on squat 1RM. The [2024 within-subject trial](best-progressive-overload-way.md) — **39 untrained people, unilateral leg extension, 4 sets, 2–3×/week for 10 weeks**, one leg adding load inside 9–12 reps, the other holding session-one load and drifting from 9 to 15 reps — found growth and strength similar.

His reframe is the durable part: progressive overload is **not training harder than last time, it is what keeps each session as hard as usual** — evidence that adaptation happened, not its cause. Since 6–35 reps grow similarly near failure, rep progression runs out around 35 reps. His claim that adding sets or sessions "is not progressive overload" is a definitional move, not a finding, and his own cited set-progression study cuts against it. On maximal strength he rightly declines the null: both trials tested at eight-plus reps, and 2–4 reps beats 8–12 for 1RM.

Block organisation adds nothing for size. The 2022 Mosegaard meta-analysis found no hypertrophy difference [periodized versus not](periodization-size-vs-strength-science-says.md), nor between linear and undulating; its 1RM advantage carries a confound he raises himself — periodized arms did more heavy low-rep work — leaving only **undulating beating linear for 1RM in trained subjects**. [A strength block first](building-strength-first-enhances-muscle-growth-later.md) (26 trained men: 3 weeks at 1–3 reps, then 5 at 8–12) beat 8 weeks of 8–12 for quad growth, and he still declines to recommend it, rejecting the authors' "heavier load = more tension" mechanism since recruitment scales with effort, not load. **That video's description carries no links**, so every study in it is unverified.

### Does cardio cost you muscle

Not at studied volumes. [The interference effect has shrunk](combine-cardio-weights-should.md): updated meta-analyses find no compromise to hypertrophy or strength, including a trained-only subgroup, superseding Wilson 2012. He bounds that by the studied protocols — cardio 2–4×/week for 20–45 min, lifting 2–4×/week — and keeps three caveats: extreme volumes probably do interfere, "trained" is not "highly trained", and **power is genuinely compromised**. Practical rule: separate sessions, since a 2021 meta-analysis in trained individuals found same-session cardio lowered strength gains; if same session, lift first.

The reverse gets a harder no. [Lifting cannot replace cardio](lifting-replace-cardio-true-false.md): in 16 well-trained powerlifters, HIIT lifting at 60% 1RM raised VO2max less over 8 weeks than duration-matched HIIT cycling. **Capillary and mitochondrial adaptations are local to the muscles trained**, so running does nothing for your upper body. [Cardio increases growth](cardio-increase-muscle-growth-here.md) is the weakest of the three: its cycling → capillary → hypertrophy chain rests on **one 14-person untrained single-leg study** plus correlational data. He volunteers the limitations; the title does not.

### Training when time is short

**Antagonist supersets** ([11 studies](increase-performance-while-saving-time-11-studies.md)): minimal rest between paired exercises reliably raises acute reps, 2+ minutes does not, but both halve session time (Robbins 2009: ~20 min versus ~10, 1RM gains not different). Long-term evidence is nearly absent: Fink's 23 untrained subjects numerically favoured the *traditional* group. **Drop sets** have the best data ([within-subject trial](they-trained-one-side-drop-sets-vs.md)): 29 trained lifters averaging 7.6 years, 10 weeks, one arm each way — thickness numerically favoured normal sets but sat inside the pre-declared smallest effect of interest, in **60% less time**; two meta-analyses agree, at 30–70% savings. **The 3/7 method** ([43 untrained men, 8 weeks](3-7-method-build-more-muscle.md)) beat 8×6 for biceps thickness and 1RM in ~5–6 min per session against 19–20. **Pre-exhaustion** ([41 trained individuals, 8 weeks](training-style-intense-but-work.md)) cut duration ~36% but produced slightly *worse* growth — and rests on an unpublished preprint, the video's only source.

### Inter-set stretching

Three notes track a documented reversal over 30 months. September 2020 ([cautiously positive](stretching-between-sets-help-build-more-muscle.md)): Evangelista, 29 untrained men, 8 weeks, a 30-second pain-free stretch inside 90 seconds of rest — **only vastus lateralis reached significance**, not elbow flexors, triceps or rectus femoris. October 2021 ([two new studies](stretch-between-sets-more-hypertrophy.md)): Nakamura (16 untrained men, within-subject, 5 weeks) found nothing at any of twelve regions; Wadhi (26 trained men, loaded chest stretch, 8 weeks) nothing at either pec region. March 2023 ([a fourth](stretching-between-sets-transform-muscle-strength.md)): 22 trained subjects, 8 weeks, 45-second stretches at 70–90% discomfort — nothing except lower long-head triceps, which he refuses to claim after reading the individual data.

Reconciled plainly: **three of five published studies find nothing, one finds a small single-region effect, the original finds one significant site of four, and none finds harm.** Strength is never harmed. The studies are too heterogeneous to pool — differing in population, loading, duration and stretch intensity — so his verdict is right: *not validated*, not *refuted*. This is weak evidence in every direction. [Flexing between sets](flexing-between-sets-enhance-hypertrophy.md) resolves more cleanly: Schoenfeld 2020, 27 trained men, 8 weeks — nothing except mid-thigh, and only because squats train rectus femoris poorly.

### When to back off

[Overtraining is hard to reach](overtraining-easier-think.md) is his most disciplined video: of 22 studies attempting to induce it, **10 produced no sustained performance decrease at all**, and 18 trained individuals doing **40+ weekly quad sets for two weeks** finished with leg press strength improved. What did work: 60 one-rep-max squat attempts a week for two weeks, costing 2–8 weeks of recovery — with testosterone unchanged, which is why the field defines overtraining by recovery time, not markers. The real risk is **too much too soon**: unaccustomed maximal eccentric work caused months-long strength loss in six untrained men, and one case of rhabdomyolysis in a single session.

Breaks are safe, probably not magic. [Ogasawara](secret-long-term-muscle-strength-gains-maybe.md): 14 untrained men, 24 weeks, 6-on/3-off matched continuous training on **25% less work**, with the continuous group's gains decelerating while the break group's did not. That resensitisation story leads him to a **10-day break recommendation no trial has tested**. The direct test since ([39 trained individuals, 9 weeks, week 5 off](train-less-more-muscle-new-training-breaks.md)) matched every lower-body growth measure on **40 fewer sets**, though strength was slightly worse. Take breaks when you want one, not on a calendar; deloads remain untested. [Psychological stress](minimize-max-muscle-strength.md) roughly doubled recovery time after a hard session (~96 h versus 48 h, n=31) and cost 1RM over 12 weeks (n=135) — but **that video has no reference list anywhere, citing on screen only, so all of it is unverified**. [Morning versus evening](morning-vs-evening-training-muscle-hypertrophy.md) goes the other way: Grgic's 2019 meta-analysis found no difference in growth or strength.

### Where to trust him, and where not

He is most rigorous when a result would flatter him, and the recurring move is spotting **which variable a study wrongly equated** — Villanueva equated volume load, so it tested proximity to failure. His volume-load arithmetic (3 sets to failure at 60% 1RM accumulate far more volume load than 3 sets at 80%, while growing the same muscle) is the cleanest debunk in the domain. He also dismantles the central-fatigue mechanism that would have *supported* his own long-rest conclusion, and [on CNS fatigue](most-misunderstood-muscle-building-area.md) shows **lighter loads produce more of it than heavier**.

Where he overruns, the pattern is mechanism-forward videos with thin outcome data: the cardio-capillary chain, the untested 10-day break, the vertical-jump fatigue metric he recommends while admitting no study has tested it, and the 1–2 minute rest figures for women and older lifters extrapolated from acute data. Several claims also rest on sources you cannot check — the strength-block and stress videos name studies with no links, the 2024 rest video relies on a creator-maintained web page, and the pre-exhaustion and drop-set videos on unpublished preprints.

## Nutrition, evidence and how he reads a study

### Protein: the number, and the scatter behind it

[The protein myth](protein-myth-much-really-need.md) argues against its own headline. Three meta-analyses put the plateau at 1.5–1.6 g/kg/day; nitrogen balance and IAAO land at 1.5–2.0. Crediting Eric Trexler, he strips the fitted line off the 2018 dataset: above 1.25 g/kg, protein explains **1% of the variance** in fat-free mass change (r² = 0.011). So he gives a spectrum instead of a number — 1.2–1.59 possibly enough, 1.6–2.0 almost certainly, above 2.0 more than enough. Experience does not raise it (Lemon 1992: similar gains at 1.35 and 2.62 g/kg; Bandegan 2017: 1.7 g/kg in eight near-maximal bodybuilders). Only the cutting advice outruns its evidence, as he says: Mettler and Longland both used **40% deficits** against 1.0–1.2 g/kg control arms.

[The protein limit for a meal](protein-limit-meal-they-were-wrong.md) is looser. The 20 g ceiling came from trials training one muscle with one or two exercises; Trommelen's 100 g dose produced greater, longer-lasting MPS with small oxidation. But he then argues MPS is a poor proxy for growth, undercutting his own title: the three growth trials show only that three servings slightly beat two.

### Pre-sleep protein: the confound is the whole story

[Pre-sleep protein disappears once total intake is matched](protein-before-bed-really-work.md) is the cleanest teardown on the channel. The 2021 Reis review everyone cites **did not control total daily protein**: its key trial gave the pre-sleep group 27.5 g extra against a non-caloric placebo, leaving them at **1.9 vs 1.3 g/kg/day**. Nothing there separates "protein before bed" from "more protein". Three trials that did match intake — n=26 trained (54 g casein, 8 weeks), n=13 recreationally active (35 g, 10 weeks), n=42 untrained (whey, 6 weeks) — found no significant differences, with directions flipping between measures — small-sample noise, as he says. Casein is not special either: same area under the amino acid curve as whey. His own flag is right — three underpowered trials give absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

### Creatine: a trivial effect size, relabelled

[The science behind creatine](science-behind-creatine-much-more-muscle-strength.md) is where a conclusion most clearly outruns its measurement. Five meta-analyses put the lean-mass benefit at 1–1.4 kg over 7–14 weeks, but lean mass includes water. The informative analysis pools 10 studies measuring directly by ultrasound or MRI: creatine adds **0.10–0.16 cm of thickness**, **effect size 0.11** — which those authors called trivial. His objection is legitimate, since those thresholds came from strength and power research; his replacement is not. He takes thresholds from Greg Nuckols' **Stronger by Science blog post**, under which 0.11 reads small-to-medium, then benchmarks against a literature average to reach "**about one-third faster muscle gain**." That is a relabelling against a non-peer-reviewed threshold set; the measurement is still 0.10–0.16 cm. He shows his working, and elsewhere reports the spread honestly — ES 0.23 in young adults against 0.06 in older ones, and a hair-loss scare traced to one unreplicated n=20 study measuring **serum** rather than **scalp** DHT.

### EMG, and the position he later softened

[Four reasons EMG can't rank exercises](ryan-humiston-has-made-mistake-emg.md) is his hardest methodological line: EMG measures neuromuscular excitation, not activation; it is unreliable even at that (constant electrical stimulation at three joint angles gave different amplitudes); it reads higher with heavier loads that grow no more muscle; and blind to passive tension. Verdict: EMG exercise rankings have "a high probability of just being plain wrong." That position did not hold. By his 2024 chest guide he argues EMG **is** informative for subtle comparisons — grip widths, bench angles, biomechanically similar presses — while staying unreliable across exercises differing in muscle length, and his upper-chest content leans on EMG throughout. The two are reconcilable, but the change is real — a live inconsistency across the channel, which he flags himself.

### Sampling variance is his master key

[3 sets is not better than 1 set](3-sets-not-better-1-set.md) is his most methodologically careful video. Pinto 2025 (15 untrained men, unilateral pec deck, 2 vs 6 weekly sets, 12 weeks) found similar growth — which he argues is *compatible with* three sets being better, via Krieger's simulation: sampling a population with known true effects, only one of five ten-per-group studies resembles the truth; pooling recovers it. The same restraint runs the round-ups: [2022](best-muscle-growth-strength-research-2022.md) flags a stretch result as an unpublished conference abstract, [2024](most-important-muscle-building-studies-2024.md) retires lengthened partials as superior on two trials (one near 300 subjects), and [Popular myths](popular-muscle-strength-building-myths.md) falsifies "strength causes size" with the 1RM-only studies.

[Defending science-based lifting](defending-science-based-lifting.md) turns the lens on the field; its concessions make it worth keeping: **85% of the 20 most-cited strength and conditioning meta-analyses contained at least one statistical error**, and he found a d_z effect-size error himself in a 2022 protein meta-analysis, which James Steele is now redoing. Weak spots: the "research has delivered" list is drawn entirely from settled, comfortable findings, and he rates recent meta-analyses "exceptional" without showing his work. **That note gives surnames and bare links only.**

Where the instinct fails: [This holds back your potential](holds-back-muscle-strength-potential.md) and [Turkesterone works](turkesterone-works-but-not-reasons-think.md) lean on placebo-steroid studies with **n=6 and n=11**, decades old, one with no comparison group, neither measuring hypertrophy — and returns to close [You cannot gain much more muscle](cannot-gain-much-more-muscle-16-studies.md), an acute strength result arguing about long-term growth. [Jeremy Ethier's new study](need-talk-about-jeremy-ethier-new-study.md) surfaces growth values "upwards of 100%", says he cannot locate the measurement error, and moves on. **Neither of those has a reference list.**

## Where he departs from the rest of this library

This library holds five sources. Where they disagree, the disagreement is the useful part, and
this channel disagrees more often than its narrow subject range suggests.

**On volume ceilings.** Jeff Nippard's [junk volume](../Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/junk-volume.md)
position treats roughly six hard sets per muscle per session as the point of diminishing returns,
resting on a Krieger meta-analysis. The meta-regression this channel reports finds growth still
climbing past 20 sets per session and 43 per week, with no inverted U anywhere. The two are not
strictly incompatible — per-session and per-week are different questions, and the high-volume
studies mostly trained one muscle group — but they pull hard in opposite directions, and a reader
following Nippard's number is training well below what the newer analysis supports. Menno
Henselmans sits closer to this channel, doubting that a ceiling has been demonstrated at all.

**On the Barbalho papers.** Both this channel and Menno Henselmans report that the Barbalho volume
trials were **retracted for implausible data**. Those trials were one of the three legs of the
volume-ceiling dispute this library records, and the Nippard material predates the retraction and
does not mention it — a correction has since been applied to the Nippard summaries. This channel
handles it best: it removes the papers from an argument they would have supported.

**On rep-range floors.** This library's older material puts the equivalent band at 5–35 reps.
This channel argues for a floor of 6 rather than 5, on the grounds that the trials defending very
low reps systematically gave the low-rep group more sets and longer rest. That is a better
argument than the number it produces, and it applies to any source repeating the 5–35 figure.

**On lengthened partials.** Nippard's material and this channel's own earlier videos lean toward
lengthened partials beating full range of motion. The 2024 trained-subject studies found no
advantage — and one of the null studies was **funded by Nippard**, which this channel discloses.
Where the library still asserts a partials advantage in trained lifters, it is behind the
evidence.

**On EMG.** This channel produced the library's hardest anti-EMG argument — the triceps long head
shows high activation in the bench press and grows minimally from it, which is exactly the
inference EMG is used to make. He then softened that stance in later videos and built whole
exercise rankings on activation data. Both positions are in this folder. The earlier one is
better reasoned.

**On creatine.** The headline "one third faster muscle growth" is a reinterpretation, not a
measurement. The direct-measurement meta-analysis found an effect size of about **0.11**, which
its own authors called trivial; the reclassification to "small-to-medium" uses hypertrophy-specific
thresholds taken from a blog post. The methodological objection is reasonable. The headline
outruns it, and he does not say so.

## Known problems in this material

Recorded here rather than quietly fixed, because a reader needs to know where the floor is thin.

- **Twenty-six videos have no retrievable reference list**, including the four most confident
  guides on the channel. Every claim traced to them is unverified. They are marked in their own
  notes and in this summary, but they are also the videos most likely to be quoted.
- **Reference lists are occasionally wrong.** One video describes a strength *frequency*
  meta-analysis and links the *set volume* meta-analysis instead. Flagged in the note rather than
  corrected.
- **The same study is reported with different durations in different notes** — one trial appears
  as 8 weeks in one video and 9 in another. His numbers, not a transcription error.
- **Titles inflate study counts.** A video titled "11 Studies" rests on two chronic trials and
  nine acute or mechanistic papers; a video titled "18 Studies" is carried by three. The notes say
  so where it matters.
- **187 reference links could not be resolved to a citation.** Most are congress abstracts,
  strength-and-conditioning journals that are not machine-indexed, and PDFs rehosted on private
  domains. They appear in the bibliography as the bare link he gave, which is honest but not
  followable in every case.
- **Caption transcription mangles researcher names**, so every name in these notes was taken from
  the description's reference list rather than the audio. Where a video has no reference list and
  a name was only spoken aloud, the note describes the study without naming an author.
