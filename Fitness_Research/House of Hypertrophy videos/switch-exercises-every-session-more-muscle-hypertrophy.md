# Changing Your Exercises Every Session: One Study, Three Objections

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Switch Exercises EVERY Session for More Muscle Hypertrophy?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nESKfZLK7W8)) · 2022-04-25 · 10 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- The "muscle confusion" idea has been tested exactly once, and it did **not** win: randomised exercise selection every session produced quad growth statistically indistinguishable from a fixed exercise list over 8 weeks in trained men.
- Some of the raw percentages actually **favoured the fixed group**, most visibly for rectus femoris — but with 19 subjects that could be noise either way.
- The one thing the variation group did better on was **motivation**, which rose across the study while the fixed group's slightly declined.
- Dhim's case against constant switching is therefore not "it kills growth" — it is three practical arguments: **skill acquisition, the repeated bout effect, and progress tracking**.
- All three arguments are mechanistic. None was measured in the study.

## The law of accommodation, and what it doesn't license
[00:00] The starting premise is standard: a biological system's response to a constant stimulus declines over time. Gains are largest in your first couple of years and shrink after — Kraemer and colleagues' review of ~140 studies established that curve for strength, and the same shape almost certainly holds for size.

The bodybuilding response to that is muscle confusion: change your exercises every single session so the muscle never gets a chance to accommodate. Dhim's point is that diminishing returns happen *regardless of how you train*, so accommodation on its own doesn't tell you that novelty is the fix.

## The only study
[01:33] Baz-Valle and colleagues recruited **19 men with at least 2 years of training**, squatting an average of 123 kg and benching 94 kg. Everyone did two upper and two lower sessions per week for **8 weeks**.

- **Fixed group:** the same six upper-body exercises on upper days, the same six lower-body exercises on lower days.
- **Variation group:** a phone app with an **80-exercise database** randomly generated three pushing and three pulling exercises for upper days, three anterior-chain and three posterior-chain exercises for lower days.

Both groups did **3 sets of 6–12 reps to failure** on everything. Ultrasound measured rectus femoris, vastus intermedius and vastus lateralis thickness at ~50% of femur length.

[03:05] Growth in all three muscles was **not statistically different** between groups. Dhim is careful here in both directions: some percentages leaned toward the fixed group (rectus femoris especially), and he raises both possible explanations — a type II error from the small sample, or simply that a handful of subjects in the fixed group happened to have better quad-building genetics. His conclusion is the honest one: this study **fails to qualify as evidence that switching helps**. It does not prove switching hurts either.

[04:06] The questionnaire result is the interesting positive: variation-group motivation **increased** over the 8 weeks while fixed-group motivation slightly declined. If the growth null is real, random switching is a legitimate way to buy adherence at no cost to hypertrophy.

## Three reasons he still doesn't recommend it
[04:36] **Skill.** Compound lifts are motor skills. Regular practice is what makes execution efficient enough to load the intended muscles. Constant switching prevents or badly delays skill acquisition — worse for novices than for experienced lifters.

[05:36] **Recovery and the repeated bout effect.** Novel training produces high muscle damage; repeating the same routine week after week produces progressively less, via the repeated bout effect. Switching exercises every session means you keep re-triggering the novel-stimulus response, so recovery windows stay longer indefinitely. He notes this wasn't a problem in the study — but the study used an **upper/lower split with 72 hours between hits of the same muscle**. Whether a variation group could recover on a full-body split with 48 hours or less is untested. He also links this to volume: progressively adding weekly sets is one route past plateaus, and that's harder to tolerate when recovery demands never fall.

[07:41] **Tracking.** With fixed exercises, adding load or reps is direct proof of progress, and *failing* to add them is a signal to reassess. With random exercises you can still train close to failure and get a stimulus, but you have no week-to-week signal at all — and no way to detect a plateau when you hit one.

## What it means for our position
This is the one direct test of random exercise swapping, and on the outcome that matters — hypertrophy over 8 weeks — it came back null, not negative. Our library's "random swapping is the number one cause of lifetime intermediates" position should be read as an argument about **progression and skill**, not about the per-session stimulus. Dhim reaches the same place by a different road: he doesn't claim the muscles grow less, he claims you lose the ability to steer.

**Overreach flag:** the recovery argument is the weakest link — the repeated bout effect is real, but the claim that variation training "likely always requires longer recovery" is inferred from mechanism, and the single study that measured outcomes found no recovery penalty.
