# Three New Studies: Unilateral vs Bilateral, Heavy vs Light, Squats vs Leg Extensions

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Unilateral vs Bilateral Training for Muscle Growth" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6lsCo8B-mPI)) · 2026-01-19 · 14 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **Unilateral training is not superior for hypertrophy.** 54 untrained women, dumbbell curls, 8 weeks: elbow flexor thickness gains were not significantly different between unilateral and bilateral groups, despite the "small muscle mass" hypothesis predicting a unilateral advantage.
- **8–12 reps and 20–25 reps grew muscle equally** in a within-subject trial, including at the **fibre level** — no support for the idea that light loads preferentially build slow-twitch fibres and heavy loads fast-twitch.
- People who grow their **arms** well tend to grow their **legs** well (though ~50% of the variance was unexplained), and people who grow well with heavy loads tend to grow well with light loads.
- **Back squats vs leg extensions:** leg extensions grew the **rectus femoris** more at all three regions; back squats grew the **vastus lateralis** more at the distal region only. Consistent with the wider quad literature.
- The squat/leg-press result is itself evidence **against** the small-muscle-mass hypothesis: those movements train far more muscle mass than leg extensions yet are not inferior for the vastus heads.

## Part I — Unilateral vs bilateral ([Kassiano 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41359274/))
The hypothesis under test: exercises training a **smaller** amount of muscle mass might be better for hypertrophy. The authors' stated reasons were the **bilateral force deficit** (force produced when both limbs contract simultaneously is lower than the summed force of each contracting alone), possibly reflecting greater neural drive and fibre recruitment in unilateral work; and lower cardiovascular demand permitting greater local muscle fatigue. They predicted unilateral would win.

Design: **54 untrained women**, 3×/week for 8 weeks. Bilateral group did bilateral dumbbell curls; unilateral group trained one arm then the other. Both did **2 sets of 8–12 reps to concentric task failure**, loads adjusted to keep failure inside the range. The unilateral group alternated which arm went first each session. Growth measured as thickness at the **60% and 70%** regions of the elbow flexors (biceps plus brachialis).

Results:
- **Strength:** bilateral 1RM gains not significantly different. Unilateral **right** arm 1RM gains significantly greater for the unilateral group; left arm favoured them but not significantly. Likely explained by load: in session one the bilateral group used slightly *more* load per dumbbell (i.e. **no apparent bilateral deficit between groups**), but by the last session the unilateral group was using slightly heavier loads.
- **Growth:** thickness changes at both regions **not significantly different**.

He immediately narrows the claim: one study, untrained women, one exercise. The authors themselves note that unilateral curls involve half the muscle mass of bilateral curls but the **absolute** difference is small because the elbow flexors are small — so a difference might still emerge between exercises with a larger muscle-mass gap.

His own view is skeptical of the hypothesis regardless, citing other unilateral-vs-bilateral studies that also find similar growth, and the [Plotkin 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37461495/) comparison where **back squats and hip thrusts produced similar gluteus maximus hypertrophy** despite squats training much more muscle mass. Verdict: unilateral vs bilateral is not a critical hypertrophy consideration. Choose on preference and goals — unilateral takes longer, but may help with size imbalances.

## Part II — Heavy vs light, and individual response ([Lees 2025](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/JP289684))
**20 previously untrained men**, 3×/week for 10 weeks. One arm and one leg trained **heavy**, the contralateral arm and leg **light**. Exercises: unilateral leg extension and unilateral dumbbell preacher curl, 3 sets each per session. Heavy side did **8–12 reps**, light side **20–25 reps**, all to volitional failure, loads adjusted to hold the rep ranges.

Measures were unusually thorough: 1RM on both exercises, isometric knee extension and elbow flexion strength, thickness and CSA of biceps and vastus lateralis, fat- and bone-free lean mass, and **muscle fibre CSA** from vastus lateralis biopsies.

- **Strength:** no significant differences on any measure. He explains rather than accepts this — the within-subject design is confounded for strength by **cross-education** (strength transfers across limbs; **muscle growth does not**, which is why the design is appropriate for hypertrophy). The broader literature does show heavier loads producing greater strength gains.
- **Growth:** no significant differences on any measure. Consistent with the meta-analytic picture ([Lopez 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/), [Grgic 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33312275/)) and with trained-subject research: **provided you train to or near failure, a wide range of loads and rep numbers produce similar hypertrophy.**
- **Fibre type:** both slow- and fast-twitch fibre size increases were not significantly different between conditions. This is the specific rebuttal to the long-standing claim that light/high-rep work preferentially builds slow-twitch fibres and heavy/low-rep work fast-twitch. A couple of other studies agree.
- **Individual response:** ranking subjects on arm growth and leg growth showed a **correlation** — high leg responders tended to be high arm responders — but roughly **50% of variance remained unexplained**, and this is one study with n=20 measuring largely just biceps and vastus lateralis. Ranking responsiveness to heavy vs light showed people who grew well with one generally grew well with the other, though a few data points suggested individual load preferences.

**Overreach flag:** he is careful here, but "there's no superior rep range for hypertrophy" is being carried by studies that mostly compare moderate to high reps in untrained people. His own [4 vs 8 vs 12 note](4-vs-8-vs-12-reps-strength.md) is more precise: **6–35 reps** are similar per set, and **3–5 reps** may need extra sets to match.

## Part III — Back squats vs leg extensions ([Kassiano 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41379528/))
**63 untrained women**, 2×/week for 8 weeks. One group Smith machine back squats, the other machine leg extensions, both **3 sets of 8–12 reps to momentary failure**. Strength via 3RM on both movements; growth via rectus femoris and vastus lateralis thickness at **three regions**.

- **Strength:** back squat gains greater for the squat group, as expected. **Leg extension strength gains were not significantly different** — suggesting carryover from squats to leg extensions is better than the reverse.
- **Growth:** rectus femoris growth greater for leg extensions at **all three** regions. Vastus lateralis growth similar at proximal and middle regions, but **greater for squats at the distal region**.

The mechanism is well-argued and not merely mechanistic hand-waving: the **rectus femoris crosses two joints** (knee and hip), acting as a knee extensor and hip flexor. In a squat, knee extension and hip extension occur simultaneously, so a strongly contracting rectus femoris would partly oppose hip extension — hence the nervous system minimises its involvement. Isolated knee extension engages it properly and is documented to grow it. He extends this to **reverse Nordics and sissy squats** as likely candidates, flagging that direct data on those is minimal.

The pattern holds in trained subjects too: a leg press vs leg extension comparison ([Burke 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42978-024-00299-4)) found **greater rectus femoris growth with leg extensions** and **greater vastus lateralis growth with leg presses across all three regions**.

He closes the loop back to Part I: squats and leg presses train more muscle mass than leg extensions and are **not inferior** for the vastus heads, which is further evidence against small-muscle-mass exercises being superior.
