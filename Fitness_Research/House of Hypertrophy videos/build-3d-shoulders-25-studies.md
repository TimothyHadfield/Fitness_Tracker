# Side Delts: What Leverage Data Says About Presses, Lateral Raises and Upright Rows

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How to Build 3D Shoulders (25 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PNNqyt9Yu6A)) · 2023-06-05 · 22 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **No study directly compares exercises for side delt hypertrophy.** Everything is inferred from moment-arm data via the principle of neuromechanical matching — the nervous system preferentially recruits the muscles with the best leverage for a movement.
- **Narrow-grip vertical presses are mostly a front delt exercise**: shoulder flexion, where the front delt's leverage is typically **three to four times** the side delt's. Wide-grip presses (or dumbbells at either side of the head) shift to shoulder abduction, where front and side delts have **comparable** leverage — these are good for side delts.
- **Bench presses and rows do very little for side delts.** In horizontal flexion the side delts have **no leverage at all**; in horizontal extension the rear delts have up to **three times** the side delts' leverage.
- **Lateral raises** are where side delts have the best leverage of any muscle. Supraspinatus dominates 0–30°, side delts dominate 31–90°, and beyond 90° front delt involvement rises. Frontal vs scapular plane makes essentially no difference to leverage.
- **Upright rows are probably very good for side delts**, and he spends four minutes demolishing the impingement argument against them — five separate lines of evidence, ending with a 2021 paper calling the impingement theory antiquated.
- Loading advice is deliberately loose: **6–35 reps to or near failure are similarly effective**, you can progress by adding reps rather than load, and rep tempos from 0.5 to 8 seconds work similarly.

## The tool
[02:03] The whole video runs on **neuromechanical matching**: given several muscles that can produce a movement, the brain and spinal cord preferentially activate the ones with the longest moment arms. Moment arm is the perpendicular distance between a muscle's line of force and the centre of the joint it moves; the door-and-hinge analogy carries the explanation. So the muscle with the best leverage in a given exercise is the one most highly activated, and exercise selection becomes a question of which joint motion you are producing.

He also opens with the anatomy that recurs across his shoulder videos: combining all three heads, **the deltoid is the largest muscle by volume in the upper body**, larger than pectoralis major or latissimus dorsi. And it divides into **seven segments** rather than three — first documented in a text from **1734**, and reproduced in modern studies using origin/insertion or functional criteria. Brown's analysis maps them by distinct origins and insertions; the working convention is **D1 = front head, D2–D3 = side head, D4–D7 = rear head**.

## Compounds: what each press and pull actually does
[04:04] **Vertical presses, narrow to moderate grip** (overhead press, handstand push-ups, machine press) produce predominantly **shoulder flexion** at higher angles. Both side delt segments have leverage for flexion, but the **front delt's leverage is generally three to four times greater**. Following neuromechanical matching, side delts are activated somewhat but probably not enough to substantially drive their hypertrophy — the front delt takes the bulk.

[05:37] **Vertical presses, wide grip or dumbbells at either side of the head** produce predominantly **shoulder abduction** at high angles. Here front and side delts have **fairly comparable** leverage. These should deliver good side delt gains as well as front delt gains. But he speculates they are still not enough to *maximise* side delt hypertrophy, because for that you want an exercise where the side delts have the **greatest** leverage for a single-joint movement.

[06:38] **Horizontal presses** (bench, push-ups, machine chest press) involve some shoulder flexion, so side delts get a trivial stimulus, and shoulder **horizontal flexion**, where pectoralis major dominates and the **side delts have no leverage whatsoever** — they have leverage for the *opposite* motion.

[07:09] **Horizontal pulls** (barbell rows, cable rows, inverted pull-ups) with a wide grip produce shoulder horizontal extension, so the side delts are trained — but the **rear delts have up to three times the leverage** for that motion, so notable side delt hypertrophy is unlikely. Narrow-grip rows produce shoulder extension, for which the side delts have **no leverage at all**.

## Lateral raises
[08:41] In both frontal and scapular planes:

| Abduction range | Best leverage |
|---|---|
| 0–30° | Supraspinatus, with side delts close behind, then front delts |
| 31–90° | **Side delts**, supraspinatus dropping off rapidly, front delts fairly close behind |
| >90° | Side and especially front delts (particularly in the scapular plane) rising; supraspinatus negligible |

Infraspinatus and teres minor have much smaller leverage throughout.

Strictly, this suggests **30–90° is the best window for isolating the side delts**. His practical call is that 0–90° is perfectly fine for most people, since the exercise is still challenging above 30° where the side delts have the best leverage. **Frontal vs scapular plane makes little difference** to the leverages; he notes many people simply report the scapular plane feeling better, and says to experiment. He is honest that he knows of **no data** comparing trapezius involvement between lateral raise variations.

[11:13] **Arm rotation.** Internally rotating likely recruits the rear delts more, externally rotating the front delts — supported by a 2020 EMG analysis. He notes he is normally critical of surface EMG but argues that using it to compare **subtle changes within the same exercise** may be more reliable than comparing different exercises. Recommendation: stay fairly neutral if the goal is maximum side delt growth.

[11:44] **Hip and elbow bend.** He is unaware of research either way. He does argue that excessive elbow bend likely increases front delt recruitment, so avoiding it is sensible.

[12:14] **Equipment and resistance curves.** Dumbbells, machines and cables all produce shoulder abduction and are all good. The one plausible difference is the resistance profile: dumbbell lateral raises provide minimal tension in the initial (stretched) range, while cables load more consistently throughout, including at the stretched position. Since the literature suggests exercises training muscles in a stretched position build muscle faster, **cable lateral raises could be slightly better** — but he flags that there are **no studies demonstrating this for the delts**, and pushes back on those who have argued from side delt sarcomere operating range, saying that literature has enough limitations that he doesn't find it useful for predicting whether a muscle benefits from stretch.

He offers a practical hedge: with dumbbells you can do **partial reps in the initial position**, citing Pedrosa's leg extension study where a partial in the lengthened position beat full ROM for quadriceps growth. Or do full reps to failure, then continue with partials in the initial range. Otherwise, pick whichever variation you prefer and vary it across training cycles.

[15:19] **Loading.** Lateral raises are never heavy, and chasing load produces momentum and poor technique. Three points he backs with literature: **6–35 reps to or near failure are similarly effective**; you can progressively overload by **adding reps at the same load** rather than adding load (2022 US study, 8 weeks, rep-progression as effective as load-progression); and rep tempos anywhere in the **0.5–8 second** range work similarly, so slow controlled reps with a light load are a valid alternative to higher rep ranges.

## Upright rows and the impingement argument
[17:23] The upright row involves shoulder abduction to roughly 90° or slightly more, so it should be effective for side delts. **No study compares it to lateral raises for side delt hypertrophy.** He speculates that its significant scapular elevation, which heavily recruits the traps, may make it slightly less effective — labelled speculative — and says if forced to pick one he'd take the lateral raise, while noting that in practice you can train a muscle with multiple exercises.

[18:24] The safety section is the most substantive part of the video, and he credits E3 Rehab for the underlying summary. The impingement claim is that abduction with internal rotation narrows the subacromial space, compressing the supraspinatus tendon against the acromion. Five counter-arguments:

1. **Wrong surface.** Compression from above should fray the **upper** side of the supraspinatus tendon; multiple analyses find damage predominantly on the **underside**.
2. **Acromion shape doesn't predict problems.** People with hooked acromions should have more shoulder problems than flat or curved; the data doesn't support it. Nor are smaller acromiohumeral distances associated with pain in those with subacromial pain.
3. **The 90° advice is backwards.** The common recommendation (from a 2011 paper) to stay below 90° of abduction appears misguided — the **smallest** distance between the supraspinatus tendon and the coracoacromial arch occurs between **40 and 75°** in most people, and at 90° the tendon appears no longer positioned to be compressed. He draws the reductio: if impingement were the danger, you'd have to avoid abducting past 40° entirely, which would make **all lateral raises** dangerous.
4. **Internal rotation doesn't narrow the space.** The same paper indicates internal rotation **increases or has no effect** on acromiohumeral distance.
5. **The Hawkins–Kennedy test** — often invoked because upright rows mimic it — has poor specificity and has been described as having little diagnostic value.
6. **Surgery doesn't help.** Subacromial decompression, which shaves the acromion to increase the space, fails to produce clinically important benefits over **placebo surgery** for pain, function or quality of life across a solid body of literature including a Cochrane review.

He closes with a 2021 paper describing the impingement theory as antiquated and recommending the term be abandoned. His conclusion: no strong evidence that upright rows are dangerous, and compelling reason to doubt the impingement theory as a whole. This is the best-sourced argument in the video and the only section resting on outcome data rather than modelling.

## Where this lands
The programming implication is modest and clearly stated: many compounds train the side delts, wide-grip vertical presses and upright rows most usefully among them, but **some form of lateral raise is helpful if you want to maximise side delt hypertrophy**, and which lateral raise variation you pick probably doesn't matter much.

**Overreach flag:** the entire exercise hierarchy is built from cadaveric and modelling moment-arm data plus one EMG study — there is not a single side delt hypertrophy outcome trial anywhere in it, and the cable-vs-dumbbell stretch argument extrapolates from quadriceps ROM research to a muscle for which he concedes no data exists. (Notably, a later video of his reports a trial comparing cable and dumbbell lateral raises in trained lifters finding **similar** side delt growth — so the stretch speculation here did not survive testing.)
