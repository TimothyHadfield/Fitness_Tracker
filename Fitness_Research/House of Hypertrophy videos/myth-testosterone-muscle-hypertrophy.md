# Natural Testosterone Levels Don't Predict Who Builds More Muscle

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The MYTH of Testosterone and Muscle Hypertrophy" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cAB49XDIJ4Q)) · 2023-02-14 · 8 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Across three training studies, **resting testosterone within the normal range did not correlate with hypertrophy** — higher-testosterone lifters did not grow more.
- Sorting subjects into low, moderate and high responders produced **no testosterone difference between the groups**.
- **Intramuscular** free testosterone also failed to separate high from low responders — the mechanism's best hiding place checked and empty.
- Most subjects saw **no rise in testosterone** from training, consistent with the wider literature.
- The critical caveat, which he states explicitly: this is **between-person** data. It cannot tell you what happens if you raise one individual's levels. That question is deferred to a follow-up video.

## The three datasets
**McCall et al. (1999)** trained 11 young trained men for 12 weeks and measured whole biceps cross-sectional area plus slow- and fast-twitch fibre sizes. Resting testosterone did not change over the 12 weeks (mean ~580 ng/dL, SD ~172, so roughly 68% of subjects between 346 and 690 ng/dL). **No significant correlation** appeared between resting testosterone and any of the three growth measures. He immediately names the weakness — n = 11.

**Morton et al. (2018)**, from Canada, recruited **49 trained men**, measured lean mass and vastus lateralis fibre sizes, and ran backward elimination regression across a panel of resting hormones. No hormone predicted hypertrophy, testosterone and free testosterone included, and the coefficients of determination were low — very little of the growth variance was explained by resting hormone concentrations. The researchers then split subjects into high and low responders and measured **intramuscular free testosterone**: no difference between them.

**Mobley et al. (2018)** trained **67 untrained young men** and sorted them into low, moderate and high responders by vastus lateralis growth. Mean testosterone before and after training was comparable across the three tiers. Dhim makes a point of the **individual data points** overlaid on the bars: one high responder had relatively low testosterone, one low responder had quite high levels. Every tier contained the full spread.

## Two side observations from the Mobley data
Most subjects showed no training-induced testosterone change — he notes this matches the majority of the literature. A handful did move, one going from ~750 to above 1,500 ng/dL, which he flags as possibly measurement error and confounded by sleep and nutrition regardless. That subject was still a low responder. A different subject sat near 2,000 ng/dL in a supposedly natural cohort; he allows both explanations — undeclared use, or a genuine anomaly — without picking one.

## The distinction he refuses to blur
The closing section is the most careful part of the video. He restates that all three studies are cross-sectional comparisons **between** people, so the defensible claim is that testosterone is not the primary determinant of hypertrophy **across** people. Whether moving one person from 400 to 800 ng/dL would change their response is a different question requiring intervention data, which he says will be the next video. This is a good example of the channel distinguishing what a design can and cannot support rather than stretching a correlational null into a universal claim.
