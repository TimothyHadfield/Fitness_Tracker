# Can Lifting Replace Cardio? Mostly No

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Lifting CAN Replace Cardio (True or False) ?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUf1GZl6KqY)) · 2023-02-06 · 10 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Lifting **does** produce real cardiovascular adaptations — increased capillary and mitochondrial density, and eccentric cardiac hypertrophy is not uncommon in bodybuilders. The "lifting isn't cardio at all" position is wrong.
- But it is **inferior to actual cardio**. In a head-to-head, HIIT performed with squats and deadlifts raised VO2max less than HIIT cycling, despite matched volume and intensity.
- The people who gain the most VO2max from lifting are **the ones who started with the worst VO2max** — so a trained lifter should expect little.
- **Circuit training** is the lifting style most likely to deliver cardio adaptations, and one small study suggests it does so **without costing strength or lean mass**.
- Adding dedicated cardio is worth it on health grounds alone: resistance training alone was associated with **21% lower all-cause mortality risk**, resistance training **plus** cardio with **40%**.

## HIIT lifting vs HIIT cycling
Androulakis-Korakakis (2018) took **16 well-trained powerlifters and strongmen** and added two weekly HIIT sessions on top of their normal hypertrophy/general-strength lifting, in one of two modes:

- **HIIT cycling:** seven 30-second sprints at RPE 8–9, 90 seconds recovery.
- **HIIT lifting:** seven sets at RPE 8–9 using **60% 1RM**, 90 seconds rest — squat in one session, deadlift in the other. Sets averaged **8–15 reps lasting roughly 16–30 seconds**, so the work intervals were genuinely comparable in duration to the sprints.

After **8 weeks**: leg extension strength gains were similar between groups, so neither protocol interfered with strength. Both groups raised VO2max — but **the cycling group raised it more**.

His explanation: most lifting **doesn't involve constant effort**. The eccentric is easier than the concentric, and there are pauses between reps, whereas cycling demands continuous exertion. Mechanism, not measured, but plausible.

A practical caveat he adds: squats and deadlifts are complex, and these subjects were powerlifters and strongmen. **Less trained people should use machine or dumbbell variations**, with higher rep numbers to compensate for the lower muscle mass involved.

## What cardiovascular adaptations lifting does produce
Not always consistently found, but resistance training can increase **capillary density** (the vessels delivering oxygen, nutrients and hormones to cells) and **mitochondrial density** — both hallmark endurance adaptations. **Eccentric cardiac hypertrophy**, prominent in endurance athletes and beneficial to cardiac capacity, is also not uncommon in bodybuilders.

Not all lifting is equal here. He argues styles that **prolong set duration and shorten rest** should enhance these adaptations, citing a 2019 study in which training at **30% 1RM** raised the content of several mitochondrial proteins more effectively than **80% 1RM**.

**Note the tension he doesn't address:** the Holloway (2018) paper in his own reference list found microvascular adaptations to be **independent of load** in resistance-trained men. The load-dependence claim holds for mitochondrial protein, not obviously for capillarisation, and the video presents it as a general rule.

## Circuit training
A 2017 Spanish meta-analysis pooled multiple studies and found **circuit training produces notable VO2max increases** — unsurprising given short rests and continuous work.

Does it cost hypertrophy or strength? Minimal data, but Alcaraz (2011) assigned **26 trained men** to circuit or traditional training, 3× weekly for **8 weeks**:

- **Traditional:** all 3–6 sets of one exercise before moving on, 3 minutes rest.
- **Circuit:** one set of each exercise with **35 seconds** rest between exercises to complete a round; 3–6 rounds; 5 minutes rest, then a second circuit done the same way.

Bench press and half-squat strength gains were **similar**. Lean body mass gains were **similar**. He states the caveats himself: one study, and **lean body mass is not a precise measure of muscle hypertrophy**.

## So can lifting replace cardio?
His answer is no, and the reasoning is careful:

1. Lifting produces cardiovascular adaptations, but **not to the magnitude of standard cardio**.
2. The biggest VO2max responders to lifting are simply **those with the lowest baseline VO2max** — an analysis he describes as a 2013 Japanese paper. *(This study does not appear in the description's reference list, so it is unverified here.)*
3. **Long-duration continuous cardio** produces adaptations likely unattainable by lifting or even HIIT — specifically mitochondrial efficiency and respiration, and substantial capillary density increases.

## Don't fear cardio
He points back to earlier videos: the interference effect is **largely overblown** unless you are highly trained and/or running very high resistance training volumes, and updated analyses fail to find that adding cardio impairs muscle and strength gains.

The mortality data is the strongest practical argument for adding it: **21%** reduced all-cause mortality risk for resistance training alone versus **40%** for resistance training plus cardio (Saeidifard 2019 — meta-analysis of observational data, so association, not causation).

## The useful inversion
The closing point is the most actionable thing in the video and is easy to miss: **local endurance adaptations — mitochondrial and capillary — are largely specific to the muscles trained**. If your cardio is cycling or running, your upper body gets none of it. Using resistance training deliberately to build local cardiovascular adaptations in muscles your cardio doesn't reach is a sound idea.
