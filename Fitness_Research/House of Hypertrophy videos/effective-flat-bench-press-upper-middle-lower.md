# Flat Bench Press and the Three Pec Regions: Relative vs Absolute Growth

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How Effective Is the Flat Bench Press for Upper, Middle, and Lower Pec Hypertrophy?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i2vPxQsHKXU)) · 2022-01-31 · 10 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- Two training studies both found the flat barbell bench press grows the **upper, middle and lower pec near-equally in percentage terms**. It is not an exercise that skips the upper chest.
- But in **absolute** terms the upper region gains roughly **half** what the middle and lower regions gain — because it starts about **half the size**. Same relative stimulus, smaller starting number, smaller millimetre gain.
- That, not neglect, is the likeliest reason so many lifters feel their upper chest lags despite years of flat benching.
- If you want the upper region to match the others in absolute size, the fix is adding exercises with **more shoulder flexion** (incline barbell/dumbbell press, feet-elevated push-ups, low-to-high cable fly) — a biomechanical argument, not a tested one.

## What counts as "the chest" here
Several muscles connect the thorax to the shoulder and upper arm — pec major, pec minor, subclavius, serratus anterior — but the research measures only **pectoralis major**, which is also the bulk of what people mean by "chest". Pec major has many origins (medial clavicle, sternum, ribs, costal cartilage, the aponeurosis of the external oblique) but they converge onto **one insertion** on the lateral upper arm.

There is no universally agreed way to subdivide it. Some anatomists give it three heads (clavicular, sternal, costal), others two (clavicular and sternocostal). He sidesteps the argument and uses plain **upper / middle / lower regions** throughout, which is the sensible call given the studies measure by position along the muscle rather than by named head.

## Study 1 — Ogasawara: untrained men, 24 weeks
Previously untrained men benched with a **two-times-shoulder-width grip**, 3 sets of 10 at **75% 1RM**, 2–3 minutes rest, **3× weekly for 24 weeks**. 1RM was retested every 3 weeks so the 75% load stayed honest — an unusually well-controlled progression for a study this long.

Cross-sectional area was measured at 25% (upper), 50% (middle) and 75% (lower) of muscle length:

| Region | CSA change |
|---|---|
| Upper | +36.3% |
| Middle | +37.3% |
| Lower | +40.0% |

Near-equal relative growth across all three.

## Study 2 — Davies: trained men and women, 8 weeks
Five men and five women with training experience; about 68% had a bench 1RM between 35 kg and 93 kg. Four sets of five at **85% 1RM**, **5 minutes** rest, twice weekly for 8 weeks, 1RM retested every 2 weeks. **Grip width was not specified** — he flags this as a limitation rather than glossing it.

Muscle **thickness** measured between ribs 2–3 (upper), 3–4 (middle), 4–5 (lower):

| Region | Thickness change |
|---|---|
| Upper | +7.44% |
| Middle | +10.06% |
| Lower | +7.45% |

He anticipates the obvious objection — why are these numbers so much smaller than Ogasawara's? — and answers it properly: **untrained vs trained subjects, 24 weeks vs 8, different loading schemes, and CSA (two dimensions) vs thickness (one)**. This is the kind of care that makes the channel worth reading. Note that this study is a **university thesis, not a peer-reviewed journal paper**, and n=10.

## The relative/absolute distinction — the actual point of the video
This is the part worth taking away. Going back into Ogasawara's raw numbers:

- **Baseline CSA:** middle and lower regions ~25–27 cm² each. Upper region ~**15 cm²**.
- **Absolute gain:** middle and lower ~**10 cm²** each. Upper only **5.5 cm²**.

Identical percentage gains, roughly double the absolute gain in the bigger regions — purely arithmetic. A smaller starting number times the same growth rate yields a smaller increment. The Davies thickness data shows the same pattern.

So the "underdeveloped upper chest" most lifters perceive is, on this evidence, **an upper region that was proportionally smaller before training and stays proportionally smaller after it**, despite receiving an equal relative stimulus from flat benching.

## If you want to change that
To make the upper region catch up in absolute size you need exercises that preferentially load it. His candidates: **incline barbell bench, incline dumbbell bench, feet-elevated push-ups, low cable fly**. The reasoning: all pec regions perform horizontal flexion (which flat bench already trains), but these four involve substantially more **shoulder flexion**, and the upper region is thought to assist the anterior deltoid in shoulder flexion.

**Overreach flag:** this recommendation rests entirely on moment-arm anatomy (the Ackland data), not on any study comparing regional growth from these exercises against flat bench. It is a reasonable hypothesis presented as the practical conclusion, and he does not label it as untested.
