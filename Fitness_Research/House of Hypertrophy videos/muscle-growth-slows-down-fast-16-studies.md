# Does Muscle Growth Really Plateau Within Three Months?

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Muscle Growth SLOWS DOWN Fast? (16 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IvoQ2VnFiU0)) · 2022-08-22 · 14 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- A 2017 review (Counts et al.) argued from time-course studies that **most of a person's lower-body growth potential is reached in the first three months**, and that upper-body size plateaus roughly 3–4 weeks after the first detectable change (i.e. around weeks 7–10). Dhim disagrees with the conclusion but refuses to dismiss the data.
- Four studies do show early plateaus — quads levelling off at **6–8 weeks**, vastus lateralis growing well from week 0–3 then barely from 3–6. Progressive overload was applied in all of them, so lack of overload is not the explanation.
- Two studies show the opposite: isometric biceps training with **no sign of plateau across 100 days**, and quadriceps growth rising consistently across **six months**.
- His reinterpretation: the plateau studies show that an untrained person can grow well for ~3 months *while ignoring everything outside the gym*. Beyond that, nutrition and training variation become progressively more important. All the plateau studies gave no nutritional instruction and held every training variable except load constant, usually with **one exercise per muscle**.
- Four levers he offers for continued gains: adequate calories and protein, exercise variation, rep-range variation, and **modest (~20%) increases in weekly sets** — plus training breaks as a resensitising strategy.

## The case for a fast plateau
[01:33] A 1999 Japanese study (Abe et al.), 37 untrained subjects: a significant drop-off in quadriceps development by week 8 in both sexes, with men also plateauing in biceps and triceps by week 8. A 2011 US study (DeFreitas et al.) of untrained men on leg press and leg extension found thigh growth levelling off after **six weeks**. A 2013 Brazilian study (Baroni et al.) using eccentric leg extensions found rectus femoris growth levelling at **eight weeks**. A 2015 English study (Brook et al.) found the vastus lateralis grew well from week 0 to 3 and then hardly at all from week 3 to 6.

[03:05] He immediately presents the contradicting evidence: a 1970 Japanese study (Ikai & Fukunaga) of maximal isometric biceps contractions found no plateau across 100 days, and a 1996 Swiss study (Narici et al.) found quadriceps growth increasing consistently across six months. His stated principle is worth recording — the plateau results are surprising, "but I don't believe we should discard or ignore them."

## Limitation 1: nothing outside the gym was controlled
[04:37] None of the plateau studies mention nutrition, and subjects were untrained with no dietary instruction. He cites a 2002 US study (Rozenek et al.) where eight weeks on a **2,000-calorie surplus** produced ~3 kg of body mass, virtually all fat-free mass, versus much smaller changes at maintenance; and a 2019 Brazilian study (Ribeiro et al.) in competitive bodybuilders where ~**6,500 kcal** beat ~**4,500 kcal**. Morton's 2018 meta-analysis of **49 studies** found intakes up to **1.6 g/kg** enhanced fat-free mass gains.

The inference: untrained people may grow well for three months *without* dialling anything in, and appreciable growth after that requires the outside-the-gym variables. That is an interpretation, not a tested hypothesis — no study here compared plateau rates under optimised versus unoptimised nutrition.

## Limitation 2: training variables never changed
[06:38] Every plateau study used one largely unchanging exercise per muscle, one rep range, one set count.

**Exercise variation.** A 2021 Brazilian study (Costa et al.) compared a non-varied group doing the same exercises across three weekly sessions against a varied group doing different exercises each day. After eight weeks, regional growth of rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, elbow flexors and triceps was similar in some regions and favoured the varied group in others. His claim is appropriately narrow: variety grows **more regions**, not necessarily more total muscle.

**Rep-range variation.** Studies of 12 weeks or less find no difference between one rep range and mixed ranges. One **nine-month** study (Kraemer et al.) in untrained women found greater fat-free mass gains in the varying group. He flags it as a single study needing replication.

**Volume.** Aube et al. studied trained men (average back squat ~165 kg) across a range of weekly set counts: those who grew the anterior thigh most had added on average **six more weekly quad sets** than their pre-study habit, low responders only **one to four**. A 2022 Brazilian study (Scarpelli et al.) used a within-subject unilateral design over eight weeks — one leg did **20% more weekly sets than the subject's own habit**, the other a prescribed **22 weekly sets**. Vastus lateralis growth favoured the +20% leg. The telling detail: for **8 of 16 subjects** the prescribed 22 sets was a *larger* jump (30–120% above habitual) than their +20% leg, and results still favoured +20%. Bigger jumps were not better than modest ones.

## Training breaks
[11:12] An Ogasawara study from Japan compared 24 weeks of continuous bench training against a periodic group training 6 weeks / resting 3 weeks, repeated to 24 weeks. Triceps and pectoralis major growth and bench 1RM gains were **similar** — the continuous group's rate of gain gradually slowed, while the periodic group, despite losing muscle and strength during each break, regained faster during training blocks. He states plainly that more research is needed before recommending it.

## Verdict and where it overreaches
[12:48] He rejects the Counts et al. conclusion: with a well-designed program and lifestyle, appreciable growth over many years is likely. The soft spot is that his four remedies rest on thin evidence — one nine-month study, one regional-growth study, two volume studies, one break study — and none directly tests whether they extend the growth curve past the three-month window. He flags each limitation himself. The closing appeal to real-world observation is the weakest step, since drug use, measurement and selection effects all confound it.
