# Maxing Out Builds Strength Without Building Muscle

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Is Maxing Out Effective for Building Muscle? (One-Rep Max Training)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQ-JcDrztj8)) · 2021-02-15 · 6 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- Two studies point the same way: **singles alone build strength but essentially no muscle**.
- In a 21-day within-subject arm study, the 1RM-only arm gained strength equal to the arm that also did volume — but showed **virtually zero** growth in elbow flexor thickness.
- In an 8-week study, a group that only worked up to 1RMs twice weekly matched the hypertrophy group on **strength**, while thigh thickness **flatlined or slightly decreased**.
- The clean interpretation: 1RM training's strength gains come from **neural adaptation and motor skill**, not tissue.
- For growth, roughly **5–35 reps (about 85% down to 30% 1RM)** taken to failure work similarly. Singles sit below that floor.

## Dankel: 21 consecutive days, one arm each way
[00:30] Five men with at least a year of training experience trained **both arms daily for 21 straight days**. Both arms worked up to a **1RM on the unilateral dumbbell curl** (back and shoulders against a wall to kill momentum) plus two 3-second maximal isometric contractions at 60° elbow flexion on a dynamometer. The **volume arm** additionally did **3x10 at 70% 1RM**.

Elbow flexor thickness was measured at 50%, 60% and 70% of upper-arm length on days 8, 15, 21 and three days after the final session, alongside 1RM and isometric strength.

**Strength rose equally in both arms.** Thickness favoured the volume arm at every site, and Dhim points out from the data that the 1RM arm grew **virtually not at all**. The added 3x10 was what produced growth.

## Mattocks: 8 weeks, rest days, untrained subjects
[03:02] 17 men and 21 women, all untrained, assigned to a **testing group** or a **hypertrophy group**, training unilateral knee extension (both legs) and chest press **2x/week for 8 weeks**. The hypertrophy group did **4 sets to failure with an 8–12RM load**, 90 s rest. The testing group simply warmed up and worked to a **1RM** each session, 90 s between attempts.

**1RM gains on knee extension and chest press were statistically similar between groups.** Anterior and lateral thigh thickness favoured the hypertrophy group at every measurement site — though Dhim is careful to state that on the lateral thigh (and at 60% and 70% of the dominant anterior thigh) those differences did **not** reach statistical significance. He then makes the point that matters more than the p-values: the testing group's before-and-after values show **no increases and in several cases slight decreases**, while the hypertrophy group increased across virtually every measure.

## Reading it
[05:05] Together these separate strength from size cleanly. Practising the test makes you better at the test; it does not supply the mechanical work that drives growth. This does not condemn heavy training generally — Dhim closes by noting there is research on **heavy sets of 2–4** for hypertrophy, which he sets aside for a separate video. The claim is specifically about singles.

## Where the evidence is thin
Dankel is **n=5** over 21 days, and Mattocks used untrained subjects on two machine exercises — a narrow base for a general rule, though the two designs fail in the same direction, which is what makes the conclusion hold. The "5–35 reps to failure" figure is his summary of the wider literature rather than a finding from either study cited here.
