# Push-Ups vs the Bench Press: Two Trials, Three EMG Studies, and the Overload Problem

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Push Ups vs Bench Press for Muscle Hypertrophy - ULTIMATE Comparison (8 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e0PY-4Ex_ss)) · 2021-11-01 · 25 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **Load-equated, the push-up and the bench press appear to grow the same muscles by the same amount.** Two longitudinal trials, three EMG studies and near-identical biomechanics all point the same way, and no study found the bench meaningfully superior.
- The evidence base is thin: **two training trials, n = 18 and n = 23, lasting 8 and 4 weeks**. That is enough to say the bench is not obviously better; it is not enough to say the two are equivalent.
- **The real difference is progressive overload, not stimulus.** Adding load to a bar is trivial; adding load to your own body weight is not. He treats this as the only defensible reason to prefer the bench long-term.
- The overload ceiling is quantifiable. Growth appears to hold from **30% to 80% of 1RM** provided reps go to within 3 of failure, and 30% 1RM is roughly a **35-rep** set. A regular push-up loads about **64% of body weight**, so once you can do about 35 clean reps of it you are pushing against the bottom of the useful load range.
- **Weighted push-ups are his preferred solution**, ahead of harder variations, because harder variations redistribute load between chest, front delts and triceps in ways you did not choose. Feet-elevated shifts toward upper chest and front delts; single-arm variants are triceps-dominant and may under-load the chest.
- Push-ups beat the bench on two muscles the bench cannot train: **rectus abdominis and external oblique** (plank position) and the **serratus anterior** (free scapular protraction, which a bench press deliberately prevents).

## The two training studies

**Kikuchi and Nakazato (2017)** is the cleaner of the two. 18 young men with at least a year of resistance training were assigned to a bench press or a push-up group. Both trained **twice a week for 8 weeks**, both did **3 sets of reps to failure with 2 minutes rest**, and both worked at a resistance equivalent to **40% of bench press 1RM** — for the push-up group that meant a regular push-up for some subjects and a kneeling variation for others, individually matched. Pectoralis major thickness was measured between the 3rd and 4th ribs; triceps and biceps at about 60% of upper arm bone length.

Result: **pectoralis major and triceps thickness increases were similar between groups**. The one divergence was odd — **biceps thickness increased significantly only in the bench press group**. Neither exercise has the biceps as an agonist, and he admits he cannot explain it, offering only a speculation that stabilising a barbell may involve some biceps contraction. He is right to leave it unresolved; a single unexplained significant result in a small trial is as likely to be noise as signal.

He also pre-empts the obvious objection to the 40% 1RM load. **Lopez et al.**'s network meta-analysis of 24 studies found similar hypertrophy across light, moderate and heavy loads, unaffected by sex, training status or muscle measured, with included studies spanning roughly **30–80% of 1RM** and reps taken to within 3 of failure. So a 40% load to failure is a legitimate hypertrophy stimulus, not a design flaw. This matters more than it first appears — it is the same fact that later underwrites the whole push-up progression argument.

**Kotarsky et al. (2018)** is weaker and he says so. 23 men aged 18–45 with only **2–6 months** of training, three sessions a week for **4 weeks**. The bench group used a double-progression scheme (3×6 up to 3×8, then +4.5 kg and reset). The push-up group progressed through a ladder of variations ordered by difficulty, split into double-arm (3×6 progressing to 3×8) and single-arm (3×3 progressing to 3×4) tiers.

Result: **neither group achieved a statistically significant increase in pectoralis major thickness**, though both increased numerically and the percentages favoured the push-up group. He refuses to read anything into that, correctly attributing the null to a **4-week duration and a small sample**, and noting the numeric edge could simply reflect a more responsive group. What the study does support is the negative claim: the bench press showed no clear superiority.

## The EMG evidence, with the caveat he insists on

Before presenting it he corrects a distinction most channels ignore: EMG measures **muscle excitation** — the electrical signalling delivered to fibres — not **muscle activation**, and he explicitly says he used to conflate the two himself. He also states that EMG amplitudes do not necessarily correlate with hypertrophy outcomes. Both points are properly caveated rather than buried, and both are backed by the Vigotsky et al. methods paper in his reference list.

- **Alizadeh et al. (2020)** — body-weight push-up vs an equivalently loaded bench press at the same hand spacing. Pectoralis major and anterior deltoid excitation similar in both concentric and eccentric phases; lateral triceps excitation slightly lower in the push-up concentric.
- **van den Tillaar and Ball (2020)** — four load-matched conditions: push-ups with body weight and with 10, 20 and 30 kg vests, against bench presses at the four matching loads, with push-up handles set to a pronated bench-style grip. Sternal and clavicular pec and long and lateral triceps excitation similar at every matched load. Differences: **anterior deltoid excitation higher in the bench concentric**, and medial triceps higher in the bench eccentric.
- **van den Tillaar** (same design, separate paper) — **no differences** across all measured pec, anterior deltoid and triceps regions at load-matched conditions.

His summary is appropriately hedged: with some disagreement between studies, load-equated push-ups and bench presses produce broadly similar agonist excitation. Combined with the two trials and the biomechanical similarity — both are shoulder horizontal flexion/extension plus elbow flexion/extension, both travel from elbow lockout to chest contact — he concludes there are "good grounds to hypothesise" similar hypertrophy. Note the verb: hypothesise, not conclude.

## The overload problem, and why it is the actual answer

This is the section that matters for anyone training at home.

With a barbell you overload by adding reps or adding load, and load is unlimited. With a regular push-up you can only add reps, and reps run into a wall. Chaining his own numbers: hypertrophy holds down to about **30% of 1RM**; a 30% load allows roughly **35 reps**; therefore beyond about **35 reps of a regular push-up the stimulus is likely no longer optimal**. He is careful to note that whether loads below 30% still work is genuinely unclear, with some data suggesting they do not.

**Solution 1: harder variations.** **Ebben et al. (2011)** measured the proportion of body weight pushed in different variations: about **64% in a regular push-up**, about **49% kneeling**, less with hands elevated, more with feet elevated. Single-arm variations extend the ladder further, as used in Kotarsky. Other options he lists: explosive (clap, Superman) variants, handles for increased range of motion, and gymnastic rings.

His concern with this route is a good one and is rarely raised: **different variations redistribute the load across the agonists, and not in a way you chose**. Feet-elevated push-ups increase total load but plausibly shift it toward upper chest and anterior deltoid, reducing mid- and lower-chest stimulus. Single-arm variations, with reduced shoulder horizontal flexion demand, may under-recruit the chest and are "seemingly quite triceps dominant" — a hedge he flags as uncertain. For someone who just wants to get better at push-ups, irrelevant. For someone using push-ups as a hypertrophy tool, it means your loading target moves every time you progress.

**Solution 2: weighted push-ups**, which he prefers precisely because they hold the movement constant while adding load — a vest, plates, plates in a backpack. Both van den Tillaar papers used weighted push-ups against load-matched bench presses and found similar excitation, so this is the variation with the most direct supporting data.

He puts the practical ceiling for weighted push-ups at roughly **50–70 kg of added load**, beyond which it gets awkward. Past that: paused reps, deficit push-ups for greater range of motion, rings, or weighted single-arm work. And because up to ~35 reps is still effective when taken to within 3 of failure, he points out you can also progress a heavy weighted push-up into higher rep ranges — his worked example is going from 8 reps with 50 kg toward 30 reps with 50 kg.

## Strength carryover, both directions

Both trials measured bench press 1RM before and after. In both, **increases were statistically similar between the push-up and bench groups**, though percentages favoured the bench group. He attributes the non-significance to small samples rather than genuine equivalence, and says outright that it is logical for bench training to build bench strength most — a case where he argues against the more flattering reading of his own data.

The reverse direction, from Kotarsky: the push-up group significantly improved the difficulty level of push-up variation they could perform; the bench group also improved but **significantly less**. He caveats this with the 4-week duration and expects bench training would show clearer push-up carryover over longer periods.

## Where push-ups are genuinely better

Two muscles, both for structural reasons rather than loading ones.

**Abdominals.** **Calatayud et al. (2014)** found a regular push-up produced significantly greater **rectus abdominis and external oblique** excitation than a bench press at 85% 1RM. The push-up is a plank; the bench press rests your trunk on a bench.

**Serratus anterior.** A well-executed push-up lets the scapulae retract on the descent and protract on the ascent, which is serratus function. A well-executed bench press pins the scapulae retracted and down against the bench, so **no protraction occurs at all**. This one is pure anatomical reasoning with no study attached, and he presents it as such.

## What this changes for the library

This supports, with real limits, the library's position that bodyweight training is adequate for early training years and constrains advanced lifters — but it sharpens the boundary. **The constraint is not stimulus quality, it is load availability**, and it has a number attached: the ceiling arrives once a regular push-up exceeds roughly 35 reps to near-failure. Below that, the trials say the push-up is not inferior. Above it, the lifter needs external load — at which point a weighted push-up and a bench press are doing the same job, and the choice is about equipment and convenience rather than effectiveness. His closing point is worth keeping: this is not a choice, both can sit in the same programme.

## Where the conclusion outruns the evidence

The equivalence claim rests on **one 8-week trial in 18 men and one 4-week trial that found no significant growth in either group**, supplemented by EMG he has already told us does not necessarily predict hypertrophy. "The bench press is not demonstrably superior" is well supported. "They produce similar hypertrophy" is a hypothesis, and to his credit he mostly words it that way — though the takeaway points slide closer to assertion than the data allow.
