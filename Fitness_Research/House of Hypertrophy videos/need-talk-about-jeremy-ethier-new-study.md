# Sorting the Muscle-Length Literature Into Three Categories

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "We Need to Talk About Jeremy Ethier's New Study" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KHB7rXLlhjU)) · 2025-12-22 · 22 min
**Topic:** Evidence & method

## Bottom line
- Despite the framing of the title, this is **not a takedown**. He calls the new study "quite well-designed" and defends its within-subject design against critics. His target is the online reaction claiming the study debunks long-muscle-length training.
- His central contribution is a **taxonomy**: the muscle-length literature splits into three non-interchangeable categories — **resistance challenge**, **range of motion**, and **exercise selection with biarticular muscles** (plus isometrics as a fourth if you want it). Most of the confusion comes from pooling them.
- The new study sits in the **resistance challenge** category, where all **three** existing studies find similar hypertrophy. So it agrees with, rather than contradicts, its own category.
- The evidence for longer lengths lives in the **other two** categories: range of motion (meta-analysed, favouring full ROM or lengthened partials over shortened partials, by ~4.76 percentage points at distal regions) and biarticular exercise selection (seated leg curl, leaning-back leg extension, standing calf raise, overhead triceps extension, shoulder-extended curls).
- His practical takeaways are deliberately mild: **don't worry about where peak difficulty falls; full ROM is an excellent default with lengthened partials a reasonable experiment; biarticular-lengthening exercise choices are a worthwhile bet.** And muscle length is not among the most important variables anyway.

## The study
[00:47] 20 previously untrained individuals, twice weekly for 10 weeks. Exercises: chest fly, reverse fly, hip extension, lateral raise, on Prime machines that allow the resistance profile to be varied per side.

- **Lengthened-bias side:** machine set for peak torque at the start of the concentric, easing off later — muscles challenged hardest when longest.
- **Shortened-bias side:** relatively easy at the start, peak torque near the end of the concentric — muscles challenged hardest when shortest.

Critically, **both sides used the same total range of motion**; only the location of peak difficulty differed. Tempo was standardised so momentum could not shift where peak difficulty landed. Chest fly and hip extension: 5 sets per session. Reverse fly and lateral raise: 4 sets. All sets 8–12 reps to momentary muscular failure, 2 min rest, loads adjusted to stay in range.

Measures: cross-sectional area of side delts, rear delts and pectoralis major at three regions each, plus gluteus maximus volume. **No significant differences between conditions at any measurement.**

## His methodological comments — and whether they're fair
This is the section worth separating carefully, because most of his methodological remarks **defend** the study.

- **On the within-subject design (defends).** Having each subject serve as their own control removes between-subject genetics, nutrition and lifestyle as confounders. Fair, and standard.
- **On the cross-education objection (rebuts).** Some critics argued training one side influences the other. He answers that the evidence, including a paper on over 100 subjects, fails to find that training one limb meaningfully affects the opposite limb's **growth**; cross-education applies largely to **strength**. Fair on its face and consistent with what this library holds on cross-education, where 7 of 11 studies found no untrained-limb growth and the positives were mostly non-significant.
- **On measurement error (raises, unresolved).** He notes some individuals appear to have increased muscle size by **upwards of 100%**, says he has no idea where the measurement error is, and moves on. This is a real red flag about the imaging data that he surfaces and then does not pursue — arguably the one criticism he under-weights.
- **On sample size (raises, applies broadly).** He argues small samples are frequently underpowered to detect real differences, and says this limitation is **most applicable to the resistance challenge category** — the category the new study is in — because there are only three studies in it. This cuts against over-reading the new study's null, and he applies it evenhandedly rather than selectively.
- **On training status (raises).** The new study used previously untrained subjects, as did much of the surrounding literature, so confident statements about trained-vs-untrained differences are not available.

The net position is unusual and worth recording: he defends the study's design, questions its data quality in passing, and argues its null result is **uninformative about the broader long-length question** because it tests a different variable than the studies people think it contradicts.

## The three categories
[04:06] **1. Resistance challenge.** Two exercises with identical body positions and identical total joint range of motion, differing only in where peak difficulty occurs. An incline curl vs a preacher curl does **not** qualify — body positions differ. Three studies exist:
- The new study (above): no difference.
- A 2020 study comparing **cable vs barbell preacher curls** in untrained subjects. Peak torque occurs where the load's line of force is perpendicular to the moving segment: near the start of the barbell preacher curl (biceps long), later in the cable version (biceps shorter). Biceps growth at one measured region: similar.
- A 2025 study comparing **cable vs dumbbell lateral raises** in well-trained subjects. Cable line of force perpendicular at the start (side delts longer); dumbbells hardest with arms straight out (side delts shorter). Side delt growth at proximal and distal regions: similar.

All three null. His conclusion: **the new study does not conflict with the evidence; it replicates it.**

[06:39] **2. Range of motion.** Same exercise, different ranges: full ROM, shortened partial, lengthened partial. The new study does not belong here at all — both conditions used the same ROM. What the literature shows:
- **Shortened partials vs full ROM or lengthened partials:** a meta-analysis across three muscle regions finds full ROM/lengthened partials produce a bit more growth, with the gap widening from proximal to distal. At the distal region, roughly **4.76 percentage points more** — additive, so 4% becomes 8.76%.
- **Full ROM vs lengthened partials:** six studies. All **three in untrained** subjects favour lengthened partials overall. Of the three in **trained** subjects, two found largely similar growth and the third found similar mid-region growth but slightly more distal elbow flexor growth from lengthened partials. His verdict: lengthened partials are **at least as good as** full ROM.

[08:42] **3. Exercise selection with biarticular muscles.** Muscles crossing two joints can be lengthened more by one exercise than another:
- **Hamstrings** — seated leg curl (hip flexed ~90°) lengthens the three biarticular heads more than lying leg curl; one paper found greater growth of those heads, while the uniarticular biceps femoris short head grew similarly.
- **Rectus femoris** — leaning-back leg extension lengthens it more and grew it more in one paper; the uniarticular vastus lateralis grew similarly.
- **Gastrocnemius** — standing calf raises lengthen the biarticular heads more and grew them more across three studies; the uniarticular soleus grew similarly, though one paper's numbers slightly favoured the seated version.
- **Triceps long head** — two studies comparing overhead extensions to pushdowns give seemingly conflicting results, but he argues the stronger design shows greater long head growth overhead, and even the weaker study has some measures favouring it. Curiously, the stronger study also found greater growth of the combined medial/lateral heads overhead, which are not biarticular and for which nobody has an explanation.
- **Biceps** — five studies comparing shoulder-extended to shoulder-flexed curling. Findings mixed; he argues the stronger designs favour shoulder-extended, and one non-significant study has numbers leaning that way.

The pattern that makes this category credible: **uniarticular control muscles inside the same studies generally grow equally**, which is what you'd predict if muscle length were doing the work.

## Why the categories bleed into each other
[11:46] He then argues against his own scheme, which is the best part of the video. **Pedrosa's leg extension study** belongs to the range-of-motion category, but leg extensions are hardest at the top — so the full-ROM group's peak difficulty fell at a short muscle length while the lengthened partial's fell at a long one. Both ROM *and* peak-difficulty location differed. You cannot tell which produced the result, and you cannot rule out some other property of a lengthened partial being responsible.

Same problem in reverse with incline vs preacher curls. Incline curls lengthen the biceps (shoulder extended) but peak at mid-to-late range; preacher curls shorten the biceps slightly but peak near the start. One paper found greater **biceps** volume from incline curls (consistent with biarticular lengthening) but slightly better **brachialis and brachioradialis** growth from preacher curls — and since neither of those is affected by shoulder position, they were trained at the same length in both, leaving peak difficulty as the tempting explanation. He notes you still cannot rule out some other property of incline curls, and that isolating resistance challenge requires the same curl variation, which is what the 2020 cable-vs-barbell preacher study did — but that one only measured biceps.

[15:22] He also excludes the **isometric** literature (long-length isometrics generally producing more growth than short-length) from the resistance-challenge argument, on the grounds that holding a contraction at two lengths is not physiologically the same as two exercises differing in where peak difficulty falls. Treat it as a fourth, separate category.

## Additional caveats he raises
[16:24] Effects may differ **between muscle groups**, and the coverage is lopsided: much of the ROM research is quads, but there is **not a single quad study** in the resistance-challenge category; conversely two of the three resistance-challenge studies used side delts, and **no ROM study has examined side delts**. He mentions the sarcomere-operating-length argument for why some muscles should benefit more from long lengths, and says he doesn't think definitive conclusions follow from it, linking to his own article rather than settling it in the video.

## Takeaways
[19:59] Deliberately unexciting, which is the point:
- **Resistance challenge:** don't worry about where peak difficulty falls in the range of motion.
- **Range of motion:** full ROM is an excellent default; experiment with lengthened partials, which perform reasonably well.
- **Biarticular exercise selection:** including such exercises is a worthwhile bet.
- And explicitly: muscle length is **not** the most important factor. Training hard enough, sufficient volume and consistency all matter more.

**Overreach flag:** the taxonomy is his own analytical framework rather than an established one, and its main load-bearing claim — that the new study belongs in a category with only two other studies, both null — means the "no conflict" conclusion rests on a very thin evidence base, which he acknowledges. He also flags 100%+ growth values as unexplained measurement error without letting that undermine his confidence in the study's design.
