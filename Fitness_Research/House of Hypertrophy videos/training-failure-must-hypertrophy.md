# Is Training to Failure Required? The Case for 3–1 Reps in Reserve

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Training to Failure Is a MUST for Hypertrophy (Or Is It?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OIsnpVs7Kc)) · 2021-11-08 · 15 min
**Topic:** Effort & failure

## Bottom line
- Four longitudinal studies compared training to failure against stopping **3–1 reps short**. All four found **similar hypertrophy**. Three used previously untrained subjects; the fourth ([Santanielo 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33343066/)) used men with ~5.1 years of training, which closes the obvious objection.
- Conclusion: failure is **not required**. Stopping 3–1 reps short is equivalently effective for growth.
- The mechanistic argument agrees. Maximal motor unit recruitment appears to be reached **well before** failure — one EMG study suggests 3–5 reps before failure for shoulder/trap musculature ([Sundstrup 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21986694/)) — and a motor-unit fatigue model ([Potvin & Fuglevand 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28574981/)) shows that by the point of failure the highest-threshold units are **fatiguing, not producing peak force**.
- This is a claim about 3–1 RIR only. Whether **more** than 3 RIR works is a separate and much messier question — he handles it in [a companion video](leaving-more-3-reps-reserve-vs-training.md), and the answer there is "unclear."
- Important practical caveat he raises himself: in the lab, researchers verify proximity to failure. In a gym, a lifter who *thinks* they stopped at 3 RIR may actually have been at 5+.

## The four studies

**[Lacerda 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31809457/)** — 10 previously untrained men, unilateral leg extension, 3–4 sets at 55–60% 1RM, 2–3×/week for 14 weeks. One leg trained to failure every set; the other stopped at 2 RIR on set one, 1 RIR on set two, 0 RIR on the last set. Rectus femoris and vastus lateralis CSA increases were similar.

**[Nóbrega 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29189407/)** — 32 previously untrained men, legs assigned to four conditions: high-load (80% 1RM) failure, high-load non-failure, low-load (30% 1RM) failure, low-load non-failure. Three sets, 2×/week, 12 weeks. Non-failure conditions stopped at the point subjects *felt* they were about to fail, which the data suggest was roughly 3–1 RIR. Vastus lateralis CSA increases were similar across **all four** conditions. He flags the side finding: 30% 1RM built muscle just as well as 80%.

**[Sampson & Groeller 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25809472/)** — 28 previously untrained men, an elbow-flexion exercise, 4 sets at 85% 1RM, 3×/week for 12 weeks. Group 1 went to failure with 2s concentric/2s eccentric, averaging 6 reps. Groups 2 and 3 did only 4 reps per set (≈2 RIR) at differing lifting/lowering speeds. Elbow flexor CSA increases were similar across all three.

He states the confounders rather than hiding them: the three groups used different rep tempos, and the two non-failure groups did one extra set to failure each week. He argues neither matters much — rep durations from 0.5–8 s appear similarly effective — which is a reasonable but not airtight defence.

**[Santanielo 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33343066/)** — the one that carries the argument. 14 men, average 5.1 years training, 74.4 kg bodyweight, leg pressing an average of 237.5 kg at baseline. Unilateral leg press and leg extension, 2×/week for 10 weeks. Set numbers were **individualised**: each subject's pre-study weekly quad set count was increased by 20% (a lifter doing 15 sets/week did 18). One leg went to failure on every set; the other stopped at roughly 3–1 RIR. Vastus lateralis CSA increases were similar.

Sample size is small (n=14), and it is one study, but it is the only trained-subject entry in the set and it points the same direction as the other three.

## Why the mechanism agrees

Mechanical tension — force generated by muscle fibres, detected by mechanosensors and transduced into a signalling cascade ending in muscle protein synthesis — is the best-characterised hypertrophic stimulus ([Wackerhage 2019](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00685.2018)). The question is whether the last few reps before failure add tension that the preceding reps did not.

Motor units span a spectrum from low-threshold (small motor neurons, slow-twitch fibres, low force, fatigue-resistant) to high-threshold (large motor neurons, fast-twitch fibres, high force, quickly fatigued) ([Heckman & Enoka 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23720261/)). Maximising the stimulus means recruiting as many units as possible and having them produce meaningful force for a reasonable duration.

Two points follow. First, the recruitment ceiling is hit **before** failure — how far before differs by muscle, but one study puts it at 3–5 reps out for the shoulders and traps. Second, at failure the high-threshold units are past their peak force output and are simply fatiguing; more motor-unit fatigue is not a documented growth stimulus.

The [Potvin & Fuglevand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28574981/) model illustrates it. Seven simulated motor units, numbered 1 (lowest threshold) to 120 (highest), during a 50%-of-max isometric contraction held 100 s to failure. All but unit 120 are recruited at onset. Units 1–60 hold steady low force throughout. Unit 80 peaks early then fades. Unit 100 climbs to ~65 s then fades. Unit 120 is recruited around 70 s, climbs steeply to ~95 s, then falls off. At the actual point of failure, no new units are recruited and no unit is producing more force than it already was.

**Overreach flag:** this is a *model* of *isometric* contractions. He says so explicitly, and says he knows of no reason the main conclusion would differ for isotonic work — a fair position, but the mechanistic section is doing more argumentative work here than the outcome data strictly needs, and the honest summary is that the four training studies are the evidence and the model is an illustration.

## Where this sits

His answer is narrow and well-supported: **3–1 RIR ≈ failure for hypertrophy**. It does not say failure is harmful, and it does not say 4+ RIR is fine. On the library's contested spectrum of expert positions (roughly 0 to 7 RIR), this 2021 video puts House of Hypertrophy at the tight end of the "you don't need failure" camp — closer to the 1–3 RIR position than to any claim that failure is stimulus-neutral. He never argues failure adds nothing; he argues the last 1–3 reps add nothing **detectable in growth outcomes**, which is a weaker and more defensible claim.

By his 2025 mechanisms video he tightens further, saying stopping "one to two reps from failure could be sufficient in certain scenarios" — a small drift toward failure, not away from it.
