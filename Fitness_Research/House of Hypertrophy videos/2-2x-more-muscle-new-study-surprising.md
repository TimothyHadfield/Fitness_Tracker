# Progressing Weekly Sets to 42-52: The First Study on Adding Sets Over Time

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "2.2x MORE Muscle: This New Study Is Surprising…" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yrtsa3swsAo)) · 2023-10-23 · 11 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- The first study to compare a **fixed weekly set count against progressively adding sets**. All groups started at 22 weekly quad sets; one stayed there, one added 4 sets every two weeks (ending at **42**), one added 6 (ending at **52**).
- **Both progressing groups out-grew and out-gained strength on the fixed group.** Between the two progressing groups, the 6-set arm's averages were higher but its **confidence interval was much wider** and overlapped the 4-set arm, particularly on cross-sectional area.
- **The study cannot separate the two candidate causes** — higher average sets, or the act of progressing sets. He says so explicitly and reasons about both branches separately.
- It sits against **three studies using long rest intervals that found gains optimised at 12-18 weekly sets** with no clear benefit beyond 18. Three *other* studies found benefits up to 30-45 weekly sets, but all three used **short rest intervals**.
- **His recommendation stays conservative**: this is grounds for some well-trained individuals to *experiment* above 20 weekly sets on a few lagging muscles — not a mandate, and he refuses to endorse exceeding 40.

## The study

31 trained men, 12 weeks, back squat + leg press + leg extension twice weekly. Session one used **6-8 reps**, session two **10-12 reps**. All sets stopped at **2 reps from failure except the last set of each exercise**, which went to failure. Load was progressed to hold those proximities. Rest was **at least 2 minutes**. Calories and macros did not differ between groups, and all subjects ate **over 1.6 g/kg protein**.

Everyone began at **22 weekly quad sets**. The fixed group held there. The four-set group added 4 sets every two weeks, finishing at **42**. The six-set group added 6, finishing at **52**.

**Outcomes.** Vastus lateralis cross-sectional area and thickness: both progressing groups tended to exceed the fixed group. Back squat 1RM: same direction, and here the confidence intervals give a **more convincing picture of the six-set group beating the four-set group** than the hypertrophy data do.

His handling of the 4-vs-6 comparison is the most careful passage. He reads the **wider confidence interval on the six-set group as indirect evidence of greater between-subject variation** — some subjects responded well to 52 sets, others did not — and concludes that superiority over the four-set group cannot be asserted. That is the right reading, and it is the opposite of what the title's "2.2x" figure invites.

## What the study cannot tell you

He is explicit: **we don't know whether the gains came from the higher average set count or from the act of progressively adding sets.** Both could contribute. He then works through the two branches separately, which is a cleaner treatment than most channels would attempt:

**If average sets did the work**, this is the first evidence that with *long* rest intervals, more than 20 weekly sets per muscle can benefit some trained individuals. He is careful to weigh it against the three studies that failed to find such a benefit, and lands on "may be justifiable for some well-trained individuals to experiment," most feasibly on a few chosen muscles.

**If the progression did the work**, this is the first evidence for a periodised-volume strategy — e.g. ramping from 10 to 20 weekly sets across a block, then deloading and repeating, or starting slightly higher next block. He notes this method was laid out in a paper by **Mike Israetel** some years back, and says it needs validation.

## Why the rest-interval confound matters

The key piece of context he imports: three prior studies found benefits up to **30-45 weekly sets**, but **all three used short rests**. His explanation is that short rests make each set less effective, so more sets are needed to reach the same stimulus. His evidence is a leg press study where **3 sets with 3 minutes rest beat 3 sets with 1 minute**, but **5 sets with 1 minute matched 3 sets with 3 minutes**. Against that, three studies using longer rests found gains optimised at **12-18 weekly sets**.

That framing is what makes the new study interesting rather than merely extreme: it used **2+ minutes of rest** and still found benefit far above 18 sets.

## The caveats he stacks on the high volumes

Before anyone copies 42-52 weekly sets, he lists what makes those numbers less extreme than they sound:

- **Quads only.** Not applied to other muscle groups simultaneously.
- **Most sets were not to failure** — 2 RIR, with only the last set of each exercise going to failure.
- **Even "failure" is ambiguous.** The researchers defined two options, one of which he considers voluntary rather than true muscular failure, so some subjects may not have reached true failure even on the final set.
- **2 RIR estimates may be inaccurate**, and accuracy is known to be worse on lower-body exercises — though he concedes 6-12 reps makes proximity easier to judge than higher reps.
- **2+ minutes of rest** made each set more manageable.
- **The authors reported some subjects experiencing excessive perceived fatigue in the final weeks**, exactly when volumes passed 40 sets. He mentions session duration as a practical objection too.

His conclusion from that stack — "I don't believe we can safely and literally recommend everyone to exceed 40 weekly sets" — is a notably restrained reading of a study he clearly finds exciting.

He also pushes back on the opposite failure mode: people discarding the study outright because it clashes with their priors. He asks for it to be read analytically rather than accepted or dismissed, and closes on individual variation — research findings as starting points to adjust from, not prescriptions.

**Flag:** the title's "2.2x more muscle" figure never appears in the spoken content, which discusses tendencies and overlapping confidence intervals rather than a multiple. Whatever on-screen number it refers to is not defended in the video, and the body of the video argues for a far weaker conclusion than the title.
