# One Set Matched Three — and Why That Doesn't Overturn the Volume Literature

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "3 Sets is NOT Better than 1 Set?! (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o78WC5m1WA8)) · 2025-05-05 · 10 min
**Topic:** Evidence & method

## Bottom line
- Pinto and colleagues (2025) had **15 untrained men** train the unilateral pec deck twice weekly for **12 weeks**, one arm doing 1 set per session (2 weekly sets) and the other 3 sets (6 weekly sets), all to volitional concentric failure. Both pec heads grew **similarly**. Tripling sets bought nothing.
- Dhim's argument is that this result is **fully compatible with three sets genuinely being better** — not a contradiction of the volume literature but an expected draw from it.
- The demonstration is a simulation by James Krieger: build a population where the true effects are known, sample five studies of ten per group, and the five studies disagree wildly. Meta-analyse them and you land back on the truth.
- The current sets meta-regression still shows **more weekly sets → more hypertrophy with diminishing returns**. A single small trial is a data point in a future meta-analysis, not a verdict.
- Bonus finding: the **clavicular head grew as well as the sternocostal head** — but from a single measurement point per region, so it is weak evidence about upper-pec growth.

## The study
[00:00] Within-subject design (one arm per condition), which is the right way to run a small trial — genetics, nutrition and lifestyle can't differ between a subject's two arms. Both pec deck conditions took every set to volitional concentric failure. Muscle thickness of the clavicular and sternocostal heads of pectoralis major was measured before and after.

Result: both regions grew comparably regardless of set number. Dhim flags immediately that this is untrained subjects, and that there are still **numerous examples of untrained people growing more from multiple sets than one**. He also stresses the flip side he doesn't want lost: low set numbers clearly do stimulate hypertrophy, even in trained lifters, and are an excellent option if you're short on time.

## Why conflicting studies are the expected output
[02:01] You could pick at the methods — a bigger contrast between conditions, a longer duration, different exercises. He notes that's often a valid exercise. But there's a more important explanation that applies **even with perfect, identical methods**.

Studies sample from a population. Small samples routinely produce samples that don't reflect the population, purely by chance. Three coin flips landing heads doesn't make the coin biased. Within-limb designs have their own sources of error: a subject whose one arm started smaller and had more room to grow, something outside the study impairing one limb, measurement error landing unevenly, and a genuine minority who really do respond similarly to 1 and 3 sets.

[04:38] **The simulation** (credit to Krieger, article linked in the description). Build a population of 3 million trained individuals where, by construction, high volume adds 3 mm of biceps thickness, moderate 2 mm and low 1 mm, each with a 2 mm standard deviation. These are the *known* true effects. Now simulate five studies with 10 subjects per group. Only one of the five resembles the truth. The others show a plateau at moderate volume, high volume numerically *worse* than moderate, and moderate numerically worse than low. Pool the five into a meta-analysis and the result lands close to the true effects.

That is the whole point: mixed results are not science failing, they are what small samples do. The corrective is more data and meta-analysis, not abandoning the literature.

## Where the volume evidence actually stands
[06:09] The latest meta-regression on sets finds **more weekly sets per muscle group produce more hypertrophy, with diminishing returns**. Dhim adds two hedges of his own: this does not prove very high volumes are best, and lower volumes still deliver real gains.

## Individual differences — an honest "we don't know"
[06:39] He raises a study in which subjects trained unilateral leg extensions and leg presses three times weekly for 12 weeks, one leg at 1 set per exercise and the other at 3. A proportion grew more with three sets, some grew similarly, and a smaller number grew more with one set.

Then he immediately undercuts it: the same sampling and measurement-error problems apply, and are *worse* when interpreting a single person's data. **The study designs needed to demonstrate true individual differences — repeated within-person replication — haven't been run in the muscle growth literature.** His position is labelled as a guess: he thinks individual differences exist, via both physiology and lifestyle (a stressful period may blunt your response to high volume), and recommends using the average literature effects as a starting point, then adjusting. If you're doing lots of sets and feel underrecovered, drop some. If you've stalled for months and are recovering well, add some.

## The pec region finding
[08:11] The clavicular head growing similarly to the sternocostal head will surprise people who assume the upper pec lags. But only **one measurement point per region** was taken, and the pec can be split into more regions than two. Regional pec research is thin. He still recommends including an upper-pec-biased exercise such as an incline press.

**Overreach flag:** none worth raising — this is the most methodologically careful video in this batch, and he explicitly labels his own individual-differences view as a best guess rather than a finding.
