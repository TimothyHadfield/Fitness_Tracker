# Lower-body exercise selection, and how to choose exercises at all

Fifteen notes spanning 2020–2026, covering quads, hamstrings, glutes and calves, the equipment
question, the compounds-versus-isolation question, and — most usefully — the handful of general
principles that actually predict which exercise grows which muscle. They are organised here by the
decision you are making. The general principles come first, because they are the transferable part:
almost every muscle-specific verdict below is an application of two of them.

---

## How to read this source

House of Hypertrophy is the most densely cited channel in this library. The creator is **self-taught
with no formal credentials**, which he says himself, and is unusually careful in practice: he leads
with the study that contradicts him, states sample sizes and training status, and discards data that
would help his own side.

Three cautions run through everything below.

**Was growth measured, or a stand-in for it?** Keep three tiers separate. **Measured hypertrophy**
(ultrasound, MRI, cross-sectional area) is the only tier that settles anything. **EMG/activation**
is an acute estimate he elsewhere demolishes — surface EMG changes with muscle length even at
constant excitation, so it is unreliable in exactly the comparisons people use it for. **Leverage
and moment-arm modelling** is a third thing again: a physics calculation, not a measurement of
anything in a training human. Where a claim below rests on the second or third tier, it says so.

**Is there a reference list?** Twenty-six of his videos have none — they cite on screen only. This
set is better off than the channel average: thirteen of the fifteen carry a usable list. The
exception that matters is **[Is the squat enough](squat-enough-leg-growth.md), whose seven sources
are dead `bit.ly` shortlinks that no longer resolve** — the Fonseca and Bloomquist study details in
that note cannot be checked. [Switch exercises](switch-exercises-every-session-more-muscle-hypertrophy.md)
has two sources, neither resolving to a full citation, and
[The best training mode](best-training-mode-muscle-growth-most-get.md) and
[Trap bar vs conventional](trap-bar-deadlift-vs-conventional-deadlift-depth.md) give bare links.

**Were subjects trained?** Most of the lower-body selection literature is untrained subjects over
8–12 weeks — the single most common reason a finding here should be held loosely.

## Part 1 — The general principles

### The one predictor that works, and it is not activation

[Maximize muscle growth](maximize-muscle-growth-new-study-exercise-choice.md) is the best video in
this set. Burke et al. 2024: **28 trained subjects, within-subject** — one leg trained the leg
press, the other the leg extension; one leg trained straight-leg calf raises, the other bent-leg,
all to failure at 8–12 reps. The within-subject design is the rigour, and his own line for it is the
right one: your left leg does not sleep more or eat more protein than your right.

**Rectus femoris grew better with the leg extension; vastus lateralis grew better with the leg
press.** Medial gastrocnemius clearly favoured straight-leg raises, lateral gastrocnemius less
clearly, and soleus mildly favoured bent-leg.

From that, two principles:

**Principle 1 — a biarticular muscle grows poorly when one of its actions opposes the compound.**
Rectus femoris crosses hip and knee; it extends the knee but *flexes* the hip. A leg press or squat
demands simultaneous knee and hip extension, so a hard rectus femoris contraction would fight the
movement, and the nervous system plausibly minimises its contribution. **This is why compounds
underdevelop the rectus femoris and why isolated knee extension is not optional.** The same logic
predicts the triceps long head under-growing from pressing, and it holds: pressing grows the lateral
and medial heads well and the long head barely.

**Principle 2 — position the other joint so the muscle is longer.** Both gastrocnemius heads cross
the knee; a bent-leg raise starts them shortened at the knee and shortens them further at the ankle
— **active insufficiency**. Straightening the knee removes it. The same principle already held
across three other muscles: **seated leg curls beat lying** (Maeo 2021), **leaning-back leg
extensions beat normal**, **overhead triceps extensions beat pushdowns** (Maeo 2023).

Two honest flags. The neural down-regulation story behind Principle 1 is **inference layered on an
outcome** — Burke measured growth only. And the calf outcomes came from a **single region per
muscle** against three for the quads, so the calf half of that study is thinner data. He then
applies Principle 1 to the biceps in pulling, lays out three counterarguments *against his own
principle*, and **ends on the fence** — with the cleanest EMG critique in the channel: the triceps
long head shows *high* EMG on the bench press and grows minimally from it.

### Longer is better — with a possible threshold

The long-length principle is real but not unbounded. [The best training mode](best-training-mode-muscle-growth-most-get.md)
supplies the counter-case: Kubo 2019 found **similar quadriceps hypertrophy between a 90° and a 140°
knee flexion squat** despite the deeper version producing a greater stretch, suggesting **a
threshold of passive tension beyond which more adds nothing**. The same caution appears in
[Bigger quads](bigger-quads-new-study-epic.md), where reverse Nordics and sissy squats are offered
as a *hypothesis* that the threshold may defeat. Treat "more stretch" as a tiebreaker, not a ranking
rule.

### Is there a single best exercise? No — and the argument has three legs

[The best exercise](best-exercise-muscle-hypertrophy-science-says.md) makes the case: **modality
doesn't matter, regional growth needs variety, and individuals recruit differently on the same
lift.** His mechanistic spine is that the body has no sensor for whether force came from a barbell,
a machine or bodyweight, and that **roughly 5–35 reps at 3 or fewer reps in reserve** satisfies the
tension conditions on any implement.

He is not saying every exercise is equal. **Three things make an exercise worse:** the target muscle
not being the limiting factor (a BOSU-ball pistol squat, where balance ends the set); no stretch
under load; and personal discomfort or pain. He flags the internal weakness himself — that "5–35
reps at 3 RIR achieves maximal recruitment on any modality" is inferred from recruitment theory, not
measured, and it carries most of the video.

The variety leg rests on **Costa 2021 — 22 detrained men, 8 weeks, 3×/week, 3 sets of 8–12** — where
a group rotating three different exercises per muscle group across the week matched a non-varied
group at some measured regions and beat it at others. **Variety buys regional coverage, not more
total growth at any one site.**

### How often should you change exercises? Not every session

[Switch exercises every session](switch-exercises-every-session-more-muscle-hypertrophy.md) covers
the only direct test. Baz-Valle: **19 men with 2+ years training** (averaging 123 kg squat, 94 kg
bench), 8 weeks, an app randomising selection from an **80-exercise database** each session versus a
fixed list, everything **3 sets of 6–12 to failure**. **Quad growth was statistically
indistinguishable, and some raw percentages actually favoured the fixed group** (rectus femoris most
visibly). The one thing variation won was **motivation**, which rose across the study while the
fixed group's slightly declined.

So the case against constant switching is not "it kills growth" — it is three practical arguments:
**skill acquisition, the repeated bout effect, and progress tracking.** All three are mechanistic;
none was measured, and the recovery limb is contradicted by the study's own null.

That sits in tension with [Underrated selection principles](underrated-exercise-selection-principles-muscle-growth.md),
which endorses **Rauch et al. — 17 men, average 5.6 years training, 9 weeks** — where an
autoregulated group free to pick its own exercises each session **gained more lean body mass** than
a fixed programme. His inference (trained lifters know what suits them) is not tested by the design;
autonomy, novelty and effort are all live alternatives. **Practical reconciliation: keep a fixed
exercise list long enough to progress on it, and choose that list yourself.**

### Unilateral vs bilateral: not a hypertrophy variable

[Unilateral vs bilateral](unilateral-vs-bilateral-training-muscle-growth.md) tests the "small muscle
mass" hypothesis — that training less muscle at once permits greater neural drive (via the bilateral
force deficit) and greater local fatigue. Kassiano 2026: **54 untrained women, 3×/week for 8 weeks,
2 sets of 8–12 dumbbell curls to concentric task failure**. **Elbow flexor thickness gains at the
60% and 70% regions were not significantly different.** The unilateral group's right-arm 1RM gain
was significantly greater, most likely a load artefact — the bilateral group used slightly *more*
load per dumbbell in session one, i.e. **no apparent bilateral deficit between groups**.

He reinforces it with the squat/hip-thrust and squat/leg-extension results: **squats and leg presses
train far more muscle mass than leg extensions yet are not inferior for the vastus heads.**
**Verdict: choose unilateral on preference, time cost and size imbalances, not on stimulus.**

### Individual recruitment — the framework to hold loosest

[Underrated selection principles](underrated-exercise-selection-principles-muscle-growth.md) argues
that **neuromechanical matching is mostly shared but not always**: Boyer 2021 found the
highest-activation hamstring head on Nordic curls and stiff-legged deadlifts differed person to
person, and Stronska found the same on the bench press (some pec-dominant, some triceps, some front
delt). Consequence: **another lifter's report that an exercise "built their X" does not transfer.**
Also from that note, and weakly supported: **your compound technique drifts toward whatever is
already strong** (Li & Zhang 2009), and a block of isolation work may raise a muscle's involvement
in a compound.

**Every recruitment claim there is surface EMG**, which he elsewhere says cannot predict
hypertrophy, and he even defends Stronska's normalisation seven months after demolishing it. The
"isolate a muscle to raise its compound involvement" principle **has no growth data at all**.

## Part 2 — Does the equipment matter?

**No.** This is the best-supported claim in the whole domain.

**Free weights vs machines.** [The dedicated note](free-weights-vs-machines-muscle-hypertrophy.md)
covers Schwanbeck 2020: **36 people (15 men, 21 women) averaging 2 years training**, 8 weeks,
programmes matched movement for movement, **every set to failure** across three blocks (4×8–10 at
1 min rest, then 4×6–8 at 90 s, then 3×4–5 at 2 min). **Similar biceps and quadriceps thickness
increases.** Strength followed specificity only half the time — machines won the Smith bench, free
weights led numerically on the barbell squat, but barbell bench and Smith squat gains were similar.

[These 3 exercises](3-exercises-don-t-build-more-muscle.md) adds the strongest trial:
**Hernández-Belmonte 2023, 36 trained men, 8 weeks**, barbell (back squat, bench, prone row,
overhead press) versus machine (hex-bar squat, chest press, seated row, machine shoulder press),
3 sets per exercise 3×/week, sets stopped at a **20% velocity loss** (roughly 50% of maximum reps).
**Similar quadriceps growth at both the 30% and 60% measurement sites, plus pec major and rectus
abdominis.** Aerenhouts & D'Hondt 2020 in novices agrees on circumference, which he correctly calls
a crude measure.

[The best training mode](best-training-mode-muscle-growth-most-get.md) then closes the escape
routes. **Hormones:** free weights do produce larger acute testosterone and growth hormone spikes
(Shaner; Schwanbeck), but the spikes do not predict long-term growth, and squatting does not
chronically raise resting testosterone. **Variable resistance:** four trials — two Finnish 2013, a
2019 Polish preacher curl trial, a 1995 Canadian one — **all null**. **Eccentric overload:** of
**11 studies, only 4 favoured it**. **Dumbbells specifically:** he rejects all three arguments —
the EMG evidence is structurally confounded, the extra horizontal flexion happens where the muscle
is short, and more stretch does not automatically mean more growth (Kubo).

**The one thing that is not equal is which muscles get involved.** Barbell squats load and stabilise
the spine, so more erector recruitment than a hack squat or Bulgarian split squat producing the same
hip and knee extension. And extreme instability is genuinely bad if balance rather than the muscle
ends the set.

### Bodyweight, and where it actually falls short

[Bodyweight training](bodyweight-training-bad-muscle-growth-true-false.md) is the note to answer
"can I train legs at home". Its headline: bodyweight stimulates hypertrophy as effectively as free
weights or machines, **provided sets go to or near failure within roughly 6–35 reps** — and the
"6–12 is the hypertrophy range" premise the criticism rests on is simply wrong. Overload upstairs is
not the pitfall people think: add reps, harder variations, slower tempo, external weight.

**The lower body is the real pitfall, and it has two parts.** First, **there is no proper bodyweight
hip-extension exercise for the hamstrings** — the Nordic curl covers knee flexion excellently and
nothing covers the other half. Second, **overload runs out**: relative novices already exceed 35
reps on calf raises and hip thrusts, and squat progressions terminate at the pistol squat. His
conclusion is properly hedged — it is reasonable to *speculate* you cannot maximise lower-body
hypertrophy with calisthenics alone, which is not the same as no growth. That note's
regional-hypertrophy section is **EMG-based, and it flags this itself**.

### Bands

The one modality he is sceptical of, and **entirely on mechanism — no band hypertrophy trial exists,
and he says so.** Three problems: it is easy to let the band snap back and lose the eccentric,
**the resistance curve peaks where the muscle is short**, and progressive overload is impractical
long-term. Bands do build muscle (Fink 2020).

### Where modalities are genuinely complementary

Because recruitment follows the *movement*, and **some movements can only be produced by certain
equipment**, a single-modality programme has real holes. **A vertical pull is impossible with a
barbell or dumbbell. Neither isolated knee extension nor isolated knee flexion can be done
effectively with free weights.** All three are things you want. Modalities are not either/or.

## Part 3 — Compounds only, or isolation too?

[Are isolation exercises useless](isolation-exercises-useless-muscle-hypertrophy-19-studies.md) is
the reference note. The case *against* isolation is three studies: **Gentil 2015** (lat pulldown vs
barbell curl, 10 weeks — similar elbow flexor thickness), **Gentil 2013** (bench + pulldown versus
the same plus a curl and a triceps extension, 3 sets of 8–12 to failure, 10 weeks — similar, despite
the isolation group doing *more* total volume), and **França 2015** (trained men, 3 sets of 10–15,
8 weeks — similar arm circumference, again unequated in the isolation group's favour). All three fed
the **Gentil 2017 review**.

His rebuttal is the right one: **every one measured only elbow flexors or arm circumference, at a
single mid-muscle site**, which cannot detect regional differences — and regional hypertrophy is
well established (Kawakami; Ema 2013). Four further Barbalho studies pointed the same way and **he
discards them for improbable data**, removing evidence that helped the side he opposed.

The affirmative case is where you should read the tier carefully. **Only Costa 2021 (22 men, 8
weeks, ultrasound) tests variety directly**, and it carries more weight than its size warrants. The
muscle-by-muscle argument — **lateral delt** (no compound abducts the shoulder), **calves** (no
compound plantar flexes the ankle), **hamstrings** (knee flexion recruits different regions than hip
extension), **rectus femoris**, **triceps long head** — is joint-mechanics and activation reasoning,
not growth outcomes, and the note says so.

**Usable conclusion: substantial hypertrophy is achievable with compounds alone, but "compounds are
sufficient" is likely wrong.**

## Part 4 — Muscle by muscle

### Quads: run both classes of exercise

The gap was found early. [Is the squat enough](squat-enough-leg-growth.md) covers **Fonseca, 49
untrained men, 12 weeks, 2×/week**, five groups: **squat-only groups failed to grow the rectus
femoris significantly** (the fixed-rep squat-only group also failed on vastus medialis), while the
varied-exercise groups grew **all four heads**. **Kubo, 17 untrained men, 10 weeks: neither 90° nor
140° knee flexion grew the rectus femoris.** Depth does not rescue it — the problem is structural.
(That note's sources are the dead shortlinks; treat its study details as unverified.)

The fix is in [Bigger quads](bigger-quads-new-study-epic.md): **22 untrained men, within-subject**,
one leg on a normal leg extension (hip flexed 90°), the other leaning back (hip flexed 40°), knee
travelling 110°→0° in both, **15–20 reps** with load raised whenever 20 reps were hit on set one.
**Rectus femoris growth was 1.4–2.7× greater with the 40° variation** at both measured regions,
while **vastus lateralis growth was similar** — the internal control that makes the result credible,
since vastus lateralis crosses only the knee and both legs used the same knee range. Mitsuya 2023
(**9 bodybuilders, MRI-based activity** at 0°/40°/80° hip flexion) suggests it extends to trained
lifters, 0° and 40° both beating 80° — but that is **activity, not growth**, and he notes the
surrogate has never been validated for comparisons at different muscle lengths.

[Unilateral vs bilateral](unilateral-vs-bilateral-training-muscle-growth.md) replicates the split in
**63 untrained women, 2×/week for 8 weeks, 3 sets of 8–12 to momentary failure**, Smith machine
squats versus machine leg extensions: **leg extensions grew rectus femoris more at all three
regions; squats grew vastus lateralis more at the distal region only.** A neat aside: **leg
extension strength gains did not differ**, so carryover from squats to leg extensions beats the
reverse. Burke 2024 shows the same pattern in trained subjects.

**Practical answer for quads: run an isolated knee-extension movement (ideally leaning back, hip at
~40°) and a quad-focused compound. They grow different heads and are not substitutes.** Reverse
Nordics and sissy squats are plausible additions on length reasoning with **no comparative growth
data**.

### Hamstrings: the biggest hole

Same biarticular problem, opposite direction. The hamstrings (bar the biceps femoris short head)
extend the hip and flex the knee, so they hold near-constant length through a squat.
[Is the squat enough](squat-enough-leg-growth.md): **Kubo found no significant hamstring growth at
either depth**, and **Bloomquist found minimal increases in some regions and decreases in others**
with squats to 60° vs 120°. [Squats vs hip thrusts](squats-vs-hip-thrusts-glute-growth.md) confirms
it — **neither exercise grew the hamstrings.** Dedicated hamstring work is not optional.

What to do instead is less well evidenced than you would like. **Seated leg curls beat lying leg
curls** (Maeo 2021, with biceps femoris short head as an internal control) — Principle 2, since hip
flexion lengthens the hamstrings. Beyond that,
[Are isolation exercises useless](isolation-exercises-useless-muscle-hypertrophy-19-studies.md)
argues that **hip-extension and knee-flexion movements recruit different hamstring regions, so you
want both**, on activation and architecture data rather than growth. And the trap-bar note's claim
that conventional deadlifts work the hamstrings more than the trap bar **has no data behind it at
all** (see Part 5). Bodyweight training has no good answer here at all.

### Glutes: squats and hip thrusts tie

[Squats vs hip thrusts](squats-vs-hip-thrusts-glute-growth.md) covers the first trustworthy
head-to-head (Plotkin 2023): **34 untrained individuals, 2×/week, 8–12 reps to volitional failure**,
squatters going "as deep as comfortable" (most reached parallel). **No difference in gluteus maximus
growth at upper, middle or lower regions.** The widely-shared Barbalho result claiming a ~2.5× squat
advantage is **set aside entirely** on data-integrity grounds.

The tiebreakers are coverage and recovery. **Squats additionally grew quads and adductors; hip
thrusts did not. Neither grew hamstrings.** Both grew the **lower** glute region most, then middle,
then upper; **gluteus medius and minimus barely moved in either group** — they abduct the hip, so
they need abduction work. Hip thrusts train the glutes in a shortened position and are likely easier
to recover from, making them **a good vehicle for adding volume rather than a superior stimulus per
set** — supported by Kassiano's **33 untrained women, 3×/week, 10 weeks**, where adding hip thrusts
to leg press and stiff-legged deadlift grew more glute, but also meant **more sets**.

Depth still matters for glutes: in Kubo, **90° produced minimal glute max growth while 140° produced
substantial growth**, with adductor volume following the same pattern (likely adductor magnus). He
refuses to close the question, because **nobody has directly compared parallel to deep squats for
glute growth**. His hypothesis for why the deeper stretch did not win in Plotkin — the glute max is
stretched at deep hip flexion but has **poor leverage** there, so it may not get stretch and high
contractile force at once, the better-levered adductor magnus taking the load — is labelled a
hypothesis, as is the regional fibre-type argument.

### Calves: the biggest effect sizes here, and a reversal

[Bigger calves](bigger-calves-ultimate-guide-32-studies.md) is the most complete note in this set,
and it is where he **partially reverses himself** — [Maximize muscle growth](maximize-muscle-growth-new-study-exercise-choice.md)
had concluded straight-leg raises may be all you need.

**Knee angle. Straight-leg (standing) raises are non-negotiable for the gastrocnemius** — every
growth study agrees and the effect is large (Kinoshita 2023, untrained within-subject; Burke 2024,
trained within-subject; an unpublished Brazilian conference paper in 52 untrained women). **The
soleus is the open question**, and it points the other way in all four data points: slightly in
Kinoshita and Burke, **clearly in the Brazilian paper**, and in Kassiano 2023's swelling study (17
young women), where soleus swelling was notably greater after the bent leg. His reason for changing
his mind is methodological — the signal points one direction in every study. **Recommendation:
straight-leg work is required; training entirely straight-legged is acceptable; adding bent-leg work
for the soleus is defensible.** All straight-leg variations (standing machine, Smith, leg press toe
press) are fundamentally the same thing.

**Range of motion. Lengthened partials beat full ROM for the gastrocnemius by a wide margin** —
Kassiano 2023: previously untrained women, 3 sets 3×/week for 8 weeks, all to failure at 15–20 reps,
bottom-half partials versus full ROM versus top-half partials. **Medial gastrocnemius growth was
126.9% greater for lengthened partials than full ROM.** **Lengthened supersets** (full-ROM set to
failure, then past-failure partials into the stretch) gave **43.3% greater** medial gastrocnemius
growth in Larsen 2025. His case for the calf being special where trained-lifter arm data are null:
the gastrocnemius produces greatest active tension at long lengths, while a full-ROM calf raise is
hardest at the *top*, where it can generate least.

**Reps do not matter.** Schoenfeld 2020 trained one leg at **6–10 reps to failure** and the other at
**20–30**: **all three calf muscles grew similarly**, directly contradicting the "soleus is
slow-twitch so it needs high reps" claim. **Volume does**: Kassiano 2024, **61 untrained women**,
3×/week for 6 weeks at 15–20 reps to failure — **12 > 9 > 6 weekly sets, with the 6→9 increase
larger than 9→12.**

**Foot position** biases the heads — toes out favours the medial head, toes in the lateral (Nunes
2020, 22 men, significant only against the opposing condition, soleus not measured). **Inter-set
loaded stretch** (Van Every 2022, 20 s in the stretch after each set then 100 s rest) gave similar
medial gastrocnemius growth, slightly favoured the lateral head, and **more convincingly favoured
the soleus** — no extra time cost and no study has found it impairs growth, so it is worth trying.

**Do squats and leg curls count?** Barely. **The gastrocnemius will not meaningfully grow from
squats** — it is a knee flexor and squats demand knee extension, the exact Principle 1 situation.
The soleus gets some growth from squats and leg presses, but has never been directly measured for
it. **Leg curls train the gastrocnemius a little at best** (Van Vossel 2024: small non-significant
lateral head growth, virtually none medial), possibly improvable by dorsiflexing the ankle, which
one study suggests costs the hamstrings nothing.

## Part 5 — Do you need the big three?

**No.** [These 3 exercises](3-exercises-don-t-build-more-muscle.md) is the direct answer: four
trials comparing squat and bench against machine or bodyweight alternatives found **no hypertrophy
advantage for the free-weight version in any measured muscle** (Hernández-Belmonte 2023;
Aerenhouts & D'Hondt 2020; Schwanbeck 2020; plus Kikuchi & Nakazato 2017, 18 trained men, and
Kotarsky 2018, 23 men over only 4 weeks with nothing reaching significance). **The big three are
highly effective, just not irreplaceable.**

**The deadlift is the gap, and he says so: no study in that video actually tested it.** His argument
that it cannot be essential is purely mechanistic. He adds that the deadlift's reputation for
outsized fatigue is **not supported**, then notes that rests on only two studies; if it did prove
more taxing, his substitutes are trap-bar variations, leg press or hip thrusts.

On the squat specifically, [Is the squat enough](squat-enough-leg-growth.md) answers no for a
different reason: it under-serves **rectus femoris and hamstrings**, does **nothing for the
calves**, and misses **gluteus medius/minimus** and the adductors other than adductor magnus.

**Trap bar versus conventional** ([note](trap-bar-deadlift-vs-conventional-deadlift-depth.md)) is
**entirely biomechanical modelling and acute data — no hypertrophy comparison exists**, and he says
so. Its best move is a correction: Swinton tested both lifts at the same absolute loads, set by the
*conventional* 1RM, and found subjects could lift **8.4% more on the trap bar**. Scaling the trap
bar moments up by 8.4% **erases the hip and spinal flexion differences and leaves the knee
difference intact**. So: **more quads on the trap bar; glutes and spinal erectors similar.** The
hamstring claim (favouring conventional) rests only on a passive-tension length argument and **has
no data behind it at all**. **Low handles ≈ conventional range of motion; high handles are ~22%
shorter.** And **the trap bar is not a squat** — ~78° of knee flexion at the start versus 100–120°
for a parallel squat, with the hip moment meaningfully exceeding the knee moment. For athletes it is
clearly superior on acute force, power and velocity at 90% 1RM, and he prefers it for lower back
pain, because fatigue shifts load to the quads rather than the back when the weight is beside you.

## Weak points

**The general principles rest on more measured growth than the muscle-specific advice does.** Burke
2024, the leaning-back leg extension trial and the calf ROM and volume studies are real outcome
data. Much of the rest — the muscle-by-muscle case for isolation, the regional argument for
bodyweight training, the individual-recruitment framework, and the entire trap-bar comparison — is
EMG, moment-arm modelling or architecture reasoning in the same confident voice.

**Untrained subjects dominate.** The rectus femoris result (22), the squat-vs-leg-extension
replication (63), the squat-vs-hip-thrust tie (34), Fonseca (49), Kubo (17) and every calf ROM and
volume study are untrained. Burke 2024 (28 trained) and Baz-Valle (19 trained) are the exceptions.

**One direct contradiction across notes.**
[Maximize muscle growth](maximize-muscle-growth-new-study-exercise-choice.md) (2024) says
straight-leg calf raises may be all you need;
[Bigger calves](bigger-calves-ultimate-guide-32-studies.md) (2025) says bent-leg work is a
defensible soleus addition. Take the 2025 position.

**One unresolved inconsistency in how he treats a source.** Gentil 2015 is used at face value in
[Are isolation exercises useless](isolation-exercises-useless-muscle-hypertrophy-19-studies.md)
(2022), but by [Maximize muscle growth](maximize-muscle-growth-new-study-exercise-choice.md) (2024)
he flags **that same paper's author for involvement in multiple studies with statistical anomalies**
and discounts it. Both readings cannot stand.

**The citation problem, precisely located.** [Is the squat enough](squat-enough-leg-growth.md) is
the note you cannot check. Its Fonseca result is load-bearing for the rectus femoris story, but
[Bigger quads](bigger-quads-new-study-epic.md) and
[Unilateral vs bilateral](unilateral-vs-bilateral-training-muscle-growth.md) now reach the same
conclusion from verifiable sources, so the conclusion survives even if the note does not.

**Two firmer-than-warranted takeaways.** The band scepticism in
[The best training mode](best-training-mode-muscle-growth-most-get.md) has zero hypertrophy data
behind it. And the free-weights-versus-machines null in
[the 2020 note](free-weights-vs-machines-muscle-hypertrophy.md) is **two muscles, ultrasound, 8
weeks** — a null in an underpowered trial. Hernández-Belmonte 2023 is what makes that verdict safe,
not Schwanbeck.
