# Mentzer's One-Set Doctrine Against the Overtraining and Volume Literature

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Scientifically Dismantling Mike Mentzer's Claims" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u9liKtcxHEs)) · 2023-12-22 · 12 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- Mentzer's claim that going from one set to two is "the biggest mistake you could possibly make", and Arthur Jones's that extra sets "may produce a state of overtraining", both fail on the outcome data. Neither the fatigue argument nor the hypertrophy argument survives.
- **Overtraining from resistance training is extremely hard to produce on purpose.** Of 22 studies that deliberately tried, **10 failed to induce anything meeting the definition** ([Grandou 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31820373/)).
- **Fatigue from multiple hard sets resolves fast, and faster with training age.** Six bench sets to momentary failure left a neuromuscular fatigue marker essentially recovered at 48 h; five sets of 15 maximal eccentrics caused 5+ days of damage in week one and almost none by week ten.
- **More sets are not merely tolerable but generally better.** Meta-analysis puts **≥9 weekly sets per muscle** above fewer; his read of the best trained-subject data is **12–18 weekly sets**; and one study got greater quad growth from progressing to **42–52 weekly sets** than from 22.
- He does not overcorrect: low-volume training produces real gains, and choosing it for time or preference is fine. His objection is to the claim that it is superior *in all circumstances*.

## The fatigue argument

[01:04] Using the 2013 consensus definition — a performance decrease with or without symptoms lasting months or longer, with no subsequent gain — plus non-functional overreaching (weeks to months, no subsequent gain), [Grandou 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31820373/) reviewed 22 studies that set out to *induce* one of these states from resistance training. **Ten found nothing that qualified.** [Ratamess 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12741860/) is his worked example: mostly full-body training five days a week with 24 hours between sessions and maximal strength tests on Fridays. Bench and squat performance did drop in week two, then rebounded and **improved** over the study. Neither overtraining nor non-functional overreaching occurred.

[03:07] Two fatigue-recovery studies fill in the mechanism. [Refalo 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36752989/) had trained subjects perform six barbell bench sets to actual momentary failure; a neuromuscular fatigue marker was **virtually fully recovered at 48 hours**. He immediately flags this as one marker in one paper. [Margaritelis 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33156414/) is the stronger one: five sets of fifteen **maximal eccentric contractions** produced substantial soreness, damage and fatigue for at least five days after the first session — and by week ten of continued training, subjects showed virtually no soreness or damage and had recovered strength almost completely within **24 hours**. Recovery capacity is an adaptation, not a constant.

[04:10] Frequency data closes the argument. Volume-matched, weekly frequency does not matter much for hypertrophy ([Schoenfeld 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/)). [Colquhoun 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29324578/) gave trained subjects 12 weekly sets each of squat and bench, split across three or **six** sessions per week — the six-times group therefore trained on 24 hours of recovery, repeatedly — and fat-free mass and strength gains were comparable, with fat-free mass leaning slightly toward the higher frequency. [Zaroni 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31260419/) went further: 15 weekly sets per muscle, trained once or twice a week versus across five days, produced **greater back squat strength and overall better muscle growth in the high-frequency group**. His point is structural rather than promotional: if the fatigue from multiple sets were the problem Mentzer claimed, high-frequency training could not work at all. It does.

He then adds the honest counterweight — one, two or three sessions per muscle per week all produce great gains, and some individuals will not handle high frequency.

## The hypertrophy argument

[06:10] [Bickel 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/) is the study he says he had never covered before, and it is a good pick. **70 previously untrained** people trained quads three times a week, three sets per exercise, for 16 weeks — **27 weekly sets**, a lot — and both younger and older subjects grew well. For the next 32 weeks they were split into detraining, one-third volume (three sets once weekly), or one-ninth volume (one set once weekly). Older adults in **both** maintenance groups lost some size. Younger adults gained a little on one-third and **largely just maintained** on one-ninth. High volume built the size; minimal volume could not add to it.

[07:13] Two studies point the other way from the same premise. [Scarpelli 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32108724/) used a within-subject design: one leg did a standardised 22 weekly sets, the other did **20% more sets than that person had been doing before the study**, and vastus lateralis growth was better in the individualised leg. [Aube 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32058362/) found that across a range of assigned set counts, the subjects with the best gains were the ones who had **increased sets the most** relative to habit. He is careful here: this shows more sets aren't inherently harmful, not that everyone should keep adding them.

[08:15] On direct comparisons: [Burd 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20581041/) found myofibrillar protein synthesis greater and longer-lasting after three leg-extension sets than one. Individual hypertrophy trials split — some find no significant difference between one and three sets per exercise, others find three better — but pooled, [Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/) shows **multiple sets win**, with **nine or more weekly sets per muscle** beating fewer. Most of that is untrained subjects; in trained subjects he reads the best-designed data as favouring **12–18 weekly sets**, with individual and per-muscle variation. And [Enes 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37796222/) found progressing to **42–52 weekly quad sets** produced greater growth than 22, which he flags as having nuances covered elsewhere.

[09:47] A caveat he supplies unprompted and which matters: in all of these studies, the instructions and rep data indicate subjects trained **to or very near failure**. The volume figures are not for casual sets.

## Two things worth noting

**A small internal inconsistency.** He uses Burd's myofibrillar protein synthesis result as evidence for multiple sets here, having argued in his own high-frequency video that acute protein synthesis data cannot support training recommendations. He does caveat it (damage confounding, trained subjects, an isolation exercise) but the caveat is thinner than the one he applied when the same class of evidence pointed the other way.

**The fair ending.** [11:20] He declines to attack Mentzer or Jones personally, noting they lacked access to this research — while adding, correctly, that making firm conclusions in the absence of strong data was questionable then too. And he explicitly says low-set training produces gains and may suit people's goals and lives. The claim he rejects is only that it is superior in all circumstances and that higher volumes cause overtraining.
