# Periodization: Nothing for Size, Something for Strength

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Periodization for Size vs Strength (What the Science Says)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H-DffGucBNk)) · 2023-11-20 · 13 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- A **2022 Danish meta-analysis (Mosegaard et al.)** found **no significant hypertrophy difference between periodized and non-periodized training**, in trained and untrained subjects alike, and **no difference between linear and undulating models** either.
- The same meta-analysis found periodization **did** produce greater **1RM strength** gains than non-periodized training, in both populations — but he identifies a confound: the periodized arms typically spent more time at lower reps and heavier loads, which independently improves 1RM.
- The finding that survives the confound: **undulating beat linear for 1RM strength in trained subjects only**, with both models using heavy low-rep work equally often, so load distribution cannot explain it.
- His hypertrophy verdict is a genuine hedge: the meta-analysis says varying rep ranges adds nothing, but the studies are limited (many periodized arms went to ≤4 reps, most lasted ≤12 weeks), and three other lines of evidence weakly favour varying. He recommends varying rep ranges **if you want to**, and explicitly says the evidence is not strong enough to say you are missing out if you don't.
- The best argument for varying rep ranges is not average effects but **individual differences**: some people grow better at 8–12 reps and others at 27–31, and it may even differ between your own muscles. Varying is a hedge against not knowing which you are.

## The definitions he uses
[00:30] **Linear periodization**: reps decrease and load increases across weeks — volume (sets × reps) down, intensity up. **Daily undulating**: rep range varies session to session within a week, so volume and intensity go up and down. **Weekly undulating**: rep ranges cycle across weeks. **Non-periodized**: one rep range throughout, progressively overloaded.

He flags at the end [12:44] that he used "periodization" because the literature does, but doesn't think the term was appropriate in all the cases studied — a fair point given that several of these comparisons are really just "varied rep ranges versus one rep range."

## Hypertrophy: no effect
[01:30] His starting position is that reps between **6 and 35 produce similar hypertrophy** when taken to or close to failure, so varying rep ranges shouldn't obviously add anything — though a synergistic effect can't be ruled out a priori.

[02:00] The 2022 Danish meta-analysis found no significant hypertrophy difference between periodized and non-periodized training, holding for trained and untrained subjects, and no significant difference between linear and undulating models, again in both populations.

## Strength: an effect, and a confound
[03:32] The same meta-analysis found **greater 1RM gains with periodization**, in trained and untrained subjects.

[03:32–04:34] The confound he raises is the strongest analytical move in the video. Lower reps with heavier loads better increase 1RM — he cites a study where **3 sets of 2–4 reps** beat **3 sets of 8–12 reps** for bench and squat 1RM. In the meta-analysed studies, the periodized arms tended to include more heavy low-rep work. In one, the non-periodized group trained only at 8–10 reps while the periodized group included sets of 4–5. So periodization *per se* may not have caused the strength advantage. The study that would settle it — periodized versus non-periodized-but-heavy — has not been done.

[04:34] What the confound cannot explain: **undulating produced greater 1RM gains than linear, in trained subjects only**, and both models included heavy low-rep training equally often across the studies.

[05:36] He then argues, from mechanism rather than data, that pure heavy low-rep training probably isn't optimal for long-term strength: the same study that showed 2–4 reps beat 8–12 for strength showed 8–12 beat 2–4 for **growth**, and muscle growth adds force-generating units that should eventually contribute to strength. So a combination — low reps for skill and neural adaptation, higher reps for efficient growth — may be best long term. He labels this his current conclusion and promises future videos on the muscle-size-to-strength link; it is a plausible chain but not directly tested here.

## Three reasons the hypertrophy null might not settle it
[06:37] **Limitation 1**: in several studies the periodized group spent time at **4 or fewer reps**, which is not the most effective range for growth. A study where periodized groups never went below 6 reps has not been run.

**Limitation 2**: most studies lasted **12 weeks or less**. The longest to date is a **9-month** study (Kraemer) comparing non-periodized training against daily undulating varying between 4 and 15 reps. **Fat-free mass gains were greater in the undulating group.** He immediately notes the two weaknesses — fat-free mass is an indirect measure of muscle growth, and the subjects were previously untrained women — and calls it early evidence rather than a result.

[08:10] **Other supporting evidence**: a 12-week study in previously untrained older women where both groups did ascending pyramid training with three sets per exercise. The narrow group did 12/10/8 reps; the wide group did 15/10/5. **Muscle mass gains favoured the wider rep range** (Dos Santos et al.).

## The individual-differences argument
[08:40] He calls this the strongest reason to vary. The 6–35 rep equivalence is an *average*. One paper found some people grew the thigh better at **8–12 reps** and others at **27–31 reps**. Another used a within-subject design: previously untrained men trained seated and standing calf raises, four sets each session, **6–10 reps to failure on one leg and 20–30 on the other**. Average calf growth was similar, but individual subjects diverged — some aligned with the average, some grew better at 6–10, some at 20–30.

Since it is hard to identify your own best rep range, and it may differ **between muscles within one person**, varying rep ranges guarantees part of your program is in the right range. (I have not attributed these two papers to specific authors — the description's ordering does not let me map them confidently to Carneiro et al. or Schoenfeld et al., and a wrong attribution would be worse than none.)

## Practical position
[10:11] Varying rep ranges may be a good idea, and **no study has found it inferior**. But if you don't want to, keep training as you prefer — the evidence isn't strong enough to say you're losing a lot. If you do vary, the method is open: linear, undulating, pyramid, or different rep ranges on different exercises for the same muscle. For strength specifically, **undulating** looks better, so it's a reasonable choice for pursuing size and strength together.

[12:13] One myth cleared: the reason to vary is **not** that low reps grow fast-twitch fibres and high reps grow slow-twitch. He says the research does not support that.
