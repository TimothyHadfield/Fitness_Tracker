# 4 vs 8 vs 12 Reps: Same Muscle, Different Strength

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "4 vs 8 vs 12 Reps for Strength & Hypertrophy" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DaM0Kvzm_bQ)) · 2022-09-12 · 14 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- [Kubo 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32304514/): 42 physically active but untrained men, bench press 2×/week for 10 weeks, volume-load-equated. **Pectoralis major volume increased similarly in the 4, 8 and 12 rep groups.** Bench press 1RM was similar between 4 and 8, and **both beat 12**.
- Generalised position: **6–35 reps are likely similarly effective per set** for hypertrophy. **3–5 reps are less efficient per set** and require extra sets to match. **1–2 reps per set may not build muscle at all.**
- For strength, **heavier loads and ≤5 reps** are better, and the advantage likely grows with training status — in untrained subjects 4 and 8 tied.
- **~8 reps is the efficient middle ground** for developing strength and size concurrently: heavy enough to drive neural adaptations, not so heavy that you need extra sets for growth. It also avoids the joint-injury signal seen in low-rep work.
- The 4-rep group spent **nearly double** the per-session bench press time of the 8 and 12 groups.

## The Kubo study
42 physically active but not resistance-trained men, assigned to a 4, 8, 12 or control group (the control did not train and made no gains). All three training groups benched **twice per week for 10 weeks**.

Weeks 1–2 were identical and deliberately easy: 3×10 at 60% 1RM, then 3×8 at 70%. He rates this a good design choice — it accustomed untrained subjects to bench technique before the intervention. Week 3 was a transition (4 group: 5×3 at 90%; 8 group: 3×6 at 80%; 12 group: 2×10 at 70%). From week 4 onward:

- **4 group:** 7 sets of 4 at 90% 1RM
- **8 group:** 4 sets of 8 at 80% 1RM
- **12 group:** 3 sets of 12 at 70% 1RM

Set numbers differed because the researchers **equated volume load** (sets × reps × load). All subjects rested **3 minutes** between sets — a real strength of the study, since most rest 1 minute or less and longer rest tends to favour hypertrophy and strength on compounds.

Loads were progressed sensibly: subjects could not always sustain target reps across sets early on, but once they could hit the target in every set, load rose 2.5 kg the next session, keeping relative intensity where it belonged.

Outcomes were measured as bench press 1RM and **pectoralis major volume** — a 3D measure covering the whole muscle rather than the single-region CSA or thickness most studies use.

**Result: similar pec volume growth in all three groups; 1RM similar in 4 and 8, both greater than 12.**

## Why volume load is not the lesson
The obvious reading is that volume load is what matters, since it was equated. He rejects that: numerous lines of evidence suggest volume load specifically is not what tracks with gains.

His alternative reading is more useful. **Low reps (≤5) are less efficient per set** for stimulating growth, so they need more sets to compensate. Two supports:
1. With rep range held constant, **more sets produce more hypertrophy up to a point** ([Schoenfeld 2017 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/)). So extra sets are a plausible compensation mechanism.
2. [Schoenfeld 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27928218/): trained men, 3 sets per exercise per session — growth outcomes were largely **better with 8–12 reps to failure than 2–4 reps to failure**. But [Schoenfeld 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24714538/) found **7 sets of 3 to failure matched 3 sets of 10 to failure**. Same message: low reps work if you add sets.

On the upper end, [Schoenfeld 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25853914/) found in trained men that **8–12 reps to failure and 25–35 reps to failure** produced similar hypertrophy outcomes at 3 sets per exercise. Hence the 6–35 window.

He anticipates the obvious objection to his own framework: if more sets are better, why didn't the 8 group (4 sets) out-grow the 12 group (3 sets)? His answer — one extra set per session was probably not a big enough difference — is speculation, and he labels it as such.

**Overreach flag:** the "6–35 reps are similar per set" claim is doing a lot of work across the channel and rests substantially on single trained-subject studies from one lab. It is a defensible summary of the meta-analytic picture ([Lopez 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/), [Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)), but the precise 35-rep ceiling is not well pinned down.

## Strength is where the rep range matters
The Kubo result — lowest strength gains in the 12 group — agrees with the wider literature that heavier loads and lower reps drive 1RM better. The 4-vs-8 tie is probably an **untrained-subject artefact**: in trained lifters there is reason to think 4 would beat 8. The Schoenfeld 2016 study is the concrete case — 2–4 reps to failure produced **greater bench and squat strength gains** than 8–12, even though hypertrophy outcomes largely favoured 8–12.

So a strength-oriented program should be substantially **≤5 reps**.

## The strength–size correlation finding
Kubo also correlated bench strength gains with pec growth. In the **4 and 8 groups there was no correlation**; in the **12 group there was**.

His explanation: muscle size contributes to strength via added contractile material, but **neural adaptations** (stronger signalling from brain and spinal cord) also drive strength, and heavier-load training stimulates those more. In the 4 and 8 groups, neural adaptations clouded the size–strength relationship; the 12 group experienced fewer, letting the relationship show.

The implication he draws is genuinely interesting: as a lifter becomes more trained and essentially maxes out neural adaptations and other non-hypertrophic factors like movement efficiency, **hypertrophy becomes the remaining avenue to strength**. Since low reps are inefficient for hypertrophy per set, higher reps become a viable and efficient route to further strength in that context.

He then attacks it himself. He has **no idea when neural adaptations max out** — the common belief that they mostly occur in the first few months may be true, but there is evidence of continued neural adaptation in well-trained individuals ([Balshaw 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30387185/)). And it is theoretically possible to practically max out hypertrophy before maxing out neural adaptations. The conclusion he keeps is deliberately weak: **higher-rep training in strength athletes can be justifiable**.

## The 8-rep case
Pulling it together: ~8 reps is highly efficient for concurrent strength and size. Not so heavy that you need extra sets for growth, still heavy enough to drive neural adaptations well — probably as well as heavy loads in untrained lifters, somewhat less well but still respectably in trained ones.

One more argument for it: there is evidence that training below five reps may not favour joint health in some individuals. In the Schoenfeld study using **7 sets of 3 to failure**, **2 of 10 subjects sustained joint-related injuries**. Small numbers, but worth knowing.

## How this bears on the rep-range position
This note supports the library's line that hypertrophy is roughly equivalent across a wide rep window at matched effort — but adds two boundaries the simple version loses. The window is **6–35 reps per set**; **3–5 reps** still work but need extra sets, so they are less efficient rather than ineffective; and **1–2 reps** may not work at all. Strength does **not** follow the same rule: it tracks load, and the gap likely widens with training experience.
