# Training by Fibre Type: No Effect, No Way to Know Your Type, and No Reason to Try

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Should You Train Muscles According to Their Fiber Type? (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BWU835hWuvg)) · 2020-05-18 · 5 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

The most heavily referenced five minutes on the channel — 20 citations. Sixteen of them are one block of fibre-distribution surveys backing a single sentence, so the argument has three legs, not twenty.

## Bottom line
- **The test:** [Schoenfeld 2020](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.14427) used a within-subject design — **26 untrained young men**, one leg trained at a **6–10RM** load, the other at **20–30RM**, standing and seated calf raise, **4 sets to failure, 2×/week for 8 weeks**, with exercise order counterbalanced across sessions.
- The **soleus (predominantly type I)** and the **mixed-fibre gastrocnemius heads** responded **similarly to heavy and light loads**. The muscle whose fibre type is most extreme showed no load preference.
- **The second leg:** outside the soleus, the premise barely applies. Sixteen histochemical and biopsy surveys covering upper and lower body muscles show most muscles sit near an **even type I / type II split**.
- **The third leg:** the popular self-test — reps at a submaximal load tells you your fibre type — does not work. Douris 2006 found a statistically significant but weak relationship (**r = −0.48**, indirect fibre typing, untrained women); Terzis 2008, using **biopsies**, found **r < 0.2** between vastus lateralis fibre composition and reps at 70% and 85% 1RM in young men.
- Practical conclusion: fibre type is not a variable to programme around.

## Limitations he raises himself
This is a good example of his habit of arguing against his own conclusion. He lists, unprompted: the subjects were **untrained young men**, so it may not generalise; only **soleus and gastrocnemius** were studied; thickness was measured at a **single site (25% of lower leg length)**, so differential growth elsewhere in the muscle would have been missed; **no biopsies were taken**, so individual fibre composition was assumed rather than measured; and the study ran only **8 weeks**, leaving open the possibility that fibre-type-specific growth emerges over longer timescales.

He then argues the missing biopsies are the least worrying of these, because the soleus being predominantly slow-twitch and the gastrocnemius being mixed are among the best-established facts in the distribution literature.

## Where this lands against the library
This is the source of the library's standing position: **fibre type barely matters for rep selection, because most muscles are roughly 50/50, with the soleus (~90% type I) the exception.**

It also sits directly opposite [Menno Henselmans' argument](../Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/training-different-muscle-fiber-types.md) that the field over-concluded from exactly this evidence. The two do not actually collide on facts — Henselmans accepts the null findings and disputes the inference, on the grounds that (a) **capillary density**, not fibre type, predicts reps at a given %1RM, and (b) fibre type shows up in **recovery** rather than in growth per set.

The tight link is that **the Terzis 2008 paper Dhim uses to kill the rep-test is titled "Fiber Type Composition *and Capillary Density*"** — the same study is the origin of Henselmans' counter-argument. Dhim reads its fibre-type correlation (r < 0.2) and stops; Henselmans reads its capillary-density correlation and continues. Neither is misreporting; they are reading different columns of one table.

Note also the difference in what each is arguing about: this video concerns **between-muscle** variation (should the soleus be trained differently from the gastrocnemius), while Henselmans is arguing about **between-person** variation. That is why both positions can stand.

**Flagged:** the closing line — that fibre type "is not a consideration that needs to go into constructing a training program" — is broader than the evidence shown. One 8-week untrained-subject calf study plus distribution surveys plus two correlation studies establish that *load prescription* need not track fibre type. They say nothing about recovery, fatigue cost, or long-term programming, which is precisely the gap Henselmans works in.
