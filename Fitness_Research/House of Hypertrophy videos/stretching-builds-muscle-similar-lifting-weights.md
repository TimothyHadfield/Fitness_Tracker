# An Hour of Daily Calf Stretching Grew Muscle — Read the Protocol Before Believing the Title

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Stretching Builds Muscle SIMILAR to Lifting Weights" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s1kdl02N5wk)) · 2023-05-15 · 6 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- One study drives the whole video: [Warneke 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37029826/), **69 subjects with *some* resistance training experience**, **6 weeks**, calves only.
- The stretch protocol is the headline number the title hides: **one hour of static calf stretch every single day, held at 7–8/10 on a pain scale, using a stretching device**. The lifting comparator was **calf presses, 5 × 10–12 full-ROM reps to failure, 3×/week**.
- Both groups increased isometric straight-leg and bent-knee plantar flexion strength and both increased lateral and medial gastrocnemius thickness, with **non-significant between-group differences**. Percentages favoured stretching for straight-leg strength and favoured lifting for lateral gastrocnemius growth.
- This is the **first human study to show it**, and it is a genuinely interesting mechanistic result. It is not a training recommendation, and he does not present it as one.
- The likely mechanism: **passive tension is still mechanical tension**. Mechanosensors respond to force whether the fibre generates it actively or has it imposed by a stretch.

## What the study actually shows, and what it does not
The population matters. Subjects had "some degree of resistance training experience", not trained calves — and calves are the classic under-trained muscle even in trained lifters, so a six-week novel stimulus has room to produce growth in either arm.

The strength result has a specificity problem Dhim raises himself: the stretching was done with a straight leg and the strength test was an isometric straight-leg contraction, so the test suited the stretch group's training better. He predicts dynamic strength tests would have favoured the lifting group, which is the right way round.

On growth, the individual data matter. Many subjects in both groups gained comparably, but a handful of data points pulled the lateral gastrocnemius result toward lifting and the straight-leg strength result toward stretching. He says plainly it is unclear whether these are real or error. Medial gastrocnemius was the most comparable outcome.

**Flagged, hard:** "non-significant difference between groups" is not equivalence, especially with roughly two dozen people per arm over six weeks. The title's claim outruns a single short trial in untrained-calf subjects on one muscle group using an intervention nobody will do. The video is far more measured than the title: he states the limitations, calls the protocol impractical, and pitches the use case as **rehab for injured people who cannot train normally**.

## Where the prior evidence sat
Animal work found enormous effects — cross-sectional area increases up to **141%** and maximal strength up to **95%** ([Alway 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2523374/)), and he recalls seeing a figure as extreme as a 318% increase in muscle mass. Those protocols held stretches for **up to 24 hours**, which is why they never transferred. In humans, stretches held **up to two minutes** produce no meaningful hypertrophy or strength gains ([Nunes 2020 review](https://doi.org/10.1111/cpf.12622)). Warneke sits in the previously untested middle: intense, hours-scale, human.

## The mechanism argument
Mechanical tension is the force experienced by muscle fibres, and it can be **active** (the fibre contracting) or **passive** (the fibre stretched); mechanosensors convert either into a hypertrophic signal. His hypothesis is that an hour at 7–8/10 generates passive tension large enough to cross that threshold — supported by animal work where **equating peak tension between stretch and eccentric contractions produced similar hypertrophic signalling** ([Rindom 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/apha.13336), a rat mTORC1 dose-response study on tension per se).

He handles the alternative explanation honestly: muscle damage cannot be ruled out as a contributor, but several lines of evidence argue damage is not the primary stimulus, and it subsides via the repeated bout effect as you adapt.
