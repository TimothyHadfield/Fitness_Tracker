# Do Heavy and Light Loads Grow Different Parts of a Muscle? One Study Says Maybe

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Do Heavy and Light Weights Produce Different Regional Muscle Growth?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4VIWBKnFWI0)) · 2021-04-05 · 11 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- The load-equivalence finding — 30–85% 1RM builds similar muscle when sets go to or near failure — comes from studies that measured growth at **one region of one muscle**. Regional differences would be invisible to them.
- **One study** has looked at this for conventional training: a 1996 paper by Hisaeda and colleagues, **11 untrained women**, leg extension, 3x/week for 8 weeks. Total quadriceps CSA gains were similar, but the **heads that grew differed**: the 15–20 rep group grew the **vastus lateralis** more, the 4–5 rep group grew the **vastus intermedius and rectus femoris** more.
- The same study reported **significant CSA increases at 30% of thigh length (upper) for the light group and at 70% (lower) for the heavy group** — but did not report which heads that applied to.
- The study is badly confounded. The two groups differed not only in reps and load but in **sets (5–6 vs 8–9), rest (90s vs as-needed), and tempo (2s/2s vs as fast as possible)**. Tempo alone has separately been shown to alter regional growth.
- **Opposing evidence:** a study of **12 untrained men** doing isometric triceps contractions at 100% vs 60% of maximum for 10 weeks measured CSA at **20 regions** along the muscle and found **similar increases at every region**.
- His practical takeaway — use a variety of loads — is reasonable but does not follow from this evidence; it follows from the low cost of load variety, not from a demonstrated regional benefit.

## The Hisaeda study in detail
The hypertrophy group did 5–6 sets of 15–20 reps to failure with a 2-second concentric and 2-second eccentric and 90 seconds rest. The strength group did 8–9 sets of 4–5 reps to failure with both phases performed **as fast as possible** and unrestricted rest. Cross-sectional area of rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis and vastus intermedius was measured at **30%, 50% and 70% of thigh length**, and the three regional values for each head were summed for analysis. Summing them is what hides the regional data: the per-region numbers were never reported, only the aggregate per head, plus the passing statement about which thigh-length regions reached significance for each group.

Read at face value, the study says light-load high-rep leg extensions bias toward the vastus lateralis and possibly the upper regions, while heavy low-rep leg extensions bias toward the rectus femoris and vastus intermedius and possibly the lower regions. He states this, then immediately dismantles how much weight it can carry. With four variables differing between groups, load is not isolated — and he singles out **tempo** as the most plausible alternative explanation, because he has previously covered evidence that tempo itself alters regional growth. So the result may have nothing to do with load at all.

## The fibre-type hypothesis, and why he abandons it
The proposed mechanism: heavy loads might preferentially recruit regions and heads richer in **fast-twitch** fibres, light loads regions richer in **slow-twitch**. It fits at first glance — the vastus intermedius and especially the rectus femoris are somewhat more fast-twitch, and grew more in the heavy group; the vastus lateralis is closer to a 50/50 split, and grew more in the light group. A supporting observation: most fibre-typing studies biopsy a **single region**, and research sampling multiple regions of the same muscle finds fibre composition varies across it — which could in principle explain upper-versus-lower differences.

Then he breaks his own hypothesis with two internal contradictions from the same dataset:

1. The heavy group grew the **vastus intermedius slightly more than the rectus femoris**, but the rectus femoris is the more fast-twitch of the two. The prediction is backwards.
2. The light group's largest gain was the **vastus lateralis**, but the **vastus medialis** is more slow-twitch. Again backwards.

He extends the hypothesis to tempo — slow tempos biasing slow-twitch regions, fast tempos biasing fast-twitch — while making clear this is speculation layered on a hypothesis he has just shown does not fit the data. That is the honest handling, and it is the strongest part of the video.

## The isometric counter-evidence
The only other relevant study used isometric triceps contractions at 90 degrees of elbow flexion, 3x/week for 10 weeks: the 100% group did 12 sets of 6 seconds, the 60% group did 4 sets of 30 seconds. With CSA measured at **20 regions** across the triceps, growth was similar at all of them.

He gives two reasons not to treat this as settling the question. It used **isometrics**, so it says nothing about exercises with concentric and eccentric phases. And it pooled the triceps across its length without distinguishing **long, medial and lateral heads** — which is precisely the level at which Hisaeda found the difference.

## Where he lands
His summary: Hisaeda gives some evidence of regional differences, the isometric study gives some opposing evidence, the mechanism is unconvincing, and much more research is needed. He then links to his earlier work on alternating rep ranges — over 12 weeks or less, varied loads and a single load produce similar growth, with a possible long-term advantage to variety — and argues that potential regional differences add a further reason to use varied loads. He also names two alternative routes to balanced growth across a muscle: **varying tempo**, and **varying exercises**.

**Flagged:** the practical recommendation is built on a single confounded n=11 study whose mechanism he has just disproved, against a contradicting study he accepts as limited. The recommendation is harmless — varying loads costs nothing — but the evidence chain supporting it is much weaker than the confident framing of the closing section suggests.
