# Three Myths: Strength Causing Size, Progressive Overload, and Lifting Making You Stiff

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "POPULAR Muscle & Strength Building MYTHS" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n4lfJG3FqCM)) · 2023-03-18 · 6 min
**Topic:** Evidence & method

## Bottom line

| Myth | Verdict | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|
| Getting stronger causes you to get bigger | **Backwards** | 1RM-only training produces large strength gains with essentially no hypertrophy; hypertrophy adds force-generating units, so size drives strength rather than the reverse |
| Progressive overload causes muscle and strength gains | **Half-true, and misleading in practice** | Overload is a *cycle* — you can add load because you grew; treating it as the cause pushes you to break form and to pick exercises that load well rather than exercises that grow the muscle |
| Lifting weights makes you stiff and inflexible | **Not supported** | An 11-study meta-analysis found no significant difference in joint range-of-motion gains between resistance training and stretching |

## Myth I — strength causes size

The pro-myth evidence is correlational: several studies on elite and competitive powerlifters find strong relationships between lean mass and bench, squat and deadlift performance ([Ye 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23828289/), [Brechue 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11990746/), [Keogh 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19826300/), [Reya 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30908368/)). Dhim reads the arrow the other way: hypertrophy is largely an increase in the force-generating units a fibre contains, which mechanically increases strength ([Taber 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01107-8)).

His falsification test is the cleaner argument. If strength caused size, training at your 1RM several times a week would be an excellent hypertrophy programme. Three studies from the Loenneke/Buckner group show it is not — 1RM practice produced large strength gains and virtually no muscle growth ([Dankel 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27875635/), [Mattocks 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28463902/), [Buckner 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33724646/)).

He is careful to keep the individual-level claim separate: the strongest version of *you* will probably also be close to the most muscular version of you, and he flags that he owes fuller videos on the complications.

## Myth II — progressive overload

Two failure modes follow from treating overload as the cause rather than the consequence. First, forcing load or reps at the expense of technique. Second, preferring exercises that are easy to overload on the assumption that they must therefore build more muscle.

His counter-example is [Maeo 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/): subjects progressively overloaded pushdowns to a greater extent than overhead extensions, yet triceps hypertrophy was clearly superior with the overhead variation. Biomechanics that let you move more load are not the same thing as biomechanics that deliver more stimulus to the muscle.

## Myth III — lifting and flexibility

A 2021 Portuguese meta-analysis of 11 studies found no significant difference in ROM gains between resistance training and stretching ([Afonso 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33917036/)). Most included studies used full-ROM lifting; two used partials at long muscle lengths, and the stretching arms were largely static.

He explicitly refuses to over-read it: the 11 studies were heterogeneous, so this is not proof that lifting always matches dedicated stretching for ROM — only that lifting does not make you stiff. He adds the supporting mechanism that full-ROM and long-length partial training increase fascicle length.

**Flagged:** the fascicle-length-to-flexibility step is mechanistic ("presumably conferring flexibility"), not an outcome finding, and he says so.
