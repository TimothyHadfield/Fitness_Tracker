# Some Sets Further From Failure: Probably Fine, Not Yet Proven

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Train EASIER for Size & Strength? This NEW Study Is Interesting" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JbU6sO9q3bs)) · 2025-09-08 · 8 min
**Topic:** Effort & failure

## Bottom line
- First study to compare a **fixed** proximity to failure against a **varied** one. 31 trained individuals; no significant difference in muscle growth or strength.
- Fixed group left 1 RIR on every set. Varied group cycled 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 RIR across each four-week block.
- The researchers **tested RIR accuracy** rather than assuming it — the largest average error was −1.5 reps (3-RIR squat estimate), and accuracy improved by the retest.
- His recommendation does **not** change: default to 0–2 RIR for hypertrophy. One small study is not enough to move it.
- Where he does think it applies now: strength goals (proximity to failure matters much less), very high-volume phases, and low-energy days where the alternative is skipping the session.

## The study
31 resistance-trained individuals ran an upper/lower programme across two four-week blocks.

- **Fixed group:** 1 rep in reserve on every set, throughout.
- **Varied group:** 4 RIR in week 1, 3 RIR in week 2, 2 RIR in week 3, 1 RIR in week 4 — repeated in the second block.

A deload sat between the blocks and after the final one, with frequency and volume halved and all sets at 1 RIR.

**RIR accuracy testing.** Subjects did back squat and bench press sets to failure at 80% 1RM, calling out when they believed they had three reps left and again at one rep left, then continuing to true failure. Four measurements in total. The worst average error was **−1.5 on the 3-RIR squat estimate** — call three, actually do 4.5 more. Everything else was tighter. On retest after the study, both groups improved on average: worst error around −1, with several measures showing essentially no average error.

**Results.** Vastus lateralis cross-sectional area and triceps thickness: no significant differences, though triceps growth leaned toward the varied group. Bench and squat 1RM: no significant differences, though men's bench press leaned toward the fixed group. He explicitly refuses to read anything into either lean.

## The limitations he raises himself
This is the video's real value — the caveat list is his, not mine.

1. **No true-failure group.** Strictly, we don't know how failure training would have compared. He argues we can infer it wouldn't have won, since other research finds no difference between 1 RIR and failure — an inference, not a result.
2. **Partial supervision.** Only week 4 was fully supervised. Mitigated by extensive weekly training logs and filmed bench and squat sets on two occasions.
3. **Individual variation in RIR accuracy.** Group averages were decent; individual data points deviated a fair bit.
4. **Accuracy tested on two lifts only.** He suspects estimation would be worse on Bulgarian split squats and near-perfect on an incline dumbbell curl. Reasonable, unmeasured.
5. **Small sample, single study.** Stated as the reason he won't shift his recommendations.

## His position on failure generally
Summarised in passing, and worth recording: with **low volumes**, training to failure may be superior. With **moderate to high volumes** — which are generally better for growth anyway — 1–2 RIR is likely sufficient. For **strength**, proximity to failure has a much smaller effect and training well short of failure is fine.

## Practical
He is clear that "some sets further from failure may be fine" does not license restructuring your programme around 3–4 RIR sets today. Where he is comfortable:

- Strength-primary training — already well supported.
- Very high-volume experiments or muscle-group specialisation blocks, where easier sets make the volume manageable while you acclimatise.
- Low-energy days, as an alternative to skipping.

## Flag
Nothing overreaches here. The one soft spot is the inference in point 1 above — using external literature to fill in the missing failure arm — which he presents as an inference rather than a finding.
