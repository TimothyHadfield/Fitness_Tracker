# Antagonist Supersets: More Reps in Less Time

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Increase PERFORMANCE While Saving Time (11 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NGiq7cpCiAE)) · 2022-09-05 · 11 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Pairing opposing muscle groups back to back (row/bench, leg curl/leg extension, curl/triceps extension) **does not reduce rep performance** — with minimal rest between the two exercises it consistently **increases** it.
- The rest interval between the paired exercises is the variable that matters. **Minimal rest raises reps; 2+ minutes does not** — but longer-rest pairing still saves time without hurting anything.
- **Stretching the antagonist** during the rest period produces a similar acute benefit (three near-identical Brazilian studies on chest stretching before seated rows).
- The **long-term hypertrophy and strength evidence is essentially absent**: two studies, both badly limited, neither showing an advantage. The acute performance benefit has not been shown to convert into more muscle.
- Fair summary: supersets are a **time-saving tool that probably doesn't cost you anything and might help**. That is a much weaker claim than the acute data alone suggests.

## Part I: acute performance
[00:32] **Maia et al. (2014, Brazil)** compared leg extensions alone against leg curls performed before leg extensions. Doing the curls first **increased leg extension reps** — and the **shorter the rest between the two exercises, the better**. Going straight into the extensions beat rest intervals of 30 s, 1, 3 and 5 minutes. Sets to failure with a 10RM load.

[01:33] A 2015 Brazilian study compared three sets of bench press then three of seated row against supersetting the two with virtually no rest. **Volume load was higher on both exercises in the superset condition** — and since sets and loads (10RM) were matched, that means more reps. The superset session was also shorter. Three further studies point the same way, all using minimal rest.

[02:35] **Robbins et al. (2010, Australia)** is the informative counter-case. The superset condition paired bench pull with bench press but used **2 minutes of rest between the two exercises** (4 minutes between repeats); the traditional condition did all pulls then all presses with 4 minutes between sets. All reps to failure at a 4RM load. **Volume load did not differ statistically** — no performance gain, but the protocol still finished quicker.

So the rule is specific: minimal rest between the paired exercises increases reps; longer rest does not, though it is not harmful and still saves time.

## Part II: antagonist stretching
[03:56] If you can't superset — busy gym, equipment unavailable — stretching the opposing muscle during the rest period may do something similar. **Three Brazilian studies of essentially the same design** found stretching the chest immediately before seated rows increased seated row rep performance versus passive rest.

### Why might either work?
[04:36] The proposed mechanism is **reduced antagonistic co-activation**. During any movement the antagonist contracts somewhat for joint stability — hamstrings during a leg extension. Pre-fatiguing or stretching it may reduce that, letting the agonist produce more force. He states plainly this is a hypothesis, not established.

[05:07] On safety, he says he doesn't know the definitive answer but sees reason to think it's fine — no study reported adverse effects, and **long-term training chronically reduces antagonist co-activation anyway** (Balshaw 2019), one of the mechanisms behind long-term strength gains.

## Part III: does it build more muscle?
[05:56] Here the video honestly deflates. For **antagonist stretching there is no long-term research at all**. For supersets there are two studies, both limited.

**Robbins et al. (2009, Australia).** 15 men, ≥1 year training experience, 8 weeks. Traditional group did all bench pull sets then bench press or bench throw, 4 min between sets. Superset group paired them with **2 min between the paired exercises**, 4 min between repeats. Sets and reps matched. Session duration: **~20 min traditional vs ~10 min superset**. 1RM gains **not statistically different**; percentages numerically favoured supersets for the bench pull, which he attributes to possible chance. Crucially this used the 2-minute rest we already know does **not** raise performance, so similar gains are expected. Hypertrophy was not measured. What it shows is that supersets **halve session time without costing strength**.

**Fink et al. (2022, Japan).** 23 **untrained** individuals, 8 weeks, 3×/week, **resistance-band** biceps curls and overhead triceps extensions. Traditional: 3 sets of each, 1 min rest. Superset: 3 supersets, minimal rest between exercises, 1 min between supersets. All sets to failure. **Biceps and triceps cross-sectional area gains did not differ**, nor did isometric strength — and in both cases the **percentages numerically favoured the traditional group**.

[09:12] He does not hide that this is unfavourable. His explanation: the superset group **did not achieve increased volume performance** here, unlike every acute study. He suspects the bands — light enough that subjects reached **30–60 reps** per set, whereas every study showing a performance benefit used free weights or machines at **10RM or heavier**. If co-activation scales with load, the effect may only exist with heavier resistance. He labels this explicitly as speculation that further depends on the co-activation hypothesis being correct.

## Practical read
[10:33] Supersets and antagonist stretching reliably improve acute rep performance, and supersets reliably save time. Whether that becomes more muscle or strength is **unknown**; his position is that at minimum they very likely don't compromise gains. Worth experimenting with if the time saving appeals.

**Where he is rigorous:** he presents the study undercutting his own thesis (Fink) in full, states its numbers favour the control group, and flags his explanation for it as speculation resting on an unproven mechanism. He also identifies why the one long-term strength study couldn't have shown a benefit given its rest intervals.

**Where the evidence is thin:** the case rests almost entirely on **acute rep-count and EMG measures**. Nine of the eleven references concern acute performance or mechanism; the two chronic studies show nothing. Extra reps within a session are a plausible route to more growth but have not been shown to deliver it here.
