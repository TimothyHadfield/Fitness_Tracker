# The Placebo Steroid Studies: What Belief Does to Strength

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "This HOLDS BACK Your Muscle & Strength Potential" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rHyu81hfrIo)) · 2022-07-04 · 9 min
**Topic:** Evidence & method

## Bottom line
- Two old and startling studies show that **believing** you are on anabolic steroids can produce large, immediate strength gains — and that removing the belief removes the gains.
- In one, six trained athletes gained 2–10× more strength over four weeks on placebo "Dianabol" than they had over the preceding seven weeks of honest training.
- In another, 11 nationally ranked powerlifters all set personal records on placebo pills — enough, the authors noted, to shift every subject from nationally to internationally ranked. Told the pills were fake, they dropped straight back to baseline.
- Neither study measured **muscle growth**. The extension from strength to hypertrophy is his speculation, and he says so.
- The practical takeaway he lands on is modest: you cannot fake-dose yourself, but you can stop telling yourself you are a bad responder.

## The framing study
Crum and Corbin gave 46 people a milkshake on two separate days. One was labelled indulgent — high fat, high calorie. The other was labelled sensible — low fat, low calorie. Ghrelin, the hunger-signalling hormone that falls after adequate intake, dropped sharply after the "indulgent" shake and barely moved after the "sensible" one.

The shakes were identical. The labels were the entire intervention.

## Ariel and Saville
Fifteen male varsity athletes with at least two years of lifting trained for seven weeks. They were told the best gainers would earn free steroids for a subsequent four-week block. Six were selected and given a daily 10mg "Dianabol" tablet — actually a placebo.

Over the initial seven honest weeks, those six gained on average **+4.35 kg bench, +0.73 kg standing press, +2.27 kg seated press, +2.65 kg squat**.

Over the four placebo weeks: **+13.28 kg bench, +7.58 kg standing press, +5.30 kg seated press**, and a squat figure the auto-captions garble (approximately 18.9 kg). Roughly two to ten times the gains in a little over half the time.

## Maganaris and colleagues
The lead author was a powerlifting coach whose 11 nationally ranked athletes had asked him about steroids. Baseline averages: **189 kg bench, 257 kg squat, 260 kg deadlift** — genuinely strong lifters.

Five minutes before testing, subjects were given two tablets described as immediate-acting anabolic steroids. Every subject hit a personal record: **+9.5 kg bench, +12.2 kg squat, +10.9 kg deadlift** on average.

They then took more placebo pills through a training week, during which all reported greater vigour and heavier or higher-rep sessions than ever. At the second testing session, **some subjects were told the truth first**. Those subjects lifted **7.5 kg less on bench, 11 kg less on squat and 12.5 kg less on deadlift** than in the first session — back to baseline. Those left unaware roughly held their gains.

## Flag
Both studies are small (n=6 and n=11), decades old, and neither is a controlled trial in the modern sense — Ariel and Saville in particular has no comparison group, and a four-week block following a seven-week block invites a training-effect explanation he does not address. He is careful about one thing: he notes neither study measured hypertrophy and calls the extension to muscle growth merely "possible."

The application is honest about its own limits — you cannot convince yourself a vitamin D tablet is Dianabol. What he asks for is the negative version: if you routinely tell yourself you have poor strength or muscle-building capacity, that belief has a documented physiological cost, and the gains in these studies came from the athletes themselves.
