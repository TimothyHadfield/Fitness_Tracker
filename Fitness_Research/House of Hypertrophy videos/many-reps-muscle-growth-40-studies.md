# The Whole Rep-Range Literature in One Place: 6 to 35 Reps, With Caveats at Both Ends

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How Many Reps for Muscle Growth? (40+ Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dN_c4sQwfTI)) · 2024-12-16 · 20 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- **Five meta-analyses agree**: growth is similar across a wide load span provided sets reach or approach failure. He puts the underlying data at roughly **6–35 reps**, not the 5–30 others quote, on the grounds that 6–35 better represents the actual rep counts in the included trials.
- The "wide range works" result **survives in trained lifters running full programmes** — two trials had trained subjects do three sets per exercise at 8–12 vs 20–35 reps to failure, with similar growth.
- **Fibre-type specificity does not hold.** Fast-twitch and slow-twitch growth were similar between high and low loads in the fibre-level meta-analysis, and a soleus-specific trial (a predominantly slow-twitch muscle) found similar growth at 20–30 and 6–10 reps.
- **The low end is where it breaks.** One-rep sets produce close to zero growth across four studies. Two to four reps produces detectable growth, but on a per-set basis it looks worse than 8–12; the five papers claiming parity gave the low-rep group more sets or longer rest.
- **The high end is soft, not hard.** 35 is not a cliff — one trial averaged 100 reps on the first set and still matched an 80% 1RM condition — but 20% 1RM (60+ reps) was clearly inferior in the cleanest comparison.
- **Higher reps cost more fatigue, not less growth.** Recovery is slower after light-load work, with even a contralateral strength drop suggesting central fatigue — yet hypertrophy still comes out equal.

## What the meta-analyses actually establish
He opens with a survey of 127 competitive bodybuilders in which the majority trained at 7–12 reps, then immediately notes this describes practice, not optimality — a good example of his habit of undercutting his own hook.

The five meta-analyses cover different growth measures: lean mass, whole-muscle size, and fibre cross-sectional area. All converge. Two boundary conditions matter and he states both. First, the included studies took sets **to or very near failure** — the equivalence claim is conditional on that. Second, most used a **normal 1–2 second concentric and 1–2 second eccentric**; the 6–35 window does not transfer to deliberately slow reps. He cites a trial in which 6–10 reps with a 10-second lift and 4-second lower was *inferior* for growth.

The obvious objection — that much of this is untrained subjects doing two or three exercises — he answers directly with the two trained, full-programme studies.

## The fibre-type question
Older Russian work suggested load-specific fibre growth. He flags its limitations rather than dismissing it, then points to the fibre-level meta-analysis showing similar type I and type II growth across loads, and to a study of the soleus specifically — a muscle where the specificity hypothesis makes its strongest prediction — where 20–30 and 6–10 reps produced comparable growth.

The mechanism he offers is recruitment: heavy loads recruit high-threshold units immediately; light loads recruit them progressively as fatigue accumulates, so both routes end at high recruitment near failure. He supports this with a study in trained subjects finding similar fast- and slow-twitch activation across four sets of leg extensions at high and low loads. **Flagged:** recruitment at failure is an acute surrogate. The hypertrophy claim rests entirely on the outcome data; the recruitment material is explanation, not evidence, and he does not always keep that boundary explicit.

## How low can you go
**One rep does essentially nothing.** Four studies compared 3–4 sets of 8–12 reps against at most five 1RM attempts per session. All four favoured 8–12, and all four found the 1RM condition produced very little to no growth.

**Two to four reps is ambiguous.** A trained-subject study with three sets per exercise found growth tended to favour 8–12 over 2–4. An untrained back-squat study using four sets at 3–5, 13–15 or 23–25 reps found no significant difference between any of them. He then handles the five papers that report parity for 2–4 reps by pointing out that each gave the low-rep condition **more sets and/or longer rest** — an unusually clean piece of methodological criticism, and the reason he treats the parity claim as unproven rather than refuted.

Two supporting lines: muscle protein synthesis after leg extensions was greater at 30% than at 90% 1RM (about five reps), caveated because muscle damage clouds MPS interpretation and light and heavy loads don't damage equally; and an older periodisation study in which lean-mass gains stalled when subjects moved to three sets of three on core lifts, caveated as an imprecise measure.

His practical line: **don't train entirely below five or six reps**, but a handful of very low-rep sets is fine. He backs the second half with a curl study where the 80% 1RM group averaged three reps on the final set yet still matched the 30% group, and with the varied-vs-constant loading study (2–4 on Monday, 8–12 Wednesday, 20–30 Friday vs 8–12 throughout) that found similar growth — noting the percentages nominally favouring variation were driven by outliers. Two further varied-rep studies, neither dipping below six reps, also found parity with a constant ~8-rep protocol. Conclusion: **varying rep ranges is neither better nor worse**.

The one contrary long-term signal is a nine-month study in which varied rep ranges beat an 8–10 rep group for fat-free mass. He declines to lean on it: one study, skinfold calipers, not a good hypertrophy measure. Worth noting, because it is the kind of finding a less careful presenter would have led with.

## How high can you go
Above 35 reps the picture is mixed. A curl study whose light condition averaged **100 reps on the first set** still matched 80% 1RM — partly because loads weren't adjusted until mid-study, so subjects progressed by adding reps. The best-designed comparison used 20/40/60/80% 1RM to failure across 3–5 sets: **40–80% were similar; 20% (averaging over 60 reps) grew muscle but less**. Two studies at 15% vs 70% split — one parity, one favouring the heavier load — and both had confounds he names: shorter rest in the light condition and sets terminated at 90 reps regardless of failure. His position: 35 is not a hard cutoff, but he would not recommend super-high reps on current data.

## Fatigue, and why it doesn't change the recommendation
Higher reps cause **more** damage and slower recovery, not less. In one leg-extension study, recovery was slower after 40% than 80% 1RM, and strength dropped in the untrained opposite leg immediately and up to 24 hours after — a signature of central fatigue. He is careful that this fatigue evidently isn't severe enough to make high reps inferior: three studies found high reps held up at 18+ weekly sets per muscle, and repeated-bout adaptation is real (untrained subjects took over five days to recover from five sets of 15 maximal eccentrics, but only a day or two after eight weeks).

He concedes the practical point: **6–12 is a defensible zone** on fatigue-management grounds, and some individuals may simply not tolerate high-rep work at volume. He just won't let that become an efficacy claim.

## Failure with light loads
The claim under test is that light loads *must* reach failure while heavy loads need not. A meta-regression did find proximity to failure mattered less at heavier loads, but he inspects the direct comparisons behind it and is unconvinced. Training *far* from failure with light loads clearly underperforms; stopping a *few* reps short may not. Supporting: a leg-extension study at 30% 1RM comparing true momentary failure to stopping at voluntary discomfort found similar growth; blood-flow-restriction studies find failure and near-failure equivalent; and a trained-subject study comparing heavy lower-body work at 1–3 RIR to high-rep BFR at 1–4 RIR found similar hypertrophy including at the fibre level.

His practical caveat is the best part of the section: lifters **underestimate how close to failure they are with lighter loads** — probably because high reps hurt more and pain masks true proximity — so with high reps, take at least some sets to failure to recalibrate.

**Individual differences:** two papers suggested some people respond better to one rep range, but a 2024 methodology paper argues those designs cannot detect individual response at all. He accepts the critique and suggests trying a different rep range only if progress has stalled with everything else dialled in.
