# Training Frequency: The Within-Subject Study That Shows It Depends on You

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The BEST Training Frequency (New Research)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X82vvocqe-8)) · 2022-11-14 · 11 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- A within-subject study (Neves et al., 24 trained men) had **one leg train once per week and the other three times per week** with weekly sets equated. Neither strength nor quad growth differed statistically.
- But **effect sizes and confidence intervals leaned toward 3x/week**, and the individual data show some men gained clearly more with the higher frequency while others gained the same or slightly more with once weekly. One subject gained markedly more strength on the once-weekly leg.
- The headline is not "3x beats 1x". It is that **frequency response varies between individuals**, and this is the first design clean enough to show that experimentally.
- Existing meta-analyses (hypertrophy 2019, strength 2017) find no frequency difference, but they pool between-subject studies, mixed frequency comparisons and low weekly set counts — and can say nothing about individual variation.
- Practical: if you train at a low frequency and are progressing, change nothing. If you are not, **higher frequency is a worthwhile personal experiment**.

## The study
[00:30] 24 trained men, average unilateral leg press 1RM of **203 kg**. Two groups, and he only uses one of them.

The first group equated **total tonnage** between the 1x and 3x conditions. That forced the once-weekly condition to do maximum reps per set while the three-times-weekly condition held back well short of failure to keep tonnage matched. He discards this group: proximity to failure is crucial for growth, tonnage relates weakly to growth, and the arrangement does not resemble real training. He points readers to a pinned comment for its details rather than dropping it silently.

[01:32] The second group is the useful one. **One leg trained once per week** (9 sets of max reps on the unilateral leg press, all in one session); **the other leg trained three times per week** (3 sets per session, three sessions). Weekly sets were identical at nine. Target rep numbers varied by week and load was adjusted per set to hit them.

[02:02] Outcomes: unilateral leg press 1RM, and quadriceps cross-sectional area at around the 50% region. **Neither reached statistical significance.**

## Why he does not stop at "no significant difference"
[02:02] He makes the methodological point that a non-significant result is not proof of no difference — citing a Nature piece on p-values — and goes to the other statistics the researchers reported.

[02:33] Effect sizes and their confidence intervals **point toward the three-times-weekly leg** for both strength and growth. The individual-data plot shows several subjects gaining notably more with the higher frequency. Equally, a number saw minimal difference, some slightly favoured once weekly, and one man gained markedly more strength on his once-weekly leg.

That mixture is the finding. The design — each subject serving as his own control, one leg per frequency — rules out genetics, nutrition and lifestyle as explanations for the spread, which no between-subject study can do.

## The cross-education objection
[05:06] Training one limb produces gains in the untrained limb, so does that contaminate a within-subject frequency comparison?

For **growth**, almost certainly not. Hubal et al. (243 men, 342 women) trained one biceps with unilateral preacher and concentration curls: the trained arm gained **18.9%** cross-sectional area, the untrained arm **1.4%**.

For **strength** the transfer is real: **54.1%** in the trained arm versus **10.6%** in the untrained arm on unilateral preacher curl 1RM. His argument that it did not wreck the frequency data is a reasonable one — a bidirectional strength transfer would have pushed the two legs toward equal gains, yet average strength gains still favoured the three-times-weekly leg. He also suspects cross-education is smaller in trained subjects training both limbs.

## Why he ranks one study above two meta-analyses
[06:39] A 2019 meta-analysis found no hypertrophy difference between higher and lower frequencies; a 2018-published strength meta-analysis found the same. Evidence hierarchies would place both above a single trial. He disagrees, and lists why the included studies differ from Neves:

- High and low frequency were classed **relative to each study**, so 3x/week counted as "low" in one and "high" in another.
- The comparisons were often different questions entirely — 1x vs 2x, 6x vs 3x, 2x vs 3x.
- Many used **fewer than nine weekly sets**, untrained subjects, or imprecise measures such as DXA and skinfolds.
- All were **between-subject**, so genetics, nutrition and lifestyle remain confounders.
- None can speak to individual differences at all.

**Overreach flag:** the case for demoting two meta-analyses on the strength of one n=24 trial is argued well, but it is still a strong methodological claim resting on a single small study. More pointedly, he reads the individual-data spread as genuine responder heterogeneity without addressing measurement error or within-subject biological noise — there is no untrained-limb or test-retest estimate here to separate true individual response from scatter, and that is the standard trap in individual-response analysis.

## Practical conclusion
[09:39] The study only compares 1x to 3x per week; 2x vs 3x or 3x vs 5x remain untested with this design. His recommendations:
- Training at a low frequency and making great gains? **Change nothing.** Some people genuinely do better there.
- Training at a low frequency and unsatisfied? **Try a higher frequency** — the data show individuals for whom this matters.
