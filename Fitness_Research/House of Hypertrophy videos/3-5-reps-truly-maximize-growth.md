# Can Sets of 3–5 Reps Maximise Growth? Two Clean Studies Say Maybe, Which Is Not Enough

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Can 3-5 Reps Truly Maximize Growth? (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GpSR3oicV_I)) · 2025-08-21 · 12 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- **The new study (preprint, 2025):** 14 subjects with 2+ years training, within-subject design, unilateral leg press and leg extension. One leg did **3–5 reps to volitional failure**, the other **20–25 reps to failure**, both **3 sets per session, 2 min rest**, twice weekly for 9 weeks with a mid-study deload. Vastus lateralis thickness rose **7.4% at region 1 and 8.5% at region 2** with **no significant difference between conditions**.
- Oddly, **neither** condition increased muscle fibre cross-sectional area on biopsy despite whole-muscle thickness increasing. The authors attribute this to biopsy sampling: ~150 fibres sampled from a muscle whose fibre count runs into the hundreds of thousands (he cites an estimate of **170,000–419,000 fibres** for the biceps).
- **He does not lower his recommended minimum.** His position stays at **6+ reps per set** to maximise the stimulus, on the grounds that only **two** studies with sets and rest equated support 3–5 reps, and a third found **2–4 reps grew less** than 8–12.
- The six older studies that appear to support ≤5 reps all gave the low-rep groups **more sets and/or longer rest**. He argues this cuts against the low-rep case rather than for it: with those advantages, ≤5 reps should have *beaten* higher reps if it were equally effective per set, and it did not clearly do so.
- **Practically it barely matters.** Growth still occurs at 3–5 reps; a few sub-6-rep sets in a programme are not a problem; and if you have been training below 6 reps with good progress he explicitly says to change nothing.

## Why an exact rep number is hard to pin down
Three problems he raises before interpreting anything:

1. **Studies report ranges, not numbers.** Subjects intended to hit four reps may land on three or five. A "3–5 rep" condition cannot support a claim about 3 reps specifically, and he thinks it plausible that five reps beats two or three — but **no study has compared them directly**.
2. **Studies often report %1RM instead of reps**, and reps at a given %1RM vary by exercise and by person. He cites a meta-analysis finding roughly **13 reps at 80% 1RM on leg press versus about 9 on bench press**.
3. **Reps fall across sets** when load is held constant and sets go to failure, so the nominal rep number drifts within a session.

He also notes that ["volitional failure"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/) is an ambiguous term, that 14 subjects is a small sample, and that we cannot know whether effort differed between the two conditions even with strong verbal encouragement.

## The "variance demon"
He borrows Data Driven Strength's term for the idea that small-sample results can misrepresent reality through measurement error, sampling variance, or a group that happens to have better genetics. This is his stated reason for not updating on two studies: the fix is large samples or meta-analysis, not accumulating individually noisy trials and treating them as settled. It is also, implicitly, why he treats the conflicting results in this literature as expected rather than as evidence of a hidden moderator.

## Where his 6–35 range now stands
He has previously argued **6–35 reps** as the practical hypertrophy range, against others' 5–30 or 3–40. This video does not move it. His summary is unusually explicit about the epistemic state: he is **not** claiming 3–5 reps fails to maximise growth, only that the evidence is too thin and too mixed to be confident, and he says he personally hopes the lower bound comes down because it would give lifters more options. He calls the answer "somewhat disappointing" and offers it anyway.

## Mixing rep ranges
One study he mentions had 19 trained men train three times weekly for 8 weeks, one group at 8–12 reps to failure every session and another rotating **2–4 / 8–12 / 20–30 reps** across the week. Growth was not significantly different; numbers favoured the varied group, but one of the authors attributed that to outliers and sampling variance. Other trials comparing single versus varied rep ranges also find similar hypertrophy — though in those, all groups largely trained at 6+ reps, so they say little about the low end.

**Flagged:** the headline study is a **preprint** (bioRxiv, not yet peer-reviewed) with n=14 and a null biopsy result its own authors could not explain. He conveys this accurately, but a reader skimming the title could easily take "3–5 reps works" as the finding when his actual conclusion is the opposite of an update.
