# One Set to Failure (HIT) Against Higher Volume and Frequency

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "1 Set to Failure (HIT) VS High Volume for Size & Strength (27 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1EHrp8-2Kg8)) · 2022-06-20 · 36 min
**Topic:** Volume & frequency

## Bottom line
- HIT rests on three claims — **train to failure, one set per exercise, train each muscle once or twice a week**. He tests each separately against the literature. The first is unnecessary, the second is wrong on average, and the third is not superior but is viable.
- **Training to failure is not required.** A 2021 meta-analysis (7 studies, 130 men and 89 women for hypertrophy; 15 studies, 265 men and 129 women for strength) found no difference between failure and stopping short. Non-failure groups were mostly at **0–3 RIR** for hypertrophy and **0–5 RIR** for strength.
- **Three sets per exercise beats one set — but barely.** Effect size difference of **0.24 for hypertrophy** (small) and **0.18 for strength** (trivial), across studies mostly lasting 12 weeks or less.
- **The individual-response data is the most striking finding in the video.** A within-subject study (34 untrained subjects, one leg on 1 set, the other on 3 sets, 12 weeks) found a **near 50-50 split** on both quadriceps CSA and strength. He flags his own scepticism that this generalises to trained lifters.
- **On the volume ceiling dispute, this video does not adjudicate.** Every study here compares 1 set against 3 sets per exercise — the bottom of the range. Nothing tests 10 versus 20 versus 40 weekly sets. What it does contribute is two studies pointing the *pro-volume* way in trained lifters: high responders were those who **raised their weekly sets most relative to their pre-study habit**, and a **+20% weekly set increase beat a fixed 22 sets/week** for vastus lateralis growth.

## What HIT actually claims

He builds the position from primary sources rather than caricature. Arthur Jones coined the term in a 1973 article and built Nautilus around machines matched to the strength curve. Jones on failure: a set is finished "only when additional movement is utterly impossible." Jones on sets: "How many sets of the exercise? 1. Additional sets usually serve no purpose." Jones on frequency: initially three full-body sessions a week, later revised to **no more than twice per week per muscle**, with some people doing better on one. Mike Mentzer's *Heavy Duty* specified **96–120 hours between sessions**, and Mentzer reported personal clients training **once every 10–14 days**. Dorian Yates: "one set at extreme intensity does the muscle-building job."

The three claims are logically linked, which is the strongest thing about the system: if effort is genuinely maximal, you can only tolerate so much volume, and only so often. He treats that internal coherence respectfully throughout, and ends by saying Mentzer's book is "extraordinarily well written" and that Mentzer was reading the evidence available in the early 2000s.

## The Colorado Experiment is not evidence

The famous claim: 1973, Colorado State University, supervised by Dr Elliot Plese. Casey Viator — youngest ever AAU Mr America at 19 — trained HIT-style for 28 days and gained **63.21 lb of muscle**; Arthur Jones trained 22 days and gained **15.44 lb**. Published in *Iron Man*, September 1973. One set to failure per exercise, no rest between exercises, all on Nautilus machines.

His demolition is straightforward and fair. Both men were **detrained for roughly four months beforehand** — Viator recovering from a serious workplace accident, Jones previously a muscular 205 lb — so **muscle memory is an unavoidable confounder**. Viator was plainly a genetic outlier, which Jones himself said. And anabolics cannot be ruled out despite Jones's stated opposition and a report (via Ellington Darden) that Jones hired someone to shadow Viator: prior use can still influence later results. He extends the same treatment to Darden's other anecdotes and to Tim Ferriss's claim of 34 lb in four weeks. The decisive objection is not any single confounder but that **none of these accounts compare HIT to anything else**.

## Does failure add anything?

For hypertrophy, the 2021 meta-analysis pooled 7 studies (130 men, 89 women) and found no difference. He then does the work most summaries skip: he reads the included studies and reports that non-failure groups were mostly leaving **3 to 0 reps in reserve**, so the finding is "0–3 RIR equals failure," not "any submaximal effort equals failure."

The subgroup analysis on trained subjects *did* favour failure — and he leads with this against his own conclusion. Then he explains why it doesn't hold: **only two studies** were in that subgroup. One compared failure against **5 RIR**, which says nothing about 0–3 RIR. The other was velocity-based, terminating sets at a fixed velocity loss, and from the reported data the non-failure group appears to have been at **5 or more RIR** (about five reps at 70% 1RM). A study published since the meta-analysis, in men averaging **5.1 years of training experience**, found **0–3 RIR similar to failure** for hypertrophy. For strength, the meta-analysis (15 studies, 265 men, 129 women) found no difference including in the trained subgroup, with most non-failure groups at **0–5 RIR**.

The mechanistic explanation is the interesting half. He routes it through the tension-primacy argument (Schoenfeld's 2010 three-driver paper superseded by the 2019 update with Wackerhage and others, which downgrades metabolic stress and damage). If tension is the driver, failure should add nothing once you have maximal motor unit recruitment at decent force. One study suggests **maximal motor unit recruitment for the shoulder and trap muscles arrives 3–5 reps before failure**. A motor unit **modelling** study makes the point vividly: seven modelled units from MU1 (lowest threshold) to MU120 (highest), during a 50% MVC isometric hold to failure lasting 100 seconds. All but MU120 were recruited from the start; MU120 came in at **70 seconds** and peaked around **95 seconds**. Low-threshold units held steady low forces throughout; MU80 peaked at the start then decayed; MU100 climbed to 65 seconds then fatigued. **At the moment of failure, no new units were recruited and no unit produced more force** — the last reps are fatigue, not stimulus.

He flags the obvious limitation himself: this is **modelled isometric contraction**, and recruitment strategies plausibly differ for the isotonic contractions of real training. He argues only that he sees no reason the two headline conclusions would change.

## Does one set per exercise cost you?

Yes, on average, but less than the HIT debate implies. A 2017 meta-analysis pooled 15 studies comparing low to high volume, **14 of which were one set versus three sets per exercise**, and found higher volume better for muscle mass. Three studies published since agree. For strength, a 2017 meta-analysis of 9 studies (7 of them one versus three sets) also favoured higher volume. A 2010 acute study supplies the mechanism: three sets to failure on leg extension at 70% 1RM produced both a **greater amplitude and a longer duration** of fed-state myofibrillar protein synthesis than one set.

Then he prices the difference honestly, and this is the part that most changes how the result should be read. The strength advantage was an effect size difference of **0.18 — trivial**. The hypertrophy advantage was **0.24 — small**. Three sets does not give three times the gains; it gives marginally more. He adds the fair caveat in the other direction: the pooled studies mostly ran **12 weeks or less**, so trivial-to-small differences may compound over years.

Against Jones's "never add sets," he cites two studies in trained lifters. In one, trained men trained squat and leg press with varied set numbers for eight weeks and were sorted post hoc into high, moderate and low responders by anterior thigh growth: the **high responders were those who had increased their weekly sets most relative to their pre-study volume**. In the other, trained men who performed **20% more weekly quadriceps sets than they had been doing** grew more vastus lateralis than men given a prescribed **22 sets per week**. His reading: a **modest increase of around 20%** is a useful tool, particularly against a plateau.

Where this lands relative to the library's volume-ceiling argument: it doesn't reach it. Every comparison here is 1 versus 3 sets per exercise, so the video is silent on whether returns continue past 20 or 40 weekly sets. But the two trained-lifter studies point the same direction as the continue-adding-sets camp, and the individually-tailored result (previous volume +20% beating a fixed 22 sets) suggests the useful variable may be **change relative to your own habitual volume** rather than any absolute weekly number — which is a third position neither side of that dispute occupies.

## Does low frequency help you recover?

With weekly sets equated, no. A 2019 meta-analysis of 13 studies found **similar growth across frequencies**, and he adds the qualifier that matters: **11 of the 13 used 12 or fewer weekly sets per muscle**. One illustrative study took 23 trained men (mean bench 1RM 103 kg, squat 1RM 132 kg), gave both groups **10 weekly sets per muscle**, and had one train each muscle **once a week** and the other **five times a week**; after 8 weeks lean mass gains were similar. Above 12 weekly sets the evidence is genuinely conflicting — some studies favour higher frequency, some lower, some neither — and he points to a separate frequency video rather than overclaiming.

For strength, a 2018 meta-analysis of 7 studies (all at ≤12 weekly sets) found frequency irrelevant when volume was equated. Above 12 sets, one study found 3–4x weekly better than 1x for strength, but three others found no difference. The genuinely useful finding: when the same meta-analysis pooled the studies that **did not** equate weekly sets — so higher-frequency groups simply did more total work — **strength gains were greater with higher frequency**. Frequency is therefore not a stimulus in itself but a **container for volume**.

His argument against the HIT recovery model is the strongest passage in the video. HIT assumes limited recovery capacity; he argues the estimate of where that limit sits is badly low, because it is drawn from **single-session recovery studies**. One such study found trained men still not fully recovered 48 hours after three sets to failure at 12RM on bench and squat — which looks like a case for long rest, until you notice it measured recovery from one session in people not adapted to that stimulus. His counter-study: **12 previously untrained men** performed **5 sets of 15 maximal eccentric-only leg extensions** on an isokinetic dynamometer, once weekly for 10 weeks. After session one, creatine kinase was elevated a day later and soreness and maximal isometric force were still impaired **five days on**. By **week 10, the same session produced no soreness, no CK rise and no force decrement** — full recovery inside 24 hours. The repeated bout effect (attributed in a 2017 paper to neural, muscular, tendon, connective tissue and inflammatory adaptations) means recovery capacity is a moving target, and low-frequency prescriptions are calibrated to the untrained state.

## Where HIT still earns its place

Three concessions, all reasoned rather than diplomatic:

1. **Learning failure.** Stopping at 0–3 RIR requires accurately judging distance from failure, which requires experience of actual failure. One set per exercise makes that psychologically easier to practise.
2. **The gap is small.** HIT unquestionably builds substantial muscle and strength; the deficit is trivial-to-small in trials up to 12 weeks.
3. **Individual response may swamp the average.** In the within-subject study — 16 men and 18 women, unilateral leg press and leg extension at 7–10RM, three times weekly for 12 weeks, one leg on one set and the other on three — quadriceps CSA showed a **near 50-50 split**, half doing better on three sets and half doing the same or better on one. Averaging three strength tests gave the same split. He says the result surprised him, then immediately constrains it: **only 34 subjects, all untrained**, and separate data show trained lifters benefit from added sets — so he doubts the distribution transfers. His conclusion is the defensible version: some people probably do just as well or better on low volume, and HIT may be optimal for them.

## Caveats & flags
- The motor-unit case against failure rests on a **modelling study of isometric contractions**, and he acknowledges this may not transfer to isotonic training. It is the weakest link in an otherwise outcome-driven argument.
- The 50-50 individual-response result is one small untrained study and should not be used to justify low-volume training in trained lifters — as he says himself.
- Volume conclusions here are entirely about **1 versus 3 sets per exercise**; nothing in this video speaks to where the upper limit of useful weekly volume lies.
- The "modest increases accelerate growth" advice comes from a post-hoc responder analysis plus one small trial, which is thin support for a prescription.
- He does not report the sample sizes for the volume meta-analyses, only for the failure ones.
