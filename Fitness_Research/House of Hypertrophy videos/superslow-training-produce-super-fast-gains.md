# SuperSlow Training: Slower Reps Do Not Mean More Tension

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Does SuperSlow Training Produce Super Fast Gains? (Research Analysis)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HV8xg0w9BHw)) · 2022-03-03 · 20 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- SuperSlow as a **protocol** means a 10-second lift and a 4–10-second lowering. That is the only thing this video evaluates — not the surrounding philosophy of one-set training, 30-minute sessions and music-free gyms.
- The famous study showing SuperSlow producing **~50% more strength gain** tested each group at its own tempo. When 1RM is tested normally, the regular-tempo group wins clearly on 5 of 8 exercises and trends ahead on the other 3.
- The only hypertrophy comparison found **greater fast- and slow-twitch fibre growth in the regular-tempo group** — 19 untrained women, 6 weeks. One study, one demographic.
- The "SuperSlow creates more tension" argument is a **misapplication of the force-velocity relationship**. With a fixed light load, moving slower *reduces* acceleration and therefore *reduces* force. A national-level weightlifter squatting 170 kg produced lower mean and peak propulsive force with a 10s/4s tempo than at maximal speed.
- Tempos between **0.5 and 8 seconds per rep are equivalent for hypertrophy** when sets are taken to or near failure. SuperSlow sits outside that tested range, and his proposed explanation — excessive central fatigue from very long set durations — is an untested hypothesis he labels as such.

## Where the protocol came from
Ken Hutchins developed it in the 1980s while running training research on older women with fragile bones — a population where a slow, controlled tempo was a safety decision, not a hypertrophy decision. Hutchins himself distinguished the protocol from the philosophy; the philosophy adds one set per exercise, sessions under 30 minutes, three to seven days between sessions, and environmental prescriptions. Claims that bodybuilders and powerlifters used it to break plateaus long before the 1980s are Wikipedia-sourced and unverifiable. He notes the family resemblance to Arthur Jones's HIT and to Doug McGuff's Body by Science, and sets those aside for another video.

## Strength: the study everyone cites, and the one they don't
**Westcott 2001** describes two studies: just over 70 previously untrained men and women aged 25–82, assigned to SuperSlow or regular, training a range of Nautilus machines 2–3×/week for 8 weeks (study 1) or 10 weeks (study 2). Regular did one set of 8–12 reps at 2s up / 4s down; SuperSlow did one set of 4–6 reps at 10s up / 4s down. Both groups progressively overloaded. On average the SuperSlow group gained roughly **50% more strength**.

The catch is the outcome measure. **Each group was tested using its own tempo** — regular on a 10RM at 2s/4s, SuperSlow on a 1RM at 10s/4s. That is not a strength comparison; it is two different tests.

**Keeler 2001** fixes it. 14 previously untrained women, average age 32, training eight exercises 3×/week for 10 weeks. Regular: one set of 8–12 at 80% 1RM, 2s/4s. SuperSlow: one set of 8–12 at 50% 1RM, 10s/5s. Both groups tested at **true 1RM with no deliberate tempo**. The regular group gained much more on 5 of 8 exercises; on row, triceps extension and biceps curl the difference was not statistically significant, though the percentages still favoured regular.

His read is sensible: SuperSlow forces you into relatively light loads, and 80%+ 1RM produces the largest 1RM gains. If you want a bigger 1RM, lift heavy and move it fast. The genuine carve-out he offers: if your goal is the maximum load you can move *at a SuperSlow tempo*, or if you cannot train heavy for medical reasons or simply dislike it, SuperSlow is specific to that and works.

## Hypertrophy: one study
**Schuenke 2012** is the only long-term hypertrophy comparison he is aware of. 19 previously untrained women, average age 21, training leg press, squat and leg extension for 3 sets each, 2–3×/week for 6 weeks, 2 minutes between sets, loads increased when rep targets were exceeded. Regular: 6–10 reps at 80–85% 1RM, 1–2s up / 1–2s down. SuperSlow: 6–10 reps at 40–60% 1RM, 10s up / 4s down. **Fast- and slow-twitch fibre growth in the vastus lateralis was greater in the regular group.**

He rules out the obvious explanation himself: loads from 30% to 80% 1RM produce similar hypertrophy when sets are taken to or near failure, which they broadly were. So the light load per se does not account for it. And he keeps the sample in view — 19 previously untrained women, one study.

## The tension argument, dismantled
SuperSlow proponents argue that the force-velocity relationship — slower contractions produce more force — means a slow tempo maximises mechanical tension.

He identifies the error precisely: the force-velocity relationship is established in **unfatigued** fibres contracting once or a few times at different velocities, or when comparing unfatigued contractions against **different loads**. Neither condition describes lifting one fixed light load repeatedly toward failure.

With a fixed load, **force = mass × acceleration**. Lifting slower reduces acceleration, so it reduces force. **Schilling 2008** demonstrates this directly: a national-level weightlifter back-squatting 170 kg produced **lower mean and peak propulsive forces** at a 10s/4s tempo than at maximal lifting speed. So slower reps start with *less* mechanical tension, not more.

Why does that not sink slow training generally? Because Henneman's size principle means fibre recruitment is driven by force requirement **and** by proximity to failure. As reps accumulate and you approach failure, additional fibres are recruited and recruited fibres produce more force in order to sustain the load. So regardless of tempo, sets taken to or near failure likely end up recruiting a similar number of fibres at comparable force — which is why **Schoenfeld's tempo meta-analysis** finds 0.5–8-second rep durations equivalent for hypertrophy.

## His hypothesis for why SuperSlow might still be worse
Central fatigue: a training-induced reduction in the neural drive reaching the muscle, which lowers recruitment and force, and therefore lowers mechanical tension. Contrary to intuition, **light, long-lasting contractions generate more central fatigue than heavy, brief ones**. **Yoon 2007** had 9 men and 9 women hold biceps contractions at 20% or 80% of maximum voluntary force until force fell by 10%: the 20% contraction lasted about **14 minutes**, the 80% about **25 seconds**, and the 20% condition produced greater central fatigue.

His argument: 30% 1RM loads to failure at normal 2–4s tempos build muscle fine, and 0.5–8s tempos at 50–80% 1RM build muscle fine, so the central fatigue generated in those ranges is not a problem. SuperSlow's combination of light load *and* a rep duration beyond 14 seconds massively extends set duration and may push central fatigue past a threshold that does interfere.

**Where a conclusion outruns the evidence:** this is his own hypothesis, generated to explain a single 19-subject study, resting on an acute isometric fatigue measurement rather than any growth outcome. He says so explicitly — "merely a hypothesis based on the findings of a single study" — which is the right label and should be preserved wherever the claim gets repeated.

## What to take away
Nothing in the evidence recommends SuperSlow. Nothing in it condemns ordinary controlled tempos either — anything from roughly half a second to eight seconds per rep is fine, so long as sets get close to failure. Deliberately extending past that has one study against it and no study for it.
