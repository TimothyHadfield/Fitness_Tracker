# Rep Tempo and Hypertrophy: A New Meta-Analysis Finds No Clear Winner

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The BEST Rep Speed For Size (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2sys7keMuy4)) · 2025-10-27 · 10 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- A new meta-analysis (**14 studies, 278 subjects**) that included only trials independently manipulating eccentric *or* concentric tempo found **no clear advantage for faster or slower reps**.
- The eccentric estimate slightly favoured faster tempos: **SMD 0.06**, roughly a **0.9 percentage-point** difference in growth (e.g. 5% versus 5.9%). The credible interval comfortably spans zero.
- A subgroup split hints that **slower eccentrics may edge it when sets are taken to failure**, but the estimate is imprecise and the subgroups are small.
- Both fast and slow tempo groups grew by **5–6%**, which matches the typical growth seen across the wider literature. Tempo is not a high-leverage variable.
- The one caution: **super-slow training may be worse.** One study using a 10 s concentric / 4 s eccentric at 40–60% 1RM produced smaller fibre-size increases than normal-tempo training to failure.

## What the meta-analysis did
[00:00] The inclusion rule matters. Studies comparing a group with *both* a slower concentric and slower eccentric against a group with *both* faster were **excluded** — only trials that manipulated one phase in isolation were pooled. That isolates the question "holding the other phase constant, does this phase's speed matter?"

Fourteen studies, 278 subjects total, with the average and range of fast and slow tempos reported for each phase.

## Eccentric tempo
[03:03] The overall estimate favoured **faster** eccentrics with a standardized mean difference of **0.06**.

He does something useful with that number rather than leaving it abstract. Using Greg Nuckols' effect-size-to-percentage heuristic, 0.06 converts to about a **0.9% additive difference** — if slower tempos produce 5% growth, faster produce about 5.9%. As a multiplicative difference that is roughly 18%, but the absolute gap is small either way.

[03:34] More importantly, the credible interval **comfortably includes zero**. His stated reading: despite the point estimate leaning faster, the honest description is **no clear advantage for either tempo**.

[04:04] The subgroup analysis is the interesting wrinkle. Splitting by whether subjects trained to failure:
- **To failure** — estimate favoured **slower** eccentrics.
- **Not to failure** — estimate favoured **faster** eccentrics.

He argues the failure subgroup generalises better: **6 of 6** of those studies used regular free weight or machine exercise, whereas **3 of 5** non-failure studies used isokinetic dynamometers, which match the subject's effort and hold velocity fixed — not how anyone trains. So slower eccentrics *might* carry a small advantage in real training. He then backs off again, noting the estimate is far from precise, and says it remains defensible to call it no clear advantage.

## Concentric tempo
[05:37] The estimate favoured **faster** concentrics, with substantial uncertainty. Data were too limited for any further analysis.

## Putting the effect in context
[06:07] The most useful framing in the video. The researchers also estimated the growth achieved *within* each tempo condition: effect sizes of **0.34 to 0.43**, roughly **5–6%** increases in muscle size.

He compares that to the typical magnitude of hypertrophy in the literature — a meta-analysis of **111 studies** found muscle size increases of approximately **5%**, and precisely estimated. Both tempo conditions land right on the normal result. You grow normally with either.

## Supporting evidence and the super-slow exception
[07:39] Studies excluded from the meta-analysis — those varying *both* phases together — tend to find no significant hypertrophy differences. An older meta-analysis and more recent reviews concluded that **provided you train to or close to failure, tempos across a wide range of durations are similarly effective**.

[08:10] The exception. One study had untrained women train back squat, leg press and leg extension:
- **Normal group:** sets to failure, 1–2 s concentric and eccentric, **80–85% 1RM**.
- **Super-slow group:** sets to failure, **10 s concentric and 4 s eccentric**, **40–60% 1RM**.

Increases in both slow- and fast-twitch fibre size in the vastus lateralis **favoured the normal group**. He pre-empts the obvious objection — the super-slow group used lighter loads — by noting that loads as light as 30% 1RM have repeatedly been shown to build muscle comparably. He then explicitly calls this one study, "anything but proof", and treats avoiding super-slow tempos as precaution rather than established fact.

**Overreach flag:** two soft spots. The failure/non-failure subgroup split is post hoc with five and six studies respectively, and he builds a directional interpretation on it before walking it back. And the super-slow conclusion rests on a single trial in untrained women using biopsy fibre-size outcomes — the load confound is argued away by reasoning from other literature, not tested within the study.

**Where he is unusually rigorous:** he stops the video to explain what a forest plot is, what a 95% credible interval means, and why a small standardized mean difference is hard to interpret before converting it into something usable. He also states plainly that trained-subject data are scarce, so a real effect cannot be definitively ruled out yet.

## Practical takeaway
[07:09] Tempo is far from the most important hypertrophy variable, and if a difference exists it is unlikely to be large. **Pick the tempo you prefer**, and vary it by exercise if you like — but avoid genuine super-slow work if maximising growth is the goal.
