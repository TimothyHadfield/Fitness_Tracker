# Cable vs Dumbbell Lateral Raises, and the Three Muscle-Length Categories

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "We Have to Talk About Lateral Raises" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ankPCJvL0Jc)) · 2025-01-07 · 10 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- First trial measuring side delt hypertrophy by exercise: **24 trained individuals, average 7.1 years' experience**, one arm cable lateral raise, one arm dumbbell, 5 sets to failure at 12–16 reps, 2×/week for 8 weeks. Growth at proximal and distal regions was **similar**.
- He had previously speculated cables would win, because the cable was set so the arm and cable formed roughly 90° at the start, making the exercise hardest where the side delt is longest. That prediction failed, and he says so.
- His organising contribution: split the long-muscle-length literature into **three categories** — resistance challenge, range of motion, and biarticular muscles — because they are not the same question and the evidence differs between them.
- Resistance challenge (where in the range the exercise is hardest): two studies, both null. This lateral raise trial, and Nunes 2020 on barbell vs cable preacher curls in untrained subjects.
- Range of motion: three untrained studies favoured lengthened partials over full ROM, but two 2024 trained-subject studies (one with just under 300 subjects) found lengthened partials broadly equivalent to full ROM.
- Biarticular muscles: this is where he thinks long lengths will hold up in trained lifters — leaning-back leg extensions grew rectus femoris more, straight-leg calf raises grew gastrocnemius more, with the single-joint comparator muscle (vastus lateralis, soleus) growing equally in both.
- Practical takeaway he actually gives: none of these choices costs you anything. Dumbbells or cables, full ROM or lengthened partials — "it's pretty hard to do something tragically wrong."

## The lateral raise trial in detail
Both arms moved 0–90° of shoulder abduction. Nominally in the frontal plane, though some subjects had pain, so both exercises were shifted to between the frontal and scapular planes. Cuffs were used to minimise grip fatigue — an unusual and sensible control. Dumbbells load hardest with the arm straight out to the side, where the side delt is relatively short; the cable setup loaded hardest at the bottom, where it is long. Side delt thickness was measured proximally and distally. No meaningful difference either region.

## Four caveats he raises unprompted
1. A single study is never proof — he uses a coin-flip analogy: five heads in a row happens.
2. **Total growth in both conditions was small and not far off measurement error**, unsurprising for 8 weeks in well-trained lifters. So the study may have been underpowered to detect a small real difference. This is the most important caveat and he leads with it.
3. Range of motion was matched at 0–90°. With a cable you can bring the arm across the body to lengthen the side delt further; that variant remains untested.
4. Both conditions increased acceleration out of the bottom as they approached failure, which with dumbbells raises tension at longer lengths — potentially blurring the intended contrast.

## The three categories
The framework is the reusable part of this video.

**Resistance challenge.** All else equal, exercises that are hardest at long versus short lengths. The lateral raise trial belongs here. The only other study is Nunes 2020: barbell preacher curls (hardest early, biceps long) versus cable preacher curls (hardest late, biceps short) in untrained subjects — mid-region biceps growth comparable. Two studies, two nulls.

**Range of motion.** Full ROM, shortened partials, or lengthened partials. The old "full ROM is best" conclusion rested mostly on comparisons against *shortened* partials. Three untrained studies then found lengthened partials beat full ROM. But two 2024 trained-subject studies, including one on just under 300 subjects, found them largely equivalent. His read: lengthened partials are not unequivocally superior, and both options are on the table.

**Biarticular muscles.** Change the position of the non-working joint to lengthen the muscle. Rectus femoris is the only quad head crossing both hip and knee; leaning back on a leg extension extends the hip, lengthens it, and grew it more than a normal leg extension, while vastus lateralis — trained at the same length either way — grew the same. Same logic for gastrocnemius: straight-leg calf raises beat bent-leg for gastrocnemius across two papers, while soleus grew equally. He notes one of the calf papers used trained subjects, and that competitive bodybuilders showed greater rectus femoris activity with leaning/lying leg extensions.

**Overreach flag:** the bodybuilder study is an activity measure, not growth. He flags this himself — the activity measure has been linked to growth in some analyses, but he calls its use here tentative rather than treating it as an outcome.

## Where this sits against the library
The library holds the cable lateral raise at hand height as the best pick, with a 2025 trial finding no cable-versus-dumbbell difference. This video **is** that trial, and Dhim's reading is more agnostic than the library's: he concludes both are similarly effective and explicitly says a dumbbell user has not been missing out. He offers no support for a specific cable height — the trial's cable was positioned for a 90° arm-cable angle at the bottom, not at hand height, and the across-body cable variant that might justify a height preference is exactly the variant he flags as untested. The library's "best pick" framing is not supported by the evidence in this video.
