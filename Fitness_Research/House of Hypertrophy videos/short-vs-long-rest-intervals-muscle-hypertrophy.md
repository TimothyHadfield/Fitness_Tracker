# Rest Intervals: Long on Compounds, Undecided on Isolation

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "SHORT vs LONG Rest Intervals for Muscle Hypertrophy (33 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UGaZ6GsIVHM)) · 2020-12-21 · 40 min
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **On compound exercises, 2.5–3 minutes beat 1 minute or less** in four of five training studies, when sets and proximity to failure are matched. The fifth found the opposite but equated volume load, which forced the short-rest group closer to failure.
- **On isolation exercises the evidence is genuinely contradictory** — one study favours long rest, one favours short, one finds no difference across 3 min / 90 s / drop sets, and one finds drop sets (no rest at all) beating three normal sets. He declines to give a number, and says rest as long as is comfortable and practical.
- **The volume-load explanation is wrong**, and he shows it arithmetically: 3 sets to failure at 60% 1RM accumulate far more volume load than 3 sets at 80%, yet produce similar growth.
- **The central-fatigue explanation is probably also wrong** — the indirect evidence says central fatigue from sub-two-minute efforts dissipates in 30 seconds or less, which cannot explain a 1-minute-versus-3-minute training difference. He raises this hypothesis, then dismantles his own preferred version of it.
- **Recovery between sets tracks your absolute strength on the lift, not your sex or age directly.** Weaker lifters recover faster, which is why women and older lifters can generally rest shorter.
- **No study compares 2.5–3 minutes to 2 minutes.** He says so explicitly, which is important, because a lot of the practical difference between prescriptions lives in exactly that untested gap.

## Where the short-rest idea came from, and why it died

[00:00] A 1987 study found bodybuilders resting **10–90 seconds** between sets against powerlifters resting **120–140 seconds**. That correlation, plus 1990 research showing shorter rests produce substantially greater acute growth-hormone elevations, produced the textbook prescription: short rest for size, long rest for strength.

The chain breaks at the hormone step. Acute anabolic hormone elevations do not correlate with subsequent hypertrophy in the current research, so the mechanism that justified the prescription no longer supports it. His move here is the right one methodologically — abandon the acute proxy, go to studies that measured size before and after a training block.

## Compound exercises: the evidence favours long rest

Five training studies, five different comparisons, and he states sample size and training status for each.

| Study | Subjects | Protocol | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Longo 2020 | 28 untrained | Leg press, 3 sets to failure @80% 1RM, 2×/wk, 10 wks | Quad CSA favoured **3 min over 1 min** |
| Schoenfeld 2016 | 13 men, ≥6 months experience | 7 exercises (6 compound), 3 sets to failure @8–12RM, 3×/wk, 8 wks | Elbow flexor, triceps, anterior quad and VL thickness favoured **3 min over 1 min** |
| Buresh 2009 | 12 men, some experience but untrained for 3 months prior | Range of compounds, 4×/wk, 10 wks | Thigh and arm CSA favoured **2.5 min over 1 min** |
| Fink 2017 | 14 untrained | Bench + squat, 4 sets to failure @40% 1RM, 2×/wk, 8 wks | Triceps CSA similar; thigh CSA favoured **2.5 min over 30 s** |
| Villanueva 2015 | 22 untrained elderly men, mean age 68 | 8 exercises, 2–3 sets of 4–6 reps, 3×/wk, 8 wks | Lean body mass favoured **1 min over 4 min** |

[05:41] The Villanueva result is the one that would overturn the picture, and he takes it seriously rather than ignoring it. Two problems: the subjects were elderly, and — decisively — **volume load was equated between groups**. If both groups perform the same sets × reps × load, the one-minute group is necessarily training closer to failure on later sets. His worked illustration (3×8 at 75% of a 100 kg bench, on one minute versus four) is the clearest way anyone puts this. The four studies pointing the other way all had subjects train to or near failure in both conditions, so the comparison isn't like-for-like.

**Verdict on compounds:** with sets and effort matched, 2.5–3 minutes beats 1 minute or less.

### The escape hatch: more sets at shorter rest

[07:13] "All else equal" is doing real work in that conclusion, and he says so. Two findings suggest shorter rest can be compensated for:

- **Longo:** 4–5 sets to failure on leg press at 1 minute produced **similar** quad CSA gains to 3 sets at 3 minutes.
- **Ahtiainen 2005:** 2 minutes produced similar quad CSA gains to 5 minutes when the 2-minute group performed on average **one extra set**.

He is careful with the second — two minutes is not a short rest by most people's standards, and an alternative reading is simply that two minutes is enough and more adds nothing. Which brings him to the gap he names outright: **no study compares 2.5–3 minutes to 2 minutes**.

## Isolation exercises: no defensible answer

[09:14] Four studies, four different verdicts.

- **Buresh** (above) used isolation work alongside compounds and favoured 2.5 minutes — but the compounds confound it.
- **Fink**, 22 subjects, 8 weeks, 2×/wk: curls, preacher curls, hammer curls, close-grip bench, skullcrushers and dumbbell extensions. One group at **20RM with 30 s rest**, the other at **8RM with 3 min rest**, both to failure, same number of sets (the paper never states how many). Arm CSA **favoured the 20RM / 30-second group**. Load was not controlled, though he notes that since loads from 30–85% 1RM produce similar growth when taken to failure, that confound may not matter much.
- **Ozaki 2018**, 9 untrained men, each arm assigned to one of three unilateral dumbbell curl conditions, 2–3×/wk for 8 weeks: 3 sets to failure @80% with 3 min; 3 sets to failure @30% with 90 s; or a drop set (80% → 65% → 50% → 40% → 30%, each to failure, no rest). Elbow flexor CSA increases were **similar across all three**. Caveat he supplies himself: the drop-set arm technically performed five sets.
- **Fink 2018**, 16 men untrained for a year, triceps pushdown only, 2×/wk for 8 weeks: a **drop set** (12RM then −20% load three times) versus **3 sets to failure @12RM with 90 s rest**. Triceps CSA **favoured the drop set** — again with one extra set in the drop-set condition.

His conclusion is a refusal to conclude: rest however long feels comfortable and practical on isolation work. Given the spread, that is the correct answer.

## Why would longer rest help on compounds? Two hypotheses, both damaged

### Volume load — he rules it out

[15:24] The Longo authors attributed their result to greater total volume load with 3-minute rests. He rejects this, and the argument is the strongest single passage in the video.

Weekly sets taken to or near failure do relate to hypertrophy (per a Schoenfeld meta-analysis). **Volume load is a different quantity.** Since 30–85% 1RM produces similar growth when sets go to failure, take a 100 kg 1RM: 3 sets to failure at 80 kg might give 7/6/5 reps = **4,320 kg**, while 3 sets at 60 kg might give 15/13/11 = **7,020 kg**. If volume load drove growth, the lighter protocol would win. It doesn't. So volume load cannot be the mechanism. He adds a second, cleaner objection: longer rests raise volume load on isolation exercises too, yet the isolation evidence is a mess.

### Central fatigue — he raises it and then breaks it

[18:57] Credited to Chris Beardsley, Eric Helms and James Krieger. The logic: central fatigue is a reduction in the drive from upper to lower motor neurons, meaning fewer fibres recruited and less force from those recruited — precisely the opposite of the hypertrophy stimulus. Group III afferents (sensitive mainly to strong contraction) and group IV afferents (sensitive mainly to metabolite accumulation) feed back and suppress motor drive. If more muscle mass generates more central fatigue, compounds would need longer rests than isolation exercises.

Then he attacks it on two fronts, using the sources he cites:

1. **The muscle-mass premise may be backwards.** A Weavil review reports that isometric unilateral elbow flexion produced *more* central fatigue than unilateral knee extension, which produced more than cycling — exactly inverse to the muscle mass involved.
2. **The timing doesn't work.** Central fatigue after 2-minute maximal voluntary contractions is essentially gone almost immediately (quadriceps), within 1 minute (adductor pollicis) or within 30 seconds (elbow flexors). Longer, lighter efforts persist far longer — 33 min at 15% MVC took 3–9 minutes to clear, 43 min at 15% took ~20 minutes, and 70 min at 5% still showed central fatigue at 30 minutes (measurement stopped there). Since typical hypertrophy sets last well under two minutes, the extrapolation says central fatigue should clear in **30 seconds or less** — which cannot explain a 1-vs-3-minute training difference.

He notes the counter-caveat honestly: all of that timing data comes from isometric single-joint contractions, so it may not transfer, and the question needs studies measuring central fatigue recovery after real compound sets. But the net effect is that he presents the field's leading mechanistic explanation for his own conclusion and concludes it has a potential flaw. That is unusual and it is why this note treats his compound-exercise recommendation as an empirical finding without a working mechanism.

## Who can rest shorter: strength, not sex, and not age directly

[27:34] **Ratamess 2002**, part one: 22 men and women with ≥1 year experience, three separate sessions of 3 sets of bench at 75% 1RM with 1, 2 or 3 minutes rest, encouraged to hit 10 reps. Women sustained more total reps at every rest interval. But **adjusting for bench press strength erased the difference** — the women's mean 1RM was 36 kg against the men's 112 kg.

Part two tested the inference directly: 22 men split by bench 1RM (mean **81 kg** vs **141 kg**), same protocol. The weaker group sustained reps better at every rest interval. **The variable is your absolute strength on the exercise, not your sex.**

He then presents the evidence against his own tidy conclusion. **Fulco 2009** matched 9 men and 9 women for adductor pollicis strength; one minute after a fatiguing isometric contraction, the women had recovered to **71%** of baseline against the men's **65%**. Supporting mechanisms: greater blood flow, and a higher proportional area of type I fibres, which recover faster and fatigue less. So both things are true — strength explains most of it, and there appears to be a residual sex difference on top. Practical read: **women may be fine with 1–2 minutes on compounds.**

[32:11] On age, two acute studies. **Theou 2008**: 20 women aged 20–30 and 16 aged 60–80, 3 sets at an 8RM load on an isokinetic dynamometer with 15, 30 or 60 seconds rest. Both groups maintained knee extension torque only at 60 seconds; for knee flexion, the older women held up at 30 seconds where the younger needed 60. **Bottaro 2010**: 17 men (mean 25) and 20 men (mean 66), knee extensions 3×10 at 60 or 120 seconds — the younger men declined more at both intervals.

Two explanations, both partial: older subjects were weaker (so the strength finding applies), and ageing brings smaller type II fibres and a higher proportion of type I. He notes the only long-term rest-interval study in older adults is Villanueva, which he has already disqualified on volume-load grounds. Practical read: **perhaps 1–2 minutes on compounds for older lifters**, offered as an inference from acute data, not a finding.

## Can you taper rest over time?

[36:46] **De Souza 2010**: 20 men with ≥1 year experience, 6 days/week for 8 weeks. Weeks 1–2 identical for both groups (3 sets, 10–12RM, 2 min rest). Weeks 3–8: 4 sets at 8–10RM, with one group holding **2 minutes** and the other tapering weekly from **105 seconds down to 30 seconds**. Thigh and arm CSA gains were similar.

He immediately limits it. The constant group rested two minutes, which by his own conclusion isn't the optimal comparator for compound work — so this shows a taper matches a suboptimal control, not that it matches best practice. And nobody has tested what happens if you then *hold* a 30-second rest for another ten weeks. Promising, not established.

## How this sits against the rest of the library

This is the video that most directly touches the library's standing position on rest, and it lands **between** the library's two existing camps.

### Against the canonical prescription

`Jeff Nippard videos/rest-between-sets.md` sets the library's position: **3–5 minutes on heavy compounds, 1–2 minutes on isolation, almost never under 1 minute**, with the old textbook table (~1 min hypertrophy / 3–5 min strength / <30 s endurance) explicitly abandoned.

1. **On the abandoned one-minute rule — full agreement, and better sourced.** Nippard reaches it via two studies. This video traces the rule to its two origins and kills both: the 1987 bodybuilder-versus-powerlifter observation was never causal, and the acute growth-hormone mechanism fails because acute anabolic hormone elevations do not correlate with growth. He then stacks four compound studies against it. Anyone wanting the full case for why the library dropped the one-minute prescription should read this video's first six minutes.
2. **On compounds — same direction, shorter reach.** He concludes **2.5–3 minutes**, not 3–5. That is not disagreement; it is the limit of the evidence. Every compound study he reviews compares a long rest to **1 minute or less**, so nothing in his evidence base speaks to 3 versus 5 minutes. The only study that tests the upper end at all is **Ahtiainen** (2 min vs 5 min), and it found no difference once the 2-minute group added one set. He states outright that **no study compares 2.5–3 minutes to 2 minutes**. The library's 3–5 minute figure is therefore not contradicted here, but it is not supported above roughly the 3-minute mark either.
3. **The shared study.** Both notes rest partly on **Schoenfeld 2016** ([PMID 26605807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/)) — 1 min vs 3 min, 8 weeks. Nippard reports greater quad and triceps hypertrophy plus greater bench and squat 1RM for the 3-minute group; this video reports the same hypertrophy result and adds the sample detail (**13 men, ≥6 months experience**) and the fact that six of the seven exercises were compound, which is what makes it a compound-exercise study rather than a general one.
4. **On isolation — the library's figure is weaker than it looks.** The library's **1–2 minutes** is a sensible practical convention, and nothing here contradicts it. But this video reviews four studies conducted specifically on isolation exercises and concludes **no rest interval can be recommended from the evidence at all** — one favours long, one favours short, one finds 3 min / 90 s / drop sets equivalent, one finds drop sets superior to 90 s. The isolation number should be recorded as a practicality convention, not an evidence-backed optimum.

### Against the source that rejects the floor

`Menno Henselmans videos/long-rest-between-sets-max-muscle.md` ("Rest Intervals Are A Volume Knob, Not A Prescription") rejects the sub-minute floor outright, on a within-subject trial resting one leg **2 minutes** (3–4 sets to failure) and the other **20 seconds** (as many sets as needed to match reps): after 10 weeks, **no significant difference in any quad head by MRI** and near-identical **42%** strength gains, with the short-rest leg finishing in about 3 minutes against 7.

This video does not side with either camp — **it supplies the reconciling principle**, five years earlier:

- On **compounds** he would resist a blanket rejection of the floor. Four studies with **sets and proximity to failure matched** found 30–60 seconds worse than 2.5–3 minutes. His position is that short rest costs reps, and lost reps cost growth.
- But **once reps are equated he already concedes the floor disappears**, twice over. **Longo** found 4–5 sets to failure at 1 minute matched 3 sets at 3 minutes; **Ahtiainen** found 2 minutes matched 5 minutes with one extra set. That is exactly the rep-equating manoeuvre the Henselmans trial performs, and in both cases the short-rest condition caught up. His **Villanueva** critique is the same principle run backwards: with volume load equated, the short-rest group won *because* it was pushed nearer failure.
- On **isolation** he is closest of all, and arguably goes further than Henselmans: **Ozaki** found 3 minutes, 90 seconds and drop sets (effectively zero rest) all equivalent for elbow flexor CSA, and **Fink 2018** found drop sets *superior* to 3 sets with 90 seconds on the pushdown. On isolation exercises the sub-minute floor has no support in the evidence he reviews. This also anticipates the Bradshaw myo-reps result recorded in `Menno Henselmans videos/3-epic-new-studies-build-more-muscle.md`, where 20-second inter-mini-set rests matched straight sets — Fink 2018 is structurally the same experiment run in 2018 on the triceps.

So the reconciliation both notes are reaching for is that **rest length is not the active ingredient — hard reps near failure are** — and rest matters only insofar as cutting it costs reps. It costs more on compounds than on isolation work, which is the same shape as the library's compound/isolation split arrived at from the opposite direction.

### A direct clash on one paper

Worth flagging because both sides cite the **same study**. The Longo paper here (listed by him as "Longo et al. 2020"; published as [PMID 35622106](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35622106/), *Volume Load Rather Than Resting Interval Influences Muscle Hypertrophy*) is described in `Menno Henselmans videos/long-rest-between-sets-max-muscle.md` as "the cleanest demonstration" of the volume-load position.

**House of Hypertrophy rejects that reading of it explicitly**, and his argument is good. Volume load cannot be the mediator, because 3 sets to failure at 60% 1RM accumulate roughly 60% more volume load than 3 sets at 80% while producing similar growth — so the quantity is not tracking the stimulus. His secondary objection is just as strong: longer rests raise volume load on isolation exercises too, yet the isolation evidence is conflicting.

The two positions are closer than they look, and the difference is about what "volume load" is being asked to do. Both agree the mechanism is **reps performed near failure**. Where the Henselmans note treats volume load as a serviceable proxy for that, this video argues the proxy is provably broken and should be replaced by hard-set counting. On the substance of that narrow point, this video has the better of it — and it is the more useful framing, because it explains why the *same* rest reduction that barely matters on a curl matters a lot on a squat.

## Flag: where the conclusion outruns the evidence

The headline conclusion is sound, but two derived recommendations are inferences from acute data rather than findings: the **1–2 minutes for women** and **1–2 minutes for older lifters** figures come entirely from single-session rep-sustainment and torque-maintenance studies, with no training study in either population that survives his own critique. He is upfront that these are extrapolations, but they are presented as numbers, and numbers travel further than their caveats.
