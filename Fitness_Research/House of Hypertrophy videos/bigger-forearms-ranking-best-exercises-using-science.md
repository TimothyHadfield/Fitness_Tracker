# Forearm Exercises Ranked by Muscle Length — An Extrapolation, Not a Finding

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Bigger Forearms: Ranking the Best Exercises Using Science" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3rdHppk4L8)) · 2024-10-21 · 12 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **Direct forearm work is needed** to maximise forearm size. Grip demand from rows, deadlifts and curls stimulates the forearms, but they are rarely the limiting factor — and if they are, you are compromising the exercise's intended target. Use straps and train forearms separately.
- The entire ranking is built on **one transferred principle**: training at longer muscle lengths builds more muscle than training at shorter lengths. He states plainly at the end that **no study has directly measured forearm hypertrophy** and the tiers are "my best guess".
- **Wrist flexion (anterior compartment, 20 muscles, the larger half):** standing wrist curls sit in **C tier** because the wrist never reaches extension and the fingers stay flexed. Adding fat grips or letting the bar roll to the fingers moves them to **B tier**. Forearm-supported wrist curls across a bench reach a genuinely extended wrist and hit **A tier**; supported plus fat grips or finger roll reaches **S tier**.
- **Wrist extension (posterior compartment):** standing wrist extensions are **A tier**; supported-across-a-bench wrist extensions are **S tier**. Here finger flexion around the bar is *good* — some extensors lengthen with the fingers flexed, so fat grips would shorten them.
- **Brachioradialis:** the widespread claim that a neutral or pronated grip increases brachioradialis activation **fails to replicate** — multiple studies find similar or slightly *better* activation with a supinated grip.
- He rejects Mike Mentzer's "no direct forearm work needed" claim, and rejects it on the right grounds — Mentzer is n=1 with unusual genetics, and other physiques with notable forearms (Draper, Cypes) trained them directly.

## The length logic, and how far it stretches
Two forearm flexors — **flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus** — cross the fingers as well as the wrist, so their length depends on *both* joints. That single anatomical fact drives most of the tier separation: a standing wrist curl leaves the wrist unextended *and* the fingers flexed, putting those two muscles at short lengths on both counts. Fat grips widen the bar and force relative finger extension; the roll-to-fingertips variation achieves greater peak finger extension but only momentarily, whereas fat grips hold the extended finger position throughout. He calls the comparison between the two **unclear** and places them in the same tier rather than guessing — a small but characteristic restraint.

He also addresses the inclined-surface variation, where peak resistance falls at a more extended wrist position rather than at neutral. He is **sceptical the difference is large** and declines to separate it out, keeping inclined and flat supported variations together in S tier and offering only a soft "possibly high tier / possibly low tier" split "with caution".

## The long-length premise is contested and he says so
He pre-empts the obvious objection: the recent Nippard-funded study suggesting long-muscle-length training does not out-build in trained individuals. His reading is that the study compared **lengthened partials to a full range of motion** — both reaching the same maximum muscle length — which is a different question from short-range versus long-range training. He commits to updating if future work goes against him. Given that the whole tier list rests on the length premise, foregrounding the contradictory study is the right call.

## Brachioradialis: activation versus growth
The brachioradialis is a large posterior-compartment muscle that does not extend the wrist; it is an elbow flexor, worked in every pull and curl. The grip-position folklore does not survive the EMG data. He wants a **growth** study comparing grips and does not have one, so he declines to recommend a grip.

For exercise selection he cites a **conference abstract** comparing incline curls to preacher curls, with better brachioradialis hypertrophy from the preacher curl — significant only at the **60% region**, so incline curls still produced clear gains. His proposed mechanism is that the preacher curl is hardest at early elbow flexion angles where the brachioradialis is long. If that holds, cable curls set up for peak tension at early flexion angles would join preacher curls in S tier, and standard curls would sit with incline curls a tier lower.

## What he leaves out
Static holds, farmer's walks, hangs and grippers train **finger flexion**, and those muscles are already worked heavily by wrist curls — so he does not consider them essential. Same for pronation/supination and radial/ulnar deviation training. Wrist rollers and rice-bucket work are deferred to a pinned comment. All of this is triaged to his longer free forearm guide.

**Flagged:** two layers of extrapolation stack here. The tiers come from length research on *other* muscles applied to the forearm, and within the forearm they are reasoned from joint angles and anatomy rather than measured. The brachioradialis ranking additionally rests on an **unpublished conference abstract**. His closing disclaimer is explicit and unusually direct — "please don't consider these rankings as 100% definitely true" — but the tier-list format itself communicates far more certainty than the evidence supports.
