# Lactate and the Burn Are Not Driving Your Gains

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "A HUGE Muscle Building Myth (22 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6T4oV4foEKM)) · 2022-10-10 · 11 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Three background myths go first: humans produce **lactate, not lactic acid** under normal physiological conditions; lactate **does not cause fatigue** (it is a usable fuel for muscle and brain); and lactate **alone does not cause the burn**.
- On the burn specifically, Pollak's infusion experiment is decisive in design: infusing **lactate, ATP or hydrogen ions individually** to high levels produced no pain. Pain appeared only when **all three were infused together**. The sensation is combinatorial.
- The pro-lactate hypertrophy evidence is **in vitro, animal, or hormonal** — none of it human growth outcomes. The one rat study cited fed lactate *and caffeine*, which confounds it. The associated testosterone spikes are a dead end: acute anabolic hormone rises do not track growth.
- The strongest disconfirming evidence: **infusing lactate into humans during leg extension training raised blood and muscle lactate but did not raise anabolic signalling above saline**.
- Multiple dissociations between lactate and growth: **400m sprinting produces comparable muscle lactate to a 5-set lower-body session**; **repeated sprint cycling** matches three sets of dumbbell curls to failure. Neither sprint protocol is a serious hypertrophy stimulus.
- The generalisation to metabolites as a class: **blood flow restriction, high reps and low reps grow muscle similarly** despite very different metabolite accumulation, and **trapping metabolites with a cuff after training does not add growth** — in one study it reduced it.

## Where the lactate hypothesis comes from, and why it thins out
A 2022 review summarises in vitro and animal work in which lactate activates **satellite cells** and downstream growth signalling. He accepts the mechanism exists in a dish and in rodents, then points out that none of it is human hypertrophy. The rat study — treadmill running for four weeks with lactate plus caffeine — cannot separate the two compounds. The remaining pillar is the acute testosterone rise accompanying exercise-induced lactate, and the literature on transient anabolic hormone spikes is clearly against a causal link to growth.

The **Swedish infusion study** is the cleanest test available and it comes out negative. He does not oversell it: the lactate was infused into the **arm** while the legs trained, so muscle lactate rose but not dramatically, and he says directly that injecting into the working muscle would have been the better design. Flagging the weakness of a study that supports his own conclusion is what makes this channel worth reading.

## The dissociation arguments
These are the most persuasive part of the video because they need no mechanism — they just show lactate and growth coming apart:

- **Sprinting.** 400m running produces muscle lactate comparable to five sets of four lower-body exercises. Nobody thinks it is the better hypertrophy stimulus.
- **Sprint cycling.** Repeated short cycling sprints match three sets of dumbbell curls to failure for muscle lactate. Same conclusion.
- **Tempo.** Different rep tempos to exhaustion produce different lactate rises, yet a wide range of tempos build muscle similarly when reps go to failure. He adds the inconvenient detail: the **slowest lowering tempo produced the *lowest* lactate**, and some evidence suggests slower eccentrics may build *more* muscle — the opposite of the lactate prediction. He notes this is not a consistent finding.
- **Rest intervals.** One minute between sets raises lactate more than five minutes, but **myofibrillar protein synthesis was largely greater after the five-minute rest**.
- **Cuffed rest periods.** Blood flow restriction applied *between* sets of leg extensions raised overall lactate, yet quadriceps growth after eight weeks was **similar** to normal rest.

He closes with a point that cuts against measuring lactate at all: once produced, it is shuttled — used as fuel, moved to other fibres, moved to organs — so it does not sit in the muscle acting on it.

## Metabolites in general, and what actually drives growth
Higher reps to failure and blood flow restriction both generate more metabolite accumulation than heavy low-rep work, yet all three produce comparable hypertrophy. The common "different pathways to the same result" rescue — that BFR and high reps work via metabolites while heavy work operates through mechanical tension — is rejected. His account is that **mechanical tension is the dominant driver in all three cases**: mechanosensors convert force into growth signalling (**mechanotransduction**), and what matters is recruiting many fibres producing meaningful force for a meaningful duration. Heavy loads reach high tension immediately; light loads and BFR start low and arrive there as fatigue forces progressive recruitment near failure. Metabolite accumulation is then a **byproduct of the effort required to reach failure**, not the cause of the growth.

Two post-exercise cuff studies test this directly. Training normally to failure, then applying blood flow restriction for **3–5 minutes** afterwards, should trap metabolites and boost growth if metabolites matter. After eight weeks, neither study found added growth, and one found **reduced** growth in its female subjects.

## The caveat he insists on
He explicitly refuses the stronger claim. "More metabolites does not mean more hypertrophy" is not the same as "metabolites play zero role". He offers two live possibilities: a **threshold effect**, where metabolites matter up to a low level that essentially all the reviewed research sits above, or an **extreme-level effect** not reachable in humans — noting that the in vitro work linking lactate to hypertrophy used **continuous high lactate exposure for two to six hours, sometimes days**. Either could be true and more research is needed.

The practical conclusion: focus on mechanical tension, achieved simply by taking reps to or close to failure. The burn is a sensation, not a target.

**Flagged:** the mechanical-tension account he substitutes is itself largely mechanism rather than outcome data — it explains the equivalence of loads rather than being independently demonstrated. That said, his negative claim (metabolites are not a potent driver) rests on genuine growth-outcome studies, which is the right way round. Note also that two references are contradictory on tempo and he keeps both in.
