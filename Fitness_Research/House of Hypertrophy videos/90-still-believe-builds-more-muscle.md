# Five Bodybuilding Beliefs the Research Doesn't Support

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "90% Still Believe This Builds More Muscle" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRIB2SkGT0E)) · 2024-02-05 · 13 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Five beliefs examined: **the pump as a growth driver, time under tension, short rest periods, muscle damage / "micro-tears", and muscle confusion**. All five come out as false or at least unsupported, and one — muscle confusion — comes out as harmless rather than harmful.
- The pump is a **byproduct, not the goal**. Multiple techniques raise the pump without raising growth. An FST-7-style program in trained lifters produced minimal, mostly non-significant arm growth, with some subjects slightly losing size.
- **Time under tension is not mechanical tension.** Long slow sets accumulate time far from failure; what matters is *time under high tension*, which is achieved by getting to or near failure anywhere in roughly the **6–35 rep range**.
- **Short rest is not superior.** Well-designed studies find **2.5–3 minutes** produces more hypertrophy than shorter rest, despite bodybuilders surveying at 1–2 minutes and, in a 1987 sample, 10–90 seconds.
- **Micro-tears are not the primary initiator of growth.** Tension is the best-characterised mechanism; damage may play some role, or none. Soreness is a poor proxy — the body reduces damage over time while growth continues.
- **Muscle confusion doesn't work, but it doesn't hurt either.** Two 8-week studies in trained subjects found similar hypertrophy between fixed programs and heavily varied ones.

## The pump
[00:31] The pump is a transient size increase from increased blood volume and fluid during intense contractions. The claim under test is that maximising it maximises gains — the reasoning behind Hany Rambod's FST-7, which uses high sets and short rest to stretch the fascia and "make room" for growth.

[01:01] A 2023 study had trained subjects run an FST-inspired biceps and triceps program with very high volume and short rest; one group stretched between some sets, the other rested passively. **Overall arm hypertrophy was minimal in both**, with multiple non-significant changes and some subjects slightly losing size. He offers a mechanism — excessive damage redirecting protein synthesis toward repair rather than growth — and marks it as speculation. He also takes care to say high volumes *can* produce respectable hypertrophy elsewhere, so this is not an anti-volume argument.

[02:04] The supporting case that pump and growth are decoupled: pharmacologically induced reactive hyperaemia does not improve protein synthesis; higher reps give a bigger pump but similar growth to lower reps; very high reps give a tremendous pump and inferior gains; drop sets give an incredible pump and are largely similar to normal sets; constant-tension technique gives a great pump and is mostly found equivalent to normal technique. Verdict: treat the pump as a byproduct. Chasing it is fine because most pump-inducing techniques don't compromise gains.

## Time under tension
[03:07] The belief is that a set should last ~40–60 seconds, hence Mike Mentzer's 4-second-up / 4-second-down tempo. The logic runs from "tension is likely the primary initiator of hypertrophy" to "so make the set longer."

[03:38] A recent review finds that **provided you train hard and get to or near failure, slower tempos produce similar hypertrophy to faster ones**. Separately, a wide range of rep numbers produces similar gains under the same proviso, at a typical 1–2 second concentric and eccentric.

[04:38] His resolution is the useful part of this section. **Time under tension is not mechanical tension.** When a set is long, most of the early time is far from failure. Getting close to failure is what forces full fibre recruitment and exposes those fibres to tension — so *time under high tension* ends up similar across very different total set durations, and across low and high reps alike. Practical version: no threshold to hit, no need to prolong sets; just get to or near failure in the 6–35 rep range.

## Short rest between sets
[05:39] Shorter rest reliably increases the pump, and it has been known since the 1990s that it transiently raises anabolic hormones such as growth hormone. Bodybuilders rest short: a **1987** paper found a subset resting **10–90 seconds**, and a **2013 survey of 127 competitive bodybuilders** found the majority resting **1–2 minutes**.

[06:10] Against this, well-designed studies find **2.5–3 minutes** produces greater hypertrophy than shorter rest. Transient hormonal spikes are not strongly related to the growth process; the likelier explanation is that longer rest dissipates fatigue that would otherwise limit fibre exposure to tension.

[06:41] Two honest qualifications: most of this research used programs heavy in compound exercises, so short rest may be fine on isolation work; and extra sets can compensate — one study found **4–5 leg press sets with 1 minute rest** produced similar gains to **3 sets with 3 minutes**.

## Micro-tears and soreness
[07:44] He quotes the standard formulation — lifting tears the muscle, the body repairs and enlarges it — and notes the corollary many people draw, that soreness is a valid proxy for growth. Soreness has multiple potential causes, damage being only one.

[08:15] Three lines of evidence against damage as the driver: programs with greater average damage produce similar hypertrophy to programs with lower damage; the body adapts and reduces damage over time while significant growth continues; and the speculated pathways from damage to growth have strong counterarguments. His conclusion is measured — damage may play some role, and researchers may yet find it has none — but it is **not the primary stimulus**. Tension is the best-characterised mechanism as of now.

## Muscle confusion
[09:16] Arnold's "shocking principle": muscles go stale and need surprising with new variables or unusual exercises. One study had trained subjects run either the same program for 8 weeks or switch training variables every session — **hypertrophy was similar**. A second compared a fixed 8-week program against a phone app generating new exercises for each muscle every session — again **largely similar**.

[10:19] His reading is the interesting one: people expecting muscle confusion to be *worse* should be surprised it wasn't, and this fits the video's theme — what matters is training a muscle hard enough to expose it to tension, and there is more than one way to do that. He still recommends a consistent, well-designed program over unpredictable variation, and concedes sensible long-term variation might be beneficial but is currently unknown.
