# Androgen Receptors: What the Evidence Actually Shows

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Hidden KEY for Building Muscle (Is it LEGIT?)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FOkJsKJzBBI)) · 2023-02-26 · 13 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Androgen receptor (AR) content **may** matter for hypertrophy in natural lifters, but the evidence is entirely associational in humans, and the two best-known studies disagree.
- **Morton et al. (2018, Canada)**: 49 trained men, 4 days/week for 12 weeks. High responders had greater AR content, with linear relationships between AR content and lean mass, slow-twitch and fast-twitch fibre gains. Resting hormones — including total and free testosterone — showed **no** relationship with growth.
- **Mobley et al. (2018, USA)**: 67 untrained men, categorised as high/moderate/low responders by vastus lateralis growth. **AR content was similar across all three groups**, as was testosterone. This is the study almost nobody citing the first one mentions.
- Two other studies (Ahtiainen 2011, Finland, 21 weeks; Mitchell 2013, Canada, 16 weeks) found the **change** in AR content with training correlated with fibre size and lean mass gains — a different association from the first study's.
- **There is no established way to raise AR content.** The L-carnitine L-tartrate claim rests on a single 10-subject, 21-day study. Intermittent fasting and caffeine claims have no long-term human AR data at all.
- His own summary is blunt: "there isn't a proactive cool takeaway from this video." The value is in seeing how thin an "evidence-based" claim can be.

## Why the question exists
[00:03] Hubal et al. trained **585 individuals'** biceps for 12 weeks. Many gained close to 20%, some over 50%, and **some lost about 5%** — on identical training. Genetics is the default explanation, and there is evidence for it, but various physiological factors may also separate responders. Capillary density is one candidate (with the interesting implication that cardiovascular training might benefit long-term hypertrophy). AR content is the one this video examines.

[01:05] The mechanism is straightforward: androgens act through ARs, which sit inside cells including muscle fibres; binding initiates events including increased myofibrillar protein synthesis. More receptors should mean more of a person's androgens directed at building muscle. The circumstantial support he offers is that traps and delts grow disproportionately in people using anabolic steroids, possibly because those muscles carry more ARs (Kadi et al.).

## The two studies that disagree
[02:37] **Morton et al. (2018)**, 49 trained men, 12 weeks. A backward-elimination regression across resting hormone concentrations found **no relationship between any resting hormone and hypertrophy**, including total and free testosterone. Splitting subjects into high and low responders, high responders tended to have greater AR content; pooling both groups gave linear relationships between AR content and lean mass, slow-twitch fibre and fast-twitch fibre gains. This is the paper everyone cites.

[03:38] **Mobley et al. (2018)**, 67 untrained men, categorised as high, moderate or low responders by VL growth. Testosterone was comparable across the three groups — consistent with Morton. But **AR content was also comparable**, which is not.

[04:38] His handling of the conflict is the most instructive part of the video. He does not pick a winner. Hypertrophy is multifactorial and hard to resolve with any one study; the second study's null is not enough to debunk the idea; and there is a plausible moderator — the first study used **trained** subjects, the second **untrained**.

## A different association: change in AR content
[05:08] In both studies above, AR content did **not** increase with training. Other work finds it sometimes does, and links that increase to growth:
- **Ahtiainen et al. (2011, Finland)**: untrained younger and older men, twice weekly for **21 weeks**. Change in AR content correlated with both muscle fibre size gains and lean mass gains.
- **Mitchell et al. (2013, Canada)**: 23 untrained men, four times weekly for **16 weeks**. Change in AR content correlated with fibre size increases.

[06:38] He flags the limitation himself, clearly: all of this is **associational and between-person**, so it cannot show that raising one person's AR content raises their muscle mass. The only causal evidence is animal work showing that **blocking** ARs reduces hypertrophy and strength gains from resistance exercise — which supports a role for ARs, not for increasing them.

## Can you increase AR content?
[07:08] Resistance training itself sometimes increases AR content, but inconsistently, and no one knows which training variables optimise it. He then offers a speculation, labelled as such twice: perhaps what raises AR content differs between people, and perhaps that partly explains why optimal training variables differ between individuals. He notes he is exploring this in a book he is writing. This is mechanism-stacking on associational data and he says so — "extremely speculative and nowhere near a definitive conclusion."

### L-carnitine L-tartrate
[09:11] The belief traces to **Kraemer et al. (2006)**: **10 trained men**, L-carnitine L-tartrate or placebo, **21 days**; resting AR content was higher in the supplemented group. One study, ten subjects, three weeks.

[09:42] His analogy is the best argument in the video. **D-aspartic acid**: the first human study in 2009 (Topo et al.) found a **42% testosterone increase**, and supplement companies sold it as scientifically supported. **Six subsequent studies failed to replicate** — one found a *decrease*, the other five found no effect. He is careful to say this does not predict L-carnitine L-tartrate will fail, only that a single supportive study is never proof.

### Intermittent fasting and caffeine
[10:47] He traced the online claims to their sources. The intermittent fasting citation examined short-term effects on **testosterone**, not AR content, and no long-term AR data exists. The caffeine citation was conducted **on rats**, with no human data on long-term caffeine and AR content. This is exactly the checking behaviour the channel is worth reading for.

## Where the reasoning is strongest and weakest
Strongest: he leads with the study that contradicts the popular one, refuses to resolve the conflict beyond the evidence, and correctly labels all human data here as associational. Weakest: the traps-and-delts steroid observation is used to motivate the AR story but is an anecdotal pattern with an uncited receptor-density explanation; and the speculation that AR responses explain individual differences in optimal programming has no supporting data at all, though he does flag it as speculation.
