# Triceps Training: Why Pressing Isn't Enough, and How Much Work the Arm Actually Needs

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The ULTIMATE Guide to Building Triceps (63 Studies)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iu2g1PEdQcc)) · 2021-05-04 · 45 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

> **Reference warning.** This video's description contains **no reference list** — only timestamps, music credits and a single link to the Costa et al. study. The 63 studies are cited on screen only. Author names in this note are legible (the transcript is human-authored, not auto-captioned) but are **unverified** against any source list. See the References section.
>
> **Currency warning.** This is a 2021 guide, and on its single most consequential question — overhead extensions versus pressdowns — it has been **superseded by evidence the same creator adopted in 2023**. See the cross-reference section before using it.

## Bottom line
- **The triceps are roughly 2.5× the size of the biceps**, so they dominate arm size, and the video is worth reading for that reason alone.
- **Pressing does not build the long head.** In the key study, barbell benching produced good lateral head growth, decent medial head growth and **very little long head growth** — because the long head shortens at the shoulder during the lowering phase of any horizontal press.
- **Skull crushers have the mirror-image problem**: significant medial and long head growth, but **hardly any lateral head growth**. Doing both grew all three heads. That head-by-head detail is this video's most useful original contribution.
- **Rep range barely matters**: 5–35 reps to failure produce similar triceps growth. Heavy loading is not required despite the triceps being fast-twitch-dominant.
- **Volume: 30–45 weekly sets with short rests, or 14–28 with long rests.** The high figure is well above the library's standing prescription — see the disagreement section.
- **Frequency: he concludes it is genuinely unresolved**, with five studies pointing four different ways, and offers individual variation as the likely explanation rather than picking a winner.
- **On overhead extensions he was right to hedge in 2021 and is now out of date** — the study he had was underpowered at n=9 and null; the study he did not yet have is the library's strongest exercise-selection finding.

## Decision 1: do your presses cover the triceps?

Partly, and not for the long head.

**Brandao et al.** had untrained men train the **barbell bench press** with a 2×-shoulder-width grip, 3–5 sets to failure at 80% 1RM, 3 minutes rest, twice a week for 10 weeks, with cross-sectional area measured at 50–60% of upper arm length. Result: **good lateral head growth, fairly decent medial head growth, very little long head growth.**

The mechanism is clean and he states it properly: the long head is the only head crossing the shoulder joint. During the lowering phase of a horizontal press the shoulder extends, which **shortens** the long head at the same time as the elbow flexion lengthens it — so its length barely changes and it cannot contribute much force on the way up. **Wakahara et al.** supports this from a different direction, with T2-weighted imaging showing long head activation below medial and lateral head activation during a dumbbell bench press.

On **vertical presses** he is admirably blank: he could find **no long-term studies and no reliable activation studies**. He reasons that since there is little shoulder extension in the lowering phase, the long head is probably involved — and then explicitly says he does not say this with confidence because the research does not exist. This is the right way to handle a hole.

Two anatomical caveats he raises against over-thinking head targeting. First, the long head's contribution to shoulder extension and adduction appears **small**, so exercises involving only those actions probably do not stimulate it much. Second, the literature on **which head does what during elbow extension is completely conflicting** — Duchenne said in 1867 that medial and lateral dominate with the long head contributing less, and since then papers have agreed, disagreed, and found all three equal. A study manipulating shoulder flexion angle (0°, 90°, 180°) found the ranking changes with angle; another found no differences at 0° or 180°. He attributes some of the conflict to genuine individual differences in activation patterns and concludes the head-role literature is **unusable for exercise selection**. That is a hard thing to say after reviewing it, and it is correct.

## Decision 2: how many isolation exercises, and which?

His answer is **2–3 isolation exercises on top of your compounds**, chosen to vary shoulder angle and to load different points of the elbow extension range.

The argument runs on two legs, one strong and one stretched:

**Strong leg — direct outcome data.**
- **Brandao** also ran a **skull crusher** group on the same protocol: significant medial and long head growth, **hardly any lateral head growth**. Wakahara's T2 imaging agrees, showing lateral head activation below medial and long during dumbbell skull crushers. He notes honestly that he found **no reliable evidence explaining why**.
- Brandao's other two groups did **both** bench and skull crushers, in either order. Both groups grew **all three heads significantly**. This is the cleanest demonstration in the video that compounds and isolation are complementary rather than redundant.
- **Costa et al.**: 22 detrained men (no training in the prior 6 months), 3×/week for 8 weeks, all exercises 3 sets of 8–12 with 90–120 s rest. Non-varied did barbell bench + triceps extension every session. Varied did bench + extension on day 1, incline bench + seated cable extension on day 2, decline bench + cable kickback on day 3. Triceps thickness at 50%, 60% and 70% favoured the varied group, particularly at 60% and 70%.

**Stretched leg — the fibre subpopulation argument.** **Zuylen et al.** identified three subpopulations of fibres within the triceps: one active during elbow extension, supination *or* pronation; one during elbow extension or supination but not pronation; one during elbow extension only. He then extrapolates twice — that subpopulations may also differ by shoulder angle, and by stage of elbow extension — to justify varying both. He flags both extrapolations ("extrapolating from this study", "if true") but they carry a lot of the recommendation's weight, and the underlying study looked at a few fibre groups during isolated joint actions, not at different exercises.

His concrete example is the resistance profile pairing: **pushdowns are hardest near lockout** (triceps near fully shortened) while **skull crushers are hardest when the triceps are stretched**, making them complementary. Note this carefully — the library takes the opposite view of what the pushdown's profile is worth, and so, later, does he.

## Decision 3: rep range and load

The triceps are fast-twitch dominant, which has produced recommendations for heavy, low-rep work. He tests that and rejects it, with two studies:

- **Schoenfeld**, 18 men with ~3.5 years experience, 7 exercises × 3 sets, 90 s rest, 3×/wk for 8 weeks (bench and overhead press were the triceps exercises): **8–12RM vs 25–35RM** produced **similar** triceps thickness at 60% upper arm length.
- **Schoenfeld**, 19 men with ~4.5 years experience, 2 min rest, same structure: **2–4RM vs 8–12RM** — thickness **slightly favoured the moderate group**.

So heavier is not better, and very heavy may be slightly worse. His summary — **5 to 35 reps taken to or very close to failure produce similar hypertrophy** — matches the library.

A related fibre-type point he handles carefully: one study (**Elder et al.**) found the lateral and long heads more fast-twitch and the **medial head more slow-twitch**, which would make the medial head harder to grow given fast-twitch fibres' greater hypertrophic potential. **Wakahara et al.** found minimal growth in the lower (mostly medial) regions after 12 weeks of dumbbell skull crushers, with the authors offering the same explanation. He hedges properly — one study, and fibre type varies between individuals — but this is the thinnest chain in the video and it produces a memorable claim.

## Decision 4: how much volume?

He splits the evidence by rest interval, explicitly referencing his own rest-interval guide (see `short-vs-long-rest-intervals-muscle-hypertrophy.md`), on the grounds that more sets with short rest can compensate for the shorter rest. That is a genuinely careful piece of study triage — most reviews pool these.

**Short rest intervals — more is better, up to a lot:**

| Study | Subjects | Rest | Comparison | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Schoenfeld | Trained, 3 days/wk, 8 wks, all compound (bench + OHP) | 90 s | 30 vs 18 vs 6 weekly sets | **30** |
| Brigatto | Trained, 2 days/wk, 8 wks, half compound half isolation | 60 s | 32 vs 24 vs 16 | **32** |
| Radaelli | Calisthenics-experienced, 3 days/wk, **6 months**, ⅔ compound | 90–120 s | 45 vs 27 vs 9 | **45** |

He does not simply bank this. He **questions how sustainable 30–45 weekly sets is long term**, notes two of the three studies ran only 8 weeks, and points out that all subjects were trained — trained lifters show blunted anabolic responses, less damage and faster recovery, so untrained lifters probably need far less, "perhaps 15–20."

**Long rest intervals — much less:** **Ostrowski et al.**, 3 minutes rest, found **14 and 28 weekly sets equivalent, both better than 7**, over 10 weeks with volume split across 2 days. He flags the likely confound: the 28-set group did **16 sets in one session and 12 in the other**, which may exceed a per-session ceiling, and the short-rest studies that found benefits from more sets spread them over 3 days. Recommendation: **14–28 weekly sets** with long rests.

On the accounting question — should a bench press set count as half a triceps set? — he argues **no, count it as one**, which is what the research did. His reasoning is that once you are running compounds plus 2–3 isolation exercises, none of your sets are identical stimuli anyway, so fractional counting introduces false precision.

## Decision 5: how often?

**First, the recovery objection.** Some argue the triceps need lower frequency because they recover slowly, citing **Chen et al.**: 9 untrained men, eccentric-only training (5 sets of 10 lowering-phase reps) across several muscle groups, with force and soreness tracked to 5 days. The triceps did show greater force loss and soreness than many other muscles.

He dismantles this using the **repeated bout effect**, and he does it with the same study: Chen repeated the identical protocol 2 weeks later, and all muscle groups showed less force loss and soreness. **Akagi et al.** closes it — 23 untrained men, dumbbell skull crushers 5×8 at 80% 1RM, 90 s rest, 3×/wk for 6 weeks; triceps shear modulus (a proxy for damage) was **no different from the untrained control group and no different from baseline** by week 6, despite the same research group having previously shown shear modulus rising one day after skull crushers. **The recovery objection to high frequency does not survive the first few weeks of training.**

**Then, the actual frequency evidence — five studies, no answer:**

| Study | Subjects | Weekly sets | Comparison | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brigatto | ~4.5 yrs experience | 16 | 1×/wk vs 2×/wk | No difference |
| Saric | ≥6 months, all isolation | 12 | 3×/wk vs 6×/wk | No difference |
| Lasevicius | ~3 yrs | 24 | 2×/wk vs 3×/wk | **2× better** |
| Schoenfeld | ~4.5 yrs | 15 | 2×/wk vs 3×/wk | **3× better** |
| Zaroni | ~6.5 yrs | 30 | 2×/wk vs 5×/wk | **5× better** |

He calls this "completely unclear" and refuses to pick, which is the right call given two studies point in exactly opposite directions on the same comparison. His proposed explanation is individual response, supported by **Damas et al.**: 19 untrained men trained one leg at high frequency and volume (5×/wk) and the other at low (2–3×/wk), 3 sets of 9–12 to failure. **6 subjects responded better to high, 7 better to low, 6 similarly.** Using a within-subject design to demonstrate individual variation is a genuinely good piece of evidence selection, and it justifies his advice to experiment or simply fit your schedule.

He extends the individual-variation point to volume as well — which quietly softens the 30–45 set recommendation above.

## Decision 6: are hypoxia techniques worth adding?

Two studies for, three lines against, and he lands on "probably fine, not established."

**For:**
- **Goto et al.**: 44 men with ≥1 year experience, skull crushers 3×8, 3×/wk for 8 weeks. Full ROM versus a **partial ROM (45°→90°→45° of elbow flexion)** that maintains constant tension and restricts blood flow. Triceps CSA at 60% was **significantly greater in the partial group** — and, more interestingly, the degree of hypoxia measured in individual subjects **correlated with their CSA increase**.
- **Fink et al.**: 16 untrained men, triceps pushdown only, 2×/wk for 8 weeks. A **drop set** (12RM then −20% three times) beat 3 sets to failure at 12RM with 90 s rest. He notes this is, at the time of recording, **the only study finding drop sets superior to normal sets** — every other one, on biceps or quads, found no difference.

**Against:**
- **Tanimoto et al.**: chest press with versus without lockout for 8 weeks — the no-lockout condition would produce more triceps hypoxia — produced **similar** triceps thickness.
- **Two blood flow restriction papers**: BFR bands on the upper arms during bench pressing produced **similar** triceps CSA increases to normal benching.

His conclusion — no clear evidence either way, no real downside, feel free to include some — is appropriately weak for the evidence.

## Flag: where the argument outruns the evidence

Two places.

1. **The medial-head-is-harder-to-grow claim** rests on a single fibre-type study plus one set of authors' speculation about their own null result. He hedges it, but it is a memorable claim built on very little.
2. **The fibre-subpopulation argument** is the load-bearing justification for "2–3 isolation exercises at varying shoulder angles," and it is a double extrapolation from a study that examined elbow extension, supination and pronation — not exercises, not shoulder angles, not growth. The recommendation itself is supported independently by Costa and by Brandao's head-by-head results, so the conclusion survives; but the mechanism offered for it is doing more work than it can carry.

## How this sits against the rest of the library

### Agreement, with a useful refinement

**On presses failing the long head, three sources now converge.** `Jeff Nippard videos/triceps-exercises-ranked.md`: "on presses the long head lengthens at the shoulder while shortening at the elbow, so its length barely changes — presses under-train it." `Menno Henselmans videos/avoid-common-triceps-training-mistake.md`: "pressing exercises don't build the long head… five sets of barbell bench = five sets for medial and lateral, roughly zero for the long head." This video supplies the same mechanism and the primary study behind it.

**And it refines the library's summary of that study.** The library records Brandão 2020 ([PMID 32149887](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32149887/)) as "skull crushers ≈ double the total triceps growth of barbell bench." True, but this video reports the **head-by-head breakdown that the summary loses**: skull crushers grew the medial and long heads well and the **lateral head hardly at all**, while the bench did the reverse. The two exercises are not ranked, they are complementary — and Brandão's combined groups, which the library does not mention, grew all three heads. That materially changes what the study recommends: not "replace bench with skull crushers," but "run both."

**On programming multiple shoulder angles**, this video and `Jeff Nippard videos/triceps-exercises-ranked.md` agree almost verbatim — Nippard prescribes "at least two or three shoulder angles," and `Jeff Nippard videos/build-huge-triceps-optimal-training-technique.md` names three arm positions (overhead, at the sides, behind the torso). This video's overhead extension + skull crusher + pressdown recommendation is the same programme.

### The one that matters: overhead extensions

The library's strongest single exercise-selection finding is **Maeo et al. 2023** ([PMID 35819335](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/)): **overhead cable extensions produced roughly 40% more triceps growth than pressdowns over 12 weeks**, and grew all three heads more, not only the long head. It is recorded in `Jeff Nippard videos/triceps-exercises-ranked.md`, `Menno Henselmans videos/avoid-common-triceps-training-mistake.md` and four other notes.

This 2021 guide reviews the **only study available at the time**: 9 untrained women, one arm on cable overhead extensions (30°→110° of elbow flexion) and the other on cable pressdowns (90°→170°), 6 sets of 6 to failure, 2 min rest, 2×/wk for 6 weeks. Long head thickness increases at 50% and 60% were **statistically similar**, with raw percentages leaning to overhead. His verdict — underpowered at n=9, could be a real effect or random variation, "more research would be needed to confirm" — is exactly correct for the evidence he had, and he notes it would match conventional bodybuilding wisdom if true.

**The research he asked for arrived, and he adopted it.** In the biceps guide two years later (`ultimate-guide-biceps-growth-73-studies.md`, April 2023) he describes the same overhead-versus-pressdown comparison as "a great study last year," reports greater long head volume growth **and** the unexpected greater combined medial and lateral head growth, and uses it as a load-bearing part of his stretch argument.

**So this note should be read as superseded on its central exercise-selection question by the same creator.** Two consequences follow:

- The **triceps head coverage problem is smaller than this video makes it**. Here, no single exercise grows all three heads well, which is much of the case for 2–3 isolation exercises. Under Maeo, the overhead extension does grow all three heads more than the pressdown — which is why the library ranks it first outright.
- His treatment of the **pressdown's resistance profile** does not survive either. Here it is a virtue: hardest near lockout, therefore complementary to the skull crusher. The library treats it as a defect — `Menno Henselmans videos/full-rom-dead-stretch-mediated-hypertrophy-lengthened.md`: "a pushdown has almost no tension in the lengthened position… resistance is effectively zero," with the general rule "put the sticking point mid-range or near the stretch, not in full contraction." **He came to agree.** In the 2024 chest guide (`grow-chest-most-detailed-guide-internet-72.md`) he repeatedly argues that tension at the shortened position adds little, on three studies where lengthened partials beat full ROM. The 2021 framing here predates that shift.

### Where it diverges

- **Volume.** 30–45 weekly triceps sets with short rests is far above the library's standing 10–20 sets per muscle per week, and above the per-session ceiling in `Jeff Nippard videos/junk-volume.md` (6 hard sets per muscle per session, 6–8 for arms). Three things narrow the gap: he counts every compound set as a full triceps set; the figure is explicitly conditional on **short rest intervals and trained subjects**; and he questions its long-term sustainability himself. It does not close the gap, though, and later library material pushes the other way — `Menno Henselmans videos/mike-mentzer-right-about-arm-training.md` reports Valério 2026 finding no advantage to extra arm volume, with ~3% growth in all groups. Treat the 30–45 figure as the upper edge of one branch of the literature, not as a prescription.
- **Injury and joint tolerance are absent.** He recommends skull crushers without qualification. `Menno Henselmans videos/elbow-pain-training-triceps-solution.md` calls them "elbow crushers" and proposes skull-overs, rope or single-handle pushdowns, and higher-rep work below the ~70% 1RM tendon threshold as substitutes. Nothing in this video contradicts that; it simply does not address it, which is a consistent blind spot for a channel focused on hypertrophy mechanisms.
