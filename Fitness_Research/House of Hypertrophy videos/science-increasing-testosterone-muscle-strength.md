# Raising Testosterone Adds a Baseline, It Doesn't Multiply Your Training

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "The Science of What INCREASING Testosterone Does to Muscle & Strength" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c4VZx2l-PXU)) · 2023-02-20 · 22 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Raising one person's testosterone **does** increase their muscle and strength — but the effect is **additive**, not synergistic. It raises your baseline; those gains sit on top of whatever training gives you, rather than making training work better.
- The arithmetic from Bhasin's 1996 NEJM trial: testosterone-only gained **6.7%** quadriceps CSA, weights-only **5.4%**, sum **12.1%**, and the combined group got **13.7%**. Close enough to additive that the synergy story fails.
- Two further four-group trials reproduce the pattern at lower doses (Casaburi, 100 mg/wk, +1.07 kg vs +0.49 kg vs +1.41 kg leg lean mass; Chasland, testosterone cream, +0.7 vs +0.7 vs +1.4 kg lean mass).
- **Large** testosterone changes do a great deal on their own — hypogonadal men on 100 mg/wk for 10 weeks with no training gained 5 kg fat-free mass, +12% triceps CSA, +8% quadriceps CSA, +22% bench and +45% squat. **Small** changes (~100–300 ng/dL) do something real but small: roughly 2–4% for muscle and strength.
- Effects on strength are **less consistent** than on muscle across these trials, a point he raises three separate times.
- He reserves two speculative routes by which supraphysiological levels might genuinely be synergistic long-term: better recovery/work capacity, and greater myonuclear addition raising the ceiling on fibre size.

## The argument this video is answering
[00:00] This is a direct follow-up to a video arguing testosterone is not the primary determinant of hypertrophy *across people*. He opens by separating two questions that the comment section had collapsed into one, and the distinction is the intellectual core of the whole video:

- **Between-person:** do people with higher testosterone grow more than people with lower testosterone? Answer: no, not reliably, because many other regulators of growth vary between people. Someone at 400 ng/dL with everything else favourable can out-grow someone at 900 ng/dL with everything else unfavourable.
- **Within-person:** does raising *your own* testosterone increase *your* muscle? A different question, which between-person data cannot answer.

This video answers the second one. Keeping the two apart is what makes the note usable, and it is worth noting he had already made the distinction in the earlier video and was still misread.

## The additive finding
[02:34] **Bhasin 1996 (NEJM).** Men with normal testosterone into four groups: weights only; weights + 600 mg/wk testosterone enanthate (supraphysiological); 600 mg/wk with no training; neither. Quadriceps CSA, triceps CSA, bench and squat strength were all measured.

Muscle gains ranked: weights+testosterone > testosterone-only > weights-only. Strength gains ranked: weights+testosterone > weights-only > testosterone-only. The naive reading is that testosterone amplified training. His reading is arithmetic: testosterone-only 6.7% + weights-only 5.4% = 12.1%, against 13.7% for the combination. Triceps CSA and the strength measures show the same largely-additive pattern. **Testosterone produced gains independent of training and those gains were simply added on.**

[05:07] **Casaburi.** Older men with low-normal testosterone, same four-group design, 100 mg/wk enanthate, lower body trained only, 10 weeks. Leg lean mass: testosterone-only +1.07 kg, weights-only +0.49 kg, sum 1.56 kg, combined group +1.41 kg — additive, if slightly under. Leg press strength was largest in the combined group, with weights-only and testosterone-only similar; here the combination came in **slightly less than additive**, which he reports rather than skipping.

[07:41] **Chasland.** Older men with low-normal testosterone, daily testosterone cream raising levels to ~458 ng/dL (trained) and ~405 ng/dL (untrained), four groups. Lean body mass rose 1.4 kg in the combined group and 0.7 kg each in the testosterone-only and weights-only groups — textbook additive. Strength was measured across several exercises and **some showed the additive pattern and some did not**, which he takes as evidence testosterone's strength effects are inconsistent.

He flags two measurement caveats properly. **Injection pharmacokinetics:** trials reporting levels seven days post-injection are reporting the trough. Cited data show 100 mg of enanthate can push levels to ~1,200 ng/dL for 1–2 days before falling to ~800 ng/dL by day seven, so reported averages understate exposure. Cream and gel studies avoid this problem, which is why he leans on them for the "small increase" conclusions. **Lean body mass is not muscle mass** — he repeats this each time an LBM number appears.

## How big are large changes, and how small are small ones?
[09:12] **Large.** Men with very low testosterone from various conditions, treated with 100 mg/wk for 10 weeks to an average of 767 ng/dL by week 10, **with no resistance training at all**: fat-free mass +5 kg, triceps CSA +12%, quadriceps CSA +8%, bench +22%, squat +45%. Nothing subtle about it.

[11:43] **Small.** In the Chasland cream study, the ~100 ng/dL rise produced trivial strength effects (weights-only far outperformed testosterone-only) and a 0.7 kg lean mass gain — real, but modest, and he immediately questions how good the training in that study was, since it was circuit training with short rests and unclear proximity to failure. In studies with more conventional programmes to or near failure, trained people commonly gain over a kilogram of lean mass in six weeks.

A second study in healthy men with normal levels, 100 mg/wk, no training, gained **1.3 kg lean mass in 8 weeks** — but the researchers measured the peak at 1,196 ng/dL 24 h post-injection (735 ng/dL at day seven), so he correctly declines to file it under "less extreme".

[13:45] The cleanest dose-response evidence is **Finkelstein**: healthy men had endogenous production suppressed with goserelin, then received placebo gel or 1.25 / 2.5 / 5 / 10 g of testosterone gel daily. Only the highest-dose group rose (to ~805 ng/dL), and only that group consistently increased thigh muscle area and leg press strength — **gains in the range of 2–4%**. Daily gel, so no peak-level confound.

[14:46] Natural levers exist — body fat, correcting nutritional deficiencies, sleep, avoiding chronic stress — but he says plainly you should not be under the illusion that natural increases have a large impact on baseline muscle and strength. Given the effect sizes above, that is the correct conclusion and it matches what the library already holds.

## Lowering testosterone
[16:18] The mirror image. In Finkelstein, the groups whose levels fell lost thigh muscle area and leg press strength. Suppressing healthy men from ~535 ng/dL to 31 ng/dL cost 2.1 kg of fat-free mass in 10 weeks with no training involved.

[17:21] **Kvorning** is the interesting one. Men with baseline testosterone of ~630–660 ng/dL trained three times a week for 8 weeks; one group suppressed to 5.7–3.1 ng/dL with goserelin. Lean mass and isometric strength gains were lower in the suppressed group. Face value: testosterone improves gains *from training*. His reading, consistent with the rest of the video: suppression lowered the **baseline**, and the missing baseline shows up as apparently smaller total gains. A group that suppressed testosterone without training would have let you do the subtraction — that group didn't exist. Notably, leg press and bench press gains were similar between groups even though isometric strength differed, again pointing at inconsistent strength effects.

## The two ways supraphysiological levels might genuinely be synergistic
[18:38] He is careful to say this section is theoretical.
1. **Work capacity and recovery.** Supraphysiological levels plausibly let you handle and benefit from more volume, which is an indirect route to better training gains. He immediately qualifies it: not everyone benefits from higher volume, possibly for reasons like ribosome biogenesis capacity.
2. **Myonuclei.** Each myonucleus governs a limited myonuclear domain; once the domain limit is reached, further fibre growth requires more nuclei. Natural training does add myonuclei, but supraphysiological testosterone likely adds many more, raising the long-term ceiling on fibre size and reducing plateaus.

He speculates that non-supraphysiological increases probably do neither meaningfully, and says future research is needed.

## Where this lands against the library
This **confirms and sharpens** the library's existing position from a single source that supraphysiological testosterone is additive rather than synergistic with training — here it is three four-group trials, not one, and the additive arithmetic is shown explicitly. It also confirms that natural levers are small: the best-controlled dose-response data puts a ~300 ng/dL rise at 2–4%.

It is **not** in tension with the acute-spike position. This video is entirely about chronic level changes over 8–16 weeks; the acute-spike question is handled in his separate video and reaches the opposite verdict there.

**Overreach flag:** the additive-vs-synergistic conclusion rests on comparing summed group means across arms rather than a formal test of interaction, and the Casaburi leg press result was actually sub-additive — the framing is cleaner than the underlying data. The myonuclei and work-capacity sections are mechanism-only with no outcome data attached, and he labels them as such.
