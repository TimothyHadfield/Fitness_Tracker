# How Light Is Too Light? Where the Load Floor for Hypertrophy Sits

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "How Light Is Too Light for Maximizing Muscle Growth?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AcO68MRcJdQ)) · 2021-02-28 · 11 min
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- Two meta-analyses put the safe band at **30–85% of 1RM** — roughly 5 to 35 reps — provided sets are taken to or very near failure. Within that band load does not decide growth.
- Below 30% the evidence **splits two against two**, and he refuses to pick a winner. Two studies say 15–20% 1RM can maximise growth; two say it cannot.
- The theoretical reason a load could be too light is **central fatigue**, not insufficient tension per rep — a hypothesis, not a demonstrated cause of the growth results.
- Practical position: keep **most** hypertrophy training between 30% and 85% 1RM. He adds, honestly, that the video has little practical payoff because almost nobody trains at 30% anyway — it is miserable.

## Why light loads work at all

His mechanism is standard and he states it as such. Mechanical tension is the best-understood driver of hypertrophy; its active component is the force the muscle produces, which rises with fibre recruitment and with the force each recruited fibre generates. Heavy loads demand both immediately. Light loads reach the same state late in a set: as fatigue accumulates, recruitment and per-fibre force climb to meet the demand, so the last few reps of a 35-rep set look, mechanically, like the reps of a 6-rep set.

He anchors this on Mitchell et al. — three sets to failure on the leg extension at **30% or 80% 1RM, three times a week for ten weeks**, with similar increases in quadriceps volume.

One caveat he raises early and which is worth carrying: **the same %1RM buys very different rep counts on different exercises**. In Mitchell's study 30% 1RM on the leg extension produced roughly 30–40 reps to failure; another study found 30% 1RM on the dumbbell curl produced roughly **60–80**. "Percentage of 1RM" is a much looser proxy for effort than it looks.

## The problem the mechanism creates

If any load taken to failure eventually maximises recruitment and per-fibre force, then a thousand reps to failure should equal ten. That is obviously wrong, so something must break at the bottom.

His candidate is **central fatigue** — a decline in the electrical drive the nervous system sends to the muscle, which reduces both recruitment and the force recruited fibres produce. The counter-intuitive part is the direction: low-load, long-duration sets generate *more* central fatigue than heavy short ones. Yoon et al. had **nine men and nine women** hold a biceps contraction at 20% or 80% of maximum voluntary contraction until force fell 10% below target. The 20% hold lasted around **14 minutes**, the 80% hold around **25 seconds** — and the 20% condition produced the greater central fatigue.

Since 30% 1RM demonstrably works, whatever central fatigue it generates is not a problem. The hypothesis is that somewhere below 30% it becomes one.

## What the sub-30% studies actually found

He discards the studies that did not train to failure as uninformative, which leaves four.

**Jessee et al.** — untrained men and women, four sets on the knee extension to volitional failure at **15% or 70% 1RM**, twice a week for eight weeks. Sets were capped at 90 reps; only 2 of 40 subjects hit the cap on all four sets, so the cap probably did not distort things. Anterior and lateral thigh thickness increased similarly. Verdict: 15% may be enough.

**Buckner et al.** — a near-identical design on the **dumbbell biceps curl**, same loads, same duration, same 90-rep cap. Subjects at 15% averaged the cap on set one but reached volitional failure on sets two to four. Elbow flexor thickness increased **more** in the 70% condition. Verdict: 15% is too light.

He flags the confound both studies share: the light condition used **30 seconds of rest** between sets and the heavy condition **90 seconds**. Since it apparently did not matter in Jessee, he cannot say whether it mattered in Buckner. He also floats and then discards the obvious reconciliation — that 15% works for the lower body but not the upper — because:

**Lasevicius et al.** — untrained men, dumbbell curl and leg press, three to five sets to failure each, twice a week for **12 weeks**, at 20/40/60/80% 1RM with 120 s rest. Elbow flexor and vastus lateralis CSA increases were similar at 40, 60 and 80%, and **all greater than 20%** — in both upper and lower body.

**Van Roie et al.** — older untrained men and women, leg press and leg extension, twice a week for 12 weeks: one set to failure at 20% 1RM against two sets to failure at 80%. Upper leg volume increased similarly. The extra heavy set bought nothing, and 20% held its own — but in older subjects.

## Where this leaves you

Two studies for, two against, with an age confound on one and a rest-interval confound on two. He does not resolve it and does not pretend to; the recommendation is simply to keep most training inside 30–85% 1RM.

**Overreach flag:** the central-fatigue argument is carried entirely by a single-joint isometric study in 18 subjects, and is never linked to any of the four growth trials — it explains why a floor *should* exist, not why Buckner and Lasevicius found one where Jessee and Van Roie did not.

Worth noting on the other side: he counts the studies rather than the conclusions, states sample sizes and training status throughout, and ends with "the current evidence does not give us a straight answer" rather than manufacturing one.
