# Do Rows and Pulldowns Build Biceps? The Regional Answer

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "“Compound Exercises Are Enough for the Biceps” (New Study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=botlghxtVtg)) · 2025-09-22 · 13 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- A 2025 two-experiment paper found dumbbell rows grew the **lower (25%) region of the elbow flexors as much as dumbbell curls**, while curls won clearly at the **upper (75%) region**. Back work is not a null stimulus for the arms.
- Curls themselves grew the elbow flexors **non-uniformly** — the 75% region grew more than the 25% region — which matters for anyone assuming a curl trains "the biceps" evenly.
- His verdict: back movements grow the elbow flexors effectively and may match curls at some regions, but **isolation is still likely needed to maximise overall elbow flexor size**, and curls are the practical way to add arm volume without also loading the back.
- The pump is a **hit-or-miss proxy for growth**. In this study it predicted correctly at one region and wrongly at another; across three swelling studies it aligns with growth outcomes roughly half the time.
- Untrained subjects grew their pecs from **8.6% acute swelling to 14.2% real growth in 8 weeks** — i.e. a beginner can genuinely out-grow an early pump inside two months.

## The new study: two experiments, two questions
Experiment one recruited **16 trained men** in a within-subject design: one arm trained a dumbbell row, the other a dumbbell curl, **4 sets of 8–12 to failure**. Unusually, the rows were done with a **supinated grip** rather than the neutral grip most people use, deliberately matching the curl's grip. Elbow flexor thickness (biceps plus brachialis, not separated) was measured immediately afterwards at **25% and 75% of upper arm length** — an acute swelling measure. Curls produced more swelling at both sites, but the difference was **statistically significant only at 25%**.

Experiment two took **9 previously untrained men** through the same one-arm-each design, **twice weekly for 8 weeks**, **4–6 sets of 8–12 to failure**, loads adjusted to hold the rep range. **Growth at 75% was greater with curls. Growth at 25% was similar between the two.** He is careful about why untrained subjects were used: the authors argued trained subjects were needed in experiment one for execution quality, and untrained subjects in experiment two to avoid ceiling effects and prior adaptation. He accepts the reasoning but says he still wants the trained-subject version.

He also does something most creators do not: he noticed the same team's **2019 study** used an identical design and found curls beat rows for *total* elbow flexor growth, **contacted the authors, and confirmed some participants overlap between the two papers**. The new paper is the first to report the regional split from that dataset.

## Lat pulldowns versus barbell curls
The only other relevant trial is from **2015**: **29 untrained men**, lat pulldown (**pronated, wide grip**) or barbell curl (shoulder-width, supinated), **3 sets of 8–12 to failure, twice weekly for 10 weeks**. Elbow flexor thickness at a single site showed **no significant difference** between the two. He says outright that he would not have predicted a wide pronated pulldown to match barbell curls, and offers a caveat rather than a story: only one region was measured, and a greater share of the pulldown growth may have come from **brachialis** rather than biceps.

## What this changes for exercise selection
The library position that rows and pulldowns are poor biceps builders **does not survive this evidence intact**. What survives is the weaker and better-supported claim: compounds cannot *maximise* elbow flexor size, because they miss the upper region and cap the volume you can give the arms without also fatiguing the back. He does not argue that curls are optional — his conclusion is that isolation is likely needed for maximum growth — but he explicitly refuses to say back work does nothing for the arms.

## The pump as a proxy
Curls produced **16–19% acute swelling** in experiment one; 8 weeks of training produced **5–11% real growth** in experiment two (different subjects). Against that, a bench press study found **8.6% acute pec swelling** and **14.2% growth after 8 weeks of twice-weekly benching** — so an untrained lifter can surpass an early pump in about two months.

On whether swelling predicts growth, he lines up three comparisons and lets them disagree. The new study: swelling favoured curls at both regions, but growth only followed at 75% — a miss. A bench press paper: flat pressing produced more mid-pec swelling than incline, which matches the growth literature, but upper-pec swelling was similar between the two, which does not — a partial miss. A calf raise paper: straight-leg raises swelled the gastrocnemii more, bent-leg raises swelled the soleus more, both matching the growth data — a hit. His conclusion is that swelling is **sometimes useful and sometimes not**, so he will not endorse it as a valid proxy. He adds the obvious practical point that gym-floor "pump" is a perception, not an ultrasound measurement.

## Where the conclusion outruns the evidence
The headline regional finding rests on **nine untrained subjects**; a non-significant difference in a sample that size is weak evidence of equivalence, not evidence of it. He flags the sample size, the untrained status, the unseparated biceps/brachialis measure, and the grip-specificity of the comparison himself — this is a case where his caveats are as substantial as his claim.
