# Nutrition, and how to read a study

Fourteen notes, split very unevenly. Ten are about method — how to read a meta-analysis, what a null
result does and does not license, why studies on the same question disagree, and which measurements
are only standing in for muscle growth. Four are about nutrition. The methodological half is the most
transferable material on the whole channel and gets most of the space here; the nutrition half is
thin enough that **this should not be your nutrition source**. For protein, creatine, supplements and
diet composition go to the ISSN position stands and the Jeff Nippard material in this library. Come
here for protein *reasoning*, and for the habit of mind the rest of the folder is worth reading with.

**The creator is self-taught and has no formal credentials**, which he states himself. The ten method
notes are the strongest evidence that it does not matter much: he states sample sizes unprompted,
presents the study that contradicts him, separates a null from a demonstrated absence, and retires
his own positions in public. Two of these fourteen notes — and 26 videos across the channel — **have
no retrievable reference list at all**; they cite on screen only, so reading them you have his
argument, not a chain back to the literature.

## Reading the evidence

### Sampling variance is the master key

**Two well-run studies on the same question will routinely disagree, and that is what small samples
do rather than a sign either is broken.**

[One set matched three](3-sets-not-better-1-set.md) is the best methods lesson in the folder. Pinto
2025: 15 untrained men, unilateral pec deck twice weekly for 12 weeks, one arm at 1 set per session
and the other at 3, all to volitional concentric failure. Both pec heads grew similarly. Most
creators would file that as a refutation of the volume literature; he argues it is **fully compatible
with three sets genuinely being better**, via a simulation credited to James Krieger. Build a
population of 3 million lifters where, by construction, high volume adds 3 mm of biceps thickness,
moderate 2 mm and low 1 mm, each with a 2 mm SD. Draw five studies of ten per group: only one
resembles the truth, and the others show a plateau, high volume numerically worse than moderate, and
moderate worse than low. Pool the five and you land back on the real effects. **A single small trial
is a data point in a future meta-analysis, not a verdict.**

The same logic runs [Defending science-based lifting](defending-science-based-lifting.md), which
gives four reasons studies conflict — unequal evidence quality (rat versus human), differing methods,
differing populations, and chance in small samples — and lands on the rule: *do not weight individual
studies heavily, and do not change your training on one*. It also gives the right reading of the
[pre-sleep protein trials](protein-before-bed-really-work.md), where growth measures lean one way and
strength measures the other. That is noise wearing the clothes of a finding.

### A null result is not a demonstrated absence

**"No significant difference" in a small study means the study could not detect a difference, not
that none exists.** He applies this in both directions, which is rarer than it sounds. In
[pre-sleep protein](protein-before-bed-really-work.md) three protein-matched trials at n = 26, 13 and
42 found nothing, and he says on camera that "no discernible difference" outruns them: absence of
evidence, not evidence of absence. In the
[2024 round-up](most-important-muscle-building-studies-2024.md) he reads a null the other way and
flags it as such — 42 trained individuals, one set per exercise, failure versus 2 RIR, differences
not statistically conclusive but raw numbers leaning toward failure on most measures. Verdict: "not
case closed", with the hypothesis that failure may buy something at low set counts and little at high
ones. In [the Ethier taxonomy](need-talk-about-jeremy-ethier-new-study.md) he notes the
small-sample objection applies *most* to the category his own conclusion depends on. Applying an
objection where it costs you is the tell that it is a principle.

### Individual response variation is mostly noise until proven otherwise

He accepts that individual differences probably exist, then explains why almost no published study
can show you one ([note](3-sets-not-better-1-set.md)). In a within-limb design, apparent individual
variation can come from a limb that started smaller with more room to grow, something outside the
study impairing one side, measurement error landing unevenly, or a genuine minority who respond
alike. **These error sources get worse, not better, when you zoom in on one person.** The designs
needed to demonstrate true individual differences — repeated within-person replication — **have not
been run in the muscle-growth literature**. His own view is labelled a guess: differences probably
exist via physiology and lifestyle (a stressful period may blunt your response to high volume), so
use the average literature effects as a starting point and adjust. Lots of sets and under-recovered?
Drop some. Stalled for months while recovering well? Add some. **Treat a claimed individual response
as noise until someone has replicated it within the same person.**

### What actually confounds a training study

**1. The comparison arm was set at the wrong place.** The whole pre-sleep protein literature
collapses on this: the 2021 Reis review everyone cites **did not control total daily protein**, and
its key trial gave the pre-sleep group 27.5 g against a non-caloric placebo, leaving them at **1.9
versus 1.3 g/kg/day** ([note](protein-before-bed-really-work.md)). Nothing there separates "protein
before bed" from "more protein". Same fault under the cutting-protein advice: Mettler 2010 and
Longland 2016 both used **40% deficits** against **1.0–1.2 g/kg** control arms
([note](protein-myth-much-really-need.md)). **Always ask what the losing arm was doing.**

**2. Unequal training dose hidden inside the variable.** The 20–25 g per-meal ceiling came from Areta
2013 (24 trained men, 80 g whey over 12 hours after *leg extensions only*) and Witard. The design
guaranteed the answer: **the training stimulus was one or two exercises for one muscle, and the total
dose was unrealistically low**, with MPS measured only to 5 hours — too short to see a large dose
working for longer. The prediction is then confirmed: Macnaughton 2016 trained the whole body and
40 g beat 20 g ([note](protein-limit-meal-they-were-wrong.md)).

**3. The programme was not a programme.** In
[The case against "trained lifters stop growing"](cannot-gain-much-more-muscle-16-studies.md) he
dismantles Counts 2017 by naming four design limits in the studies behind it: **no calorie or protein
control, one exercise per muscle, no rep-range variation, set numbers held constant.** His
counter-evidence is picked the same way — trained men with documented lifts (Brigatto 2019, bench
94 kg / squat 125 kg; Schoenfeld 2015, bench ~101 kg; Rauch 2020, squat 140 kg) adding **5–11% muscle
thickness** over 6–9 weeks, several without a surplus. Stating the subjects' actual lifts every time
is how "trained" gets to mean something; he flags unreported nutrition in Kreipke 2015 and Colquhoun
2018 as he goes.

**4. Untrained subjects.** The early lengthened-partials case came from untrained people on a narrow
set of exercises ([2024 round-up](most-important-muscle-building-studies-2024.md)); the 2022
stretch-partial results were in **untrained women**, twice, which he says makes two studies and not a
body of evidence ([2022 round-up](best-muscle-growth-strength-research-2022.md)); the Pinto pec-deck
study was untrained, and he notes there are still numerous cases of untrained people growing more
from multiple sets.

**5. Unpublished sources presented as findings.** In the
[2022 round-up](best-muscle-growth-strength-research-2022.md) he states plainly that the Silva
inter-set stretching result is a **conference abstract, never published or peer-reviewed** — which
costs him the neatest result in the section and exposes how mixed the rest is (Van Every 2022: soleus
but not gastrocnemius, and slight; Wadhi and a flywheel-squat paper: nothing; Evangelista 2019: a
benefit in untrained subjects). His explanation — the stretch may only work taken **immediately after
the eccentric** — is a hypothesis about why studies split, not a finding.
### Proxies: four things that stand in for growth, and why each fails

**EMG.** [Four reasons EMG cannot rank exercises](ryan-humiston-has-made-mistake-emg.md) is his
hardest methodological line and the most portable single note here. (a) **Wrong quantity** — it
measures neuromuscular excitation, the electrical signal arriving at the muscle, not activation,
which happens several steps downstream. (b) **Unreliable even at that**: a 2017 study electrically
stimulated the tibialis anterior at *constant* intensity across three foot angles and got different
EMG amplitudes, so joint angle alone moves the reading; recruitment order compounds it across an
estimated 172,000–418,000 fibres in the biceps. (c) **It rises with load** even when both conditions
go to failure — and heavy versus light to failure grows muscle about equally, so an exercise can read
higher purely for being heavier (his case: dips out-reading close-grip push-ups for triceps).
(d) **Blind to passive tension**, which appears to contribute to growth, so it systematically
under-reads exercises loading a muscle at long lengths — exactly where the interesting comparisons
are. Verdict: EMG exercise rankings have "a high probability of just being plain wrong or at least
misleading." **Where it is still usable:** exercises with similar relative loading and joint angles
(weighted push-ups versus bench at matched ROM), before-and-after within one person, and the same
exercise across individuals. Note the reversal — by his 2024 chest guide he argues EMG *is*
informative for subtle comparisons such as grip widths and bench angles, while staying unreliable
across exercises differing in muscle length.

**Muscle protein synthesis.** He stops the 100 g inference at the right place: **this is still MPS,
not hypertrophy** ([note](protein-limit-meal-they-were-wrong.md)). He cites a review documenting
established cases where MPS fails to track growth, and lists why acute MPS is a poor model of eating
anyway — measurements are all post-training, people eat protein before training too, people eat on
rest days, and protein arrives with other food that changes digestion.

**Strength.** Two lessons. From the [2024 round-up](most-important-muscle-building-studies-2024.md):
more sets keep adding muscle in the largest analysis to date while strength gains max out much
earlier, and the popular inference — that the extra growth must be swelling — is rejected, because
there is not enough evidence swelling confounds these results and post-training swelling **decreases
notably** as a programme continues. Crediting Greg Nuckols, the better explanation is that **strength
is a bad instrument for inferring hypertrophy**: an energy deficit blunts lean mass gains without
necessarily blunting strength. From [Three myths](popular-muscle-strength-building-myths.md), the
causal arrow runs opposite to how people state it. The pro-myth evidence is correlational (Ye 2013,
Brechue 2002, Keogh 2009, Reya 2021), and his falsification test is clean: **if strength caused size,
practising your 1RM several times a week would be an excellent hypertrophy programme** — and Dankel
2017, Mattocks 2017 and Buckner 2021 show it produces large strength gains and virtually no growth.
The same note demotes progressive overload from cause to *cycle*: Maeo 2022 had subjects overload
pushdowns more than overhead extensions and get clearly less triceps growth. **Biomechanics that let
you move more load are not biomechanics that deliver more stimulus.**

**Mechanism.** The sharpest paragraph in
[Defending science-based lifting](defending-science-based-lifting.md). Arguing that mechanistic data
outranks outcome data assumes we already know the mechanisms; the Roberts review he quotes says
future work "will continue to confirm or refute which of the discussed mechanisms are obligatory for
rather than coinciding with" hypertrophy. He allows the escape hatch — maybe outcome data misses a
real effect for reason X — but insists those are **testable hypotheses**, and acting certain before
testing is disingenuous. Worked examples: the stability claim (machines are generally *more* stable
than free weights, and meta-analytic evidence finds similar hypertrophy, including in trained
lifters) and isometrics (implied inferior on no cited evidence, when most evidence finds no worse
hypertrophy). He is not immune: in the
[2022 round-up](best-muscle-growth-strength-research-2022.md) he moves from size-strength
correlations to a mechanism (more force-generating units in parallel), which is the move he warns
about.

### What the growth measurement is worth

**Lean mass is not muscle** — it includes water, connective tissue and organ mass, which is why five
creatine lean-mass meta-analyses read so much better than the one pooling direct ultrasound and MRI
measurement ([creatine note](science-behind-creatine-much-more-muscle-strength.md)). **Estimated area
is not measured area** — the near-300-subject range-of-motion trial estimated arm and thigh muscle
area from *circumferences and skinfolds*, a limitation he lists unprompted
([2024 round-up](most-important-muscle-building-studies-2024.md)). And **imaging can be badly
wrong**: [the Ethier note](need-talk-about-jeremy-ethier-new-study.md) surfaces individuals appearing
to gain upwards of 100% muscle size, says he cannot locate the measurement error, and moves on — the
one criticism he under-weights. The fuller treatment of swelling and of ultrasound versus MRI as
instruments lives in the volume and mechanisms notes outside this domain.

### Effect sizes, thresholds, and meta-analysis

**An effect size is a number divided by a yardstick, and the yardstick is a choice.** The
[creatine note](science-behind-creatine-much-more-muscle-strength.md) is the case study both ways. A
10-study meta-analysis measuring directly found creatine added **0.10–0.16 cm of thickness** across
triceps, elbow flexors, quads and hamstrings — **effect size 0.11**, which its own authors called
trivial. His objection is legitimate and worth carrying: **those thresholds came from strength and
power research, and importing them into hypertrophy data is not sensible.** His replacement is
weaker — thresholds from a Stronger by Science blog post, under which 0.11 reads small-to-medium,
then a ratio against a literature average producing "about one-third faster muscle gain". The
measurement is still 0.10–0.16 cm.

He catches the same class of error in someone else and volunteers it. In
[Defending science-based lifting](defending-science-based-lifting.md) he describes finding a **d_z
effect-size error** in a 2022 protein meta-analysis — dividing by the pooled change-score SD rather
than the pooled pre-intervention SD, the wrong denominator for judging magnitude — contacting the
authors, and James Steele now redoing it. He also hands over the finding that **85% of the 20 most
highly cited strength and conditioning meta-analyses contained at least one statistical error**, then
supplies the mitigations rather than banking the win: severity varies, all the analysed papers
predate 2019 (one is from 2004), most were not hypertrophy-focused. **Meta-analysis corrects sampling
variance; it does not guarantee correctness.**

### Ask whether the studies are answering the same question

[The Ethier taxonomy](need-talk-about-jeremy-ethier-new-study.md) is his own framework, but the move
generalises. The muscle-length literature holds three non-interchangeable questions — **resistance
challenge** (same body position, same total ROM, peak difficulty in a different place), **range of
motion** (same exercise, different range), and **exercise selection with biarticular muscles** — plus
isometrics as a fourth. Most of the online argument comes from pooling them. The new study (20
untrained subjects, 10 weeks, machines set lengthened-bias on one side and shortened-bias on the
other, **same total ROM both sides**, tempo standardised, 8–12 reps to momentary failure) found no
significant differences anywhere, and sits in the resistance-challenge category where all three
existing studies are null. So it **replicates** its category rather than refuting a different one.
The evidence for long lengths lives elsewhere: a meta-analysis favouring full ROM or lengthened
partials over *shortened* partials, the gap widening proximal to distal and reaching about **4.76
percentage points** distally; and the biarticular results (seated leg curl, leaning-back leg
extension, standing calf raise, overhead triceps extension, shoulder-extended curls). The
credibility test there is worth stealing: **the uniarticular control muscles inside the same studies
generally grew equally**, which is what you would predict if muscle length were doing the work.

Then he argues against his own scheme, which is the best part. Pedrosa's leg extension study is filed
under range of motion — but leg extensions are hardest at the top, so the full-ROM group's peak
difficulty fell at a short muscle length and the partial group's at a long one. **Both variables
differed, so it cannot tell you which produced the result.** Incline versus preacher curls has the
same problem in reverse. Coverage is lopsided too: much ROM research is quads, yet there is no quad
study in the resistance-challenge category, while two of its three studies used side delts and no ROM
study has examined side delts at all.

### Expectation is a confounder with a measured size

Two notes cover the placebo-steroid literature —
[The placebo steroid studies](holds-back-muscle-strength-potential.md) and
[Turkesterone works](turkesterone-works-but-not-reasons-think.md) — and the second uses it better.
Ariel and Saville 1973: 15 varsity athletes trained seven honest weeks, the six best gainers were
promised free steroids and given a daily 10 mg placebo "Dianabol"; gains went from +4.35 kg bench and
+2.65 kg squat over seven weeks to +13.28 kg bench and roughly +18.9 kg squat over four. Maganaris
2000: 11 nationally ranked powerlifters (189 kg bench, 257 kg squat, 260 kg deadlift) took saccharin
described as immediate-acting steroids five minutes before testing, and every one hit a PR, averaging
**+9.5 / +12.2 / +10.9 kg** — enough, the authors said, to move each from nationally to
internationally ranked. Told before the second session that the pills were fake, those subjects
dropped 7.5 kg on bench, 11 kg on squat and 12.5 kg on deadlift, back to baseline; those left unaware
held their gains.

The transferable use is the turkesterone argument: **an anecdotal track record is not evidence,
because expectation alone is a sufficient explanation for it.** He is careful — he does not claim
turkesterone is all placebo, notes there is **not a single long-term human study** on turkesterone
and hypertrophy or strength (the human work usually cited is on ecdysterone, a structurally similar
compound), credits Greg Nuckols as the only reason he knows the two studies, and guards the general
point with Bhasin 1996, where 600 mg/week testosterone with **no lifting at all** beat natural
lifting on quad area, triceps area and fat-free mass. The caveat he does not give: n = 6 and n = 11,
unblinded by design, 25 and 50 years old, and Ariel and Saville has **no comparison group** at all.

## Nutrition

### How much protein per day

Three meta-analyses in six years put the plateau at roughly **1.5–1.6 g/kg total bodyweight/day**:
Morton 2018 at 1.6, Tagawa 2020 at about 1.5, and Nunes 2022, which modelled the data differently and
only *looks* like more-is-always-better because its **confidence intervals widen as intake rises** —
uncertainty, not benefit. Nitrogen-balance and IAAO work lands in the same 1.5–2.0 window.

The valuable part of [the protein note](protein-myth-much-really-need.md) is the scatter behind the
number. Crediting Eric Trexler, he strips the fitted line off the 2018 plot: restrict to intakes at or
above 1.25 g/kg and **r-squared is 0.011** — protein explains **one percent of the variance** in
fat-free mass change. The line is real; the precision people read into "1.6" is not. So he gives a
spectrum: **1.2–1.59 g/kg possibly enough, 1.6–2.0 almost certainly enough, above 2.0 very likely
more than enough** — compatible with maximal gains, not required for them. Where you sit is a
preference about risk, not a fact about physiology. Two modifiers push higher: lower dietary protein
quality, and older age.

**Training harder or being more advanced does not reliably raise the requirement.** Lemon 1992 had
novice bodybuilders train 1.5 hours a day, six days a week, with similar gains at **1.35 and
2.62 g/kg**; Bandegan 2017 estimated 1.7 g/kg by IAAO in eight bodybuilders near their maximum
muscular potential; Tarnopolsky 1988, also elite bodybuilders, estimated 1.2; Roberts 2017 found
recovery similar at 1.8 and 2.9. **Bulking needs no elevation** — though he defers the Antonio
overfeeding studies to a future video, leaving that section an assertion rather than an argument.
**Cutting is the one place he allows an increase**, and he dismantles his own case in the same breath:
the supporting trials used 40% deficits against 1.0–1.2 g/kg control arms, and nobody has run 1.6
against something higher at a moderate deficit. His conclusion is explicitly hypothetical — *the
leaner you are and the harder you are dieting, the more reason to sit high, as insurance.*

Two footnotes. All these figures are per kilogram of **total** bodyweight, which overestimates needs
at high body fat — use fat-free mass, or estimate what you would weigh lean. And on kidneys, citing
the ISSN protein position stand: the concern traces to renal-failure patients and to teaching never
revised against contrary evidence; in otherwise healthy people there is no indication of harm,
including in trials well above 2.2 g/kg. The framing to keep: in the 2018 meta-analysis, **training
accounted for a substantially greater share of the variance in gains than protein intake did.** You
cannot eat your way to hypertrophy; protein lets you realise the gains training has earned.

### How to distribute it

There is **no meaningful per-meal protein ceiling** ([note](protein-limit-meal-they-were-wrong.md)).
Trommelen had active men train the full body then take 0 g, 25 g or 100 g of milk protein: MPS was
**greater and longer-lasting after 100 g**, and **relative oxidation rates were small** — direct
evidence against the claim that anything over 25 g is burned off. He flags the limitation himself:
milk protein is roughly 20% whey and 80% casein, so digestion is slow and a faster protein might
differ. Note he never argues from *absorption*, the usual mistake, and goes straight to oxidation.

The long-term data are modest and he says so — "not plentiful or super strong". Three studies: a 2020
12-week trial in untrained subjects favouring **three servings over two**, but not by much (both
groups ate only 1.3–1.45 g/kg/day); 10 male rowers bulking over 8 weeks, three versus six servings
comparable on everything including fat-free mass; 24 elite rugby players over 6 weeks, similar lean
mass whether shakes went with meals or between them. **Three servings slightly beat two, nothing
beyond three, daily total is what matters, and training matters more than either.**

### Protein before bed

Do not bother optimising for it ([note](protein-before-bed-really-work.md)). The confound above is
the whole story; three protein-matched trials found no significant differences; and casein is not
special — whey peaks higher and casein lasts longer, but **area under the amino acid curve is
similar**, and myofibrillar and mitochondrial synthesis rates did not differ head-to-head. Blood
amino acid levels do not reliably track synthesis anyway. A shake near bedtime did not disrupt sleep
in the evidence he found, with the caveat that solid food with real carbs and fat is different.

### Creatine

**Monohydrate, 3–5 g/day, whenever you like, no loading needed, no cycling needed**
([note](science-behind-creatine-much-more-muscle-strength.md)). Loading (20–25 g/day in 4–5 doses for
5–7 days) saturates faster; skipping it saturates in roughly 28 days at 3 g or 14 days at 5 g, costs
less and is less likely to cause the mild GI discomfort some get. Outcomes are similar at 3 versus
5 g long term, 2 g maintains saturation after loading, and larger individuals scale to about
0.04 g/kg/day. Timing is not make-or-break: the post-training meta-analytic edge rests on **three
studies** with no strength difference, and a later paper found nothing either way. Monohydrate is the
most studied form; alternatives cost more, none is proven superior, some do not consistently beat
placebo.

**Effect size, honestly:** five lean-mass meta-analyses give **1–1.4 kg** over 7–14 weeks, but lean
mass is not muscle; direct measurement gives 0.10–0.16 cm at effect size 0.11, and the "one-third
faster" reframe is the weakest claim in the note. Strength effects are small-to-moderate for bench
and squat, with a classic review giving **+8 percentage points strength and +14 percentage points
muscular endurance**. He shows the *spread* rather than only the average, which is the good habit:
one 12-week study had creatine add 22.6 kg to bench 1RM against 15 kg for control, another 8-week
study 6.8 versus 5.7 kg.

**Responders:** roughly **20–30% non-responders**, who start with higher intramuscular creatine,
smaller fibres and lower fat-free mass — which fits the saturation logic (stores average about
120 mmol/kg, ceiling near 160). Vegetarians and vegans look like high responders, though that rests
on one trial (18 vegetarians, 24 non-vegetarians, 8 weeks, strength gains similar) and he says so. On
age, the direct-measurement meta-analysis gave 0.23 in younger adults against 0.06 in older, and he
**declines** to conclude older adults benefit little, noting they build less muscle overall so the
denominator changes.

**Hair loss:** the concern traces to one 2009 study in 20 rugby players. Never replicated, the DHT
rise stayed within normal range, there is a case it was a false positive, and they measured **serum**
DHT, which is not strongly related to hair loss, where **scalp** DHT is. His wording is careful —
"there is zero scientific evidence associating creatine with actual hair loss" — and he refuses the
stronger claim that it definitely does not cause it. **Safety:** no need to cycle; the kidney concern
comes from misreading creatinine metabolism plus a flawed 1998 case study.

Two honest touches worth copying. He is sceptical of the *mechanism* usually given — that extra work
capacity drives growth — because other supplements raise work capacity without producing long-term
hypertrophy; **refusing a tidy mechanism the outcome data does not require** is the same instinct
running the EMG and MPS sections. And he concedes creatine is an osmolyte raising intracellular
water, so some of the size gain is fluid, in unknown proportion to contractile protein.

## Weak points

**Four notes cannot carry a nutrition question.** Nothing on calories or energy balance beyond a
passing 500 kcal recomposition threshold, nothing on carbohydrate or fat, nutrient timing around
training, hydration or micronutrients, and no supplement coverage beyond creatine. Use the ISSN
position stands and the Nippard material for that; use this source for the reasoning.

**The creatine headline is the clearest case of a conclusion outrunning its measurement.** The
objection to strength-and-power effect-size thresholds is right; replacing them with blog-post
thresholds and multiplying out to "one-third faster growth" is a relabelling, and unlike most of his
overreaches he does not flag this one.

**The placebo-steroid material is used harder than n = 6 and n = 11 can bear.** Decades old, neither
measured hypertrophy, one has no comparison group, both unblinded by construction. Fine as an
argument that anecdote is confounded by expectation
([turkesterone](turkesterone-works-but-not-reasons-think.md)); not fine as the mindset conclusion
closing [You cannot gain much more muscle](cannot-gain-much-more-muscle-16-studies.md), where an
acute strength result argues about long-term growth. That note has a second soft spot: percentage
thickness gains over 8 weeks in trained-but-not-elite subjects say nothing about the far end of the
curve, which is what its title invites. It contributes **no evidence about where a natural ceiling
sits**.

**Two of these fourteen notes have no reference list.**
[The Ethier taxonomy](need-talk-about-jeremy-ethier-new-study.md) and
[The placebo steroid studies](holds-back-muscle-strength-potential.md) cite on screen only, so every
study named in them is recorded as unverified — including the taxonomy's load-bearing meta-analysis
and its Pedrosa, Nunes and Lawson references. A third,
[Defending science-based lifting](defending-science-based-lifting.md), gives surnames and bare links
with no full citations. Across the channel the figure is 26 videos, disproportionately his longest and
most confident work.

**The "research has delivered" list is drawn from settled, comfortable findings** — creatine works,
BCAAs are not essential, protein timing is minor, short rest is not required — which never tests the
harder claim that research resolves the contested variables people argue about. In the same note he
rates recent meta-analyses "done to what I think is an exceptional standard" without showing his
work: a quality judgement offered without the evidentiary support he demands of others two sections
earlier.

**The taxonomy is his own framework**, and its load-bearing claim — that the new study belongs to a
category holding only two other studies, both null — rests on a very thin base. He acknowledges it;
the "no conflict" conclusion still outruns three small studies.

**His EMG position moved and both versions live in this folder.** The 2022 note is the hard line; by
2024 he builds exercise rankings on activation data. He flags the change himself. Use the earlier
reasoning and note the softening rather than silently picking one.

**The round-ups age differently.** The
[2022 round-up](best-muscle-growth-strength-research-2022.md) headlines stretch-position partials on
two untrained-women studies; the
[2024 round-up](most-important-muscle-building-studies-2024.md) retires that superiority claim on two
trained-subject trials, one with just under 300 subjects and one — he discloses — **funded by Jeff
Nippard**. Read the later one as current, and the earlier one as a record of how fast a well-hedged
position can be overturned.
