# How Myofibrils Actually Grow: Length, Thickness and Splitting

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Stronger and Bigger Muscles (Myofibrillar Packing/Hypertrophy)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3wSteknqWSM)) · 2021-08-16 · 19 min
**Topic:** Mechanisms

## Bottom line
- Myofibrils can grow three ways: **longer** (more sarcomeres in series), **thicker** (more sarcomeres in parallel), or **more numerous** (existing myofibrils splitting). Both myofibrillar hypertrophy and myofibrillar packing depend on some combination of these.
- **Length is the one you can train for.** Animal work overwhelmingly shows added sarcomeres in series, not stretched existing ones — and nearly all of those studies involved the muscle held at long length or contracting eccentrically.
- In humans the proxy is **fascicle length**, and it responds to full or long-length-biased range of motion, and responds even more strongly to **eccentric-overload training**.
- Two studies suggest the fascicle-length window may **close after roughly 5–8 weeks** in untrained people, with no further increase after that. Both were untrained-only; nobody has tested trained lifters new to eccentric overload.
- For myofibril **thickness and number** there is essentially nothing in humans. The entire case rests on two collections of animal work — chickens and mice — and offers no training guidance beyond an indirect inference from the previous video.

## The measurement problem that shapes the whole video
He is unusually blunt about this: **there appear to be no human studies examining changes at the myofibril level.** Assessing ultrastructure inside living human subjects is methodologically and practically out of reach, so everything below the fibre comes from animals. He states this twice and asks you to carry it through the video. That is the correct framing, and it means most of what follows is mechanism, not outcome data.

## Increasing myofibril length
**The chain of inference.** Resistance training increases fascicle length in humans. Fascicles run the length of the muscle, so longer fascicles mean a longer muscle. But muscles are anchored to bone at fixed points, so a longer muscle cannot get visibly longer — it bulges, which registers as increased cross-sectional area. Fascicles contain fibres, so longer fascicles most likely mean longer fibres; longer fibres most likely mean longer myofibrils, with the sarcoplasm presumably extending lengthwise alongside them. If both extend at the same rate, this is technically myofibrillar hypertrophy.

Note the number of "presumably"s in that chain. He marks them; they are load-bearing.

**How length increases.** In theory, either existing sarcomeres get longer or sarcomeres are added in series. Animal work overwhelmingly supports **added sarcomeres in series** — which also makes mechanical sense, since chronically over-lengthened sarcomeres would lose force capacity. Where the new sarcomeres go is unresolved: some animal studies say they are appended to the ends of myofibrils, others say they are inserted between existing sarcomeres.

**Training for it.** Nearly every animal study finding added sarcomeres in series either held the muscle in a stretched position for a set duration or used eccentric contractions. The stretching protocols are irrelevant to humans — they involved extreme sessions or days-to-weeks of continuous stretch — but the long-length and eccentric findings do transfer:

- Two range-of-motion studies (named as "McMahon", unverified) found greater vastus lateralis fascicle length from a **fuller range of motion (0–90° knee flexion) versus a short-length partial (0–50°)**, and from a **long-length partial (40–90°) versus a short-length partial (0–50°)** on knee extensions.
- A study named as "Reeves" (unverified) found that leg presses and knee extensions performed with **eccentric-overloaded reps only** produced greater vastus lateralis fascicle-length increases than the same exercises performed normally. Several other eccentric-only studies agree.

**The window.** A study named as "Baroni" (unverified) had untrained subjects perform eccentric-overloaded knee extensions for 12 weeks: vastus lateralis and rectus femoris fascicle length increased **up to week 8, then stopped**. One named as "Blazevich" (unverified) had untrained subjects do knee extensions for 10 weeks: vastus lateralis fascicle length increased **up to week 5, then stopped**. He calls this two studies and asks for more — the right call. The unanswered question he raises is a good one: could a highly trained lifter who has never done eccentric-overload work still add sarcomeres in series?

## Increasing myofibril cross-sectional area and number
Thickness most likely comes from **sarcomeres added in parallel**. Number comes either from wholly new myofibrils being manufactured or from existing ones **splitting**.

The evidence is two bodies of animal work, and he says so:

- **Ashmore and Summers** (unverified) overloaded chicken muscle by stretching the wing for one week: **+55% fibre cross-sectional area**, and in a separate experiment **+36% myofibril diameter**, with **45% of myofibrils showing signs of splitting**.
- **Goldspink** (unverified) found in mice of various ages a positive relationship between myofibril diameter and fibre cross-sectional area, *and* a positive relationship between myofibril number and fibre cross-sectional area. His proposal: a myofibril grows in cross-section via sarcomeres in parallel up to a critical size, then splits in two — supported by the observation that splitting myofibrils were roughly twice the size of non-splitting ones, splitting longitudinally via a break at the sarcomere Z-discs.

So both thickness and number appear to happen, and appear to be linked. **There is no evidence that entirely new myofibrils are manufactured and added.**

**Can you train for it?** No direct research exists. His indirect route: fibre cross-sectional area increases are commonly achieved by myofibrillar hypertrophy or packing; the majority of those studies used moderate loads, moderate frequency and **low-to-moderate volume**; therefore those variables may favour myofibril thickness and number. He immediately undercuts himself — the sarcoplasmic-hypertrophy studies also used moderate loads and moderate frequency, so only the **volume** difference survives.

**Where a conclusion outruns the evidence:** the entire second half is chicken and mouse ultrastructure plus an inference borrowed from the previous video's volume tallies. Read it as a description of what is physically happening inside a growing fibre, not as programming advice. He flags the animal-data limitation clearly and repeats the "no heavy-only or light-only research exists" gap, which is the honest close.

## Practical residue
Only the first half yields anything usable, and it converges with material already in this library from other angles:
- Train at least some work through a **full range of motion**, or bias partials to the **long-length** end rather than the short.
- **Eccentric-overload methods** appear to be the strongest available lever on fascicle length, if you have the equipment or a partner.
- Fascicle-length adaptations may be **front-loaded** in a training career; treat this as a reason to include long-length work early, not as a reason to abandon it later.
