# Ranking Side Delt Exercises on Leverage and EMG — With No Hypertrophy Data to Rank On

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "Bigger Side Delts: Ranking the Best Exercises Using Science" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kVpEp6jORqg)) · 2024-09-15 · 28 min
**Topic:** Exercise selection

## Bottom line
- **There are no studies directly comparing side delt hypertrophy between exercises.** Every ranking here is inferred from moment-arm (leverage) data and surface EMG, and he says so at the top and again in a dedicated limitations section at the end.
- **S tier: leaning/lying dumbbell lateral raises, cable lateral raises with a high setup ("hand height"), and machine lateral raises.** The single criterion doing the work is *where in the range the exercise is hardest* — all three shift peak resistance toward longer side delt lengths.
- **A tier: standard dumbbell lateral raises, and lateral raises with internal rotation.** A− tier: super-ROM (Larsen-style) raises and, surprisingly, the **upright row**. B tier: externally rotated raises and abduction-dominant overhead presses. C tier: 90°-bent-elbow raises. D tier: flexion-dominant overhead presses.
- **The impingement case against upright rows does not hold up.** He runs through five separate lines of evidence and concludes the fear is unjustified; if it hurts, don't do it, but that is a pain argument, not a damage argument.
- **The "supraspinatus does the first 15°" textbook claim is misleading** — it does not appear to activate earlier than the side delt, which is the load-bearing premise for training the delt at long lengths at all.
- He discloses that he and colleagues are **running the first trial comparing dumbbell to cable lateral raises for side delt hypertrophy** — an unusual and creditable disclosure given he is ranking those two exercises in the same video.

## The one criterion the whole ranking runs on

Two concepts carry almost every call:

**Internal moment arm (leverage).** Draw the muscle's line of action and a parallel line through the joint centre; the perpendicular distance between them is the moment arm. Bigger moment arm, more mechanical advantage and more potential joint torque. He invokes **neuromechanical matching** — evidence that the nervous system preferentially recruits the muscles with the best leverage for a given movement. The side delt's main function is shoulder abduction and it has strong leverage for it, which is why lateral raises work at all.

**External moment arm.** Same construction, but drawn from the line of the resistance. Where the external moment arm is longest is approximately where the exercise is hardest. His shortcut for free weights: **the hardest point is where the moving segment is parallel to the ground**. For cables: **the hardest point is where the cable and the moving segment form a 90° angle**.

Stack these on top of the emerging finding that training at longer muscle lengths builds more muscle overall, and the ranking writes itself: an exercise is better the closer its hardest point sits to a lengthened side delt.

## S tier, and why

**Leaning or lying dumbbell lateral raises.** Change your body position and you move the hardest point. Leaning against a wall puts the longest external moment arm below 90° of abduction. Leaning on a low-incline bench moves it earlier still. Lying on a flat bench makes the exercise hardest with the arm right by your side — the longest attainable side delt length.

**Cable lateral raises.** A higher pulley setup — cable at roughly hand height — moves the 90° cable-to-arm point into a more lengthened position.

**Machine lateral raises.** Not all machines are equal, but good ones challenge the delt hard with the arms near the sides. He notes the Prime Fitness adjustable cam as an example of a machine where the hardest point can be moved deliberately.

His honest hedge on all three: we have data showing shorter lengths are *worse* than comparatively longer lengths, but **not** data showing the longest attainable length is always best. Machines and cables also can't take the arm across the body the way a dumbbell can.

## A tier and the rotation question

**Standard dumbbell lateral raise: A tier, not S.** It works — the side delt has excellent leverage for abduction — but the hardest point is at 90°, where the muscle is moderately shortened. That single fact is the whole demotion.

**Internal rotation ("emptying a can"): A tier, level with standard.** The EMG paper comparing neutral, internally rotated and externally rotated raises found neutral and internal similar for side delt activity, with external slightly worse. Internal rotation raised rear delt activity (plausible — it may improve rear delt abduction leverage) and upper trap activity (the authors attribute this to greater scapular elevation). A second study comparing only internal to external rotation in the scapular plane found side delt activity **much** better with internal rotation; the magnitudes disagree but the direction is consistent.

**External rotation ("full can"): B tier.** Externally rotating appears to increase the *front* delt's abduction leverage, so it shifts work forward.

**Bent elbows to 90°: C tier, flagged as low confidence.** The same three-variation paper included this and found much lower side delt activity with better front, rear and upper trap activity. He says outright that he is surprised by this, that it is specific to 90° of elbow flexion, and that a slight bend likely costs nothing. This is a case where he ranks against his own intuition on one study and labels it as needing more data.

**Super-ROM / "Larsen" raises (going past 90°): A−.** Three problems: the extra range is at short muscle lengths, where the added growth stimulus is doubtful; most people have to externally rotate around 50–80° of abduction to clear the head; and past 90° of abduction the front delt matches or exceeds the side delt in both leverage and activity. He does not think the loss is large — you still get neutrally rotated abduction in the first half — hence a made-up A− tier rather than a demotion to B or C. He states plainly that the A− tier exists because "externally rotated raises are C tier" would overstate a small difference. That is a subjective call and he says so.

## Two things he refuses to endorse

**Scapular plane over frontal plane.** The relative leverages between muscles are not that different between the two planes; if anything the scapular plane may bring in more front delt. One study found side delt activity was not meaningfully different, with numbers slightly favouring the frontal plane and the authors themselves calling the difference possibly not meaningful. Verdict: preference.

**Seated or braced raises for "stability."** Yes, there is research showing greater stability enhances fibre recruitment and tension — but he argues that research doesn't generalise. The study demonstrating it strapped down the legs and torso on a leg extension, which is nothing like sitting on a bench. More relevant comparisons go the other way: back squats are less stable than hack squats and growth appears similar; a study comparing seated to standing overhead press found deltoid activity no better seated. If you prefer the stability for technique reasons, fine.

## Compound movements

**Abduction-dominant overhead presses (wide barbell grip, or dumbbells moving out): B tier.** The start position is only ~30–40° of abduction, so no long length; the movement involves external rotation (put your hands in press position and straighten the elbow — thumbs and biceps point up); and it is hardest at and above 90° of abduction, where the front delt catches up. One study did find abduction-dominant overhead presses elicited side delt activity **similar to dumbbell lateral raises**, which is a striking result — but another failed to replicate it, and weighed against the leverage data he isn't convinced.

He pre-empts an objection worth recording: does the extra triceps involvement dilute the delt stimulus? He argues no, citing cases where compound movements match isolation work — squats matching hip thrusts for gluteus maximus growth in one study despite also loading quads and adductor magnus; leg extensions not necessarily beating squats or leg presses for the vastus muscles.

**Flexion-dominant overhead presses (narrow grip): D tier.** The side delt has some shoulder flexion leverage, the front delt has far more, and side delt activity is weaker in flexion than abduction.

**Upright rows: A−, which he predicts will surprise viewers.** Mechanically they are abduction with internal rotation, which the rotation data says is fine or good for the side delt. One study found higher side delt activity with upright rows than with abduction-dominant overhead presses. Against that, one study found upright rows less effective than dumbbell lateral raises for side delt activity — but the subjects were **women with chronic neck pain**. A grip-width paper found wider grips better for side delt activity, but he flags a methodological problem with it. He calls A− a judgement call under uncertainty.

## The impingement argument

He credits E3 Rehab's two videos and summarises five lines of evidence against the standard story that abduction with internal rotation compresses tissue in the subacromial space:

1. We would expect the upper supraspinatus to show damage from acromial compression. It does not.
2. We would expect people with hook-shaped acromions to have more problems. They do not.
3. We would expect smaller subacromial distances to correlate with pain in people who have subacromial pain. They do not.
4. We would expect subacromial decompression surgery to help. It does not outperform placebo surgery.
5. The clinical impingement tests that motivate the fear have poor specificity and may have little diagnostic value.

He adds that internal rotation may actually *increase* or not affect acromiohumeral distance, and that the smallest acromiohumeral distances have been observed between **40–75°** of abduction rather than above 90° — so the common "keep upright rows below 90°" advice targets the wrong range, and if impingement worked as claimed, all lateral raises would be dangerous. He supports the field's move away from the term "impingement" toward "subacromial pain," and closes on the correct caveat: pain is multifactorial, someone reports pain from every exercise in existence, and pain is not proof of danger. If it hurts, use one of the many alternatives.

## Two upgrades for the standard dumbbell raise

He offers two ways to push A tier toward S:

1. **Keep going past the point of failure with progressively shorter partials.** When you can no longer get to 90°, keep abducting as far as you can. Your ROM shrinks rep by rep, which means you are increasingly loading the delt at longer lengths. He cites research on cheat/partial continuation styles finding better growth.
2. **Lengthened partials** — skip the top and do heavier partial reps at the bottom only.

## Speculation he labels as speculation

The most interesting unsupported idea in the video: if the long-length advantage is regionally uneven, then challenging the side delt at long lengths might produce only slightly better (or similar) growth at the **proximal** region near the origin, while producing notably better growth at the **distal** region. He calls this "extremely and heavily speculative" because there is no side delt regional growth data at all, and invites viewer reports rather than presenting it as a finding. He also mentions that the deltoid can be resolved into **seven segments** rather than three, a division traced back to 1734 and formalised in an intricate Australian analysis — then explicitly sets it aside for simplicity.

## The limitations section, which is the best part

He closes by dismantling his own method:

- The long-length premise comes from **other muscle groups**. Direct side delt research is needed.
- Surface EMG measures electrical events preceding force production, not growth. There are lines of evidence relating activity to hypertrophy, and there are documented cases where EMG fails to predict growth.
- His resolution: **EMG is more trustworthy when comparing similar exercises and less trustworthy when comparing dissimilar ones**. So the lateral-raise-rotation comparisons are probably reasonably informative; the comparisons between upright rows, overhead presses and lateral raises are much shakier. That is a genuinely useful heuristic and it is applied consistently to his own conclusions.
- Same for leverage data: it relates to electrical activity, and may or may not relate to hypertrophy case by case.
- "Don't consider these rankings as definitely true — consider them as my best guess."

**Overreach flag:** the S/A/B tiers are built almost entirely on acute measures and biomechanical modelling, with the hypertrophy premise imported from studies on other muscles. The rankings are stated with more confidence in the tier-list format than the limitations section can support — though he is the one who points this out.

## How this compares with the library's other side delt ranking

`Jeff Nippard videos/shoulder-exercises-ranked.md` (2024-09-10, five days earlier) reaches the **same top pick from the same reasoning**: cable lateral raise at hand height, S tier, "his number one side delt exercise," because tension peaks where the arm and cable form a 90° angle. Identical mechanism, identical conclusion, arrived at independently.

They **differ on two calls**:

- **Standard dumbbell lateral raise.** Nippard puts it in **B tier** ("zero tension at the bottom, peak tension at 90°"). HoH puts it in **A tier** — same criticism, milder penalty, on the grounds that the side delt still has excellent abduction leverage throughout.
- **Upright row.** Both defend it against the impingement charge, but for different reasons and to different ends. Nippard holds it at **B tier** because "the upper traps take over before the delt fibres reach true exhaustion" — a fatigue-distribution argument. HoH puts it at **A−** on EMG showing higher side delt activity than overhead pressing, and explicitly rejects the general form of the "extra muscle mass dilutes the stimulus" argument using the squat/hip-thrust and leg-extension/squat comparisons. Neither has hypertrophy data on the upright row; the disagreement is between two mechanistic stories.

The library also records Nippard's **2018** position (`lateral-raise-only-must-exercise.md`) that the upright row was his single most "must-do" movement, later downgraded to B tier — so across the library the upright row has been ranked must-do, A−, and B, all without a single hypertrophy trial on it.

**On cable versus dumbbell, this video is now out of date.** It ranks cables S and dumbbells A on resistance-profile reasoning alone, and announces the trial that would test it. That trial — an 8-week study — subsequently found **no difference in side delt growth between cable and dumbbell lateral raises** (Larsen 2025, recorded in `Jeff Nippard videos/7-things-helped-shoulders-grow.md` and `only-25-exercises-need.md`). The resistance-profile logic that both channels used to separate the two exercises did not survive its first direct test. Worth reading this note as the strongest available statement of the *pre-trial* case.
