# Full-Range Bench Beat Partials — Even at Building Partial-Range Strength

**Source:** House of Hypertrophy, "HALF Bench Press Reps for More Strength and Size? (New Research)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5KJtIDUZpGI)) · 2022-03-28 · 11 min
**Topic:** Range of motion & tempo

## Bottom line
- Martínez-Cava and colleagues trained **49 men with 6 months' experience** on the Smith machine bench press, **2x/week for 10 weeks**, in full-, two-thirds- and one-third-range groups (partials all trained the **top** portion) plus a non-training control.
- The **full-range group gained most on all three 1RM tests** — full-, two-thirds- *and* one-third-range bench. Training the partial range was *worse* for building strength in that very partial range than training the full range was.
- The **one-third group was worst on all three tests**; two-thirds beat one-third. Load exposure was auto-regulated per group using velocity, and each group's loads were set relative to their own range-specific 1RM, so the comparison is reasonably clean on that axis.
- **Contradicting data exists and he presents it.** Two Massey studies used a top-range partial (first 2–5 inches of motion): in women with limited experience full-range won, but in **men with some training experience, full, partial and combination groups did not differ** on full-range 1RM.
- The hypertrophy claim is **speculation, not data** — no study compared full versus partial bench for growth. His reasoning is that the full range achieves a much larger **stretch under load** of chest, shoulders and triceps.
- The genuinely useful open question: partials trained at the **bottom** of the bench might behave completely differently, because they *do* load the stretched position.

## What the study did
The full-range group lowered from lockout to the chest, where the bar rested on the Smith machine's spotters for **2 seconds** before reversing. The two-thirds and one-third groups did the same with the same 2-second pause, stopping two-thirds and one-third of the way down respectively. Everyone lifted with **maximal intended concentric velocity** and a controlled eccentric.

The velocity-based design matters. Because first-rep bar velocity tracks relative load, subjects could auto-regulate to the intended relative intensity session to session. Critically, **each group's percentages referred to its own range-specific 1RM** — the one-third group's 70% meant 70% of their one-third 1RM, not of their full-range 1RM. Without this, the heavier absolute loads available in a short range would have confounded everything. Results were expressed as **1RM-to-bodyweight ratios**.

## Why the transfer result is the interesting part
That full-range training beats partial-range training at full-range strength is unsurprising — specificity predicts it. That full-range training also beat one-third training at **one-third-range strength** inverts the specificity expectation, and he is right to treat it as the headline.

He offers a hypertrophy-mediated explanation and immediately labels it speculation. The chain: full range produces greater stretch under load; stretch under load appears to be a strong hypertrophic stimulus in studies of *other* exercises; muscles grow partly by adding **contractile units to myofibrils**, which would raise force capacity. So more growth from full-range training could underwrite more strength across all ranges. His own hedge is the correct one — strength is also driven by neural adaptation and movement efficiency, so even if the hypertrophy difference is real it may not be what produced the strength difference. Note also that this study **did not measure hypertrophy at all**.

## The powerlifting implication
Martínez-Cava and colleagues raise a point he clearly finds compelling: many powerlifters, through heavy arching and scapular retraction, are effectively performing a **top-range partial bench** in competition. If training the full range builds partial-range strength better than training that partial, then powerlifters might improve their competition bench by training with **less arch and less retraction**. He extends this to speculate that **cambered-bar benching**, which extends range further still, could help maximise a normal bench 1RM.

This is an interesting inference but a long one. The study's partials were mechanically defined stopping points on a Smith machine, not the altered shoulder and scapular positions of an arched competition bench, and the transfer logic assumes those are equivalent enough. He does not flag that gap.

## Limitations he raises
- **Smith machine**, not a free barbell. He judges the similarities to outweigh the differences but concedes stabilisation demands and bar path differ, and that the result might not replicate with a barbell.
- **The Massey pair conflicts.** The 2005 study in women with limited training experience found full range best — consistent with Martínez-Cava. The 2004 study in men with some training experience found **no statistically significant difference** between full, partial and combination groups on full-range 1RM. He states plainly that it is not clear what explains the conflict and that more research is needed. Presenting a directly contradicting result at this length, without explaining it away, is the mark of quality this channel is worth reading for.
- **All partials trained the top.** Every study covered used top-range partials. A **bottom-range partial** would achieve stretch under load, which is the mechanism he credits for the full range's advantage. He points to Pedrosa and colleagues, where a **bottom-range partial leg extension at long quadriceps lengths produced more overall quadriceps growth than the full range** — then correctly notes this was a leg extension and cannot be assumed to transfer to the bench.

**Flagged:** the title asks about strength *and size*, but no hypertrophy was measured in any study discussed. The size half of the video is mechanism and inference from other exercises. He says "I think it's almost certain" full-range benching produces more hypertrophy than a top-range partial — that confidence level is not supported by any bench-press growth data, which does not exist.
