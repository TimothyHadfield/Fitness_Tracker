# HRV Does Not Track Recovery From Lifting, and Does Not Improve Lifting Outcomes

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Understanding Heart Rate Variability for Strength Training" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/heart-rate-variability-hrv/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2025-03-06 · 3,932 words
**Topic:** Volume & recovery

## Bottom line
- **The two head-to-head trials in resistance training both came back null.** HRV-guided training produced **no difference** in strength, hypertrophy, functional tests or the number of non-responders versus a fixed schedule — in 21 older women over 7 weeks ([De Oliveira-style protocol, Front Physiol 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1472702/full)) and in 22 young men racing to 20 sessions ([De Oliveira 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1080/17461391.2019.1572227)).
- **HRV recovers *later* than performance in lifters.** In national/international weightlifters, 1RM performance in squat, deadlift, seated press and front squat returned to baseline in **~30 hours**; HRV took **~60 hours** ([Chen 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21273908/)). If you wait for HRV, you wait past readiness.
- **Higher is not simply better.** High HRV is associated with atrial fibrillation ([Geurts 2023, Mendelian randomisation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10241681/)); low HRV is associated with heart disease, diabetes, MS, ageing, low fitness, low muscle mass, stress and alcohol.
- **The consumer devices are mostly unvalidated.** An umbrella review of systematic reviews found **under 5%** of the biometric parameters wearables report have been validated ([Doherty 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11560992/)). The Polar H10 chest strap validates against EKG; **Apple Watch and WHOOP underestimate HRV**.
- **Their actual recommendation is narrow and labelled experimental:** use a 5–7 day rolling average under consistent conditions, and use it only to *add* a session when you are at or above baseline — never to skip one when below.

## What HRV is, and what it is not

HRV is the variation in the interval between successive beats, an **indirect, non-invasive** proxy for autonomic nervous system function ([Ernst 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5600971/)). The article is careful on two points other coverage gets wrong.

First, HRV is not an arrhythmia. Arrhythmia is a deviation from normal sinus rhythm; HRV is beat-interval variation within it. The two are conceptually distinct even though a-fib patients tend to show high HRV.

Second, the parasympathetic/sympathetic model is not a see-saw. Activity in one branch can rise with, fall with, or not affect the other — after exercise, sympathetic activity stays elevated while parasympathetic activity also rises ([Billman 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3576706/)). That paper is specifically an argument that the LF/HF ratio *does not* measure sympatho-vagal balance, which makes it a well-chosen citation: they are citing the critique, not the folk model. They also flag that the very-low-frequency band's meaning is unclear ([von Rosenberg 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469891/)), and that different time- and frequency-domain methods give different numbers, making cross-method comparison unreliable.

## The measurement problem

Wearables use photoplethysmography — LED light at wrist arteries, measuring reflected light as arterial blood volume pulses ([Charlton 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7612541/)). Raw data is then passed through proprietary filters before the user sees a number.

The validation picture is the strongest material in the article. Against EKG, the **Polar H10** holds up; **Apple Watch and WHOOP tend to underestimate HRV** ([O'Grady 2024, Apple Watch Series 9/Ultra 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11478500/); [Miller 2022, six devices](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9412437/)). Separate concerns have been raised about the filtering software and about *when* data is captured — sleep versus waking hours ([Bellenger 2021, WHOOP validation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8160717/)). Their conclusion is blunt and correct: for now, wearable-generated HRV in performance settings is largely speculative.

## Does HRV measure recovery?

The article does something unusual here — it defines the outcome before testing the tool. Recovery for resistance training is defined as **return to baseline strength and/or restoration of a physiological state permitting further adaptation**. That definition is what makes the negative verdict meaningful.

In endurance sport, HRV is higher and more stable in more experienced athletes ([Flatt 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36135035/); [Seiler 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762370/)), and drops during rapid volume increases and overreaching, recovering with a few days' rest ([Baumert 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17016118/); [Dupuy 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23438233/)) — though highly trained athletes may show *preserved* HRV through overreaching ([Le Meur 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24136138/); [Bosquet 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14767413/)). The article's own caveat: **none of those studies assessed HRV against performance or the ability to adapt.** That is the right criticism to make of one's own supporting literature.

In lifting, HRV falls further and for longer after sets taken to failure than after matched-intensity work stopped short of failure, alongside longer-lasting countermovement-jump decrements ([Pareja-Blanco 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26970332/); [González-Badillo 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26667923/)) — but the effect on low-speed strength was not established. The Chen weightlifting study then breaks the recovery framing outright: performance came back at half the latency of HRV.

The one prospective-injury signal is a study of competitive CrossFit athletes, where a **low 7-day average HRV** marked higher overuse-injury risk; workload increases depressed HRV in those who later reported injury and not in those who did not ([Williams 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29238242/)). This is a moderating-factor analysis in one cohort, not a screening tool.

## Can HRV be used to manage lifting?

Their best case is that HRV might be used to *increase* volume in people who tolerate it, since higher volume generally drives better outcomes ([Figueiredo 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29022275/); [Ralston 2017 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28755103/); [Lixandrão 2024, higher volume offsets hypertrophy non-response in older adults](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38174375/)).

Both trials that tested this delivered more training and no more result. In the older-women study (mean age ~66), the HRV group averaged 27 sessions of a possible 35 versus 21 in the fixed group — and one fixed-group participant completed 21 sessions despite HRV indicating recovery on only 9 of them, with no apparent penalty. In the young-men study, the HRV group reached 20 sessions in just over 5 weeks versus 7 weeks for the fixed group. No difference in strength, hypertrophy, or non-responder count either time.

**Flagged as overreach:** the article then speculates that the higher-volume HRV group "would see better results in the long-term." That is a hypothesis contradicted by nothing but also supported by nothing in the two trials, which found the extra volume bought no measured advantage. The related claim that individual response is driven by genetics, nutrition and androgen-receptor content is supported by mechanistic and association studies ([Walsh 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3500611/); [Zillikens 2017 GWAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5517526/); [Hulmi 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18661258/); [Mitchell 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3793973/)), and the article correctly cites the paper arguing non-responders may be an artefact of measurement and dose ([Pickering & Kiely 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6349783/)).

**Evidence vs. recommendation.** The evidence conclusion is that HRV has little stand-alone use in managing resistance training. The *recommendation* — 5–7 day averages under consistent conditions, baseline set during a deload, add a session when at or above baseline, train as planned when below, and drop the nearest rest day to fit the extra session in — is coaching judgement layered on top of a null result. To their credit they label it experimental and quote Sagan on open minds. This is a clean example of the distinction the library should keep visible.
