# Creatine: A Clinician's Restrained Read, and Where It Sits Between the Meta-Analysis and the ISSN

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Creatine: Benefits, Side Effects & Safe Consumption" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/what-is-creatine/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2024-04-02 · 4,679 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The hypertrophy claim is the one to check. Feigenbaum cites **[Burke 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu15092116)** — the direct-measurement meta-analysis this library treats as the best available estimate — and reports it as "a small increase in hypertrophy… likely on the order of a couple of pounds or so in a year." **That paper measured regional muscle thickness by ultrasound and MRI, not body mass**: 0.10–0.16 cm across triceps, elbow flexors, quadriceps and hamstrings, overall effect size **0.11**, which its own authors called of possibly minimal practical significance. The pounds-per-year figure is a unit conversion the paper does not license.
- Even so, **this is the most restrained of the three positions in the library.** He calls the effects "modest" and "small to moderate," and he cites the [ISSN 2017 stand](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z) only for dosing — not for its claim that creatine is "the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement currently available." He is also well short of House of Hypertrophy's "about one-third faster muscle gain," which reinterprets the same 0.11 against hypertrophy-specific thresholds. Ranked by enthusiasm: ISSN > House of Hypertrophy > Barbell Medicine > Burke's own reading.
- **Performance**, where the evidence is stronger: ~5–15% improvement in maximal power/strength and reps-to-failure at a fixed load **in responders**; smaller gains in 1RM and sprint ([Buford 2007](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-4-6); [Lanhers 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0571-4), [2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0337-4)). Older adults appear to gain more strength ([Forbes 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13061912)).
- The best clinical material is on **creatinine**, not creatine: supplementation raises serum creatinine and can mimic kidney disease on a lab panel read in isolation. This is the section only a physician-authored source in this library would write, and it is the most useful thing in the article.
- **They sell creatine.** The supplement-contamination section — ~20% of supplements contaminated, 30–50% mislabelled, under 10% of Amazon creatine third-party certified — ends by linking their own supplement line, and the "best form" section names their product PeriRx as an example of what to buy. The underlying facts are properly cited; the placement is a sales funnel and should be read as one.

## What the article gets right about mechanism, and how hard it leans on it
The physiology is textbook and correctly hedged. 95% of body creatine sits in skeletal muscle, two-thirds of that bound as phosphocreatine; ~1–2% turns over daily to creatinine and is excreted ([Balsom 1994](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-199418040-00005)). Endogenous synthesis from arginine, glycine and methionine downregulates on a meat-heavy diet and upregulates on a plant-based one — which is why vegetarians start lower. Phosphocreatine stores are nearly exhausted in 10–15 seconds of intense work, half-restored in a minute, fully restored in about eight ([McMahon 2002](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200232120-00002)). Supplementation raises muscle total creatine and phosphocreatine by **10–40%**.

Three proposed hypertrophy mechanisms are offered — cellular energy buffering, cell swelling as an anabolic signal, and satellite cell activation ([Olsen 2006](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2006.107359), where creatine plus training increased myonuclei more than either alone). He is honest that the cell-swelling data are "mixed," and he explicitly says mechanisms don't tell the whole story before turning to outcomes. That is the right move, and it is worth contrasting with the House of Hypertrophy note in this library, which is openly sceptical that the work-capacity mechanism explains the growth at all.

## The effect-size question, stated plainly
This is the claim the library needs adjudicated, so here is the chain in full.

Burke 2023 pooled ten studies using site-specific ultrasound or MRI rather than DXA lean mass. Creatine added **0.10–0.16 cm** of muscle thickness; the pooled effect size was **0.11**; the authors concluded the practical significance may be minimal. Feigenbaum cites exactly this paper and translates it into "a couple of pounds or so in a year." Those are not the same quantity — one is regional thickness at four sites, the other is whole-body mass — and the translation moves the claim from a trivial effect on the measure that was taken to a tangible-sounding effect on a measure that was not. **He does not report the effect size, and he does not report that the authors called it minimal.** That is the article's one substantive omission.

Against that, he does not do what the ISSN stand does. He never calls creatine the most effective supplement available; he says the strength/power gains are "typically modest" and "likely proportional with the increase in muscle phosphocreatine"; he is agnostic on endurance ([Fernández-Landa 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01823-2)), explicitly unconvinced on cognition ("mixed and inconsistent study design," [Prokopidis 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuac064)), and describes the clinical applications as active research rather than established. For a company selling the product, that is a notably conservative reading, and it deserves saying alongside the criticism.

## Safety, and the one section that earns its clinical byline
The safety claim rests on long-term markers of kidney, liver, immune and metabolic function, plus a renal-function meta-analysis ([de Souza e Silva 2019](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.jrn.2019.05.004)) and the small [Poortmans 1999](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-199908000-00005) athlete study.

The **creatinine** discussion is the article at its best. A trivial fraction of stored creatine cyclises to creatinine, so a high supplemental dose raises the marker clinicians use to estimate kidney function — potentially producing a lab result that looks like renal impairment in someone with entirely normal kidneys. He reports serum creatinine peaking at ~2.5× baseline on monohydrate and ~6.7× on creatine ethyl ester within hours. **Flagged:** those two numbers are sourced to unpublished personal communication. A striking figure carried by an unpublished source should not be quoted downstream as if it were data.

Two honest gaps: **no safety data in children or adolescents** (performance studies exist and report no adverse events, but none assessed safety — [Jagim 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2018.00115)) and **none in pregnancy** ([De Guingand 2019](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-026756) is a protocol paper). Note the ISSN stand in this library takes the opposite line on adolescents, arguing the under-18 label warnings are legal cover rather than science. Barbell Medicine's "don't" here is a clinical recommendation under uncertainty, not an evidence claim, and they frame it as such.

The **hair loss** deconstruction is good. The entire scare traces to one 21-day study in college rugby players ([van der Merwe 2009](https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0b013e3181b8b52f)) that measured DHT, not hair; the creatine group started **over 20% below** the placebo group, so the "significant increase" is substantially a regression toward the placebo group's baseline. Never replicated, no long-term hair outcome study exists. He concludes creatine "probably doesn't increase the risk" in men without genetic predisposition — appropriately hedged.

**Flagged (internal contradiction):** the summary bullets assert creatine "does not cause… water retention, weight gain, hair loss, or an increase in body fat," but the body of the article twice acknowledges early weight gain — enough to hurt short-term swimming and running performance, and reason enough to skip a loading phase. The ISSN stand lists weight gain as the one consistently reported side effect across ~1,000 studies. The summary overstates what the article itself says.

## Practical dosing
Loading is **20–25 g/day (0.3 g/kg) split across the day for 5–7 days**; maintenance is **3–5 g/day (0.05–0.07 g/kg)**. After about a month the two routes converge ([Hultman 1996](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1996.81.1.232)), so loading is only worth it if you need the effect inside that window — and is a bad idea if you are weight-sensitive or have had GI trouble at high doses ([Ostojic 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/15438620701693280)). Timing and co-ingestion don't matter much ([Ribeiro 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13082844)). Monohydrate, ideally micronised, third-party tested; alternative forms have no evidence of superiority ([Escalante 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2022.e12113)).

**Responders**: the entire responder/non-responder literature here is one study of 11 men — 27% responders, 45% quasi-responders, 27% non-responders, with responders having more type II fibre area and lower baseline muscle creatine ([Syrotuik 2004](https://doi.org/10.1519/12392.1)). He says outright that we don't really know the population proportions. That is more cautious than the "20–30% non-responders" figure quoted confidently elsewhere in this library from the same source.
