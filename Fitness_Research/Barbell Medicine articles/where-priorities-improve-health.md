# Seven Health Priorities, and a Firm Refusal to Screen You for Anything

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Where should my priorities be to improve my health?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/where-should-my-priorities-be-to-improve-my-health/)) · Austin Baraki · 2020-03-03 · 3,231 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- Seven priorities, in the article's order: **exercise, body weight and composition, sleep, avoiding tobacco and substance abuse, basic medical screening, pain self-management, social connection.**
- **The most useful content is what they tell you *not* to do.** No routine lab or imaging screening in asymptomatic people — explicitly including **vitamin D, testosterone, thyroid, cortisol, stress tests and whole-body scans**. No sleep trackers. No default recommendation for low-carb, keto or intermittent fasting. These are the positions that distinguish the article from generic wellness writing, and they are consistent with the US Preventive Services Task Force rather than with the supplement-and-biomarker industry.
- **Nutrition is ranked, not enumerated:** calories → protein → fibre → preferences. Protein at **~1.6 g/kg/day**, fibre at **30–35 g/day**. Meal timing, carb-versus-fat split and specific food choices are all placed in the "matters less" column.
- **Waist circumference is the single body measurement they'd keep.** Cutoffs given as low risk <94 cm men / <80 cm women; increased 94–102 / 80–88; high >102 / >88, with ~7 cm subtracted from each threshold for people of Asian descent. Pants size is not waist circumference.
- **Exercise dose:** 150–300 min/week moderate or 75–150 min vigorous, **plus** resistance training 2+ days/week. Only **26% of US men, 19% of women and 20% of adolescents** meet the guidelines.
- **They sell against this article heavily.** Coaching is linked three times, training templates once, and their own nutrition guide, beginner programme and podcast repeatedly. The advice itself is conservative and mostly points at free public resources, but every "want help?" routes to a paid Barbell Medicine service.

## The seven, and what sits under each

**Exercise.** Physical inactivity is the fourth-largest global risk factor for death per the WHO's 2009 Global Health Risks report, behind high blood pressure, tobacco and elevated blood sugar. Uptake figures come from Piercy 2018, the paper accompanying the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans. The one piece of outcome evidence in this section is **Bennie 2020 — a pooled analysis of 1.7 million US adults** finding that people who meet *both* the aerobic and the strength-training components do better across obesity, hypertension, diabetes and other conditions than those meeting either alone. That is the strongest single citation in the article and it is doing the work the section needs.

Their stated reason for ranking exercise first is not primarily mortality: it is that training is "the principal method for maintaining physical independence across the lifespan." That is a values judgement about what health is for, following the Huber 2011 *BMJ* definition they adopt — health as "the ability to adapt and self-manage in the face of social, physical, and emotional challenges." It is worth noticing that the same definition anchors their [range of motion article](range-motion-considerations.md); it is a house position, not an offhand quotation.

**Body weight.** Obesity is framed as always multi-causal — biological, psychological, social and environmental — with an explicit refusal to blame any single variable (carbohydrates, animal foods, hormones, sugar, fat), citing the 2016 AACE obesity guideline. Waist circumference is recommended over other measures, following the Ross 2020 consensus statement. The treatment ladder runs from a small sustainable deficit through hunger-management strategies to, where appropriate, obesity medications and bariatric surgery — mentioned without hedging, which for a fitness publisher in 2020 was not a given.

**Diet.** The ranked list (calories → protein → fibre → preferences) is the article's most transferable content. The stated basis for declining to recommend keto, low-carb or intermittent fasting is that the literature does not show consistent superiority **at equivalent adherence** — the right formulation of that argument, and one that leaves room for any of them to work for a given person. **Protein at ~1.6 g/kg matches this library's position of 1.6–1.8 g/kg.** Note that Barbell Medicine give three different protein numbers across three articles: 1.6 here, 1.4–1.6 in their [weight-gain article](reasons-re-working-out-but-gaining-weight.md), and 1.3–3.1 in their [protein guide](barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md). This one is closest to the library's verdict.

**Sleep.** Standard sleep-hygiene behaviours (fixed schedule, naps under 30–45 min, no caffeine or alcohol within 4–6 hours, cool dark quiet room, bed for sleep and sex only), with CBT-I named for more severe insomnia. The clinical content is the sleep-apnea screening: **neck circumference above 17 in / 43 cm in men or 16 in / 40 cm in women** flags risk, and they direct readers to the **STOP-BANG questionnaire** and then to a physician. The refusal to recommend sleep trackers is an active position against a large consumer category.

**Tobacco and alcohol.** Smoking is cited via CDC as the leading cause of preventable death in the US, roughly **1 in 5 deaths**, with about 14% of adults still smoking. Alcohol is given as responsible for about **88,000 US deaths annually**. On the moderate-drinking J-curve they take the sceptical side: the association exists but "there is no probable cause for this stemming from alcohol itself," with social interaction offered as a confounder. That is stated as settled and is stronger than the wording of the underlying epidemiology, though the field has since moved in their direction.

**Medical screening — the clinically substantial part.** High-value: blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes screening when appropriate, age-appropriate cancer screening, vaccinations. Low-value: whole-body scans, unnecessary panels, screening without indication, chasing minor lab abnormalities. Then the strong version, in their own capitals: **no other routine lab, imaging or other testing in asymptomatic people**, naming vitamin D, testosterone, thyroid, cortisol and stress tests. They cite overdiagnosis literature and route clinicians to the USPSTF. Immunisation is endorsed without qualification per ACIP.

This is the part of the article that earns the source its place in this library. It is a physician position about the harms of over-testing, argued from guideline evidence, in a market where selling biomarker panels is the business model — and it is written by people who also sell things.

**Pain.** Brief and consistent with modern pain science: pain is normal, self-management reduces "unnecessary fear, disability, and the harms of over-medicalization." No primary evidence is given in the section itself; the pointers are to the **2018 *Lancet* low back pain series** and **Lin 2020 in BJSM** on best-practice musculoskeletal pain care, both of which are appropriate anchors.

**Social connection.** Explicitly the weakest section, and they say so — "the most indefinite of our recommendations," no minimum number of friends, no quantification. No citation at all. The claim that isolation is an unfavourable environment for health is well supported in the wider literature; this article does not support it.

## Where it outruns its evidence

**Flagged:** the headline claim that consistently doing these seven things "covers **~90%** of what matters for long-term health" carries no citation and no derivation. The seven priorities are defensible and largely guideline-aligned; the number is invented, and it is the one figure a reader is most likely to repeat.

Second, several sections give practical prescriptions with no supporting reference — the entire sleep-hygiene list, the neck-circumference thresholds, and the social-connection section. These are ordinary clinical advice, and the article does not pretend otherwise, but the citation density falls sharply outside the exercise and screening sections.

**Sells:** coaching (three links), training templates, their "To Be A Beast" nutrition guide, the Beginner Prescription, and multiple in-house podcast and YouTube episodes. Nothing recommended is a supplement, and the medical advice actively discourages the testing that most of this industry monetises — but the article's structure funnels every action step toward a paid Barbell Medicine service.
