# Nutrition, supplements and how this source reads evidence

Twenty notes covering protein, energy balance, dieting, diet composition, supplements, food quality
and hydration, spanning 2012 to 2026 — a range wide enough that the source contradicts itself
repeatedly and never once says so. Barbell Medicine is written by practising physicians, which shows
in the clinical sections (kidney function, creatinine, low energy availability, safety ceilings) and
in an unusual willingness to publish a negative result about a product they stock. It also sells a
supplement line, coaching, consultations and templates, so every recommendation below is marked for
whether the article routes you to something they charge for — and, just as importantly, for the
places where it argues *against* a category they could have sold you.

Three distinctions run through everything: **measured** (a trial or meta-analysis produced this
number), **recommended** (a clinician's judgement, honestly labelled), and **uncited** (a number
appearing in an article with no source attached to the sentence). Barbell Medicine are better than
most at the first two and worse than their reputation suggests at avoiding the third.

## Protein: four numbers, one publisher, no reconciliation

**This is the defect to know about before you quote them on anything.** Across these notes Barbell
Medicine give at least four different daily protein targets spanning better than a two-fold range,
and no article acknowledges that any of the others exist.

**Number one — 1.3–3.1 g/kg/day.** The 2021 [protein guide](barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md)
splits it: **1.3–2.2 g/kg** is "more than adequate" for maintaining or gaining, and **2.3–3.1 g/kg**
for people who are lean *and* actively dieting, training hard 5+ days a week, or anabolically
resistant. (Its own Key Points box states the range as 1.6–3.1, disagreeing with its body text on the
first page.) The bottom of the band is properly sourced: **Morton 2018, 49 studies, 1,863 healthy
adults**, a meta-regression finding a breakpoint near **1.62 g/kg** above which resistance-training
gains stop improving. The top rests on a single source — **Helms 2014**, a systematic review titled
*A Case for Higher Intakes* — seconded by the ISSN's 2018 review. Two things the article does not
say: the endpoint generating the high tier is **minimising lean-mass loss**, not hypertrophy or
strength; and the underlying trials compare higher protein against *inadequate* protein, so they
establish that too little is bad, not that 3.1 beats 1.8.

**Number two — 1.6 g/kg.** Given in [how to eat a healthy diet](eat-healthy-diet.md) and as the floor
in [training while losing weight](train-while-losing-weight.md), both traced correctly to Morton's
breakpoint. **This is their best-supported figure.** The healthy-diet article then also offers
**3.1 g/kg for physique athletes in a deficit with no citation at all**.

**Number three — 1.4–1.6 g/kg.** The current line in the 8,500-word
[protein and weight loss](protein-weight-loss-maximizing-fat-loss-preserving.md) guide, in
[protein on Ozempic](protein-ozempic-much-need.md), and in
[working out but gaining weight](reasons-re-working-out-but-gaining-weight.md). Its anchor is the
**2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines' general-population band of 1.2–1.6 g/kg**, not a deficit trial. It
is the lowest number anywhere in this library, and it is **below the 1.62 breakpoint from the
meta-analysis they themselves cite elsewhere**. Worse, the weight-loss guide quotes their own house
guideline as **1.4–2.2 g/kg** in its opening pages and then concludes at 1.4–1.6, so the article
disagrees with itself. The two references in its list that argue for higher intakes — Morton 2018 and
Helms 2014 — are **printed but never attached to a sentence**; **19 of its 49 references are never
cited at all**.

**Number four — 1.6 g/kg of *target* body weight**, from
[creatine on Ozempic](creatine-ozempic-prevent-muscle-loss.md). For someone with obesity that is a
materially smaller denominator, so the absolute gram target is lower than the number looks, wearing
the clothes of number two.

The 2012 archive swung the other way entirely. [To Be A Beast](to-be-a-beast.md) sets a muscle-gain
template at **1.1–1.25 g/lb**, which is **2.4–2.8 g/kg** — and then argues on the same page *against*
higher protein intakes on ammonia-toxicity grounds, while conceding it is unknown whether dietary
protein raises ammonia to a harmful level in healthy people. The template and the reasoning
contradict each other within one article.

**The clash with the ISSN is the protein target, and it is sharp.** The 2017 ISSN protein stand
frames 1.4–2.0 g/kg as a *minimum* for exercising adults and says more may be needed under energy
restriction; the ISSN diets and body-composition stand endorses **2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass** for
lean trained athletes cutting, on the strength of Longland 2016 (four-compartment model, all food
provided, **2.4 g/kg gave +1.2 kg lean and −4.8 kg fat versus +0.1 kg and −3.5 kg at 1.2 g/kg**).
Barbell Medicine's ceiling of 1.6 sits at or below everyone else's floor. The gap is visible in the
evidence they chose: their deficit numbers come from meta-analyses of overweight and obese
non-lifters at **1.1–1.6 g/kg** against comparators eating **0.6–0.8 g/kg**, where 1.6 genuinely is
the top of the studied range.

**The defensible answer: 1.6–1.8 g/kg of current body weight, deficit or not, and stop optimising.**
That sits inside their own best-sourced figure and above their lowest. Below it you are trusting a
general-population dietary guideline over the meta-analysis they cite themselves; above 2.0 g/kg you
are buying insurance rather than effect, because nobody has run 1.6 against 2.4 in a *moderate*
deficit — the cutting trials always compared adequate against inadequate.

Their **GLP-1 triage order survives all of this** and is the most useful practical thing in the
protein material: **training first, total calories second, total protein third, distribution a
distant fourth**. If a drug has taken your appetite and you are at 0.6 g/kg, the move that matters is
0.6 → 1.2, not 1.4 → 1.6.

### Protein source, and per-meal distribution

**Source does not matter once the dose is adequate**, and this is the strongest evidence in the
protein guide. Four trials, with the intakes reported, which is the detail that makes it work:
**Banaszek 2019** (whey vs pea, 8 weeks, no difference in strength, power, lean mass — intakes
**1.8 and 1.7 g/kg**); **Joy 2013** (24 college-aged men, 8 weeks, **48 g/day** whey or rice, no
difference in bench or leg press 1RM or Wingate peak power); **Babault 2015** (161 men, 12 weeks,
**25 g twice daily** of whey, pea or placebo — both protein arms beat placebo, no whey-vs-pea
difference); **Hartman 2007** (56 young men, 12 weeks, milk vs soy vs sugar — a barely significant
milk edge, confounded because the soy group ate 1.2 g/kg and the milk group 1.4 g/kg). The acute
mechanism is not disputed — **25 g of whey (2.7 g leucine, 10.9 g EAAs)** near-maximally stimulates
muscle protein synthesis where you would need **31 g of corn protein or 105 g of hemp protein** for
the same doses (Gorissen 2018) — but those acute differences do not translate into training outcomes.
PDCAAS figures given: **whey 1.0, soy 0.99, pea 0.893, wheat gluten 0.25** (from Rutherfurd 2015,
which is a **growing-rat** study the article does not flag).

On per-meal ceilings, the 2014 [seven protein rules](rules-optimize-protein-intake.md) got there
early and for the right reason. It killed the absorption version of the myth: tracer work shows
essentially complete absorption of what enters the gut — in fact **~110–120% of intake**, because
enterocytes secrete their own protein into the lumen — failure to absorb would present as
post-prandial osmotic diarrhoea, and **rate is not extent**. The companion
[protein slows digestion? Nope](protein-slows-digestion-nope.md) does the same job for gastric
emptying: protein-rich and carbohydrate-rich meals empty at roughly the same rate, normal emptying is
**2–6 hours**, whey raises plasma amino acids within about **20 minutes**, and what actually governs
emptying is fat, fibre type (soluble slows, insoluble speeds), total calories, tonicity and hormonal
state — not protein. Both articles are correct. **Both cite absolutely nothing.**

The same 2014 article then builds a system that has not held: a **3–4 g leucine per-meal threshold**,
a **3–5 hour refractory period**, "eat 3–5 times a day tops", and the claim that protein eaten inside
that window "would contribute nothing to MPS and then, by definition, be stored away" as glycogen or
fat. That last inference is unsourced and does not follow — it treats an acute MPS measurement as a
complete accounting of protein fate. The practical advice survives; the mechanism given for it does
not. Later articles soften without abandoning it: **~20–45 g (0.25–0.6 g/kg) per meal** in the 2021
guide, **25–40 g** in the weight-loss guide (uncited, and quietly raising the ISSN's lower bound from
20 g to 25 g). Stating a band still implies a ceiling; the position to hold is that there is none.
Their own meal-spacing advice is immediately downgraded — every 3–5 hours, "less important than the
total daily intake."

### Is high protein safe?

Yes, in people with normal kidney and liver function, and this is where the physician authorship
pays. **Hyperfiltration** — a rise in GFR under a higher nitrogen load — is presented as a normal
adaptive response to increased solute clearance rather than as damage, which is the point most
kidney-protein scares miss. Meta-analytic data around **1.8 g/kg/day** show trivial or absent GFR
effects in normal kidney function (Cheng 2024); intakes to **4.4 g/kg** appear safe (Antonio 2014).
The careful turn is clinical: they cite the **Cochrane review (Hahn 2020)** plus the KNOW-CKD cohort
to say it is *unclear* whether restricting protein to ~0.6 g/kg in existing kidney disease preserves
function or improves quality of life — a physician reading a Cochrane review accurately rather than
past it. **Two citation problems**: Cheng 2024 is an observational meta-analysis of association,
converted into "may even be protective against kidney disease," which the design cannot carry; and
the sentence about blood ammonia is supported by **Tsompanaki 2023, a meta-analysis of diet and
non-alcoholic fatty liver disease**, not of ammonia handling. Credit where due: **Recommendation 4 of
the protein guide states that intakes up to 4.4 g/kg are safe but have not been shown to improve
strength, hypertrophy, body composition or performance** — a supplement company saying more protein
does nothing above a threshold.

## Energy balance, and why the scale moves the way it does

**Energy balance is the frame and they never break it**, including in the articles most sympathetic
to appetite physiology. TDEE splits roughly **60% resting / 10% diet-induced / 30% activity**
([working out but gaining weight](reasons-re-working-out-but-gaining-weight.md)), and the response to
an identical deficit or surplus varies more than **five-fold** between people, largely genetically —
the Bouchard identical-twin overfeeding (1990) and constant-intake exercise (1994) studies, small and
old and still the cleanest demonstrations available.

**The single most useful number in this domain**, from
[does your metabolism change](metabolism-change-weight-loss.md): expenditure falls about
**30 kcal/day per kilogram lost**, while intake rises about **100 kcal/day per kilogram lost** — the
appetite response is over threefold larger than the metabolic one everybody worries about. The
evidence is one clever trial: **Polidori 2016**, canagliflozin removing a constant **~90 g of glucose
per day in urine**, an imperceptible and therefore placebo-controllable deficit; **153 on drug vs 89
on placebo over 52 weeks**, weight plateauing about **3.5 kg lower** while a validated model put the
steady-state rise in intake at about **350 kcal/day**. Two caveats the article does not raise: the
350 kcal is **modelled, not measured**, and the population is diabetic patients on a glucose-lowering
drug rather than dieters. That article's behavioural advice is its weakest part and is not labelled
as such — the National Weight Control Registry figures it quotes (**98% modified intake, 94% raised
activity, 78% eat breakfast, 75% weigh weekly**) come from a **self-selected registry of over 10,000
people who already succeeded**, so they are correlates, not causes.

**You cannot usefully raise your metabolic rate.** RMR is ~**80%** determined by lean body mass, and
adding lean mass barely moves it — **18 elite rugby players gained 2.0 kg of lean mass over 14 weeks
with no significant RMR change**. Using Hall's coefficients (**19 kcal/kg/day for lean tissue,
4.5 kcal/kg/day for fat**), a 10 kg loss at the usual composition predicts an RMR drop of about
**81 kcal/day**; anything beyond that is adaptive thermogenesis. The "muscle weighs more than fat"
claim is handled properly: densities are **1.06 kg/L for muscle and 0.92 kg/L for fat**, so a
kilogram of fat simply takes up more space.

**Weight regain is physiology, not character**, and
[the article making that case](weight-regain-not-failure-willpower.md) is structured as a falsifiable
claim. **BMI heritability is 40–70% of between-person variance**, from **540 Danish adoptees**
(adult weight tracked biological parents, not the adoptive kitchen) and **93 identical twin pairs
reared apart** (BMI correlations **0.66–0.70**). Their handling is unusually careful — heritability
describes differences between people, not the share of *your* body that is genetic. **Sumithran
2011**: 50 people, ten weeks on a 500–550 kcal formula diet, **~13.5 kg lost**; a year later hunger
hormones were still up, satiety hormones still down, and self-rated hunger was **higher than before
the diet started**. **Fothergill's Biggest Loser cohort** ran ~**610 kcal/day** below baseline at 30
weeks and ~**700 kcal/day** below at six years — from **14 contestants**, which the article never
says, though it does concede via Hall's own reinterpretation that the contestants' sustained exercise
increases may account for part of it. The strongest single item is **Wright 2023**: over **12,000
siblings** across four longitudinal studies, where the population-level "smarter is leaner"
correlation survives between strangers and **shrinks to indistinguishable from zero between siblings
raised in the same family**.

**GLP-1 drugs are their rhetorical trump card**: STEP 1 gave ~**15% loss over 68 weeks** versus ~2%
on placebo, SURMOUNT-1 ~**21%** at the top tirzepatide dose, against ~**3%** average maintained loss
at five years from lifestyle alone. Their inference — that if turning one appetite dial makes eating
this much easier, appetite was running the show — is inference, not measurement. To their credit they
explicitly refuse the "hormones, not calories" framing: the hormones govern hunger and food
behaviour, and **none of them act independently of energy balance**.

**Read the scale as a trend, never a reading.** A **2% shift in total body water in a 70 kg person is
close to two pounds**, fat-free mass is **70–80% water** against **10%** for fat mass, and regular
training increases water retention as an adaptation — which is the whole explanation for "I started
lifting and gained 4 lb in a week." Weight has a weekly rhythm, heaviest Sunday and Monday, lightest
Friday, varying **1–2% of bodyweight**. **Judge energy balance on a two-to-four-week rolling
average.**

## Dieting: rate of loss, and what training does and does not do

[Training in a deficit](train-while-losing-weight.md) is the best-argued nutrition article in the
corpus, and its conclusions are deflationary in both directions.

**Strength is essentially deficit-proof; lean mass is not.** A meta-analysis of **52 studies**
(Murphy & Koehler 2022) found energy deficiency impairs resistance-training gains in **lean mass but
not strength**, with the severity of the deficit not predicting strength outcomes either. The
authors' own reading — that the surviving strength gains are neural or microarchitectural rather than
hypertrophic — is both correct and the caveat.

Three trials carry the strength claim, and their populations matter. **Longland 2016**: 40 young
untrained men with overweight, **4 weeks at ~40% below maintenance** with lifting and HIIT — leg press
**+98%**, bench **+32%**, push-ups **+32%**, relative VO2max **+15%**. The article does not name it,
does not list it, and does not mention that it was designed as a high-versus-lower-protein comparison
or that supervised untrained men for four weeks is the single most favourable case that exists.
**Zachwieja 2001**: 24 untrained adults confined to a research facility, **two weeks at a 750 kcal/day
deficit**, lost 1.3 kg with **no detriment** to endurance, strength, five-mile run time or Wingate.
**Garthe 2011** is the one that matters: **24 elite Norwegian athletes** randomised to fast
(**1 kg/week**) or slow (**0.5 kg/week**) loss to the same endpoint — the slow group lost **31% of
fat mass** versus 21% and **gained 2% lean mass** where the fast group was unchanged; squat 1RM rose
similarly in both, bench more in the slow group.

**The prescription: a 200–500 kcal/day deficit, 0.25–0.50 kg/week, ≥1.6 g/kg protein, and keep
training as it is.** A larger deficit is explicitly "acceptable" for people with overweight or obesity
prioritising health — clinical judgement, labelled. The mechanism for keeping training is **anabolic
rescue**: resting muscle protein synthesis falls under restriction regardless of training status, and
resistance training normalises it and raises it by ~**30%** (Pasiakos 2010; Areta 2014). Muscle *gain*
in a deficit is possible in untrained people, people with obesity and older adults — a six-month trial
in **36 subjects aged 60–80** found both arms lost 2.5–3 kg but **lean body mass rose 0.5 kg only in
the lifting group** (that population was **type 2 diabetic**, which the article does not say) — but it
will almost certainly be less than at maintenance, particularly for trained lifters.

**Exercise is a poor weight-loss lever and a good health lever, and these are separate claims.**
Long-term (>6 month) exercise-only weight loss lands between **0 and 2 kg**, some people gaining. The
one-year Oslo Diet and Exercise Study: exercise-only **1.3 kg**, diet plus exercise **6.5 kg**. A
ten-month trial in men with obesity: exercise-only **nothing**, diet plus exercise **~9 kg**.
Goodpaster 2010 is the most telling: diet for a full year with exercise added only in the last six
months produced the **same** one-year loss as diet plus exercise throughout. Two mechanisms are
offered — compensatory reductions in non-exercise movement, and the fact that the exercise volume
needed to shift energy balance runs to **two or three times** the Physical Activity Guidelines. Their
conclusion: **cutting 500 kcal of intake produces more weight loss than adding 500 kcal of exercise.**
Their activity target is nonetheless the standard one — **150–300 min moderate or 75–150 min vigorous
per week**, with a **4–6 week ramp** for the currently inactive.

**The best evidence-reading move in the entire corpus sits here.** Baraki takes apart
**Jayedi 2024**, a meta-analysis on his own side of the argument, which reported 0.52 kg and 0.56 cm
of waist per 30 min/week of aerobic exercise — pointing out it is built mostly on trials of
**≤12 weeks** and that its own long-term entries show the effect fading, exactly the mechanism
(Riou 2015: shorter interventions show less compensation) that makes short-trial meta-analyses
over-read.

**The boundary condition is Low Energy Availability**, with the standard thresholds: **<30 kcal/kg
fat-free mass high risk, 30–45 moderate, >45 no risk**, and severe LEA a risk factor for RED-S. They
state plainly that both remain active research areas and link the LEAM-Q and LEAF-Q screening tools.

Diet method, from the 2012 archive and still the durable part of it:
[To Be A Beast](to-be-a-beast.md) argues nutrition should be programmed the way training is — **track
your intake for a week without changing it** to find where maintenance actually sits, then adjust in
small increments (**10–30 g carbohydrate, 4–10 g fat**) rather than 500 kcal jumps, reading weight,
waist and training performance together. Its "metabolic damage" section is the best clinical writing
in it: a proper differential — adaptive reduction in expenditure, loss of training novelty,
post-competition binge eating, disordered eating, hypothyroidism, medication effects, plain
non-compliance — with the diagnosis of choice almost always the last, and a stated BMR figure of
**~30 kcal per kilogram of body weight lost** that carries no source. He names the commercial
incentive too: being told you can lose weight while eating *more* is an attractive product, and
coaches sell it. What has not aged: **GOMAD** as default beginner advice (a gallon of whole milk a
day on top of three meals, **over 4,400 kcal**), the expectation of **20 lb of bodyweight in two
months**, and the gluten aside.

## Diet composition: what to eat, and which targets have a paper behind them

[How to eat a healthy diet](eat-healthy-diet.md) is their consolidated position, and it is best read
as a list sorted by citation strength.

**No named diet is superior.** From **Ge 2020**, a BMJ network meta-analysis of **14 popular named
diet programmes across 121 randomised trials**. Flagged: it compared **weight and cardiovascular risk
factors over roughly 6–12 months**; the article generalises the finding to "health or performance,"
which is broader than the paper.

**Fibre 25–30 g/day**, from Veronese 2018 (umbrella review) and the Reynolds 2019 *Lancet* series,
with the correct hedge that fibre "may not be entirely causal" and the benefit likely comes from the
whole pattern of fibre-rich foods. **Saturated fat ≤10% of calories** is recommended **with no
citation on the sentence** — and the one saturated-fat paper in the reference list (Li 2015) is never
cited anywhere in the article, an orphan reference sitting under an uncited recommendation.
**3 g/kg/day carbohydrate minimum for "performance-minded folks" — uncited.** The **entire
essential-fatty-acid-deficiency section — uncited.** The Twinkie Diet is n=1, uncontrolled and
uncited, and it illustrates rather than demonstrates. National-average intake context is solid, from
Shan 2019 (NHANES 1999–2016): **81 g protein / 252 g carbohydrate / 74 g fat**.

**On low-carbohydrate diets and lifting**, their position — do not use very low carbohydrate diets in
trained lifters chasing hypertrophy or strength, though it may be fine for beginners losing weight —
rests on **Burke 2017**, which is **21 elite race walkers over three weeks** showing impaired exercise
economy, plus **Paoli 2019**, a narrative review. So the endurance half has a study and the muscle and
strength half rests on a review. They do label it "based on the existing evidence." The same thin
support reappears in [training in a deficit](train-while-losing-weight.md). The 2012
[To Be A Beast](to-be-a-beast.md) leans the same way from the other side: it is openly a
**carbohydrate advocate for lifters**, on personal experience and client outcomes, and prefers
starting carbohydrate high specifically so there is room to cut later — a coaching argument, labelled
as one.

**Red meat** gets its own 9,300-word treatment in
[the science of red meat](science-red-meat-health.md), and it is a model of design labelling followed
by a lapse. **Every study linking red meat to colorectal cancer is observational**, and it says so
first. IARC puts colorectal risk up about **17% per 100 g/day of red meat** and **18% per 50 g/day of
processed meat**; against a US lifetime risk of ~**4.5%** (about **39 new cases per 100,000**) that
increment is roughly **7 extra cases per 100,000**, taking it to 46. It even reports the deflationary
consistency figure: only **7 of 14 cohorts and 7 of 15 case-control studies** found a positive
association for *unprocessed* red meat, against **12 of 18 cohorts and 6 of 9 case-control** for
processed — arguably the most decision-relevant finding in the section, reported in one sentence.
**Average North American and European intake (~70 g/day, 490 g/week) already sits inside the
guidelines** it supposedly violates, which reconciles most of the 2019 NutriRECS controversy.

Their actual dietary recommendation, from that article: calories set for body composition; **protein
1.6–3.1 g/kg/day**; **fibre ≥25–30 g/day**; **fat mostly unsaturated with saturated fat ≈10% of
calories or less**; **red meat 12–18 oz cooked per week** ("although we feel less strongly about this
if the other criteria are being met"); **processed red meat under 50 g/day**. **No product is sold in
that article** — unusual for the site.

The saturated-fat argument underneath it is their strongest piece of scientific writing, because it
separates the **lipid hypothesis** (apoB-containing lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic disease — 200+
studies, >2 million participants, 30+ RCTs, roughly **20% fewer events per 1 mmol/L (38 mg/dL) of LDL
lowering**, with Mendelian randomisation doing the causal work) from the **diet-heart hypothesis**
(cutting dietary saturated fat lowers those lipoproteins and therefore events — supported, but
conditional on baseline intake and on what replaces it). **What you replace it with decides whether
replacing it helps**: from Li 2015, swapping **5% of energy** from saturated fat for **PUFA** gives
~**25%** lower coronary risk, for **MUFA** ~**15%**, **10%** for complex carbohydrate ~**9%**, and
for **sugar or refined carbohydrate** **no significant change**. The Cochrane review of **15 RCTs**
found cutting saturated fat from high intakes to <10% of calories reduced cardiovascular events by
**17%**, with no evidence of harm. Calibration: **a 10-ounce ribeye carries about 28 g of saturated
fat**, roughly 10% of a 2,500-calorie day on its own; average US intake is ~**11% of calories**; and
**red meat is not in the top five US sources of saturated fat** (cheese, pizza, grain-based desserts,
dairy desserts and chicken dishes are) — a population-average argument used next to an individual
recommendation, and worth noticing as such.

**Fibre** gets the cleanest reframing in the set.
[The fibre guide](dietary-fiber-guide-viscosity-fermentability-gut-health.md) argues the
soluble/insoluble split is the wrong axis — lab solubility does not predict physiological effect — and
replaces it with **viscosity** and **fermentability**. **Viscous fibres** (beta-glucan in oats and
barley, psyllium husk) trap bile acids, forcing the liver to pull cholesterol from blood; Baraki says
raising viscous fibre is one of the most effective non-drug interventions for lipids in his practice,
offered as a clinical impression, not a trial result. **Fermentable fibres** produce short-chain fatty
acids, principally **butyrate**; fermentation *location* matters, because fast fermenters (FOS,
pectin) are consumed in the proximal colon, so the distal colon needs slow fermenters — resistant
starch from green bananas, legumes and cooked-then-cooled potato or rice, plus arabinoxylans in wheat
bran, rye and corn. **Target 30–40 g/day**, against a guideline floor of **14 g per 1,000 kcal**;
if you are at 10–15 g, ramp by **~5 g every 3–5 days**. Shopping heuristic: the **10:1
carbohydrate-to-fibre rule** — excellent is 5–7:1 (lentils, berries), good is 10:1 (oats, whole wheat
pasta), sub-optimal is 20:1 or worse (brown rice, granola bars, wheat bread). Label numbers are soft:
assays miss resistant starch and inulin without extra steps, ripening converts fibre to sugar, and
**raw dried onion carries ~18 g FOS per 100 g against about 3 g cooked** — which they turn into a
lever, since cooking cuts the fermentable load if raw onion bloats you. They are sceptical of isolated
added fibres on the grounds that the epidemiology is built on **intrinsic fibre from whole foods**.
Only **four external links across 2,229 words**; nearly every specific number here is uncited.

## Meal timing and frequency

Consistently ranked as a secondary parameter, which is the right ranking, and consistently asserted
rather than argued. [To Be A Beast](to-be-a-beast.md) gives **4–5 meals a day, 3 to 5 hours apart**,
with **25–35% of daily carbohydrate in the meals immediately before and after training** kept low in
fat to avoid slowing gastric transit — and **30–40%** around training for a higher-body-fat client.
The [seven rules](rules-optimize-protein-intake.md) derives **3–5 meals, 3–5 hours apart** from the
refractory-period model discussed above and explicitly rejects "eat every two hours to stoke the
metabolism," which is correct for the wrong reason. The later
[protein on Ozempic](protein-ozempic-much-need.md) note is the most honest: the reason given for
spreading protein is **behavioural** — people who eat protein often end up with a higher daily total —
"not that the mechanism pays." The one exception they carve out is **anabolic resistance**, attributed
to comorbidity and inactivity rather than to age itself, where **30–40 g per meal** matters more.
Practical GLP-1 tactics, all appetite-shaped: **front-load 40–50 g into the meal where appetite still
works** (usually breakfast), eat the protein on the plate first, keep a **20–40 g liquid backup** for
days when solids fail (cold tolerates better than warm, sipped better than gulped), pick lean
protein-dense sources because total calories are the binding constraint, and rotate out aversions —
commonest with chicken, eggs and dairy — for **two to four weeks** rather than persisting. Collagen is
ruled out as a protein source there, correctly, on muscle-protein-synthesis grounds.

## Supplements, one at a time

**Their general position argues against the category, in an article that then sells two of their
own.** [How to eat a healthy diet](eat-healthy-diet.md) reports that ~**20% of supplements are likely
contaminated and 30–50% mislabelled** (Mathews 2018; Tucker 2018), that most people need none, and
cites three negative outcome reviews: **Barbarawi 2019**, a meta-analysis of **21 RCTs in more than
83,000 people** finding no cardiovascular benefit from **vitamin D**; the AHA science advisory on
**fish oil**; and the USPSTF evidence report on **vitamins and minerals** for primary prevention.
Protein powder is the carved-out exception, cited to Pasiakos 2015 and framed narrowly as useful for
people who would not otherwise hit their target. Then in roughly 5,000 words the reader is directed to
their whey product page twice, their Peri-Rx supplement once (**a broken link — it points at the whey
page**), and their coaching service four times.

**Whey / protein powder — they sell it, and the criteria they give are usable against any brand.**
From the [protein and weight loss](protein-weight-loss-maximizing-fat-loss-preserving.md) guide:
**cGMP manufacture, third-party testing (NSF, Informed Choice or USP), under ~120 kcal and ~20 g
protein per serving, no amino-spiking with glutamine, creatine, taurine or glycine** — and then their
own **WheyRx isolate** named as meeting them at **20–21 g protein and 90 kcal**. WheyRx also appears
mid-paragraph in the [protein guide](barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md), at the close of Rule 6 in the
[2014 rules](rules-optimize-protein-intake.md) straight after whey is asserted superior to every other
source, twice in [protein on Ozempic](protein-ozempic-much-need.md), and via the "whey protein
isolate" link in [collagen protein](collagen-protein-work.md). Their own price heuristic, from 2014:
**ignore anything over ~$20–25/lb**, and use concentrate unless it upsets your gut.

**Creatine — they sell it, and their read is the most restrained in the library.**
[The creatine article](creatine-benefits-side-effects-safe-consumption.md) cites **Burke 2023**, the
direct-measurement meta-analysis, and reports it as "a couple of pounds or so in a year." That paper
pooled ten studies using **site-specific ultrasound and MRI**, found **0.10–0.16 cm of muscle
thickness** across triceps, elbow flexors, quadriceps and hamstrings, a pooled **effect size of
0.11**, and its own authors called the practical significance possibly minimal. **Feigenbaum reports
neither the effect size nor that verdict, and converts a regional thickness result into a whole-body
mass unit the paper never measured** — the article's one substantive omission. Everything else is
conservative: effects called "modest" and "small to moderate," the ISSN 2017 stand cited **only for
dosing** rather than for its "most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement available" line,
agnostic on endurance, explicitly unconvinced on cognition. **Performance** is the stronger claim:
~**5–15%** improvement in maximal power and reps-to-failure at a fixed load **in responders**, smaller
gains in 1RM and sprint; older adults appear to gain more strength. Mechanism is textbook and hedged:
**95%** of body creatine sits in skeletal muscle, phosphocreatine is exhausted in **10–15 seconds** of
intense work and fully restored in about eight minutes, and supplementation raises muscle total
creatine and phosphocreatine by **10–40%**. **Dosing: loading 20–25 g/day (0.3 g/kg) split across the
day for 5–7 days; maintenance 3–5 g/day (0.05–0.07 g/kg)**; after about a month the two routes
converge, so loading is only worth it inside that window and is a bad idea if you are weight-sensitive
or have had GI trouble. **Monohydrate, ideally micronised, third-party tested** — alternative forms
have no evidence of superiority. The responder literature is **one study of 11 men** (27% responders,
45% quasi, 27% non-responders), and he says outright that we do not know the population proportions —
more cautious than the confident "20–30% non-responders" quoted elsewhere from the same source.

The best clinical material is about **creatinine**, not creatine: supplementation raises the serum
marker clinicians use to estimate kidney function, so a lab panel read in isolation can look like
renal impairment in someone with entirely normal kidneys. **Flagged**: the peaks he reports —
**~2.5× baseline on monohydrate, ~6.7× on ethyl ester** — are sourced to **unpublished personal
communication** and should not be quoted downstream as data. Honest gaps: **no safety data in children
or adolescents, none in pregnancy** — note the ISSN takes the opposite line on adolescents, arguing
the under-18 warnings are legal cover rather than science, so this is a clinical recommendation under
uncertainty and they frame it as one. The **hair-loss** deconstruction is good: the entire scare
traces to one **21-day study in college rugby players** that measured DHT, not hair, where the
creatine group started **over 20% below** placebo, so the "significant increase" is substantially
regression toward the placebo baseline; never replicated, no long-term hair outcome study exists.
**Flagged internal contradiction**: the summary bullets assert creatine "does not cause water
retention, weight gain, hair loss, or an increase in body fat," while the body twice acknowledges
early weight gain.

[Creatine on Ozempic](creatine-ozempic-prevent-muscle-loss.md) argues against its own shelf. **No
trial has tested creatine during GLP-1 therapy**, and the article says so in those terms. The effect
is **mediated entirely by training**: creatine plus resistance training beats training alone by
roughly **1–2 kg of lean mass and 5–10% strength over 8–12 weeks**, and about **1 kg over 10–12 weeks**
in older adults; **creatine without training does close to nothing for body composition**. It takes
the marketing syllogism apart link by link — the muscle loss is overstated because DXA "lean mass"
includes water, glycogen, organs and connective tissue, and the **SURPASS-3 MRI substudy** (a post-hoc
analysis of an open-label phase 3 trial) showed a substantial fraction of tirzepatide "lean mass" loss
was **intramuscular fat**; creatine is not standalone; and nothing about the mechanism is
GLP-1-specific. **Dose 3–5 g/day (0.03–0.05 g/kg), any time, skip loading**, and the **1–2 kg scale
increase in the first weeks is intramuscular water**. It names gendered creatine gummies and drink
mixes as a "pink tax" on inferior forms at low doses. On cognition it is appropriately uncertain —
one 2024 crossover found improved reaction time and working memory after sleep restriction with a
single large dose, and their verdict is "a possible secondary upside if you already take it, not a
reason to start." Minor overreach: "is creatine safe with GLP-1 medications?" is answered with a flat
**yes**, reasonable inference from an absence of any known mechanism, but inference — there is no
interaction study.

**Collagen — they could stock it, and they argue the whole category down. This is the most credible
supplement writing in the corpus.** [Collagen protein](collagen-protein-work.md) gives the arithmetic:
**PDCAAS 0**, because collagen contains no tryptophan and the score is set by the limiting amino acid,
against **1.00** for whey; ~**80% protein by weight** against >90% for whey isolate; **BCAA content
roughly half** whey's. The mechanistic objection is the real one — **glycine and proline are
non-essential, dietary deficiency is rare, and amino acids do not choose where they are used**. On
muscle: **30 g collagen did not stimulate muscle protein synthesis where 30 g whey did**
(Aussieker 2023); a meta-analysis of **19 RCTs** (Bischof 2024) found collagen beat *no extra protein*
and lost to whey even with leucine added — the "any protein beats no protein" result, not a collagen
result. On tendon: **Balshaw 2023, 15 g/day for 15 weeks**, found no patellar tendon or functional
difference. On joints: **Benito-Ruiz 2009, n=250**, reported a **4.7 mm** advantage on a 100 mm visual
analogue pain scale against a minimal clinically important difference of **13.7 mm** — statistically
significant, clinically irrelevant, and that distinction is the article's sharpest move. Trč 2011
compared collagen to glucosamine **with no placebo arm**, which the article flags itself. The
Creutzfeldt-Jakob item is a **single case report published as a conference abstract**, immediately
contextualised with an EFSA assessment putting the risk at exceptionally rare — the right handling of
a scary-sounding, near-zero-probability finding.

[Collagen and tendinopathy](collagen-protein-tendinopathy.md) dissects the one trial in detail.
**Praet 2019: n=20** with mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy, **double-blind placebo-controlled
crossover, 2 × 3 months, 2.5 g hydrolysed collagen peptides twice daily versus 2.5 g maltodextrin**,
on top of **2 × 90 eccentric calf raises daily for six months, performed despite pain**. Raw VISA-A
moved **58.5 → 60.8** on collagen and **55.9 → 62.8** on placebo, against an **MCID of 6.5 points** —
**only placebo cleared it**. Three observations do the real work: the **crossover-order artefact** (the
placebo-first sequence did better overall, the opposite of what a real collagen effect predicts),
**unblinding** (**60% and 55%** correctly identified collagen, only **40% and 44%** identified
placebo), and the **maltodextrin control**, which cannot separate "collagen" from "any additional
dietary protein." Tendon vascularity fell in both arms with **no between-group difference at any time
point**. Context: a 2018 meta-analysis of **69 studies** found no supplement with a clinically
important long-term effect on joint pain. **One correction to their critique**: Feigenbaum treats the
linear mixed model as a rescue manoeuvre, and mixed models are the standard handling of repeated
measures with missing data in a crossover — his conclusion survives, because the order effect and the
blinding failure do the work, but that specific dismissal is not sound methodological criticism. **The
qualification on the credit**: whey is the comparator they favour, and they sell whey. The argument is
not "supplements don't work," it is "this supplement is worse than the one we stock." The reasoning
does not depend on it, but a reader should see the shape.

The one place their scepticism outruns the evidence is **skin**: a **26-RCT, >1,700-patient
meta-analysis** reported positive effects, and the article dismisses it for unspecified methodological
flaws and concludes there is "no need" — absence of confident evidence reported as evidence of
absence.

## Food quality and processing

[The modern food environment](modern-food-environment-makes-overeating-default.md) is the anchor. US
adult obesity went from ~**15% in the late 1970s to >40%** without a change in the genome or the
population's character, so **the explanatory variable is the environment**. Three changes did the
work — bigger portions, more restaurant and delivery meals, more ultra-processed food — and each
raises intake without a decision being made. **Doubling the portion in front of someone raises intake
~35%** (Zlatevska 2014, meta-analysis; Cochrane agrees). Average US daily intake rose ~**570 kcal per
person between 1977 and 2006**. Marketplace portions against federal serving standards: **a typical
cookie exceeds the standard by 700%, cooked pasta by 480%, a steak by 224%**. Rolls' chip-bag
experiment is the mechanistic punchline — bigger bag, more chips, and **the same dinner three hours
later**, no compensation. Calories from food prepared outside the home have roughly doubled, and
**more than six in ten adults** eat restaurant or fast food on a given day.

**The load-bearing experiment is Hall 2019**, and it appears in two of these notes. **20 adults,
inpatient, crossover, two weeks per arm, menus matched for calories *offered* plus protein,
carbohydrate, fat, sugar, salt and fibre, ad libitum intake**; people ate roughly **500 kcal/day
more** and gained about **0.9 kg** on the ultra-processed arm versus losing about 0.9 kg on the
unprocessed arm. Enormous effect, **n=20 over four weeks total**. Two caveats the article does not
raise: the ultra-processed menu was more **energy dense** in practice, and subsequent analyses
attribute much of the effect to energy density and eating rate specifically. Notably,
[how to eat a healthy diet](eat-healthy-diet.md) cites the same trial as its strongest causal evidence
and **gives none of its numbers**. The mechanism offered is the **food matrix**: a whole food's fibre,
water, structure and phytochemicals act together to slow eating and trigger fullness signals, and
pulling one component out and dosing it alone loses most of the effect. Adding fibre or vitamins back
does not rebuild it.

**Their best move is against their own side.** The BMJ umbrella review pooling nearly **ten million
people** reports ~**50% higher cardiovascular mortality, ~55% higher odds of obesity, 12% higher type
2 diabetes risk per 10% increase in intake, ~22% higher depression risk** — and the article
immediately says the review itself grades that **low to very-low certainty**, and that the most likely
causal path is simply that these foods make you eat more. They call **NOVA a blunt instrument** ("a
bit like saying a bicycle and a 747 are the same because both contain metal" — whey isolate and an
Oreo land in the same bin), and give **seed oils the opposite of the popular verdict**, citing the
largest pooled biomarker analysis linking dietary omega-6 to **lower** cardiovascular risk. The Red
Dye 40 claim in the same sentence carries **no citation of its own**. On food addiction — the Yale
Food Addiction Scale puts roughly **14% of adults and 12% of children** over the clinical bar — he
states his position rather than hiding it: the mechanism is an environment that overrides stop
signals, not a chemical hook.

**The screen they propose is not "processed or not" but does this food defend or defeat your
fullness.** Practical rules, all clinical judgement with no citations, which is fine as long as it is
understood: decide once at the shop rather than every evening; stock for satiety per calorie — their
packaged-food heuristic is **≥30% of calories from protein, or ≥1 g fibre per 10 g carbohydrate**, an
invented threshold, not a validated one; cook at home more; use friction deliberately (opaque
containers, high cupboards, high-satiety food at eye level); eat without a screen. And if appetite
signalling still fails, **that is a medical problem with medical tools — GLP-1s, CBT and metabolic
bariatric surgery all act on the same appetite machinery, and using them is not cheating.** Both this
article and the [weight-regain one](weight-regain-not-failure-willpower.md) close by selling coaching
or consultations, and the recommendation to "treat the physiology" routes to a consultation they sell.

One caution on the industrial-history section: the R.J. Reynolds/Nabisco and Philip Morris/General
Foods acquisitions, the "bliss point," the 1999 Pillsbury meeting and the Sanger quote all trace to
**one piece of journalism** (Moss 2013). Good journalism; still four separate factual claims on a
single non-peer-reviewed source. The checkout-lane audit beside it is independent and properly cited —
~**70%** of food and drink at California store checkouts was unhealthy, rising to ~**89%** among
snack-sized packages.

## Hydration and sodium

Hydration guidance comes from
[working out but gaining weight](reasons-re-working-out-but-gaining-weight.md) and is presented as
clinical advice rather than trial findings. **Establish a euhydrated reference by weighing first thing
on three consecutive mornings**, after the bathroom and before eating or drinking. **Start workouts
within 1% of that; avoid losing more than 2% during.** Do nothing special afterwards unless training
twice a day, in which case replace **~1.5 L per kilogram lost, at 0.4–1.5 L/hour**. The evidence
underneath is that hypohydration of ~2% or more impairs sweat rate, sweat onset and performance, plus
the NATA fluid-replacement position statement.

Sodium is the thinnest article in the set. [A word on salt](newsletter-word-salt.md) is **712 words
with zero references**. Its position: **WHO recommends 2,000–2,400 mg sodium/day** and the **AHA under
2,000**, but the **Institute of Medicine's 2013 review** found the evidence for going below
**2,300 mg/day** "insufficient and inconsistent" for cardiovascular or all-cause mortality benefit in
the general US population — so they land on a **sweet spot around 3–6 g/day**, with the sodium–event
association confined to hypertensive people, the elderly and very high consumers, and a
**paradoxically higher event rate below 3 g/day**. US average intake is given as ~**3,400 mg
sodium/day**. Athletes are a separate case: **sodium losses from vigorous exercise can exceed
17 g/day**, sports drinks dilute rather than replace (he cites two high-school football players who
died of hyponatremia after drinking large volumes of Gatorade), and the ACSM starting figure they
endorse is **500–700 mg sodium per litre of fluid, pre- and post-workout**, taken from food (bouillon,
salting meals) rather than pills. **The exclusions come before the recommendation** — salt-sensitive
hypertensives, congestive heart failure and other cardiovascular disease are told the argument does
not apply — which is what separates it from the usual genre. **Nothing is sold here.**

**Two failures in it.** "**Sea salt has about 10% as much sodium as table salt**" is wrong by roughly
tenfold — both are predominantly sodium chloride by mass, and crystal size changes sodium per
*teaspoon*, not per gram. And "most who train seriously will become hyponatremic after training" is an
unhedged prevalence claim with nothing behind it. The underlying logic also inverts: an absence of
evidence for *restriction* is not evidence for *supplementation*, though he does label the add-salt
conclusion as "my main takeaway."

## How this source reads evidence

**When they are good, they are the best source in this library at design labelling.**
[The red-meat article](science-red-meat-health.md) spends a full section on cohort versus case-control
design *before* touching the meat data; [training in a deficit](train-while-losing-weight.md)
dismantles a meta-analysis that supports its own conclusion;
[collagen and tendinopathy](collagen-protein-tendinopathy.md) reads a crossover's order effects and
blinding failure rather than its headline;
[the food environment](modern-food-environment-makes-overeating-default.md) reports its strongest
epidemiological numbers and then quotes the review's own low-certainty grade back at itself;
[the creatine article](creatine-benefits-side-effects-safe-consumption.md) declines to repeat the
ISSN's superlative about a product it sells.

**Their explicit methodological position** is that GRADE, built in the early 2000s largely for
pharmaceutical evidence, initialises certainty as *low* when the evidence base is mostly
observational, which guarantees a low-certainty verdict on any diet–chronic disease question because
long randomised feeding trials are impractical and often unethical. Their alternative is to weigh
**multiple converging lines of evidence** — consistency across populations plus mechanism. The best
supporting example they give: **trans fats were banned in the United States in 2019 without a
randomised controlled trial**, on epidemiology showing roughly a **23% increase in ASCVD risk per 2%
of calories from trans fat**. That is a serious argument, and it is also a licence they then use
selectively.

**The failure mode is consistent: they label design meticulously in the section they are criticising
and casually in the section they are defending.** Li 2015's substitution estimates are presented as
"the data suggest" without noting they are **substitution models inside a prospective cohort** — the
least certain numbers in the piece, delivered with the most confidence, immediately after the most
careful design labelling in the corpus. The protein articles do the same at whole-article scale:
[the weight-loss guide's](protein-weight-loss-maximizing-fat-loss-preserving.md) summary uses "may,"
"likely" and then **"definitely"** for the muscle claim — the one claim in the article with **no
resistance-training trial attached to any sentence**. Its satiety section, by contrast, is the model
of the good version: three proposed mechanisms tested against outcomes rather than plausibility, with
the **aminostatic theory rejected** (fasting amino acids do not track appetite) and
**gluconeogenesis rejected** (the supporting paper, Azzout-Marniche 2007, is **a rat study** the
article does not label), leaving only a partial gut-hormone route that does not reliably translate
into eating less. **Wycherley 2012 — 24 RCTs, ~1,000 participants, ~12 weeks** — still holds the
outcome without the mechanism: higher-protein arms ate ~**300 kcal/day less**, lost **0.8 kg more
weight, mostly fat**, and retained **0.5 kg more lean mass**. Their preferred explanation is **food
displacement** rather than satiety, and they label it a hypothesis, which is right — none of the work
they cite tests it.

**The one evidence-and-method note that is not about nutrition belongs here anyway.**
[The science of training motivation](science-training-motivation.md) reports its featured study
properly rather than by headline: **Rodrigues 2020, 575 gym members, six months of objective turnstile
attendance data**, where **enjoyment had the largest indirect effect on persistence (β=0.83)** and
autonomous motivation the largest effect on enjoyment (β=0.85); **472 of 575 (82%) were still training
at six months**, at 2.89 days/week against a self-reported baseline of 3.52. Miles states the design
limits himself — a cross-sectional survey with a prospective behavioural outcome and a mediation
model, in a sample that had **already** chosen to join a gym and train for six months. It matters for
nutrition because it is the conclusion the diet articles keep arriving at from the other direction:
**adherence is the binding constraint, and a plan you enjoy beats a better plan you abandon** — which
is exactly the argument [To Be A Beast](to-be-a-beast.md) makes when it recommends fasted walking on
adherence grounds while conceding the fasted-versus-fed distinction does not do what tradition claims.
The motivation article's clearest overreach has the same shape as the nutrition ones: a causal claim
about population inactivity resting on implicit-association studies that never measured attendance. It
also contains the corpus's most candid line about their own trade — exercise as a whole does not
produce large effect sizes for treating many conditions. **Nothing is sold in it.**

## Weak points

- **The protein numbers do not reconcile, and the lowest one is indefensible.** Four targets from one
  publisher spanning better than two-fold, with **1.4–1.6 g/kg** sitting below the **1.62 g/kg**
  breakpoint from the meta-analysis they cite themselves, anchored to a **general-population dietary
  guideline** rather than any deficit trial. In the article recommending it, the two references that
  argue for higher intakes are printed and never used. Take **1.6–1.8 g/kg** and ignore the spread.
- **The satiety verdict clashes with the ISSN and never addresses the counter-evidence.** They
  conclude "we cannot yet confidently say that more protein = more satiety"; the ISSN diets stand
  calls protein the most satiating macronutrient, citing Weigle 2005, where raising protein from 15%
  to 30% of energy ad libitum cut intake by **441 kcal/day** and produced **−4.9 kg in 12 weeks**.
  Barbell Medicine never mention Weigle. The positions are reconcilable — a real short-run effect that
  decays, which is what de Carvalho 2020 reports across **10 studies in over 1,000 adults** — but they
  are not the same claim.
- **A resting-energy-expenditure figure that cannot be right.** The weight-loss guide reports REE
  "about 300 Calories/day higher in the high protein group," attributed to retained muscle. At
  **13 kcal/kg/day** for skeletal muscle, Wycherley's extra **0.5 kg** of lean mass is worth roughly
  **6 kcal/day**, not 300.
- **Uncited numbers in cited articles.** The 3.1 g/kg physique-athlete figure, the 3 g/kg carbohydrate
  minimum, the ≤10% saturated fat target and the entire essential-fatty-acid section in
  [eat-healthy-diet](eat-healthy-diet.md); the 25–40 g per-meal band; the DNP section in
  [working out but gaining weight](reasons-re-working-out-but-gaining-weight.md); the "DXA overstates
  lean loss" claim in [weight regain](weight-regain-not-failure-willpower.md); the Red Dye 40 claim in
  [the food environment](modern-food-environment-makes-overeating-default.md); the National Weight
  Control Registry figures in [does your metabolism change](metabolism-change-weight-loss.md); nearly
  every specific figure in [the fibre guide](dietary-fiber-guide-viscosity-fermentability-gut-health.md).
- **Four articles cite nothing at all.** [To Be A Beast](to-be-a-beast.md) (by the author's stated
  choice), the [seven protein rules](rules-optimize-protein-intake.md),
  [protein slows digestion? Nope](protein-slows-digestion-nope.md) and
  [the salt newsletter](newsletter-word-salt.md). Three of the four make claims this library
  independently endorses; none of them is the evidence for those claims.
- **Outright mis-citations.** Reference [17] in
  [working out but gaining weight](reasons-re-working-out-but-gaining-weight.md), inside the block
  supporting compensatory energy expenditure, is **Labbok 1997, a study of the Lactational Amenorrhea
  Method as contraception**. Reference [27] in the same article cites **Heymsfield 2014** for the
  75/25 weight-loss composition rule — a paper whose title is *a critical review and critique of this
  widely cited rule*. In [weight regain](weight-regain-not-failure-willpower.md) the US obesity
  prevalence sentence is marked to **Speakman's set-point theory paper**, while the NCHS prevalence
  report that plainly contains the data is never cited anywhere. In the
  [protein guide](barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md) the Groen 2015 hyperlink points at the
  **Leenders 2011** leucine paper, and the RDA is given as "0.8–1.0 g/kg" when the US RDA is 0.8. In
  [eat-healthy-diet](eat-healthy-diet.md) the appetite-regulation claim is partly supported by
  **Westbury 2023, a review of obesity stigma**, and the Peri-Rx product link points at the whey page.
- **Internal contradictions left standing.** [Training in a deficit](train-while-losing-weight.md)
  says "no special training modifications are necessary" and then says volume-led hypertrophy training
  "can be helpful with maintaining muscle mass in a deficit" — with no trial comparing modified against
  unmodified programming cited either way. The
  [creatine article](creatine-benefits-side-effects-safe-consumption.md) summary denies water
  retention and weight gain its own body twice concedes. The
  [collagen and tendinopathy](collagen-protein-tendinopathy.md) key points say "some low-quality
  evidence suggesting collagen may improve tendinopathy" while the body concludes the effect is very
  small or non-existent. [To Be A Beast](to-be-a-beast.md) prescribes 2.4–2.8 g/kg protein and argues
  against high protein on the same page.
- **Unpublished sources carrying striking numbers.** The **2.5× and 6.7× serum creatinine peaks** rest
  on personal communication. The entire GLP-1 protein-gap article rests on **one conference abstract**
  — 116 users, 5,741 app-logged days, self-reported, commercial dataset, presented in 2026 and
  unpublished — and **every intake figure in it traces there**, including the headline
  **0.6 g/kg/day**.
- **Two record-keeping artefacts.** [eat-healthy-diet](eat-healthy-diet.md) carries the wrong title in
  its stored transcript header ("Understanding Heart Rate Variability for Strength Training"); the URL,
  body and references are all the healthy-diet article. The
  [fibre guide](dietary-fiber-guide-viscosity-fermentability-gut-health.md) transcript is truncated
  mid-sentence in its FAQ.
- **Where the commercial layer touches the recommendation.** WheyRx appears in the sentence carrying
  the recommendation in several articles; the supplement-quality section reporting **~20%
  contamination and 30–50% mislabelling** is the section immediately preceding the product link in
  both [the creatine article](creatine-benefits-side-effects-safe-consumption.md) and
  [eat-healthy-diet](eat-healthy-diet.md); the "you don't need to change your programme" conclusion in
  [training in a deficit](train-while-losing-weight.md) sits directly above a catalogue of **six paid
  template lines**. Against that: [the science of red meat](science-red-meat-health.md),
  [protein slows digestion](protein-slows-digestion-nope.md),
  [the salt newsletter](newsletter-word-salt.md) and
  [training motivation](science-training-motivation.md) sell nothing at all; both collagen articles
  and [creatine on Ozempic](creatine-ozempic-prevent-muscle-loss.md) argue against categories they
  profit from; Recommendation 4 of the [protein guide](barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md) says intakes
  to 4.4 g/kg improve nothing; and
  [the protein and weight loss guide](protein-weight-loss-maximizing-fat-loss-preserving.md) spends
  8,500 words concluding that the flagship satiety mechanism for its own whey does not hold up.
  **That is what makes the positions credible where they are credible.**
