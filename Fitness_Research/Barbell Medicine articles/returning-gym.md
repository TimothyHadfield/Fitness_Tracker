# Coming Back After a Layoff: Undershoot on Purpose

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Returning to the Gym" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/returning-to-the-gym/)) · Tom Campitelli · 2020-07-24 · 2,023 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The central claim: **a deliberately easy re-introduction is the fastest route back**, and the first session after a layoff is neither a test nor a foundation stone. Written for the COVID gym reopenings but not specific to them.
- The mechanism offered is behavioural, not physiological: overshooting produces heavy DOMS, which reduces the odds you turn up for the next few sessions. **Frequency early on matters more than the quality of any one workout.**
- **Autoregulation** is the tool — select load by target effort (RPE) rather than by remembered numbers. Adjust down when warm-ups feel heavy; adjust up when the first working set is trivially easy. Both directions count.
- The three-week sample program caps everything at **RPE 8** and never asks for a grind. Volume and intensity ramp week to week: 3 sets in week 1, one added top set in week 2, two added top sets in week 3.
- **Variety is programmed in deliberately** — paused squats, RDLs, split squats, close-grip bench alongside the competition lifts — justified as reducing overuse-injury risk and broadening the adaptation base before any later specialisation.
- **The article states plainly that its recommendations "are not based on direct evidence from the literature."** That honesty is the note's most important content: this is coaching heuristic, and it is labelled as such by the author.

## The autoregulation argument

Campitelli's framing is unusually plain about what autoregulation is *for*. It is not a sophistication; it is a way of hitting an intended stimulus when your capacity on the day is unknown — which is exactly the situation after a layoff, when the number you remember is no longer the number you can do.

Two worked scenarios are given. First: you plan to return to what you lifted three months ago, the warm-ups feel unexpectedly heavy, so you abandon the plan and pick something "mildly annoying" instead. Second: your conservative opener turns out to be far too light, so you add weight. He is explicit that the second case is autoregulation too — the concept is symmetric, and people who only ever use it to cut loads are using half of it.

The bias he recommends for the first couple of sessions is **undershooting**, and the stated reason is soreness management rather than tissue protection. Unaccustomed activity produces DOMS; enough DOMS and you skip sessions. He also gives the follow-up instruction that matters more than the first: **if you are unusually sore, still go back on schedule**, and moderate substantially within the session — different exercises, less load, less volume — rather than skipping it. Building the habit outranks the content of any single session.

The claim that "easy can be more effective than difficult" is presented as paradoxical and then resolved through fatigue management: appropriate doses leave manageable fatigue, which permits more successful subsequent sessions and higher motivation. This is a reasonable account and it is consistent with fatigue-management arguments elsewhere in this library, but **no study is offered for it here**.

## The RPE definition they use

Worth recording because it is the house scale used across Barbell Medicine's material: a 0–10 rating where numbers below 5 are rarely prescribed; **10 = maximal or near-maximal**; **9 = one more rep possible**; **8 = clearly submaximal, two more reps possible**. Explicitly related to Reps in Reserve. He adds that precision is not required — "make some ballpark observations, and adjust accordingly."

## The three-week program

Three lifting days per week plus two conditioning sessions, all barbell-based because of the audience.

**Day 1** squat (4s) / press (6s) / Romanian deadlift (8s).
**Day 2** bench press (4s) / paused squat (8s) / barbell row (10s).
**Day 3** deadlift (4s) / close-grip bench (8s) / split squat (10s per leg).

The loading progression is the whole design:

- **Week 1** — three ascending sets per exercise at RPE 5, 6, 7. Conditioning: 2× 20 min steady state at RPE 6, accumulated in 5–20 minute bouts.
- **Week 2** — RPE 6, 7, 8, with one repeat set at RPE 8 on most exercises. Conditioning: 2× 25 min.
- **Week 3** — RPE 6, 7, 8, with **two** repeat sets at RPE 8 on the main lifts and one on the accessories. Conditioning: 2× 30 min.

So the intensity ceiling rises once, in week 2, and then holds; everything after that is added volume at the ceiling. Nothing goes above RPE 8 in three weeks, and he gives the reason directly — there is no convincing case for grinding near-maximal efforts this early.

He also notes the program is one example, open to substitution, and that sticking to familiar movements has benefits after a layoff — while adding that this is a decent moment to learn variants, provided new movements get the same conservative loading treatment.

## Evidence versus recommendation, and what they sell

**There is no evidence section, because there is no evidence cited.** The article carries zero references, zero inline study links and no numbered markers. What makes it worth keeping in the library is that the author flags this himself for the layoff-duration table — "realize these are not based on direct evidence from the literature" — which is a level of candour rare in programming content. Every claim in the piece is coaching judgement: the DOMS-to-adherence chain, the ramping structure, the RPE 8 cap, the variety-reduces-overuse-injury rationale.

**Products recommended in the text:** the Barbell Medicine **Beginner Prescription** is recommended for anyone off training a long time, and the program itself is lifted from the **May 2020 Barbell Medicine newsletter**, which the article encourages you to subscribe to. The Beginner Prescription is Barbell Medicine's own free blog programme rather than a paid template, but the recommendation is still to their own material.

**Nothing here is an exercise ranking**, and no hypertrophy or activation data appears — the article makes no muscle-growth claims of any kind.
