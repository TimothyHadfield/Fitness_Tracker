# Specificity Cuts Both Ways: Why the SAID Principle Has an Off-Switch

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Pendulum of Specificity in Application: Part I" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-pendulum-of-specifity-in-application-part-i/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2018-10-06 · 3,299 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Specificity is not one property but three, following Reilly 2009 [1]: **energy system, muscle group, and skill**. Two exercises can match on one and diverge on another, which is why "is this specific?" is usually the wrong question.
- **The muscle-group component is finer-grained than most programming treats it.** Contraction type, velocity, joint angle and muscle length are all axes of specificity, not just "which muscle" [2,3].
- **Quarter squats can beat full squats — for the right test.** Rhea 2016 [4], 28 subjects, 16 weeks, three depth groups: strength improved at the depth trained, and quarter squatting transferred best to vertical jump and 40-yard sprint. Feigenbaum calls the finding "heretical" and publishes it anyway.
- **The article's actual argument is against specificity, not for it.** Push SAID to its logical end and a golfer should never squat. Feigenbaum's counter is that there is an **inflection point** past which more technical practice stops paying and general strength work — which *violates* SAID — becomes the better return. This is his reasoning, not a cited finding, and he is explicit about that.
- **The Repeated Bout Effect [6] is the setup for the sequel.** The same lifter doing the same squat at the same intensity accrues less damage and recovers faster over time — which is the mechanism by which a fixed programme stops working. The article ends on that question deliberately.
- **This is a 2018 rewrite of a 2014 post**, with the original text quoted in italics and the updated commentary in plain type. That formatting is a genuine transparency device: you can read what he changed his mind about.

## The three components, and where the granularity matters

Reilly 2009 [1] supplies the definition the article builds on: a training programme must stress the systems engaged in the target activity, across energy system, muscle group and skill. The energy-system review is textbook — phosphocreatine (3–10 s, maximal force), anaerobic glycolysis (10–120 s), aerobic oxidation (above ~2 min) — with the sensible caveat that all three contribute to everything and the time domains describe relative share, not exclusive use.

The muscle-group section is where the article does work. Specificity does not stop at naming the muscle; it extends to **contraction type, velocity, and joint angle / muscle length** [2,3] — Behm & Sale's velocity-specificity review and Aagaard 1996 on isokinetic knee strength. Three worked examples follow.

**Eccentric training** produces its largest gains in eccentric strength, with structural changes described as increased titin and costamere protein, and comparatively less transfer to concentric and isometric force [8–11]. The worked case: most people have **20–30% more eccentric than concentric strength** [12], so a lifter doing eccentric-only singles at their concentric 1RM is working at roughly **80% of eccentric 1RM**. Some transfer is expected, more so if they also train the full lift — which he uses to argue for weight releasers in context.

**Reduced range of motion** produces its largest gains at the joint angles trained. If the goal is a bigger full squat, half squats will underdeliver; if the goal is a vertical jump, the half-squat joint angles may be the more relevant ones, and Rhea 2016 [4] is the evidence. This is the same finding the library's [range of motion note](range-motion-considerations.md) records, from the same paper, in a different Barbell Medicine article — the two are consistent.

**Velocity** limits transfer even when everything else matches. The power clean and the deadlift share energy system, muscles and (for the first two pulls) joint angles and ROM, but differ in velocity — and the article expects markedly less carryover than from another deadlift variation, in both directions. That last part matters: he says the deadlift-to-clean carryover is *also* overstated, which cuts against a common weightlifting talking point.

## The SAID principle, and the argument for breaking it

SAID — Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demands — is presented as the logical extension of specificity, and then partly dismantled. The pro-specificity half is straightforward and applies mainly to competitive lifters: a powerlifter's test is a 1RM, so time spent driving a 5RM is time spent on the wrong variable. His sharper version is that a 5RM going 500 → 520 in six weeks tells you very little about the 1RM without RPE, bar speed or some measure of effort — an epistemic point about what an untracked performance number actually shows, not just a programming preference.

The anti-specificity half is the article's real thesis, and it is the more useful one:

- **Hyperspecialisation has costs.** Early sport specialisation in children is cited as producing worse outcomes when it eventually matters [5] — Jayanthi 2013's evidence-based recommendations on youth specialisation.
- **The golfer case.** Strict SAID says strength training is not specific to golf. Feigenbaum's reply is that increasing force production in the muscles used for a shot gives the golfer *more options* — the ability to hit further under the ball out of sand, to go long into a headwind — applied through motor patterns already refined by years of golf practice. He is careful not to claim the golfer's absolute strength requirements resemble a powerlifter's.
- **The inflection point.** Every sport has a point beyond which more technical practice yields less than general physical development, and past that point the highest-return training is the training that violates SAID.

**This is reasoning, not evidence.** No trial is cited for the golf claim, the inflection point, or the return-on-investment framing, and the article does not present them as findings — the language is "in my view," "I would argue." That distinction is kept cleanly, which is more than most programming writing manages.

## The Repeated Bout Effect and the question the article ends on

Repeated exposure to a movement reduces the muscle damage that movement causes [6] (Hyldahl 2017 on RBE mechanisms). The consequence for programming: a lifter squatting the same variation, reps and average intensity accrues progressively less damage — and therefore less stress and less fatigue — unless volume, intensity, frequency or total work change.

RBE also transfers between similar movements, evidenced by Zourdos 2015 [7] showing the effect across different curl variations. From there the article extrapolates: **carryover scales with similarity**, so a low-bar squatter gets some RBE protection when high-bar squatting and more from a paused low-bar squat. He marks this as his own reading — "as I understand it currently" — and it is an inference from one curl study, not a demonstrated gradient.

The closing question is the setup for Part II: what happens to the rate of improvement in a lifter whose training stress is falling while recovery capacity rises? The implied answer is that a static programme stops producing adaptation not because the lifter is doing anything wrong but because the same input has become a smaller stimulus.

**Flagged, twice.** The "20–30% more eccentric than concentric strength" figure is attributed to Munger 2017 [12], an acute study of eccentric overload on concentric front squat performance — a reasonable source for the phenomenon existing, thin for a population-level percentage. And the block citation [8–11] for eccentric-training structural adaptations includes Ervasti 2003, a costamere biology review in *J Biol Chem*, which describes what costameres are rather than showing that eccentric training enlarges them. The mechanism is plausible; the citations are of muscle biology, not of training adaptation.
