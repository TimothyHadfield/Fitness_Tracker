# Six Rear Delt Exercises Ranked Entirely on EMG — And the Article Says So Itself

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The 6 Best Rear Delt Exercises & Workouts" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-rear-delt-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-01-16 · 5,561 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **No ranking in this article is backed by hypertrophy outcome data. Not one.** There is no study anywhere in its reference list that measured posterior deltoid growth after a training period. Every exercise claim rests on surface EMG, anatomy, or nothing at all.
- The article **says this itself**, in the clearest terms of any exercise article in this library: EMG differences "shouldn't be taken as gospel," existing data "doesn't show that the differences are predictive of actual outcomes, e.g., differences in muscle size and strength," and it is unclear that picking exercises by EMG beats simply making sure the exercise loads the muscle. **Then it ranks six exercises anyway.** That contradiction is the single most useful thing here.
- The two exercises with the strongest activation case — **reverse pec deck** and **bent-over dumbbell fly** — come from two EMG studies ([Botton 2013](https://doi.org/10.5604/17342260.1055261), [Campos 2020](https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2020-0033)) both finding the reverse pec deck produced the highest posterior deltoid activity of the exercises tested. The other four (Pendlay/barbell row, wide-grip lat pulldown, 1-arm dumbbell row, face pulls) are included on the basis of "muscles worked" lists with no rear-delt-specific evidence presented at all.
- Programming: rows and pulldowns at **3–4 × 6–12, RPE 7–8**; flyes, reverse pec deck and face pulls at **2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–10** — heavier compounds further from failure, light isolation closer to it. Sensible, uncited.
- Honest moment worth keeping: "there is no data to support a specific 'push to pull' ratio." The gym-lore justification for face pulls is stated and then declined.
- Barbell Medicine sells the Beginner, Strength I and Strength III templates and individualised coaching, all three named and linked in the conclusion.

## The six, and what sits under each

| Exercise | Prescription | Evidence offered for the *rear delt* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pendlay / barbell row | 3–4 × 6–10, RPE 7–8 | One EMG study of rowing variants ([Fenwick 2009](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e3181942019)) — trunk muscles and lumbar loading, not deltoid |
| Wide-grip lat pulldown | 3–4 × 8–12, RPE 7–8 | Six EMG grip-width studies, all reporting on lats/biceps/traps — none on posterior deltoid |
| 1-arm dumbbell row | 3–4 × 8–12, RPE 7–8 | None. The section is a single- vs multi-joint digression |
| Bent-over dumbbell fly | 2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–10 | EMG ([Bergquist 2018](https://doi.org/10.1515/hukin-2017-0137)) |
| Reverse pec deck fly | 2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–10 | EMG, highest posterior deltoid activity in two studies |
| Face pulls | 2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–10 | An anatomy paper on deltoid segments ([Sakoma 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7580.2010.01325.x)) |

Half the list is back training that happens to involve the rear delts. That is defensible as programming — you do need to row — but it is not a *rear delt* ranking, and the article does not distinguish the two.

The one grip-position finding that is genuinely about the posterior shoulder is [Schoenfeld 2013](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e318281e1e9): during horizontal abduction, a **neutral grip** produced noticeably higher posterior deltoid and infraspinatus EMG than the alternative. The article passes this on as a "Pro Tip" for the reverse pec deck. It is an acute activation finding presented as a technique instruction, which is exactly the inferential move the article warned against four sections earlier.

## The lat pulldown grip-width literature contradicts itself, and the article says so

This is the best-handled passage in the piece. Three studies are laid out honestly:

- [Lusk 2010](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e3181ddb0ab) — **similar** activation of lats, mid-trapezius and biceps between narrow and wide grips.
- [Signorile 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12423182/) — **higher** latissimus activity with a wide grip at 10RM loads.
- [Andersen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1097/jsc.0000000000000232) — **no major differences** for lats, biceps, infraspinatus or trapezius across medium, narrow and wide grips at 6RM, and the 6RM load itself was **lower** with the wide grip.

Conclusion drawn: no definitive difference, pick by preference, and "we wouldn't place too much emphasis on EMG studies for selecting exercises." That is the right reading of a contradictory literature and it is unusual to see it written out.

## Single-joint versus multi-joint

The 1-arm dumbbell row section detours into whether isolation or compound exercises are better, and reaches the correct answer — **use both** — via a set of citations that do not all say what the sentences claim.

- [Gentil 2015](https://doi.org/10.5812/asjsm.24057) — review concluding the choice should depend on preference, equipment, movement specifics and time. This is the one the conclusion actually rests on.
- [Brigatto 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33414877/) — randomised longitudinal crossover, trained men: **similar strength increase** from single- and multi-joint exercise.
- [Mannarino 2021](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003234) — **single-joint produced higher elbow flexor hypertrophy** than multi-joint after eight weeks. This is the only measured hypertrophy outcome in the whole article, and it concerns the biceps, not the rear delts.

**Flag:** the sentence claiming "certain studies have found that single-joint exercises are better for increased muscle hypertrophy, as they're easier to learn" is supported in the printed list by a 1986 paper on learning and coordination in strength training and a 1997 study of strength and mass increases in young women. Neither compares single- to multi-joint hypertrophy. The claim outruns those two references, whatever the numbering intends.

## The benefits section is where the evidence gets thin

Roughly a third of the article is generic benefits, and this is where claims and citations separate.

**Back pain.** The article correctly notes that core-strengthening trials for chronic low back pain have produced mixed or negative results while resistance training has fared better, and cites a **45-adult, 16-week** trial of whole-body progressive resistance training that improved strength, pain and quality of life where aerobic training did not. That single small trial, published in a low-visibility physiotherapy journal, is carrying the entire "relieves back pain" heading. The direction is consistent with the broader literature; the weight put on this specific study is not proportionate to it.

**Metabolic and cardiovascular health.** Three of the citations here ([Srikanthan 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjcard.2016.01.033), [Burrows 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/pedi.12505), [Son 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-016-4196-9)) are **observational studies of muscle mass**, not trials of training — a cross-sectional adolescent cohort, an adult cohort, and a Korean prospective cohort. They establish that low muscle mass associates with cardiometabolic risk. They do not establish that rear delt training reduces it. The intervention meta-analyses that do belong here (Igarashi's resting blood pressure meta-analysis, and Jansson's HbA1c meta-analysis with its finding that *change in strength* moderates the effect) are the better support and are cited alongside.

**Overreach flag — osteoarthritis.** The article states that beginning to exercise early "slows down the development of osteoarthritis or completely stops it." No cited work supports arrest of structural disease. [Kristensen & Franklyn-Miller 2012](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.2010.079376) is a systematic review of resistance training in musculoskeletal rehabilitation; [Turner 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1941738119887183) covers dosing effects on **pain and physical function** in knee OA. This is the same over-claim that appears verbatim in Barbell Medicine's cable triceps article — a house sentence, reused, and wrong in both places.

**Mental health.** Handled well. Two Gordon meta-analyses of RCTs (anxiety 2017; depressive symptoms, *JAMA Psychiatry* 2018) plus a systematic review of RCTs on sleep, with the honest addition that the effect appears "irrespective of whether the trainees grow their strength or not" and "the mechanism behind it is unknown at this time."

## What to take from this

The article's own epistemics are better than its own headline. Its stated position — EMG differences are probably real but have not been shown to predict growth, so make sure the exercise loads the muscle and stop optimising — is correct and applies to the very list it publishes. **Treat the six as a reasonable menu for loading the posterior shoulder and upper back, not as a ranking, because there is nothing in this literature that could rank them.** If you want a rear-delt-biased movement, the activation data points at the reverse pec deck and the bent-over fly; that is an acute measurement, not a growth result, and it is the strongest statement the evidence permits.
