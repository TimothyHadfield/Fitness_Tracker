# Tennis Elbow in Lifters Is a Load Problem, Not an Inflammation Problem

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Lateral Elbow Pain for Lifters" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/lateral-elbow-pain-for-lifters/)) · Derek Miles · 2025-05-05 · 2404 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- "Lateral epicondyl**itis**" is a misnomer: inflammation is not the dominant feature, so the clinic prefers *epicondylalgia* — "-algia" claims only pain, not a mechanism. The practical consequence is that anti-inflammatory logic (rest, ice) is treating a process that mostly isn't there.
- The model is capacity vs. demand on the common extensor tendon. Total load includes non-gym load — typing, dressing, carrying — which is why gym-only accounting under-reads the problem.
- **Complete rest is explicitly rejected.** It reduces pain short-term while doing nothing for capacity, so the athlete returns to the same demand with less tolerance than before. Modify load instead.
- The recovery signal is a **moving threshold, not a pain switch**: pain doesn't vanish, the load that provokes it rises. Track the number (pain at 225 lb deadlift → pain at 315 lb is real progress).
- Evidence is cited for exactly three things — surgery, corticosteroid injection and PRP, none better than conservative care. **Everything else in the article, including the entire rehab prescription, is uncited clinical recommendation.**

## Why the "-itis" label matters clinically
The wrist and finger extensors share a common tendon off the lateral epicondyle, so anything loading grip or resisted wrist extension loads that one attachment. Miles's argument is that the condition is overuse of that tendon and that inflammation is not the driver — hence the terminology objection. He cites no pathology literature for this; it is consistent with mainstream tendinopathy thinking, but the reader takes it on the author's clinical authority.

The article gives **no natural history** — no recovery rates, time course or prognostic data, only "there is no specific timeline". Honest, but expectations end up set qualitatively rather than from cohort data.

## Which lifts provoke it, and what modification looks like
The provoking pattern is high volume of "power grip" work: deadlifts, pull-ups, lat pulldowns, heavy carries — and sometimes pressing. Triggers are framed as recent changes in three variables:

- **Intensity** — loads nearer 1RM, or sets pushed nearer failure
- **Volume** — added pulling exercises, or added sets on existing ones
- **Frequency** — an extra training day (3-day to 4-day split; or untrained to 2-3 days)

Modification, not cessation: **straps** on pulls to offload the grip (explicitly not licence to add weight); **handle changes** such as mag grips instead of a straight bar on pulldowns; **squat** low bar → high bar or a specialty/safety bar to get the hand out of the provoking position; **bench** with a wider, narrower or reverse grip. Preferred technique returns as symptoms allow.

## The rehab prescription (recommendation, not evidence)
Note that **isometrics are not part of this protocol**, and neither is an eccentric-only programme in the classic sense — one eccentric wrist extension variation appears in a three-exercise progression, but the stated principle is *heavy slow resistance*. The rope analogy given is: take the slack out and build force gradually rather than yanking.

- **Core**: wrist extension, progressing eccentric-only → extension to neutral → full ROM. **2-4 sets of 8-10 at RPE 8, 3-0-3 tempo**, 2-3×/week, done at the *start* of the session so it also serves as a warm-up.
- **Accessories**: pronation/supination with a hammer (choke down the handle to progress), wrist flexion, grip trainers, and loaded carries with varied grips (end-cap dumbbell, pinch-grip plates, bottom-up kettlebell). Roughly 2-3 sets of 10 at RPE 8, 2 days/week. Variety is stated to matter more than any specific variation.
- **Pain rules**: keep session pain ≤4/10 during and for ~24 hours after. Two consecutive sessions above that → cut volume on the main lifts.
- **Speed**: for athletes doing kipping pull-ups or clean pulls, speed has to be reintroduced deliberately once slow work is near-painless — set a tolerance ceiling (e.g. 70% of pre-injury clean pull load), programme below it, retest periodically.
- Keep the heavy slow work in for **at least a month** after pain settles, moved to accessory slots.

Every one of these numbers — 8-10 reps, RPE 8, 3-0-3, 4/10, the one-month tail — is presented without citation. They are plausible clinical defaults, not derived quantities, and the note should be read that way.

## Passive and invasive treatments
The article's one evidence-backed section. Surgery, corticosteroid injection and PRP are each said to be no better than conservative care, with invasive options additionally carrying risk, so they are not recommended.

Icing, scraping, braces and elbow supports are dismissed as having "no good evidence" — stated **flatly and with no citation**, which is stronger than the literature warrants; brace trials exist and are equivocal rather than absent. There is also a small internal tension: straps are recommended as a temporary offloading aid while braces, which offload similarly, are dismissed. Stretching gets the same treatment: not necessary, fine if it helps. The escape hatch throughout is "use it if it helps, as long as it isn't all you're doing" — reasonable, but it makes the position hard to falsify.
