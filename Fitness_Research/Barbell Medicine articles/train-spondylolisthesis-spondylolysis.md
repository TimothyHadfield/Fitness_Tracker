# Training With a "Broken Back": Spondylolysis and Spondylolisthesis

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How to Train With Spondylolisthesis or Spondylolysis" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/spondypartone/)) · Derek Miles · 2022-07-23 · 4,059 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **Terminology, decoded.** *Spondylolysis* is a fracture of the pars interarticularis, the bony bridge between a vertebra's upper and lower joints. *Spondylolisthesis* is one vertebra slipping forward on the one below. Miles groups them as "spondy" throughout.
- **The base rates undercut the diagnosis.** Spondylolysis appears in **3–7% of adolescents with no pain or symptoms** and **11.5% of asymptomatic adults**. So in roughly 3–12% of symptomatic patients the finding was already there and is unlikely to be causing the new pain.
- **A 45-year follow-up found no association between spondy and back pain risk**, with slowed progression on ageing and no participant progressing to a problematic degree. A systematic review of observational studies reached the same conclusion for low back pain and lumbar spondylolysis with or without isthmic spondylolisthesis.
- **Imaging healing does not predict outcome.** One study found **time and activity modification** predicted successful return to sport, with **no relationship between degree of bony healing and outcome**.
- **The recommendation is graded loading, not rest.** Progression runs **light and slow → heavy and slow → light and fast → heavy and fast**, with effort capped at **RPE 7** in early rehab and full range of motion typically restored in **2–4 weeks** for squatting.
- **They say so themselves:** "treating this diagnosis falls more in the realm of expert opinion than rigorous scientific evidence." Almost all the programming here is that expert opinion.

## The evidence, such as it is

This is a five-citation article, and Miles is unusually candid that the evidence base is thin. What the five papers do support is a negative claim, and they support it reasonably well: a spondy finding on imaging is a poor explanation for a person's back pain.

Three strands. First, prevalence in people without symptoms — the "base rate" argument, which he explains explicitly as a reasoning tool before applying it. Second, [Beutler's 45-year follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12768144/), which found no association with back pain risk and, importantly for a frightened patient, no progression to a problematic slip. Third, [Andrade's systematic review of observational studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25833204/), which found no association between low back pain and lumbar spondylolysis.

Read Beutler with its design in mind. It is a small longitudinal cohort followed from childhood into middle age — powerful for its duration, weak for its size, and not a randomised anything. Presenting "45-year follow-up studies find no association" as settled is a slightly stronger reading than one cohort supports, though it points the same way as Andrade.

Miles then does the thing that separates careful writing from slogan: he refuses the absolute version of his own argument. "That is not to say the finding does not matter at all, but rather raises the question of whether it is a 'contributor' more than a 'cause'." He gives the concrete case — an athlete returning from off-season to a sport with heavy lumbar extension and rotation needs time to adapt and may hurt in the interim — and concludes that this is a reason to modify load, not to withdraw the athlete entirely, because withdrawal removes the stress adaptation requires.

He also names non-tissue contributors — training load changes, sleep, stress, mental health, and for adolescents specifically **early sport specialisation and burnout**. That last one is the kind of thing only a clinician who sees these patients would put in.

## The red flag, stated early

Worth extracting on its own because it is the one part of this article a reader should act on immediately: **new bowel or bladder problems — incontinence or inability to urinate or defecate — or numbness in the groin region require immediate medical evaluation.** These are cauda equina symptoms. Miles puts them in bold in the second section, before any of the reassurance, which is the correct order.

## Short-term modifications

The starting point is whatever the person tolerates. For someone in a lot of pain that may mean glute bridges, walking lunges and banded side-steps — and he is careful to explain why these are *not* being prescribed for the usual reasons. Not because muscles are "turned off", "not firing properly", or need to "fire local before global"; simply because an easy entry point is needed before progressing. That is a direct swipe at motor-control rehab orthodoxy, and it is his position rather than a cited finding.

Upper body training continues as normally as possible, with modifications like feet-up bench pressing and limiting lumbar extension in overhead work. Aerobic work continues too — bike, walking, sled push/pull — with the principle of limiting end-range lumbar extension early, which is why rowing is deferred. The target is at least the standard **150 minutes of moderate activity per week**.

Two load-setting rules carry most of the practical weight:

**The RPE cap.** Effort is capped at **RPE 7** in early rehab, on the reasoning that an RPE 7 that accidentally becomes an 8 is far safer than a 9 that becomes a 10. "Light" is below RPE 6; "heavy" is RPE 7 or above.

**The symptom-plus-effort rule.** Rate pain and effort each out of 10; **the sum should rarely exceed 10**. Pain at 4/10 caps effort at RPE 5–6; pain at 8/10 means easy movement only. It doubles as a return-to-max-effort gate: you should be close to symptom-free before max effort training. This is a clean, usable heuristic — and it is entirely invented. No evidence is offered and none is claimed. Treat it as a sensible clinical rule of thumb, not a validated tool.

## Returning to the barbell lifts

The trigger to reload is functional, not radiographic: full range of motion and symptom-free activities of daily living.

**Squats.** Safety squat bar work (including Hatfield squats) to stay upright and reduce forced spinal extension; goblet squats if that fails; hack squat or leg press machines as constrained alternatives. Two sessions a week, 3–4 sets of 8–10 at an RPE 7 cap. The pin-squat progression is neat: RPE is anchored to "one pin lower than last session" so range of motion, not load, is the progressing variable, then load progresses at fixed height. Full range within **2–4 weeks** is expected for a typical athlete. Note his explicit aside that **lumbar extension is fine in normal training** — the constraint is a temporary rehab device, not a lasting rule.

**Bench press.** The limiting factor is the arched competition setup, so feet-up or flat-backed benching starts early, with a machine chest press as fallback. Lumbar extension is reintroduced via feet returning to the floor or elevated on plates, and via dumbbell incline pressing. Same 3–4 × 8–10 at RPE ≤7, moving through a higher-rep hypertrophy block before returning to heavy low-rep work.

**Deadlift.** "Contrary to popular internet lore, it does not matter if a person deadlifts using conventional or sumo technique," though sumo *may* help early by allowing a more upright torso. The useful distinction is between a *training* variation (well tolerated, driving adaptation) and an *exposure* variation (less tolerated, done lighter to rebuild tolerance) — session 1: tolerated variation, 4×8 at RPE 6–7; session 2: less-tolerated variation, 4×8 at RPE 5–6; session 3: machine hamstring curls 3×10 at RPE 9 and single-leg RDLs 3×8 at RPE 7.

**Back work.** Glute-ham raises, back extensions, good mornings, reverse hypers — and here Miles flags his own reasoning explicitly: "While we don't have good scientific evidence for specific strengthening of the area, it stands to reason that the stronger an athlete's back, the more stress it can tolerate." That is exactly the right label on that claim.

## Return to sport and competition

Sport-specific work follows the same light/slow to heavy/fast ladder — soft toss and a wiffle bat for a baseball player, single stunts before chained ones for a gymnast, then contact drills and position modifications (a baseball player starting at first base before returning to the outfield to limit long throws).

The argument he keeps returning to is a volume argument: three months of two hours' weekly "rehab" cannot prepare an athlete for ten hours of weekly sport, so clearing someone on the basis of X-ray healing while they have been detraining sends them back underprepared. His analogy — no one would take three months off a school subject and then sit the exam — is the article's organising idea.

For lifters returning to maximal work, he flags a specific and very recognisable problem: a stall at **75–80%** of prior lifts that is largely confidence rather than capacity. His fix is drop sets emphasising bar speed — if RPE jumps from 7 to 10 between 315 and 325 for 4 reps, drop **3–5%** and chase speed and rep quality to rebuild confidence.

**Overreach to flag:** "there is no evidence that rest speeds up healing or helps people return to their prior level of function" is an absence-of-evidence claim stated as a finding, and it sits uncited. The defensible version — that rest has not been shown to outperform graded activity, and reliably produces deconditioning — is what the rest of the article actually argues.
