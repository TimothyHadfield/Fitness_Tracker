# The Grip in the Deadlift: Friction, Asymmetry, and Almost No Citations

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Grip Problem" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-grip-problem/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2017-02-28 (updated 2024-12-05) · 4,644 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **This is a 4,600-word mechanics and anatomy essay with exactly two citations, both attached to one sentence.** Almost every claim in it is reasoning from anatomy plus Feigenbaum's own lifting experience. That is a legitimate way to write about grip, but the reader should not mistake it for an evidence review.
- The practical algorithm: **double overhand for as long as it holds, then hook grip and actually train it, then alternate grip if hook is not viable for your hands or equipment; chalk always; straps instead of alternate grip if you will never compete.**
- Grip is not the limiting factor by accident — it is limited by **friction**, and friction is set by hand tension, knurl pitch and tooth depth, bar diameter, chalk, and (for the hook and alternate grips) hand-on-hand contact. Feigenbaum's own bests span **220 kg double overhand to 330 kg alternate grip** — roughly 110 kg of difference in the same lifter from grip and bar choice alone.
- The clinical core is the **alternate grip's asymmetry**: distal biceps tendon rupture at the radial tuberosity is the strength-sport pattern, whereas ~90–97% of biceps tears in the non-athletic population are proximal long-head tears. Mechanism given: rapid tension onset on a supinated arm with a slightly flexed elbow — the classic "jerk it off the floor" failure.
- **He explicitly flags his own speculation.** The claim that back tweaks happen more often on the supinated side is offered as an observation with "no real data", which is the right level of confidence and rarer than it should be in this genre.
- The one place he leans on a paper, **the citation does not clearly support the sentence** (see References).

## Grip strength, and what a dynamometer measures

Feigenbaum opens with the clinical measure — isometric handgrip on a dynamometer — and immediately notes its limitation: it directly measures hand musculature, which may not track whole-body strength. The supporting sentence is that "handgrip strength in male lifters was about the same as non-lifters."

He then narrows the topic usefully. For the deadlift, grip strength means the force-producing capacity of hand *and* forearm musculature in that specific position. Humans use a **precision grip** (fingertips and thumb, fine manipulation) and a **power grip** (palmar opposition — fingers, thumb and palm enclosing the object). Barbell work is always the power grip, whichever variant is used; what changes between double overhand, hook and alternate is how much extra *friction* is layered on top of the muscular squeeze.

## The equipment argument, which is the best part of the article

The most concrete section is the one on hardware, and it is entirely uncited but internally coherent:

- **Knurl geometry.** Pitch is the circumferential distance between adjacent teeth at the base diameter; tooth depth is the tooth height. Aggressive knurl = wider pitch, greater depth, embeds into the hand. Smooth knurl = narrower pitch, shallower depth, more surface contact.
- **Bar diameter.** A 27 mm whippy deadlift bar is easier to hold than a 29 mm stiff bar, with a 28 mm Olympic bar in between. Diameter determines how completely the fingers close into the palm, which determines available squeeze. The women's 15 kg / 25 mm / 200 cm bar was designed for smaller hands while preserving whip comparable to the men's 20 kg / 28 mm / 220 cm bar.
- **His own numbers as illustration**: 220 kg double overhand on a stiff 29 mm bar; 265 kg hook on the same bar; 280 kg hook on a 27 mm whippy bar; 320 kg alternate on a 28.5 mm Texas Power Bar; 330 kg alternate on the 27 mm bar. One lifter, five results.

The friction digression is careful about its own limits: coefficient of friction for steel on concrete is ~0.45 and steel on ice ~0.03; dry skin on steel has been measured at 0.5–0.75, the highest of any body region tested; he could find **no experimental CoF data for athletic tape**, and says so, then argues the "tape increases friction" story is weak by pointing at runners taping feet to *prevent* blistering. This is the article being honest about a gap rather than filling it.

## The clinical section: alternate grip asymmetry

This is where a physician author earns his keep, and where the reasoning should be read as clinical inference rather than tested fact.

**At the elbow.** The supinated arm's biceps sits at a different length-tension relationship than the pronated arm's. In strength sports, biceps ruptures occur distally at the radial tuberosity; in the general population, proximal long-head tears are 90–97% of cases. The deadlift mechanism is a sudden load onto a slightly flexed, supinated arm. His mitigation is coaching, not avoidance: get tight, pull with a fully extended elbow, do not jerk the bar. He is careful to say the alternate grip is "probably not terribly dangerous" in itself — the risk lives in the technique fault, not the grip.

**At the shoulder and back.** Supination produces small humeral external rotation, which rotates the intertubercular groove and alters the latissimus dorsi's line of pull and length-tension relationship. Since the lat works isometrically in the deadlift to keep the bar against the legs, reduced lat force on that side plausibly explains **"windmilling"** — the bar drifting away on the supinated side — which lengthens the moment arm and raises the force required. From there he speculates that lumbar tweaks may cluster on the supinated side via the lat's thoracolumbar fascia attachment. **He labels this speculation outright.**

There is also a practical answer buried here worth extracting: switch to your competition grip **no later than your last warm-up set**, for the same reason you do not introduce a belt for the first time on a work set.

## Where this sits against Barbell Medicine's later positions

One passage has aged awkwardly against the rest of this library. Arguing for the double overhand and against straps, Feigenbaum makes a strong **form-determines-adaptation** claim: deadlifting with a rounded back means the erectors "weren't required to do their job today", so they will not be able to do it tomorrow. The article was edited by Mark Rippetoe and carries that lineage. Barbell Medicine's later output — including their own writing on pain, load management and technique — argues that "good form" is far less load-bearing than the industry claims. **Read this piece as an artefact of the earlier position**; the grip mechanics stand independently of it.

There is also a mild tension in the grip advice itself: the case against straps is that they do not build grip strength, but the case for the double overhand in warm-ups is that it *does*. Both cannot be maximally weighted at once, and the article resolves this by judgement ("straps tend to be over-utilized by younger bros and under-utilized by older geezlings") rather than by evidence — and says as much.

**Flagged:** the second reference is a **normative reference-values study**, establishing population grip norms by age and sex. It does not compare lifters with non-lifters, so it cannot support the sentence it is attached to; at most it supplies the norms against which such a comparison could be made. The claim may well be true, but on this evidence it is under-supported. Everything else in the article — the friction physics, the bar geometry, the biceps rupture epidemiology, the windmilling mechanism — is uncited.
