# The "40% Muscle Loss" Headline Is Mostly a Measurement Problem — What GLP-1 Body-Composition Data Actually Show

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "GLP-1 Muscle Loss: Ozempic, Wegovy & Mounjaro Evidence Review" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/glp-1-muscle-loss/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum (medically reviewed by Austin Baraki) · 2026-04-25 · 8,441 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

> Not medical advice, and this note is a summary of one article's argument, not a clinical recommendation. GLP-1 receptor agonists are prescription medicines; starting, continuing or stopping one is a decision for a prescriber.

## Bottom line
- The famous number — **38–39% of weight lost in [STEP-1](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2032183) classified as "lean mass"** (n=1,961 adults with obesity, ~15% weight loss over 68 weeks, RCT) — is a **DXA output, not a muscle measurement**. DXA reports three compartments (bone, fat, everything else) and cannot separate muscle from water, glycogen, organ tissue or fat sitting inside muscle.
- Their strongest evidence is that **the instrument moves without muscle moving**: glycogen loading alone shifts DXA leg lean mass 1–2% within days and creatine raises total body water ~1.4%, with no change in contractile protein ([Bone 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000001174)). The same critique cuts both ways — it also deflates "added 5 lb of muscle in 8 weeks" claims.
- Where better instruments were used, **muscle function held or improved**. [SEMALEAN](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70141) (n=106, mean BMI 46, mean age 52, 12 months max-dose semaglutide, **no control group and no prescribed exercise**) saw fat mass −18%, lean mass −5%, grip **+4.5 kg**, and sarcopenic-obesity prevalence fall from 49% to 33%.
- **No pair-fed human trial exists**, so "the drug causes muscle loss" is untested in humans. The pair-fed comparison that does exist is [Langer 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2026.102665) — **mostly mice**, plus a small proof-of-concept human arm — and it found no drug-specific atrophy beyond matched weight loss.
- Their practical order is **train first, then sleep, then protein (1.2–1.6 g/kg/day)**. The training claim is well supported in general deficit literature but **extrapolated** to GLP-1 users; the GLP-1-specific training data ([T-REX](https://www.anzctr.org.au/Trial/Registration/TrialReview.aspx?id=385806)) is a **trial registration and preliminary result, not a published paper**.
- **Clash with the library:** their 1.6 g/kg ceiling sits below the ISSN 2017 stand's ≥1.6–2.4 g/kg for holding lean mass in a deficit, and their "training beats protein" framing is stronger than either body of evidence tests directly.

## Should the headline number worry you?

The article's whole argument turns on one distinction the coverage collapses: **DXA lean mass ≠ muscle mass ≠ muscle function.**

DXA passes two X-ray beams through the body and sorts what it finds into bone, fat, and "lean soft tissue" ([Buckinx 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcsm.12268)). That third bucket is what trials report as lean mass. It contains skeletal muscle — and water, glycogen, organs, connective tissue, and intramuscular fat. The instrument assumes a fixed ~73% hydration of the lean compartment, an assumption that holds in a weight-stable adult and breaks in someone losing 15% of body weight over 68 weeks. Same-day precision is roughly 0.3–2%.

Two observations do real work here. First, the direct manipulation study: change nothing but muscle glycogen or creatine and DXA lean mass moves by 1–2% ([Bone 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000001174)). Second, and more telling, **the STEP-1 placebo group also lost DXA lean mass despite minimal weight change**. A signal that appears in the arm that did not lose weight is more consistent with measurement drift than with a drug effect. That is a genuinely sharp piece of reasoning and the article deserves credit for it.

Where the article overreaches is Table 1, its breakdown of a "3 kg lean mass loss" into compartments — liver glycogen 0.3–0.8 kg, liver fat 0.3–1.0 kg, intramuscular fat 0.2–0.5 kg, and **actual contractile muscle 0.2–0.5 kg**. The footnote concedes these are "bounded estimates derived from published compartment physiology and body-composition modeling," citing refs 4, 6, 25, 26, 27 and 31. That is a **model, not a measurement**, and its own stated total (1.5–3.9 kg) does not pin to the 3 kg it purports to decompose. The "only 200–500 grams was muscle" figure is the article's most quotable line and it is the least measured thing in it.

The liver argument beneath it is more defensible. In [Langer 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2026.102665), **liver mass change accounted for ~71% of the body-weight-change signal at day 7 and muscle in the untreated leg for ~9%** — but that is mice. The human bridge is that survodutide and retatrutide phase 2 trials in MASLD/MASH show 60–80% relative reductions in intrahepatic triglyceride ([Sanyal 2024a](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2401755), [Sanyal 2024b](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-03018-2)). Those are real reductions in a real population, but no human study has quantified how much of a GLP-1 user's DXA lean-mass loss is hepatic. The mouse-to-human step is inference.

## What happens with a better instrument?

[SURMOUNT-1](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2206038) (tirzepatide, 72 weeks, ~21% weight loss) put DXA lean mass at ~25% of weight lost — better than STEP-1, same instrument limitation. The [SURPASS-3 MRI substudy](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(22)00070-5) used MRI, which can separate contractile muscle from fat inside it, and found a significant reduction in intramuscular fat. Note the population: SURPASS-3 was a **type 2 diabetes** trial, not the obesity-without-diabetes population of SURMOUNT-1.

That matters because intramuscular fat is not neutral tissue. It lowers force per unit cross-section and is independently associated with disability and mortality in older adults ([Goodpaster 2006](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/61.10.1059) — an observational cohort, so association). When it shrinks, the muscle works better and DXA books it as lean-mass loss.

The direct-measurement comparison comes from Langer: in the tirzepatide arm, **DXA said lean mass fell 13%; dissecting and weighing individual muscles put it near 10%** — DXA overstated by about a third. Gastrocnemius mass predicted ~37% of running-performance variance; total lean body mass predicted almost none. In the human proof-of-concept arm, maximum knee-extension and grip strength were unchanged despite reduced muscle size. Weigh that appropriately: dissection data are mice, the human component is small and uncontrolled.

The measurement scepticism is also applied honestly to the benchmark the drugs are judged against. The "75% fat / 25% lean" quarter rule comes from [Heymsfield 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.12143), and the article uses that same paper to argue the benchmark is itself a DXA-era aggregate of every non-fat compartment. Their mechanistic prediction follows: because GLP-1s resolve the conditions (steatosis, inflammation) that inflate the lean compartment, they **should** look worse on DXA than plain dieting while losing the same or less contractile muscle. That is a coherent argument. It is also unfalsified rather than confirmed.

## Is it the drug, or is it the deficit?

The article is unusually clean about this: **almost no GLP-1 trial has a pair-fed or matched-restriction control**, so "muscle loss from the drug" cannot be separated from "muscle loss from weight loss." The claim "GLP-1s cause muscle loss" would need a pair-fed human trial, and it does not exist.

What exists is a set of numbers clustering near the same place — STEP-1's 38% as the outlier, SURMOUNT-1 ~25%, the semaglutide arm of [REDEFINE 1](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2502081) at 28%, and the retatrutide phase 2 body-composition substudy (Coskun 2025) in line with total weight loss — plus one pair-fed comparison in mice showing no incremental atrophy.

**Where the evidence sits on the sarcopenia question.** [EWGSOP2](https://doi.org/10.1093/ageing/afy169) makes **strength** the primary diagnostic criterion with mass demoted to confirmatory; the [SDOC](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.16372) position statements, pooling >18,000 participants across eight epidemiologic studies and six RCTs, found DXA lean mass did not predict falls, fractures, mobility limitation or mortality once other factors were accounted for, and dropped mass from the definition entirely. [PURE](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(14)62000-6) (139,691 adults, 17 countries, prospective cohort) found each 5 kg lower grip strength associated with 17% higher cardiovascular and 7% higher non-cardiovascular mortality. So the outcome that predicts hard endpoints is the one that improved in SEMALEAN.

**Flagged:** SEMALEAN carries most of the weight for "function improves" and it is a **single-arm cohort with no control group**. A +4.5 kg grip improvement over 12 months in adults with severe obesity is plausible as a real effect of unloading the body, but familiarisation with the dynamometer and regression to the mean are not excluded by that design. "Muscle function typically improves with treatment" is stated more firmly than one uncontrolled cohort and a narrative [*Circulation* review](https://doi.org/10.1161/circulationaha.124.067676) support.

## What actually protects muscle — and how strong is that evidence?

This is where the article moves from evidence review to clinical advice, and it mostly says so.

The **evidence** is from non-GLP-1 deficit literature: a 2018 meta-analysis of resistance training during caloric restriction in older adults with obesity found training groups preserved ~93% of lean mass versus diet-only controls ([Sardeli 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu10040423)); a controlled trial comparing restriction alone, exercise alone and the combination found diet-only lost ~16% of weight as lean mass while diet-plus-exercise lost close to none ([Weiss 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000001074)). Neither involved a GLP-1.

The **GLP-1-specific** support is thinner than the confident prose suggests. T-REX (training arm roughly halving fat-free-mass loss) is cited as **a trial registration with preliminary data** — not a peer-reviewed paper. The two pharmacological proofs of concept are stronger on the mechanism than on the recommendation: BELIEVE (bimagrumab + semaglutide, phase 2 RCT, [Heymsfield 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04204-0)) had the combination group lose 22% of body weight with 93% of it fat; QUALITY (enobosarm + semaglutide) reported <1% of weight lost as lean mass versus 34% on semaglutide alone — but that one is **a company presentation at ObesityWeek 2024, not a published trial**, and the article does note both are early-stage and unavailable by prescription.

**The recommendation.** Two to four resistance sessions per week across squat, hinge, horizontal and vertical push and pull; two to three working sets of 4–12 reps per pattern at 2–4 reps from failure; 2–4 minutes rest on compounds; progress load or reps. Sessions of 30–60 minutes. Train lighter through the nausea window rather than skipping — "keep the signal on." **None of this prescription carries a citation.** It is ordinary, defensible clinical programming, and it should be read as their judgement rather than as a finding.

**Protein: 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day**, with 1.6 as the working target for someone training in a deficit, resting on the [Morton 2018 meta-regression](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608) where the benefit of extra protein on resistance-training outcomes plateaued around 1.6 g/kg. Note the extrapolation: Morton pooled healthy adults doing resistance training, **not people in a large deficit and not people on a GLP-1**. The 2026 US dietary guidelines' 1.2–1.6 g/kg floor is a government document, not a trial. They call 2.0–2.4 g/kg harmless but unlikely to add benefit, hold that total daily intake beats per-meal distribution, and give ~25–40 g per meal (2–3 g leucine) as the practical unit.

**Clash with this library.** The ISSN 2017 protein stand puts the dieting-to-hold-muscle range at **≥1.6–2.4 g/kg/day**, treating 1.6 as a floor where Barbell Medicine treats it as a ceiling; ISSN also gives per-meal distribution (0.25 g/kg, 20–40 g) more standing than "does not seem to have a large effect." Menno Henselmans's Ozempic note in this library reports the 33–40% lean-mass figure as **genuinely unusual** against a ~25% norm; this article's answer is that the 25% norm is the same artifact measured on the same instrument. Both notes flag the DXA caveat; only Barbell Medicine builds the whole case on it.

**Flagged:** "Why does training matter more than protein intake?" is asserted, not tested. No trial in this reference list compares training against protein in GLP-1 users. The supporting citation for the mechanism — [Pasiakos 2010](https://doi.org/10.3945/jn.109.118372) — is an acute stable-isotope study of energy deprivation in physically active adults reporting a synthesis-rate outcome, not a mass or function outcome, and not in this population.

## Monitoring, bone, and the honest parts

Their monitoring advice is that **DXA is not the tool**: [Tavoian 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-46428-w) compared DXA thigh lean mass against MRI thigh muscle volume across a training intervention and found only 46% of scans agreed within 3%, with some participants gaining by MRI while apparently losing by DXA. BIA is worse — ~3–5% body-fat error ([Ling 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2011.04.001)), swinging 2–3 points with hydration. Trend DXA at 6–12 months if at all, never month to month.

In its place they offer weekly waist circumference, training performance every 4–6 weeks, and a rolling eight-week **weight-to-waist ratio**, with "0.6–0.8 kg per cm" as good and ">1.0" as a warning sign. **Flagged:** those thresholds carry no citation and read as clinical heuristics dressed in numeric precision. Waist circumference itself is well justified; the ratio cutoffs are invention.

The bone section is the article at its most rigorous. It takes a conference abstract generating osteoporosis headlines ([Wajahath 2026, AAOS](https://aaos-annualmeeting-presskit.org/2026/research-news/studies-explore-glp-1-receptor-agonist-use-and-its-impact-on-long-term-musculoskeletal-health/)), notes it is a retrospective EMR review with no prospective fracture or fall outcomes, and converts the scary relative risk into absolute terms: **41 per 1,000 GLP-1 users versus 32 per 1,000 matched controls over five years** — a nine-person difference reported as "29% increased risk." Against it they set a secondary analysis of a randomised trial ([Jensen 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.16775)) where liraglutide plus structured exercise preserved bone mineral density over 52 weeks while medication alone lost density at the hip. That is the correct way to handle a scary headline, and it is worth noting they do it for a risk that cuts against their own thesis's optimism.

On the newer glucagon-containing agents (retatrutide, survodutide) they state the theoretical concern — glucagon agonism drives hepatic amino-acid demand while GLP-1 cuts intake — then report that the retatrutide substudy and the mouse pair-fed comparison do not show extra atrophy, and explicitly say more study is needed. Appropriately hedged.

**Things they sell.** The recommended starting programme is adapted from their **Beginner Prescription / Beginner Template** (paid, with a free 4-week programme attached), the appetite-suppression protein tactics route to **Whey Rx**, their own third-party-tested whey isolate, protein and calorie targets link to their calculators, and both the side-effect section and the closing section pitch **Barbell Medicine Coaching**. The creatine recommendation (3–5 g/day monohydrate, skip loading) points to third-party-tested monohydrate generally rather than their own product. None of this invalidates the analysis, but the article's practical half is an on-ramp to their services.
