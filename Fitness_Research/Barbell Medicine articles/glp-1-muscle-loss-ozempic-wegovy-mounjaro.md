# The "40% Muscle Loss" Headline Is Mostly a Measurement Problem

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "GLP-1 Muscle Loss: Ozempic, Wegovy & Mounjaro Evidence Review" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/glp-1-muscle-loss/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum, medically reviewed by Austin Baraki · 2026-04-25 · 8,441 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

> Not medical advice. This is a summary of one article's argument and the evidence behind it. GLP-1 receptor agonists are prescription medicines; starting, continuing or stopping one is a decision for a prescriber.

## Bottom line
- The famous number — **38–39% of weight lost in [STEP-1](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2032183) classified as "lean mass"** (RCT, n=1,961 adults with obesity, ~15% weight loss over 68 weeks) — is a **DXA output, not a muscle measurement**. DXA cannot separate muscle from water, glycogen, organs or fat sitting inside muscle.
- Their strongest evidence is that **the instrument moves without muscle moving**: glycogen loading alone shifts DXA leg lean mass 1–2% in days, creatine raises total body water ~1.4%, both with no change in contractile protein ([Bone 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000001174)). The critique cuts both ways — it also deflates "added 5 lb of muscle in 8 weeks."
- Where function was measured, it held or improved. [SEMALEAN](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70141) (n=106, mean BMI 46, mean age 52, 12 months max-dose semaglutide, **single-arm, no control, no prescribed exercise**): fat mass −18%, lean mass −5%, grip **+4.5 kg**, sarcopenic-obesity prevalence 49% → 33%.
- **No pair-fed human trial exists**, so "the drug is catabolic" is untested in humans. The one pair-fed comparison, [Langer 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2026.102665), is **mostly mice** plus a small human arm, and found no atrophy beyond matched weight loss.
- Their order is **train, sleep, then protein (1.2–1.6 g/kg/day)** — evidence drawn from general deficit literature and **extrapolated** to GLP-1 users; the GLP-1-specific training data (T-REX) is a **trial registration, not a paper**.
- **Clash with this library:** 1.6 g/kg is their ceiling, where the ISSN 2017 stand treats **1.6–2.4 g/kg as the dieting range**.

## Should the headline number worry you?

The argument turns on a distinction the coverage collapses: **DXA lean mass ≠ muscle mass ≠ muscle function.**

DXA sorts what it finds into bone, fat, and "lean soft tissue" ([Buckinx 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcsm.12268)). That third bucket is what trials report. It contains skeletal muscle — and water, glycogen, organs, connective tissue and intramuscular fat. It assumes a fixed ~73% hydration of the lean compartment, which holds in a weight-stable adult and breaks in someone shedding 15% of body weight. Same-day precision runs 0.3–2%.

Two observations do real work. First, direct manipulation: change only muscle glycogen or creatine and DXA lean mass moves 1–2% ([Bone 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000001174)). Second, and sharper, **the STEP-1 placebo group also lost DXA lean mass despite minimal weight change** — a signal in the arm that did not lose weight looks more like measurement drift than a drug effect.

**Flagged overreach.** Table 1 decomposes a "3 kg lean mass loss" into compartments — liver glycogen 0.3–0.8 kg, liver fat 0.3–1.0 kg, intramuscular fat 0.2–0.5 kg, and **contractile muscle 0.2–0.5 kg**. Its own footnote concedes these are "bounded estimates derived from published compartment physiology and body-composition modeling." That is a **model, not a measurement**, its stated total (1.5–3.9 kg) does not pin to the 3 kg it decomposes, and the "only 200–500 g was muscle" line is simultaneously the article's most quotable claim and its least measured one.

The liver argument beneath it is more defensible. In Langer, **liver mass change accounted for ~71% of the body-weight-change signal at day 7, muscle in the untreated leg for ~9%** — in mice. The human bridge is that survodutide and retatrutide phase 2 trials in MASLD/MASH show 60–80% relative reductions in intrahepatic triglyceride ([Sanyal 2024a](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2401755), [Sanyal 2024b](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-03018-2)). Real reductions in a real population — but no human study has quantified how much of a GLP-1 user's DXA lean-mass loss is hepatic. That step is inference.

## What a better instrument shows

[SURMOUNT-1](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2206038) (tirzepatide, 72 weeks, ~21% weight loss) put DXA lean mass at ~25% of weight lost. The [SURPASS-3 MRI substudy](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(22)00070-5) used MRI, which can separate contractile muscle from fat inside it, and found a significant reduction in intramuscular fat — note that SURPASS-3 was a **type 2 diabetes** trial, a different population from SURMOUNT-1.

Intramuscular fat is not neutral tissue: it lowers force per unit cross-section and is independently associated with disability and mortality in older adults ([Goodpaster 2006](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/61.10.1059), observational cohort). When it shrinks the muscle works better, and DXA books it as lean-mass loss.

The direct comparison comes from Langer: in the tirzepatide arm **DXA said lean mass fell 13%, while dissecting and weighing individual muscles put it near 10%** — overstating by roughly a third. Gastrocnemius mass predicted ~37% of running-performance variance; total lean body mass predicted almost none. In the human arm, knee-extension and grip strength were unchanged despite reduced muscle size. Weigh that accordingly: the dissection data are mice, the human component small and uncontrolled.

The scepticism extends to the benchmark. The "75% fat / 25% lean" quarter rule comes from [Heymsfield 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.12143), and the article uses that same paper to argue the benchmark is itself a DXA-era aggregate of every non-fat compartment. Their prediction follows: because GLP-1s resolve the conditions that inflate the lean compartment, they *should* look worse on DXA while losing the same or less contractile muscle. Coherent — and currently unfalsified rather than confirmed.

## Is it the drug, or is it the deficit?

The article is clean about this: **almost no GLP-1 trial has a pair-fed or matched-restriction control**, so drug effect cannot be separated from weight-loss effect in humans. What exists is a cluster of numbers — STEP-1's 38% as the outlier, SURMOUNT-1 ~25%, the semaglutide arm of [REDEFINE 1](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa2502081) at 28%, the retatrutide phase 2 substudy in line with total weight loss — plus one pair-fed comparison in mice showing no incremental atrophy.

On sarcopenia they are on firm ground. [EWGSOP2](https://doi.org/10.1093/ageing/afy169) makes **strength** the primary criterion with mass demoted to confirmatory; the [SDOC](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.16372) statements, pooling >18,000 participants across eight epidemiologic studies and six RCTs, found DXA lean mass did not predict falls, fractures, mobility limitation or mortality, and dropped mass from the definition. [PURE](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(14)62000-6) (139,691 adults, prospective cohort) associated each 5 kg lower grip with 17% higher cardiovascular and 7% higher non-cardiovascular mortality. The outcome that predicts hard endpoints is the one that improved in SEMALEAN.

**Flagged:** SEMALEAN carries most of the weight for "function improves," and it is a **single-arm cohort with no control** — dynamometer familiarisation and regression to the mean are not excluded. That claim is stated more firmly than one uncontrolled cohort and a narrative [*Circulation* review](https://doi.org/10.1161/circulationaha.124.067676) support.

## What actually protects muscle, and how good is that evidence?

The **evidence** is from non-GLP-1 deficit literature: resistance training during caloric restriction in older adults with obesity preserved ~93% of lean mass versus diet-only controls ([Sardeli 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu10040423), meta-analysis); a controlled trial of restriction alone versus exercise alone versus both found diet-only lost ~16% of weight as lean mass while diet-plus-exercise lost close to none ([Weiss 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000001074)). Neither involved a GLP-1.

The **GLP-1-specific** support is thinner than the prose suggests. T-REX — training halving fat-free-mass loss — is cited as a **trial registration with preliminary data**, not a peer-reviewed paper. Of the two pharmacological proofs of concept, BELIEVE (bimagrumab + semaglutide, phase 2 RCT, [Heymsfield 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04204-0)) had the combination lose 22% of body weight with 93% of it fat; QUALITY (enobosarm + semaglutide, <1% of weight as lean mass over 16 weeks versus 34% on semaglutide alone) is **a company presentation at ObesityWeek 2024, not a published trial**. To their credit, the article says both are early-stage and unavailable by prescription.

**The recommendation** — two to four sessions weekly across squat, hinge, horizontal and vertical push and pull; two to three sets of 4–12 reps at 2–4 reps from failure; 2–4 minutes rest on compounds; progress load or reps; train lighter through the nausea window rather than skipping — **carries no citation at all**. It is ordinary, defensible clinical programming and should be read as their judgement, not a finding.

**Protein: 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day**, with 1.6 the working target, resting on the [Morton 2018 meta-regression](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608) where added protein's benefit plateaued near 1.6 g/kg. Morton pooled **healthy trainees, not people in a large deficit and not GLP-1 users** — extrapolation, twice over. They call 2.0–2.4 g/kg harmless but unlikely to add benefit, put total daily intake above per-meal distribution, and give 25–40 g per meal (2–3 g leucine) as the practical unit.

**Clash with the library.** The ISSN 2017 protein stand puts the dieting-to-hold-muscle range at **≥1.6–2.4 g/kg/day** — treating as a floor what Barbell Medicine treats as a ceiling — and gives per-meal distribution (0.25 g/kg, 20–40 g) more weight than "does not seem to have a large effect." Separately, Menno Henselmans's Ozempic note in this library reports the 33–40% lean-mass figure as **genuinely unusual** against a ~25% norm; this article's reply is that the 25% norm is the same artifact on the same instrument. Both flag the DXA caveat; only Barbell Medicine builds the whole case on it.

**Flagged:** "Why does training matter more than protein intake?" is asserted, not tested — nothing in the reference list compares the two in GLP-1 users. The mechanism citation, [Pasiakos 2010](https://doi.org/10.3945/jn.109.118372), is an acute stable-isotope study of energy deprivation in physically active adults reporting a synthesis-rate outcome, not mass or function, and not in this population.

## Monitoring, bone, and the honest parts

**DXA is not the monitoring tool.** [Tavoian 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-46428-w) compared DXA thigh lean mass against MRI thigh muscle volume across a training intervention: only 46% of scans agreed within 3%, and some participants gained by MRI while apparently losing by DXA. BIA is worse — ~3–5% body-fat error ([Ling 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2011.04.001)), moving 2–3 points with hydration. Trend DXA at 6–12 months if at all.

In its place: weekly waist circumference, training performance every 4–6 weeks, and a rolling eight-week **weight-to-waist ratio** with 0.6–0.8 kg per cm as good and above 1.0 as a warning sign. **Flagged:** those thresholds carry no citation and read as clinical heuristics wearing numeric precision. Waist circumference itself is well justified; the cutoffs are invention.

The bone section is the article at its most rigorous. It takes a conference abstract driving osteoporosis headlines (Wajahath 2026, AAOS), notes it is a retrospective EMR review with no prospective fracture outcomes, and converts the relative risk into absolute terms: **41 per 1,000 GLP-1 users versus 32 per 1,000 matched controls over five years** — the nine-person difference reported as "29% increased risk." Against it they set a secondary analysis of an RCT ([Jensen 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.16775)) in which liraglutide plus structured exercise preserved bone mineral density over 52 weeks while medication alone lost density at the hip. On the glucagon-containing agents they state the theoretical concern, report no extra atrophy in the data so far, and say more study is needed.

**The framing they are arguing against.** Their closing move is that the risk of the medication is usually weighed against nothing, when the comparator is untreated obesity plus lifestyle-alone weight loss that mostly does not hold — DPP (n=3,234; over half the loss regained by ten years), Look AHEAD (n=5,145; fewer than half maintaining clinically significant loss by year four), POUNDS Lost (n=811; only 15% losing ≥10%), and a meta-analysis of 29 US trials where >80% was regained by five years. Against that they set SELECT (cardiovascular events) and FLOW (kidney outcomes). This is a fair point, and it is also the section where the article is most obviously making a case rather than reviewing evidence.

**Things they sell.** The starting programme is adapted from their **Beginner Prescription / Beginner Template**; the appetite-suppression protein tactics route to **Whey Rx**, their own whey isolate; the targets link to their calculators; two sections pitch **Barbell Medicine Coaching**. The creatine advice (3–5 g/day monohydrate, skip loading) points to third-party-tested monohydrate generally, not their own product.
