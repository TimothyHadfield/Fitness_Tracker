# The Two Best Olympic Weightlifting Programs Are Both Theirs — and the Numbering Is Broken

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "2 Best Olympic Weightlifting Programs for Maximum Performance" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-olympic-weightlifting-programs/)) · Barbell Medicine (unattributed staff) · 2023-11-04 · 3,580 words
**Topic:** Programming

## The mapping does not line up — do not use the numbers

This article **does** use numbered inline markers, and they **do not** correspond to its own reference list. There are 12 references and markers running to **[14]**, so two markers point past the end of the list entirely. The drift is visible: marker [3] is attached four separate times to claims about Olympic lifting versus traditional training, including "a 2022 meta-analysis showed that Olympic weightlifting programs improve strength in non-Olympic lifts to similar levels as traditional strength training" — while reference 3 is Plotkin 2022, *Progressive overload without progressing load?*, a study of load versus repetition progression that has nothing to do with Olympic weightlifting. Later markers drift by one and then by two against the list.

**Nothing below is attributed by number.** The reference list is treated as a reading list for the article as a whole, exactly as it would be for a YouTube source. Where the article names a source in the sentence itself, that is noted.

## Bottom line
- **This is a product page.** The "2 best Olympic weightlifting programs" are Barbell Medicine's own **Olympic Weightlifting Template** (12 weeks, 4 lifting days plus up to 2 GPP sessions, for lifters with 9+ months of training) and **SuperTotal Template** (16 weeks, 6 sessions/week in three blocks, blending powerlifting and weightlifting, for 12+ months of experience). No third-party programme is considered. Beginners are routed to their Beginner Template, then Strength I or General Strength & Conditioning, then these. That is a product ladder, and it is also defensible sequencing advice — but the reader should see both things at once.
- **The genuinely useful content is deflationary and cuts against the sales pitch.** Olympic lifting does **not** build more maximal strength than traditional strength training; a 2022 meta-analysis is cited (unnamed) for the finding that Olympic programmes improve non-Olympic-lift strength to similar levels while improving the snatch and clean & jerk far more. That is specificity working exactly as expected.
- **Where it does win is high-velocity strength.** Countermovement jump, sprint speed and change of direction improve more with Olympic lifting than traditional resistance training — and the article immediately adds that **plyometric training produces the same adaptations**, so Olympic lifting is not the only route. Reported figures: weightlifters show 13–36% more lower-body power than other strength athletes on jump-type tests, while upper-body power is comparable to handball players.
- **Olympic lifting is a poor hypertrophy choice, and they say so.** The lifts are predominantly concentric, performed fast (less time under tension), and appear to preferentially hypertrophy type II fibres while maintaining type I; bodybuilding-style training grows both. "Olympic lifting programs tend to produce less hypertrophy than bodybuilding programs when studied."
- **The resting-metabolic-rate argument for lifting gets debunked in their own article.** One kilogram of muscle uses ~13 kcal/day; a kilogram of fat, ~4.5. Gaining 10 kg of muscle — which takes years — adds **under 100 kcal/day** of total daily energy expenditure. They call the popular version of this claim "probably overstated," which it is.

## What the two templates actually are

**Olympic Weightlifting Template.** 12 weeks. Four days of full-body strength training plus up to two general physical preparedness sessions carrying conditioning and direct arm, upper back and core work, run after a lifting session or separately. Aimed at lifters with more than nine months of strength training behind them.

**SuperTotal Template.** 16 weeks in three blocks, six sessions/week (four resistance training, two GPP), for lifters with at least twelve months of experience. Trains the squat, bench and deadlift alongside the snatch and clean & jerk, with customisable exercise selection and adjustable volume and intensity. They are clear that anyone wanting to focus on one discipline should take the specialised template instead.

Both prescriptions — block lengths, session counts, experience gating — are **recommendations**, not findings. The article does not claim otherwise, but it does not mark the line either: nothing in the reference list evaluates either template.

## The training-science content, and where it is careful

**Programming differences.** The snatch and clean & jerk are executed quickly and load the body differently from slow resistance training, so they are programmed with **fewer reps per set, lower total volume and higher loads**. That is a technical-demand argument rather than an adaptation argument, and it is the right framing.

**Injury risk.** The article states that the Olympic weightlifting injury rate is "virtually the same as other forms of resistance training at 2 to 4 injuries per 1000 participation hours." The figure is in the correct range for the published literature on the sport, but the marker attached to it points into the broken numbering and the reference it lands on is unrelated, so it cannot be checked from this article.

**Neuromuscular adaptation.** Increased firing frequency, greater motor unit recruitment, and improved coordination and efficiency of force production during complex movements, with the last of these specifically attributed to high-velocity training. Standard and uncontroversial; the reference list contains Sale's 1988 review, which is the canonical source for this material.

**Fibre type, handled well.** The article reports that elite weightlifters' leg musculature averages ~67% fast-twitch fibres and then does something most articles in this genre do not: it flags the confound explicitly. "It's currently unknown if the differences in muscle fiber type hypertrophy is mostly a result of training or due to genetics, e.g. the sport choosing the individual or the individual choosing the sport." That is exactly the right caution for a cross-sectional comparison of elite athletes.

**Core strength, hedged correctly.** Olympic lifting develops the trunk strength needed to perform the lifts, but "the core strength developed from Olympic weightlifting is not universal and additional ab training is likely to be beneficial, particularly for non Olympic lifting tasks." Again, specificity.

## Where it stops being careful

**Bone density is the clear failure.** The article claims Olympic weightlifting "is one of the best types of weightlifting to increase bone density, producing much better results than some other types of weightlifting," and explains it causally — stress "allows the bone to adapt and grow." The reference list's bone paper is a **cross-sectional comparison of bone mineral density across female Olympic lifters, powerlifters and soccer players**. A cross-sectional comparison of athletes in three sports cannot show that one training style *produced* the difference; it is exposed to precisely the self-selection confound the article correctly identified two sections earlier for fibre type. The same reasoning is applied in one place and abandoned in the other.

**The conclusion contradicts the body.** After establishing that Olympic lifting does not build more maximal strength than traditional programmes, the closing section says the benefits are "possibly even greater than that of a traditional strength training program." Those are not compatible claims.

**Fat loss.** Resistance training in general is said to reduce weight-regain risk and waist circumference, and exercise to increase sensitivity to fullness cues. Both are plausible and both sit on markers that land in the broken region of the numbering. The RMR debunk that follows is the better-supported part of that section and the reference list does contain the classic paper on skeletal muscle's contribution to resting energy expenditure.

**A stray specificity claim** worth noting for its numbers: the strongest lifters move >250 kg in the clean & jerk and >200 kg in the snatch at barbell velocities above 2 m/s. (The original text has a typo, ">200k g".)

## What they sell

Everything. The article exists to sell two templates, and it also links personalised coaching, the beginner template, Strength I, the general strength and conditioning template, the powerlifting template category, the bodybuilding template category, the forum and four social channels. The "Why Should You Train With Barbell Medicine?" section sits above the article's actual content. None of the training-science material is wrong because of this, and much of it is honest against interest — but the framing that there are two best Olympic weightlifting programmes in the world and both are theirs is marketing, not analysis.

**Flagged:** the strongest overreach is the bone-density claim, stated causally on cross-sectional group-comparison evidence in an article that elsewhere flags exactly that confound. The broken numbering compounds the problem: readers who follow a marker to check any of the specific figures — the injury rate, the 13–36% power difference, the 67% fibre proportion, the calories-per-kilogram numbers — will land on the wrong paper.
