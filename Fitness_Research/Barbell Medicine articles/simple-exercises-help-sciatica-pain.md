# Sciatica Exercises That Work as Symptom Relief, Not as Repair

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Simple Exercises to help with Sciatica Pain" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/simple-exercises-for-sciatica-pain-relief/)) · Charlie Dickson · 2024-03-06 · 1,530 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **Read the red flags first.** Four situations warrant a doctor before self-managing: a fall or injury after which you cannot stand or bear weight; **new bowel or bladder problems, or altered sensation in the groin** (cauda equina — this is the emergency); back pain with unexplained weight loss, fever, recent illness or a cancer history; and leg pain that is progressively worsening. Everything else in the article is contingent on none of these applying.
- The framing is honest and unusual: these are **symptom-modification exercises** [2] — light or unweighted, limited range, small area — aimed at reducing symptoms and improving function *now*. They are not presented as fixing anything structural.
- **Dickson explicitly undercuts the standard rationale for his own exercise list.** Trunk work is normally sold as building "core stability", but there is no consensus definition of core stability, and the evidence does not show these exercises reliably prevent injury or reduce pain **more than any other kind of exercise** [3]. See the companion note `core-stability-matter.md`.
- The load-bearing sentence: **"it's far more important that you move rather than doing specific movements."** No exercise here is necessary; most cases improve with time, patience and graded exposure.
- **Traditional training should return early**, modified rather than removed — the article gives an explicit modification grid rather than a rest prescription.

## Prognosis, and what it means for the advice
Sciatica has no official definition; it is used loosely for back pain travelling down the leg, presumed nerve or nerve-root related. **Radiculopathy** is the loss of normal nerve function, associated with pins and needles or a dull burning ache (Bogduk 2009 [1] — a definitions and physiology paper, so it supports terminology, not treatment).

The prognosis numbers are the most decision-relevant content in the article: non-specific low back pain resolves in **6–8 weeks on average**, while radicular symptoms are more variable — roughly **a third recover quickly, a third improve somewhat, and a third stay the same or get worse**. That last third is a large fraction and it justifies the red-flag list. **Flagged: neither the 6–8 week figure nor the thirds are cited.** For a clinical article with only three references, the two numbers a reader will actually act on are unsourced.

## The exercises and their doses
Presented as four categories, all bodyweight, all "challenging but not more painful than when you started":

| Category | Examples | Prescription |
|---|---|---|
| Trunk strengthening | Bird dogs, planks, sit-ups | 2–4 × 10–20 reps (planks: 2–4 × 10–30 s holds) |
| Nerve glides | Sciatic, femoral | 10 × 5-second holds through a tolerable range |
| Repeated motions | Cobra push-ups, Jefferson curls, cat-cow | 2–4 × 10–20 reps |
| General movement | Walking, stretching, yoga | Three 20-minute walks a day; 3 × 30 s on 5–6 stretches |

The stated mechanism is not tissue-specific. The value is **non-threatening movement that builds confidence** in someone who is currently in pain and afraid of moving — Lehman's symptom-modification framing [2]. That is a psychological and behavioural mechanism, and Dickson says so rather than dressing it up as decompressing a nerve or reactivating a muscle. Practically: experiment, find which ones give you relief, use those daily or before training.

## Returning to normal training
The article's second half is the part most sources omit. Traditional loaded exercise "should likely be a part of rehabilitation from early in the process" — if you feel up to more after the light work, do it. The modification grid:

| Variable | Normal | Modified |
|---|---|---|
| Exercise | Barbell squat | Goblet squat |
| Volume | 5 × 5 | 3 × 12 |
| Intensity | RPE 6–8 | RPE 5–7 |
| Tempo | Fast | 3-1-0 (3 s down, 1 s pause, normal up) |

The target is a session completed **without a significant increase in symptoms**, repeated until confidence returns. Note that this is a "we recommend" — it is the source's clinical approach, consistent with graded-exposure principles, but no trial is cited for the specific parameters.

**Flagged — the sales close contradicts the article.** The final section claims "some of the exercises we've listed today are best done until failure, so it's crucial to program correctly to avoid injury or fatigue", which contradicts the body, where everything is bodyweight and explicitly kept below a symptom threshold. It then offers "the perfect hypertrophy program to maximize your gains" — boilerplate that has nothing to do with sciatica. **They sell things:** readers are directed to Barbell Medicine's paid low back pain rehab programme and to paid Pain & Rehab consultations. The advice in the body of the article does not depend on buying either, and the article's own conclusion — that time and general movement do most of the work — cuts against the pitch.
