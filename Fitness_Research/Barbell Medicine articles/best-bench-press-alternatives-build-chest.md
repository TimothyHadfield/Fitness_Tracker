# Seven Bench Press Alternatives, and Why "Best" Here Means Specificity Not Growth

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The 7 Best Bench Press Alternatives to Build Your Chest" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-best-bench-press-alternatives/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-08-08 · 2,961 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **This is not a hypertrophy ranking.** The seven are not ordered by growth outcomes and no study here compared their hypertrophy. The organising principle is **strength specificity** — pick the substitute closest to the lift you actually want to improve. Only two of the six references measured training adaptations at all; the rest are activation studies.
- The seven: **close-grip bench, floor press, incline bench, overhead press, machine chest press, dip, push-up.** All classed as "primary" movements, all prescribed at 2–4 sets (2–5 for push-ups) of 4–8 reps at RPE 6–8, two to four reps short of failure.
- **They actively refuse two popular claims.** The incline bench does not have evidence behind the "builds upper pecs better" story, and the vertical-versus-forward-lean dip distinction is called **"speculative"** with a recommendation to try both and pick by preference. That restraint is the best thing in the article.
- **Machines are not inferior.** Hernández-Belmonte 2023 (ref 4) found free-weight and machine training equally effective for strength and hypertrophy — with the caveat the article states correctly, that strength gains are specific to the mode trained.
- **Push-ups are a real load, not a warm-up.** A full push-up supports **~64% of body weight**, a kneeling push-up **~49%** (Suprak 2011, ref 5), and progressive calisthenic push-up training does increase strength and muscle thickness (Kotarsky 2018, ref 6). This is the article's strongest evidence-to-claim fit.
- No product is pitched. Internal links only — an unusually clean article for this source.

## Which claims have evidence under them

The mapping is **verified**, so the four numbered markers really do sit where they appear. All four fit reasonably.

**Refs 1–3, on bench angle.** Saeterbakken 2017 (EMG and performance in competitive athletes), Christian 2023 (upper-extremity activation across chest press modalities) and Chaves 2020 (horizontal vs incline bench, neuromuscular adaptations in untrained young men) sit under the sentence *"the more vertical the angle, the more similar it is to the overhead press."* Two are activation studies; Chaves is a training study. The claim they support is about **mechanical similarity**, not growth — which is exactly how the article uses it. It then draws the practical inference itself: a ~15° incline transfers better to flat bench, a 45–70° incline better to overhead press. That inference is reasoning from specificity, not a finding.

**Ref 4, on machines.** Hernández-Belmonte et al. randomised free-weight against machine-based training and found strength and hypertrophy comparable. The article uses it precisely, including the direction-of-specificity caveat: barbell training improves the barbell lift more, machine training improves the machine lift more. This is the one place where the citation is stronger than the claim needed.

**Refs 5–6, on push-ups.** Suprak's percentage-of-body-mass figures are measured, not estimated, and the article quotes them correctly. Kotarsky's progressive calisthenic push-up trial supports the progression logic. Together they justify treating the push-up as a loadable primary exercise rather than a filler movement — the article's most defensible position.

**Unsupported by citation, but honest about it:** the floor press "overload variation" claim carries no reference; the article says it *"may help improve strength"* and leaves it there. The claim that dips target pec major, pec minor, anterior delt and triceps is anatomical background.

## Advice versus evidence

Almost everything programmatic here is **"we recommend"**, and the article does not pretend otherwise. The RPE 6–8 / 2–4 reps-in-reserve prescription applied uniformly across all seven exercises is a house convention, not a study finding. The movement-category label ("primary") is Barbell Medicine's own programming taxonomy.

The summary states the governing rule cleanly: *"strength is measured in specific contexts"* — substitute a near-neighbour if you want bench press strength, use variety if you want general upper-body strength. That is the actual answer to the question in the title, and it is a selection principle rather than a hypertrophy claim.

**Where they push back on the industry, they do it with the right level of confidence.** On the incline bench they say the evidence "doesn't really support" superior upper-pec growth — a claim about absence of evidence, correctly phrased. On dip lean angle they say the research has not been done and recommend personal preference. Neither is overstated into "angle does not matter."

**Flagged, mildly:** grouping the overhead press among "bench press alternatives to build your chest" is a stretch the article half-acknowledges — it notes overhead presses "don't train the muscles of the chest as much as the other more horizontal options." It earns its place on transfer and pressing strength, not on chest development.
