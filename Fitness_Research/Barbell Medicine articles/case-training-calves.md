# Train Calves for Achilles Tolerance and Walking Function, Not Just Looks

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "A Case for Training Calves" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/a-case-for-training-calves/)) · Michael Amato · 2021-01-15 · 768 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- The case for calf training here is **functional, not aesthetic**: running mechanics, Achilles tendinopathy risk, and walking capacity in older adults.
- The plantarflexors generate **higher ground reaction forces than the quadriceps** across running speeds, which the author reads as running being predominantly an ankle activity in force terms (Schache 2014).
- From walking at 1.5 mph to running at 11.0 mph, **peak ankle power rises 23-fold while peak soleus activity rises only 3.5-fold** (Lai 2015) — the soleus shifts toward near-isometric force generation, transferring power through the Achilles.
- **Cross-sectional** data: healthy runners produced **200% bodyweight** plantarflexor torque; runners with *unilateral* Achilles tendinopathy managed only **~130%**, and were deficient **on both limbs** (O'Neill 2019). The design cannot say whether weakness caused the tendinopathy or followed it.
- In healthy older adults, **plantarflexor strength predicted walking capacity and speed better than lower-limb lean mass or leg extension strength/power** (Stenroth 2015) — leg extension power a close second.
- Prescription: **two movements, one knee-extended and one knee-flexed**, 1–2×/week, 2–3 sets of 8 reps at RPE 7–8.

## The anatomy the argument turns on
The gastrocnemius crosses both knee and ankle; the soleus crosses only the ankle. Both feed the Achilles. Because the soleus is unaffected by knee position, it retains force output with the knee flexed, and it has the larger cross-sectional area and higher force capacity (Dorn 2012). That is the basis for programming a bent-knee variation.

## Where this sits against the rest of the library

**Agreement.** The knee-angle logic matches the House of Hypertrophy calf guide (*Bigger Calves: The ULTIMATE Guide*), which finds straight-leg raises clearly superior for gastrocnemius growth while three growth studies plus a swelling study point to the soleus responding at least as well, and possibly better, to the bent-leg variation. Both land on "train both angles" — Barbell Medicine in 2021 from force-production anatomy, the hypertrophy evidence in 2023–2025.

**Clash.** Two, both in the prescription rather than the anatomy:
- **RPE 7–8** leaves 2–3 reps in reserve. The calf hypertrophy trials held in the library trained **to failure**, and the volume study showed growth still climbing at 12 weekly sets. Two to three sets of 8 at RPE 7–8 twice a week is a maintenance-to-modest dose by that standard. It is defensible for a tendon-tolerance goal appended to a GPP day, but it is not a growth prescription and the article does not claim it is.
- **Range of motion is never mentioned.** The library's largest single calf effect — 126.9% greater medial gastrocnemius growth from lengthened partials versus full ROM — postdates this article, so this is a gap rather than a contradiction.

Separately, the article uses the O'Neill bilateral-deficit finding to motivate strengthening as protection against tendinopathy. It is a **cross-sectional comparison**, so "protective" outruns the design; the author does hedge the soleus interpretation as a hypothesis, which is the right level.

**Product mention:** readers are routed to Barbell Medicine's own training programs (as a GPP-day addition) and to their paid Pain & Rehab division for anyone already dealing with Achilles tendinopathy.

## The programme as given
- **Day 1:** seated barbell calf raise — 2–3 × 8 @ RPE 7–8 (knee flexed, soleus-biased)
- **Day 2:** single-leg deficit calf raise — 2–3 × 8/side @ RPE 7–8 (knee extended)

The deficit is the detail worth keeping: it adds stretch at the bottom, where the later ROM evidence says the gastrocnemius stimulus lives.
