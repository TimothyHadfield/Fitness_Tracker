## Bottom line
- **Scale weight is a poor instrument, for concrete reasons.** Glycogen swings 300–500 g on the last 48 hours of eating and each gram carries 3–4 g of intracellular water, so a carb-heavy weekend adds 2–4 lb with no change in fat tissue. Muscle is denser than fat (≈1.06 vs 0.9 g/mL), so a pound of fat takes 15–20% more space — recomposition makes you physically smaller while the scale sits still.
- **They rank the three mechanistic theories by evidence, and the winner undercuts the headline.** The best-supported account is that visceral fat is a **marker** that safe subcutaneous storage capacity has been exceeded, not an independent cause — the overflow goes to the liver, and hepatic fat does the damage.
- **The waist is a repeatable proxy, not a precise one.** Correlation with imaged visceral fat runs **0.4 to 0.75** by site and population; a *change* in waist captures only about **40% of the change** in visceral fat; and waist correlates more strongly with subcutaneous than visceral fat at every measurement site.
- **Exercise reduces visceral fat without weight loss; diet restriction largely does not.** A 2016 meta-analysis gives roughly **6.1% visceral reduction from exercise alone versus 1.1% from diet alone**. Diet plus exercise produced ~5.7 cm of waist reduction versus 2.2 cm from diet alone, and exercise roughly halved lean-mass loss (20–30% of weight lost on diet alone, 10–15% with exercise).
- **The GLP-1 lean-mass panic is substantially a DXA artefact.** DXA counts everything that is not fat or bone as lean — water, glycogen, organ tissue, intramuscular fat — so fluid shifts and *reductions in myosteatosis* (a favourable change) both score as lean-mass loss.

## The three theories, ranked

This is the most rigorous section in the article and the reason to keep it.

**1. Overspill / ectopic fat (strongest evidence).** Every person has a critical fat-storage threshold; above it, fat overflows into ectopic sites, principally the liver. When fatty liver and visceral fat are entered into the same model, **fatty liver predicts metabolic syndrome with an odds ratio above 70 while visceral fat loses independent significance entirely.** Omentectomy trials — surgically removing large amounts of visceral fat — deliver no consistent benefit beyond the weight loss.

**2. Portal theory.** Abdominal depots drain through the portal vein straight into the liver, delivering free fatty acids and cytokines at concentrations the systemic circulation never sees — portal interleukin-6 runs **roughly 50% above systemic levels**. Real, but the same omentectomy trials weaken it: severing the portal route surgically does not consistently improve hepatic metabolism beyond weight loss.

**3. Aromatase feed-forward loop.** Visceral fat overexpresses aromatase, converting testosterone to estradiol; estradiol suppresses the HPG axis; lower testosterone makes visceral adipocytes better at accumulating fat; more fat, more aromatase. The article calls this "well-validated" but places it as a secondary accelerator rather than the foundational driver — the correct placement.

Downstream, the adipokine signature: **PAI-1** impairs clot breakdown (thrombotic risk), **angiotensinogen** feeds the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (hypertension), and **adiponectin** — which raises insulin sensitivity, reduces hepatic glucose output and protects vessels — is underproduced.

None of these figures carries a citation. They are traceable in principle (Tchernof & Després 2013 and an omentectomy trial are in the list) but the article connects nothing.

## Measurement, with the trade-offs stated

Three landmarks, not interchangeable: **NHANES/NIH** (iliac crest at the midaxillary line) reads ~2 cm higher than the WHO site in women; **WHO** (midpoint between lowest rib and iliac crest) has the **best correlation with visceral fat of the three** and is the site behind the published thresholds; **umbilicus** is most practical for self-tracking but migrates downward as abdominal fat accumulates and typically reads 2–4 cm higher in people carrying central fat.

**Action Level 1** is 94 cm (37 in) for men, 80 cm (31.5 in) for women — where risk of adiposity-related disease is likely increased. **Action Level 2** is 102 cm (40 in) and 88 cm (35 in), where fat reduction is likely to improve health, which they correctly call "a late find." Ethnic adjustments: roughly 85–90 cm (men) and 75–80 cm (women) for East and South Asian populations, tighter still for many South Asian groups; standard thresholds may *overestimate* risk in people of West African descent. **Waist-to-height ratio below 0.5** (and above 0.4) is associated with substantially lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality across virtually every population studied and outperforms BMI across ethnicities — it is one of Barbell Medicine's **Vital 5**, their own free health-metric framework.

The concept carrying the most clinical weight is the **personal fat threshold**: published cut-offs are flags, not thresholds, because an individual's own threshold can sit well above or below them, and no measurement identifies it in advance — it announces itself in labs (liver enzymes, fasting glucose, triglycerides, TyG index). Their rule: Action Level 1 is a reasonable trigger to begin weight loss in someone without adiposity-related disease, while someone who *already has* impaired glucose regulation, dyslipidaemia, hypertension or fatty liver has crossed their threshold and likely benefits regardless of the tape.

The **weight-to-waist ratio** — kilograms lost per centimetre of waist reduced — is the most practically useful device here. General reference ~0.7 kg/cm; well-structured programmes run 0.4–0.6; **a ratio above 1.0 sustained over 8–12 weeks signals lean tissue loss** and calls for protein (1.6–2.4 g/kg/day) and progressive resistance training. Women show a less favourable ratio early (~2.8 cm per 3 kg versus 3.5 cm in men) because peripheral subcutaneous fat mobilises before abdominal depots. The article states plainly that these figures derive primarily from male populations, which is the right disclosure.

## Exercise, and the honest caveat about the tape

Two mechanisms are offered for why exercise hits visceral fat specifically: visceral adipocytes carry a higher density of **beta-3 adrenergic receptors** and a lower density of the alpha-2 receptors that brake fat mobilisation, so the catecholamine surge of vigorous exercise mobilises them preferentially; and contracting muscle releases **IL-6 as a myokine at 5–30 times resting levels**, acting on adipose tissue independent of caloric deficit — a pathway dietary restriction cannot access. Dosing: **~150 min/week of moderate aerobic work at 60–80% max heart rate**, with dose-response above that. Resistance training does a different job — protecting lean mass in a deficit and appearing more protective against age-related waist gain over years.

Then the caveat, the article at its best: **waist circumference is not very responsive to exercise alone.** In a randomised trial of 300 adults, doubling prescribed exercise energy expenditure produced no additional waist reduction, though every exercise arm lost about 5 cm versus non-exercisers. Because the tape reads mostly subcutaneous fat, and visceral fat is a small share of the total, a real 6% visceral reduction can simply fail to appear at the waist.

## GLP-1s, testosterone and body composition

Lean-mass fractions by DXA: **STEP-1** (semaglutide, Wilding, NEJM 2021) ~39% of weight lost over 68 weeks; **SURMOUNT-1** (tirzepatide, Jastreboff, NEJM 2022) ~24%; **retatrutide** (*Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol* 2025) ~33% in a diabetic population. The argument is that these overstate skeletal muscle loss, because DXA cannot distinguish muscle from water, glycogen or intramuscular fat. The **SURPASS-3 MRI substudy** showed tirzepatide significantly reduced myosteatosis versus placebo — a favourable change DXA scores as lean loss. **SEMALEAN** reported a 4.5 kg *improvement* in handgrip strength at 12 months with sarcopenic obesity prevalence falling from 49% to 33%, and a 2024 *Circulation* review concluded the muscle changes look adaptive rather than pathological. The **BELIEVE** trial (bimagrumab plus semaglutide) produced 22% total weight loss with 93% from fat mass versus 72% on semaglutide alone. Roughly **1 in 10** people maintain clinically significant weight loss at five years even in intensive lifestyle programmes, so the risk-benefit calculation has to include the risks of untreated obesity.

On testosterone they use three tiers. **Deficient** (confirmed low with symptoms): testosterone is rate-limiting, and restoring physiological levels adds roughly 1–3 kg of lean mass over 6 months. **Eugonadal** (~300–1000 ng/dL): no longer the limiting factor — men at the low and high ends of normal gain similar muscle from the same programme. **Supraphysiological**: a dose-dependent androgen-to-lean-mass relationship appears that is absent within the normal range, and the **Bhasin four-group study** showed the no-exercise testosterone arm gained more lean mass than the exercise-only arm. Two clinically useful notes: **Finkelstein** showed that blocking estradiol conversion in men with adequate testosterone caused fat gain *regardless of testosterone dose*, so aggressive aromatase suppression on TRT works against the metabolic goal of treatment; and exogenous testosterone suppresses the HPG axis, most men recovering within six months of stopping but some far longer or not at all after high doses.

## What they sell

The waist-to-height metric is linked to Barbell Medicine's **Vital 5 action plan**, their own framework. There is no template or coaching pitch here; the other links are to their free articles on cholesterol, blood pressure and fatty liver.

**Flagged:** the title and the "At a Glance" box overstate what the body concedes. "Waist circumference is the best practical proxy" sits a few paragraphs from the admission that waist correlates more strongly with subcutaneous than visceral fat at every site. The take-home recommends the umbilicus while the measurement section names the WHO midpoint as best-correlated — the repeatability trade-off is stated, but the two sections give different answers.
