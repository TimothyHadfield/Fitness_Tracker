# Health, labs and medicine

Thirty-two notes on the material no other source in this library touches: lab results, lipids, blood
pressure, hormones, GLP-1 drugs, body measurement, ageing and pregnancy. Written by practising
physicians — Baraki and Feigenbaum are both MDs — which is exactly why the gap between *what the
evidence shows* and *what we recommend* is widest here: a clinician is licensed to advise on judgement
where no trial exists, and these articles do it constantly, sometimes flagged and sometimes not.
**Four of the thirty-two carry no citations at all.** Organised by the question you arrived with.

---

## My blood test came back abnormal

The most useful cluster in the source. **A lifter's out-of-range creatinine, transaminases and CK are
routinely muscle-derived rather than disease** — with hard numbers above which that excuse fails.

### Creatinine

[Creatinine is a muscle metabolite used as a kidney test](abnormal-labs-exercise-part-i-kidney-function.md)
— the breakdown product of creatine in skeletal muscle. It is stable only while muscle mass, diet and
filtration are all stable, and in a lifter one of those is moving. Kidneys filter around **125 mL/min**
(normal quoted as **120 mL/min/1.73 m²**), cycling ~3 L of plasma **about 60 times a day**. GFR is
inferred via **CKD-EPI**, an equation fitted on populations that cannot tell "filtration fell" from
"muscle mass rose."

**The ceiling is 1.5 mg/dL (133 µmol/L).** Even people who train will not exceed it without impairment,
and values above it "**should always be evaluated further**," attributable to muscularity or diet only
after other causes are confidently excluded. **1.4–1.5 mg/dL against a typical 0.7–1.2 mg/dL range** is
the realistic muscularity case; below the line impairment is still possible.

Two things discriminate. **A prior value beats a threshold**: 1.3 today and 1.3 five years ago is
reassuring, but **0.8 → 1.4 warrants evaluation even if muscle was gained in between**. **Cystatin C**
is a filtration marker unaffected by muscle mass — normal beside a muscular lifter's creatinine-driven
"abnormal" eGFR, raised alongside creatinine in genuine impairment. That decisive test is why the kidney
case is cleaner than the liver one. Then exposures: dehydration, **NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen)** named
as a lifter habit that damages kidneys at high doses over long periods, plus antibiotics, PPIs and
diuretics; steroids and adulterated supplements cause outright injury. **Urinalysis with microscopy** is
the next step. **Creatine supplementation is a separate confound** — metabolising a load raises plasma
creatinine with no GFR change, worst near dosing; pure creatine has never been shown to damage kidneys,
which is absence of evidence in short-to-medium trials in healthy people, not a safety profile in kidney
disease.

**The mirror-image trap gets missed more often.** Very low muscle mass may baseline below **0.4 mg/dL**;
kidney failure taking that to **1.0 mg/dL** looks normal while representing real decline. The claim that
**below 0.4 mg/dL (35 µmol/L)** carries increased complications and death rests on a **critically ill ICU
cohort** — sound mechanism, poor basis for a gym-goer. **Chronic hypertension and diabetes are the
commonest causes of chronic kidney disease.** Sells nothing.

### AST, ALT and the "liver panel"

**AST and ALT are not liver-exclusive.** AST sits in liver, skeletal and cardiac muscle, red cells,
brain, pancreas, kidney and lung; ALT is predominantly hepatic but also in muscle, and because total
muscle mass vastly exceeds liver mass, muscle contributes materially.
[The short version](exercise-affect-liver-function-tests.md) gives normal ALT as **29–33 U/L for men,
19–25 U/L for women** (ACG guideline) — lower than most labs report.

Magnitude and timeline rest on **one experiment: Pettersson 2008, n=15 healthy men with no lifting
experience, one session, no control.** All showed significant rises; **AST/ALT exceeded 1.0 in every
subject**; all five markers (AST, ALT, LDH, CK, myoglobin) stayed elevated **at least seven days**. The
short article puts the peak at **3–4 days** normalising by **7–12 days**;
[the longer case-based article](elevated-liver-enzymes-after-exercise-means.md) has enzymes still
**rising for up to 48 hours**, peaking **4–7 days** (ALT later than AST, half-life ~47 h), baseline only
at **10–12 days** — so a regular lifter is never outside the window. Same study, two different peaks.

The case: ALT and AST at **1.5–2× ULN** with mild ALP and GGT elevation for apparently twelve years,
every draw after a morning workout. **One week off normalised the panel.** Three functional categories:
**hepatocellular (ALT, AST)** leak from eccentric sarcolemmal tearing; **cholestatic (ALP, bilirubin)** —
ALP also comes from bone, placenta, prostate and gut, and the **bone isoform peaks about 20 minutes
post-workout**, so raised ALP with raised GGT means hepatobiliary and with normal GGT means bone; and
**synthetic (albumin, INR)**, which is the discriminator — **both unaffected by exercise, and a normal
INR effectively excludes acute liver failure**. The hypothesis that GGT is absent from muscle is
undercut by the article's own case (this patient's GGT was raised): **a normal GGT supports the exercise
explanation, an elevated one does not rule it out.** **AST > ALT points away from liver; ALT > AST
toward it.** The physician-behaviour evidence is **Tiller & Stringer 2023**: **56% of surveyed physicians
did not list exercise-induced muscle damage in their differential, over 60% named primary liver disease**
— a survey of reasoning, not outcomes, but the right sort for a knowledge-gap thesis.

**The defect: three escalation thresholds in one article.** The body sets "substantially less plausible"
at **>5× ULN**, the FAQ sets urgent evaluation at **>10× ULN**, the patient scripts invoke **<3× ULN** as
the expectant-observation range. Someone taking the FAQ number could sit on a 7× elevation. **Use >5×
ULN.** Timing is inconsistent too: uninterpretable without **10–12 days** rest, but retest recommended at
**one week** (7–10 days in the FAQ).

**Investigate regardless of training:** transaminases **>5× ULN**; **elevated bilirubin** (jaundice is
never a normal exercise response); **rising INR**; a **purely cholestatic** pattern; symptoms (fatigue,
right upper quadrant pain, weight loss, dark urine, pruritus); **worsening on repeat despite rest**.
Exclude first: MASLD/NAFLD (commonest US cause), alcohol (**AST/ALT >2** classic, intake under-reported),
**supplement-induced injury** (oral anabolic steroids highest risk, plus ephedra, synephrine with
caffeine, Hydroxycut, Garcinia Cambogia, Herbalife, herbals), hepatitis B/C, drug injury from statins,
NSAIDs, antibiotics, anticonvulsants. Ask about supplements **repeatedly and specifically** — patients do
not classify protein powders or pre-workouts as medications.

**The recommendation — retest after a full week off, adding CK and LDH, before imaging or referral — is
clinical judgement, untested as a pathway.** The takeaway that "**100% of people who perform resistance
training will show some elevation**" rests on fifteen untrained men. **Its reference list is internally
corrupt** (see Weak points), and it links to **paid Medical Consultations**.

### CK and rhabdomyolysis

[Rhabdomyolysis is a clinical diagnosis, not a lab value](rhabdomyolysis-causes-treatments-return-sport.md).
**The classic triad appears together in fewer than 10% of cases**, and **over half of patients report
neither pain nor weakness**. Absence of soreness is not reassurance.

**CK >5× ULN is sensitive but not specific**: below it you can be confident it is absent, above it you
have learned little, because **athletes routinely exceed it in normal training**. Normal is **26–140
U/L**; rhabdomyolysis runs **1,500 to over 100,000 U/L**. **CK is non-toxic — myoglobin obstructs the
renal tubules.** Between DOMS and rhabdomyolysis sits **HyperCKemia**: CK above 5× ULN with urinary
myoglobin, no symptoms, no organ injury — seen in military personnel and Division I footballers.
**Kinetics:** rises **2–12 h**, peaks **24–72 h**, falls from **3–5 days**, half-life **~1.5 days**,
dropping **40–50% of the previous day's value** — a genuinely useful heuristic **stated without a
citation**. **Discharge is not decided by CK**: two reviews (30 and 41 cases) discharged at
**1,410–94,665** and **10–61,617 U/L** with no link to readmission.

**Return criteria: no symptoms, CK below 5× ULN, normal urinalysis**, plus four gates — syndrome
resolved, episode adequately explained (an episode from light housework is *not* explained and warrants
work-up), risk factors addressed, graded return planned. **Not recovered within one week of rest means
higher recurrence risk.** The four-phase protocol is explicitly labelled expert opinion: **Phase 1**
(≥72 h) rest, 7–8 h sleep, fluids, increased sodium, no lifting; **Phase 2** (days 3–7, gated on CK
<5× ULN and normal urinalysis) walking and resistance work **below 20–25% 1RM**, with **NSAIDs and
acetaminophen avoided so pain stays a usable progression signal** — a judgement call trading comfort for
information; **Phase 3** **50–75% 1RM**, agility **70–80%** of maximal, running **50–75%** of normal
distance; **Phase 4** full training. Statins almost never cause it: **102 cases in over a million users
(0.009%)**, interactions responsible for nearly half; in a blinded trial of "statin-intolerant" patients
**25% reported muscle pain on statin and placebo alike**; community intolerance **8–12%** against ~**5%**
blinded. Risk-benefit: per 10,000 patients with cardiovascular disease over five years, **~5 cases of
muscle damage against ~1,000 major vascular events prevented**.

## My cholesterol is high

The best-cited work in the batch. Organising claim: **risk is cumulative exposure, not today's number —
"the lower, the longer, the better."**

**[The basic guide](cholesterol-causes-prevention-treatment.md).** Lipoproteins below **70 nm** cross the
artery wall and LDL runs **20–30 nm**, so **every** LDL particle qualifies — the fact that later
dismantles the size argument. Read **non-HDL-C (total − HDL-C)**; target **under 130 mg/dL**, lower for
high risk, with no level "too low." **ApoB is better** (particles, not cargo). **HDL-C is demoted to a
marker** — trials raising it do not reduce events and Mendelian randomisation finds no causal effect,
while LDL passes both tests. **Triglycerides above 500 mg/dL** are urgent in their own right. **LDL-C
above 190** raises suspicion of familial hypercholesterolaemia. **Hypothyroidism occurs in 1.4–13% of
patients with high cholesterol.** **Diet is two levers: fibre ≥30 g/day and fat *type*, not fat total** —
replacing saturated with unsaturated beats replacing it with carbohydrate, even high-fibre carbohydrate
(Li 2015, cohort). The **"18–20% of calories" saturated-fat threshold carries no citation.** Retest at
**2–4 months**. Their heterodoxy: **rejecting the 10-year risk window for younger people** in favour of
30-year/lifetime estimation — a policy preference, not a guideline position, that would make more young
people statin-eligible. Sells nothing.

**[Part 2, the myths article](cholesterol-myths-misconceptions.md)**, is where the evidence is densest.
**LDL-C tracks plaque linearly inside the normal range**: among **1,779 people with no conventional risk
factors**, half had plaque, rising from **11% at LDL-C 60–70 mg/dL to 64% at 150–160**; below **70 mg/dL**
progression halts. **1 mmol/L (~38 mg/dL) of lowering yields 22–24% fewer events regardless of how it is
achieved.** Safety: LDL-C to **6.9 mg/dL** in a FOURIER analysis with no excess death, cancer, cognitive
decline, muscle problems or hypogonadism, backed by Mendelian randomisation. Anchors: **newborn LDL-C
~30 mg/dL**, hunter-gatherers **35–70 mg/dL** lifelong, US average near **130**. **HPS randomised 20,536**
with no dementia difference over five years including the 75–85 subgroup; **PROSPER n=5,804** no cognitive
difference; no randomised cancer signal either way. On size: two patients at LDL-C 140, pattern A (>25 nm,
~27 nm mean) and B (<25 nm, down to 18 nm) — patient 2 is higher risk only because smaller particles carry
less cholesterol each, so more are needed; adjust for number and size vanishes. **ApoB earns its place
through discordance** — high LDL-C with low apoB is low risk, low LDL-C with high apoB is high risk, and
discordance is commonest in insulin resistance and diabetes, exactly the population most likely to be
falsely reassured.

**On CAC they recommend against universal screening.** Zero means much lower short-to-medium risk, but
calcification is *late*: soft plaque is invisible, and **a quarter to a third of events in young people
occur at CAC 0**; calcium also rises benignly with high-intensity exercise and with statins, both of which
*reduce* risk. **Zero → repeat in 5–10 years; above zero → don't re-scan, treat the risk factors.** More
sceptical than the 2018 ACC/AHA position. **Their sharpest methodological point:** LDL-C and total
cholesterol are **negative acute-phase reactants** (with albumin, vitamin D and testosterone), so
cholesterol drawn on admission for a heart attack reads falsely low — manufacturing much of the confusing
"low cholesterol, severe illness" literature. **Statin muscle symptoms**: objective damage in well under
1%; in a trial of over 10,000 patients symptoms matched placebo *while blinded* and diverged only once
patients knew; **SAMSON** (60 prior quitters cycling statin, placebo and no pill, rating symptoms 0–100
daily) found statin and placebo indistinguishable and **both roughly double the no-pill months, 90% of
statin symptoms also reported on placebo**.

**Saturated fat is the weakest section, and the critique runs one way.** Three legitimate objections:
**insufficient contrast** (PURE's Asian quintiles ran 2–7% of calories with a top quintile at 12%; Malmö
compared 13% against 22%, its own authors writing the hypothesis "is thus not fully testable in this
population"); **replacement nutrient ignored** (Sydney Diet-Heart's "polyunsaturated" arm was 1960s
trans-fat margarine); and **over-adjustment** (adjusting for LDL-C controls away the mediator and
guarantees a null — the most transferable idea in the piece). But null studies get three pages of
dissection and supporting ones none, and **Hooper 2020, their own citation, finds a modest reduction in
combined events with little or no effect on mortality**. The **"18–20% versus <10%" threshold is an
established breakpoint in neither cited source** yet functions as the criterion for dismissing nulls.
Useful aside: **dairy saturated fat other than butter behaves differently**, attributed to milk fat
globule membrane, which churning removes. **On seed oils** the human data are null: **551% more or 90%
less linoleic acid produced no change in tissue arachidonic acid**, radiotracer conversion **0.2–0.3%**;
**83 RCTs, >41,000 participants** show little or no inflammatory effect; **30 cohorts, 68,659 people**
using linoleic acid biomarkers found *lower* cardiovascular disease and death, **not one cohort showing a
significant increase**. "The theory must be rejected" is stronger than the evidence class — "unsupported"
is the defensible version. Heart disease death *rates* fell about **72% between 1950 and 2018**, premature
death at 25–64 about **70% since 1968**.

**[The action plan](lower-heart-disease-risk-action-plan-cholesterol.md) carries the drugs.** **ApoB below
90 mg/dL** for most, lower for high risk; the test costs **under $30**. Targets: **LDL-C <100 or <55;
non-HDL-C <130 or <70; ApoB <80 or <50; triglycerides <100 for everyone** — though non-HDL-C appears as
"<100–130," "<130" and "below 100" in three places. **Remnant cholesterol** (TC − HDL-C − LDL-C), flagged
by **triglycerides above 150**: **below 20 optimal, above 30 very high**. **HDL-C above ~97 mg/dL in men
and 135 in women is paradoxically associated with increased mortality.** FH flagged at **LDL-C >190 or
ApoB >130**. The anchor is **CTT: over 170,000 participants across 26 trials, 25–30% fewer major events
per 38 mg/dL (1 mmol/L)**. High-potency statins reach **65%** lowering at max dose but **each doubling
adds only ~6%** — hence **early combination over dose escalation**. **Ezetimibe** 10 mg adds **~20%**;
**bempedoic acid 25% alone, +18% on a statin, 40% with ezetimibe**; **PCSK9 inhibitors ~50% LDL-C plus
18–36% Lp(a)**, with FOURIER and ODYSSEY outcomes. **Pitavastatin** gets space: fewer interactions,
glucose-neutral, Lp(a)-neutral where other statins raise it **10–20%**, lowest muscle-symptom rate.
**Icosapent ethyl (REDUCE-IT)** has the omega-3 evidence; carboxylic acid formulations (STRENGTH) did not.
**Fibrates** for triglycerides above **~500 mg/dL**; **niacin rejected**. New-onset diabetes said to occur
"almost exclusively" in prediabetes or metabolic risk — **"concentrated in" would be accurate**. **The CAC
teaching inverts with age and is the best in the batch**: in a young adult **CAC >0 is highly informative**
and zero weak; in an older adult **zero is highly informative** and positive expected; in established
disease irrelevant. The CAC-in-athletes passage overreaches, reading exercise-raised calcium as plaque
stabilisation on a narrative review and two small composition studies. Diet: raise the P:S ratio, **30 g+
fibre**, and note **unfiltered coffee (French press, espresso) raises LDL via diterpenes**. Exercise:
**150–300 min moderate or 75–150 vigorous plus twice-weekly resistance training**.

**Supplements get a near-uniform rejection from a company that sells supplements** — red yeast rice
(same active compound as lovastatin with worse dosing control and contamination risk), citrus bergamot
(lab values, no outcomes) and **over-the-counter fish oil** all turned down. Only **phytosterols**:
**2–3 g/day lowers LDL-C 9–12% by cutting intestinal absorption 30–45%**, with a sitosterolemia caveat
affecting **~0.05%**. **The overreach:** "**a 1% reduction in plaque volume is associated with an 18%
reduction in major cardiovascular events**" closes the case for aggressive therapy with **no source at
all** — a surrogate doing an outcome's work, in an article whose other drug claims rest on hard endpoints.
Free lead-magnet PDF promoted four times; **paid consultations linked three times**.

**[Lp(a)](lipoprotein-levels-testing-treatment-heart-disease.md)** is an LDL particle carrying
apolipoprotein(a), making it a preferential carrier of oxidised phospholipids and pro-thrombotic and
anti-fibrinolytic. Per particle more atherogenic than LDL; in aggregate LDL still does more damage.
**Over 90% of the variance is *LPA* genetic, levels are set by age 5 and stable for life, and diet and
exercise do not move it** — so **test once, in every adult** (NLA and EAS positions; US ACC/AHA remains
more conservative). **Rule out below 30 mg/dL (75 nmol/L); rule in above 50 mg/dL (125 nmol/L); 30–50 is
a grey zone**, with risk continuous and cut-offs a convenience. **Never convert units by a fixed factor** —
multiplying mg/dL by 2.0–2.5 misclassifies risk because apo(a) isoform size varies; **nmol/L preferred**.
**Statins raise Lp(a) 10–20%** (net benefit still positive, and a poor LDL-C response in an adherent
patient may partly be Lp(a) counted inside measured LDL-C); **PCSK9 inhibitors lower it 20–30%**;
**niacin lowers it 20–30% and has repeatedly failed to add outcome benefit**; aspirin has a MESA signal
alongside **increased major bleeding**; apheresis works but is invasive. **Genetic estimates suggest
>50–100 mg/dL (>100–200 nmol/L) of lowering may be needed for a detectable effect in a 3–5 year trial.**
Management is **"compensatory risk reduction"** — a different treatment intensity for everything else.
**It calls Lp(a) "causal" flatly, then concedes the Lp(a) hypothesis is untested**; the later statement is
the accurate one. **No in-text markers at all** — a bare list of seven links, only Tsimikas 2017 resolving
— so every number is untraceable. Links **twice to paid physician consultation**, including as its final
line. Genuinely useful free resource: **lpaclinicalguidance.com**.

**[Statins and muscle](statins-muscle-they-affect-strength-growth.md).** **STOMP: 420 statin-naive adults
randomised to atorvastatin 80 mg or placebo for six months, no meaningful strength difference, CK about
21 U/L higher on statin.** **True drug-caused symptoms run 1–5% above placebo in trials against 10–20% in
clinic.** Three real mechanisms amplified by exercise: **CoQ10 depletion** (plasma falls **16–54%**;
largest trial **38% atorvastatin, 27% lovastatin**, with the correct caveat that intramuscular data are
less consistent); **isoprenoid depletion** weakening sarcolemmal resilience against eccentric load
(restoring isoprenoids reverses statin-induced cell death while CoQ10 alone does not — an elegant argument
against the popular supplement fix); and **ryanodine-receptor calcium leak**. Risk stacks: dose, drug
interaction, an **SLCO1B1** variant, **age over 65**, hypothyroidism, kidney disease, exercise. **The 2026
ACC/AHA dyslipidaemia guideline names vigorous exercise as a SAMS risk factor for the first time** — the
most useful new fact here. Illustrative case: a 43-year-old on fenofibrate plus atorvastatin who started a
home dumbbell programme and hit **CK 18,979 U/L**, hospitalised two days with normal kidney function, later
resuming atorvastatin monotherapy and training without recurrence. **Their test: CK while symptomatic,
5–7 days off training, repeat** — normalises on rest → training; stays up → the statin may contribute. A
heuristic, not a validated protocol. **CoQ10 supplementation is not supported** by meta-analysis or the
2026 guideline, though they add that trying it does "little harm," which is preference. Endurance detour,
well hedged: **marathon runners show CAC above 100 in ~36% of cases versus ~22% generally**, scaling with
volume in men not women, the most active still having the lowest all-cause mortality, and **atrial
fibrillation risk rising above roughly 2,000 lifetime hours** of vigorous endurance training. **Three
errors matter**: the takeaway claims no difference in **hypertrophy** when **STOMP never measured muscle
size**; **SAMSON is misattributed** to *NEJM* 2020 when the reference is Howard, *JACC* 2021, with the
design compressed (twelve randomised one-month periods, not three four-month blocks); and **reference [24]
contradicts the sentence it supports** (Allard 2021 shows training *improves* muscle performance in statin
users). **At least seven references point at unrelated papers.**

## My blood pressure is high

**Since 2017 ACC/AHA, hypertension means SBP ≥130 or DBP ≥80** (previously ≥140/90), with **≥120/<80
"elevated"** — a redefinition that moved millions into diagnosis. **1 in 3 US adults (108 million)** meet
criteria and **fewer than half are controlled**. [The longer guide](blood-pressure-causes-prevention-treatment.md)
bands it: below 120/80 normal, **120–129 elevated**, **130–139/80–89 stage 1**, above **140/90 stage 2**.
Blood pressure is the leading contributor to premature death worldwide at **13% of global deaths** — cited
to "WHO 2009" as a bare text string. The dose-response comes from Lewington 2002, **one million adults
across 61 prospective studies**. Most people have no symptoms, which is the whole argument for screening,
and Baraki names blood pressure as one of the very few screenings they recommend for everyone.

**"Lifting weights does not cause hypertension" is the sentence their audience most wants and the one
empirical claim in the article with no citation.** The physiology is uncontested — a Valsalva produces a
large brief spike — but the step to "these spikes do not raise *resting* pressure and act as a hormetic
stimulus" is asserted. The upshot is unambiguous: **people who already have hypertension can and should
still strength train.**

[The exercise-versus-drugs article](effect-resistance-training-high-blood-pressure.md) rests on **Naci
2019: a network meta-analysis of 391 RCTs — 197 exercise, 194 drug — totalling 39,742 subjects.** In
hypertensive subgroups **exercise lowered SBP 8.96 mmHg against 8.8 for drugs**; **endurance −8.69,
resistance −7.83, combined −13.5**. Across all 197 exercise trials including normotensives, only **−4.84**
(endurance −4.88, **resistance −3.5**, isometric −5.65, combined −6.49). **Higher starting pressure, more
effect. Intensity did not matter.** Earlier resistance-only work: Cornelissen 2011, **33 RCTs / 1,012
subjects**, about **−3.9 mmHg** on both numbers; de Sousa 2017, **5 RCTs / 201 subjects** in
prehypertensives and hypertensives, **−8.2 / −4.1** — converging with Naci's −7.83. Only **26%** of
diagnosed hypertensives are counselled to exercise at all, which is the article's real target.
**Two caveats: 8.96 versus 8.8 is not a difference**, and it is an **indirect comparison across separate
populations** — the drug trials were larger (29,281 subjects), older (mean 55 vs 50) and started higher
(mean 150 vs 132 mmHg), and **nobody has been randomised to exercise or a drug**. The outcomes also differ
in kind: hard endpoints for drugs, **millimetres of mercury over weeks** for exercise. To its credit the
article flags its own weak data — isometric results came from very small samples, with **no isometric
studies in the hypertensive subgroup at all**.

**The medication section is the strongest part.** First-line: thiazides (hydrochlorothiazide,
chlorthalidone) and mineralocorticoid antagonists (spironolactone), calcium channel blockers, ACE
inhibitors and ARBs. **One drug at standard dose 8.7 mmHg; doubling adds 1.5; two drugs 14.9; doubling
those adds 2.5; three at quarter dose 11.2; four at quarter dose 22.5 mmHg** — with fewer side effects
than high-dose monotherapy, and the honest counterweight that more pills is more burden. **Weight loss of
5–10% of body weight** significantly lowers pressure, with **roughly one pound ≈ one point** labelled a
rule of thumb. Sodium under **2,300 mg**, potassium **4,700 mg**, with the aside that milligram targets
are useless for telling people what to eat. **DASH works with and without weight loss.** Sleep
**7–7.5 hours**, and **screen everyone with high blood pressure for apnoea via STOP-BANG**. **Genetics
explains 30–50% of the variation**, framed correctly as not a failure of effort. Raising it: stimulants,
NSAIDs, oral contraceptives, anabolic steroids, EPO, contaminated supplements. **New guideline point: screen
all newly diagnosed patients for aldosterone disorders**, a change from reserving it for resistant or
young-onset cases. **White-coat hypertension** carries higher risk than persistent normotension and predicts
sustained hypertension over a decade; **masked hypertension** is more dangerous because undiagnosed. For
athletes, **all diuretics are banned and beta blockers are banned in precision sports**. **Sells nothing.**

[Measuring it](measure-blood-pressure-correctly.md): **a high office reading is not a diagnosis** —
out-of-office confirmation is required. **Cuff size is the biggest error you control**: too small reads
high, too large low. By arm circumference: **22–26 cm → 12×22 small adult; 27–34 → 16×30 adult; 35–44 →
16×36 large; 45–52 → 16×42 extra large** — lifters should check they are not above the supplied range. Use
a **validated automatic device** (ValidateBP.org), not a wrist gadget. Quiet room, **chair with back
support**, artery line over the inner arm with the cuff's lower edge **2–3 cm above the elbow crease**, arm
**at heart height, palm up**, **legs uncrossed, feet flat**, **sit quietly five minutes**, then two
readings. For a home evaluation, **two readings morning and two evening for at least three days** (Bello
2018, the only primary citation). **Below 120/80 healthy; if diagnosed, target under 130/80, ideally under
120 systolic.** **24-hour ambulatory monitoring** confirms white-coat and masked hypertension. Check **at
least once a year**. **A high reading without symptoms is a primary-care problem, not an emergency** —
escalate only with chest pain, breathlessness, vision changes, confusion or stroke symptoms.

## I'm on a GLP-1

[The "40% muscle loss" headline is largely a measurement problem](glp-1-muscle-loss-ozempic-wegovy-mounjaro.md).
**38–39% of weight lost in STEP-1 (n=1,961, ~15% loss over 68 weeks) was classified "lean mass"** — a
**DXA output, not a muscle measurement**. DXA's "lean soft tissue" bucket contains muscle *plus* water,
glycogen, organs, connective tissue and intramuscular fat, assumes a fixed **~73% hydration** that breaks
during 15% weight loss, and has same-day precision of **0.3–2%**. Two observations carry it: **manipulating
only glycogen or creatine moves DXA lean mass 1–2%** (creatine raises total body water ~1.4%) with no
change in contractile protein — a critique that also deflates "added 5 lb of muscle in 8 weeks" — and
**the STEP-1 placebo group also lost DXA lean mass despite minimal weight change**, which looks like
instrument drift. Comparators: **SURMOUNT-1 ~25%**, **REDEFINE 1 semaglutide arm 28%**, retatrutide in
line with total loss. The **"75% fat / 25% lean" quarter rule is itself a DXA-era aggregate** of every
non-fat compartment.

**Where function was measured it held or improved.** **SEMALEAN** (n=106, mean BMI 46, mean age 52,
12 months max-dose semaglutide, **single-arm, no control, no prescribed exercise**): **fat −18%, lean −5%,
grip +4.5 kg, sarcopenic obesity 49% → 33%**, lean loss stabilising after month 7, a **4:1** fat-to-lean
ratio. That matters because **strength predicts hard endpoints**: EWGSOP2 makes strength primary; the SDOC
statements (**>18,000 participants** across eight epidemiologic studies and six RCTs) found DXA lean mass
did not predict falls, fractures, mobility limitation or mortality and dropped mass from the definition;
**PURE (139,691 adults) associated each 5 kg lower grip with 17% higher cardiovascular and 7% higher
non-cardiovascular mortality**.

**Two cautions.** **Table 1 is a model presented as a measurement** — decomposing a 3 kg lean-mass loss
into liver glycogen **0.3–0.8 kg**, liver fat **0.3–1.0 kg**, intramuscular fat **0.2–0.5 kg** and
**contractile muscle 0.2–0.5 kg**, with a footnote conceding "bounded estimates" from compartment modelling
and a **stated total (1.5–3.9 kg) that does not pin to the 3 kg it decomposes**. The "only 200–500 g was
muscle" line is the most quotable claim and the least measured. And **SEMALEAN carries most of the weight
for "function improves" as an uncontrolled cohort** — familiarisation and regression to the mean are not
excluded. **No pair-fed human trial exists**, so "the drug is catabolic" is untested; the one pair-fed
comparison (Langer 2026) is **mostly mice** with a small human arm, found no atrophy beyond matched loss,
and reported **DXA saying lean mass fell 13% where dissection put it near 10%**, **gastrocnemius mass
predicting ~37% of running-performance variance while total lean mass predicted almost none**, and
unchanged knee-extension and grip strength in humans despite reduced size. **Liver mass change accounted
for ~71% of the day-7 body-weight signal** in mice; the human bridge is **60–80% relative reductions in
intrahepatic triglyceride** in survodutide and retatrutide phase 2 trials — real, but no human study
quantifies the hepatic share of DXA lean-mass loss. The **SURPASS-3 MRI substudy** found significantly
reduced **intramuscular fat** — tissue that lowers force per unit cross-section and independently predicts
disability and mortality — a favourable change DXA books as lean loss; note it was a **type 2 diabetes**
trial.

**What protects muscle** comes from non-GLP-1 deficit literature: resistance training during restriction in
older adults with obesity preserved **~93% of lean mass** versus diet-only; diet-only lost **~16% of weight
as lean mass** against near-zero for diet-plus-exercise. **T-REX, the GLP-1-specific training data, is a
trial registration, not a paper.** **BELIEVE** (bimagrumab + semaglutide, phase 2 RCT) lost **22% of body
weight with 93% of it fat**; **QUALITY** (enobosarm + semaglutide, **<1% of weight as lean over 16 weeks
versus 34% on semaglutide alone**) is **a company presentation at ObesityWeek 2024, not published**. Both
are flagged as early-stage. **Protein 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day**, working target 1.6, from Morton 2018 where
benefit plateaued near 1.6 in **healthy trainees, not dieters and not GLP-1 users**; 2.0–2.4 called harmless
but unlikely to help; total intake ranked above per-meal distribution, with **25–40 g per meal (2–3 g
leucine)** as the practical unit. **This clashes with the ISSN stand, which treats 1.6–2.4 g/kg as the
dieting *floor* where they treat 1.6 as the ceiling** — and with their own visceral-fat article, which
prescribes **1.6–2.4** when the weight-to-waist ratio warns of lean loss. **The training prescription
carries no citation at all**: two to four sessions weekly across squat, hinge, horizontal and vertical push
and pull, two to three sets of 4–12 reps at 2–4 reps from failure, 2–4 minutes rest on compounds, training
lighter through the nausea window rather than skipping.

**Monitoring: not DXA.** Against MRI thigh muscle volume, **only 46% of DXA scans agreed within 3%**, some
participants gaining by MRI while apparently losing by DXA. **BIA is worse — ~3–5% body-fat error**, moving
2–3 points with hydration. Trend DXA at **6–12 months** if at all. Instead: **weekly waist, training
performance every 4–6 weeks, and a rolling eight-week weight-to-waist ratio, 0.6–0.8 kg per cm good, above
1.0 a warning** — **thresholds with no citation, clinical heuristics wearing numeric precision.** **The bone
section is the article at its most rigorous**: it takes the conference abstract driving osteoporosis
headlines (retrospective EMR review, no prospective fracture outcomes) and converts it to absolutes —
**41 per 1,000 GLP-1 users versus 32 per 1,000 controls over five years**, a nine-person difference reported
as "29% increased risk" — against an RCT secondary analysis where **liraglutide plus structured exercise
preserved bone density over 52 weeks while medication alone lost density at the hip**.

**[Stopping](gain-weight-back-after-ozempic-data-shows.md): three randomised withdrawal trials converge.**
**STEP 1 extension** — ~17% loss over 68 weeks, **about two-thirds regained** within a year of the last
injection, ending **5–6 points below baseline**, with blood pressure, glucose, HbA1c and lipids drifting
back. **SURMOUNT-4**, the cleanest design (everyone lost weight on drug, then randomised, so only the drug
changed) — 21% lost over 36 weeks, continuers reaching **~25%**, **the placebo arm regaining ~14% of body
weight** yet still finishing **~10% below** starting weight. **STEP 4** — continuers lost a further **7.9%**,
placebo **regained 6.9%**. **The "4× faster regain" headline is a scaling artefact**: right about absolute
kg/month, but the drug groups had lost three to four times more to begin with. **The regained tissue is
predominantly fat**, and **no published trial shows post-GLP-1 composition worse than pre-treatment** —
correctly stated, but an absence-of-evidence argument. **Their reframe is fair and it is the point:
lifestyle does not hold weight off better, it holds off less weight.** **POUNDS Lost: only 15% of 811 adults
lost ≥10%, versus 69% in STEP 1 and 83% on 15 mg tirzepatide.** **29 US lifestyle trials: >80% regained by
five years, average sustained loss ~3%.** **DPP (n=3,234)**: two-thirds hit ≥5% at 12 months, **over half
regained by ten years** despite continued engagement. **Look AHEAD (n=5,145)**: 8.6% average loss, 68% hit
5% at one year, **fewer than half still had it at year four**. Summary: half achieve clinically significant
loss in year one, one in four hits 10%, **about one in ten still holds a meaningful result at five years**.
The **dual intervention point** mechanism is **explicitly labelled "a framework, not a proven mechanism"**.
Recommendations: **stay on the drug** (the only one with direct trial support); **resistance training plus
1.2–1.6 g/kg protein** (three supporting studies, all lifestyle deficits, two in older adults); and **use
the window for habits**, with a BMJ review reporting **6.7 kg greater regain** without a structured
behavioural programme. FAQs are calibrated: **no evidence a taper prevents regain**; early pounds may be
water and glycogen but sustained regain is fat. **Both GLP-1 articles route to Coaching, the paid Beginner
Template and Whey Rx.**

## Should I get my testosterone checked? (Mostly: no)

**The most valuable clinical content in the batch is a refusal.** [The seven priorities](where-priorities-improve-health.md)
— exercise, body weight and composition, sleep, avoiding tobacco and substance abuse, basic medical
screening, pain self-management, social connection — are ordinary; what distinguishes the article is the
flat instruction against **routine lab or imaging screening in asymptomatic people, naming vitamin D,
testosterone, thyroid, cortisol, stress tests and whole-body scans**, plus no sleep trackers and no default
keto or fasting. High-value: blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes when appropriate, age-appropriate cancer
screening, vaccination. Low-value: whole-body scans, unnecessary panels, chasing minor abnormalities. **A
physician position on the harms of over-testing, argued from USPSTF, in a market whose business model is
selling panels.** Nutrition is ranked, not enumerated: **calories → protein → fibre → preferences, protein
~1.6 g/kg, fibre 30–35 g/day**, with keto and fasting declined on the correct formulation — no consistent
superiority **at equivalent adherence**. Waist: **low risk <94 cm men / <80 cm women; increased 94–102 /
80–88; high >102 / >88**, minus about **7 cm** for people of Asian descent. Strongest citation: **Bennie
2020, 1.7 million US adults**, showing that meeting *both* aerobic and strength components beats either
alone. Only **26% of US men, 19% of women, 20% of adolescents** meet the guidelines. Apnoea flags: **neck
above 17 in / 43 cm in men, 16 in / 40 cm in women**, then STOP-BANG. **Its "~90% of what matters" headline
is invented**, its sleep-hygiene list and social-connection section are uncited, coaching is linked three
times, and one sleep link is still a literal unreplaced placeholder.

[The long testosterone article](low-testosterone-men-symptoms-causes-testing-trt.md) carries it through.
**More than half of men meeting deficiency criteria at one visit are normal on a repeat draw with no
treatment** (MMAS, >1,700 men). **The HIM study found 38.7% of middle-aged US primary-care men below 300
ng/dL; requiring symptoms *plus* labs drops prevalence to 2–6%** — **a six-fold spread, and that is where
overprescribing lives.** **Only three sexual symptoms cluster reliably with biochemical hypogonadism:
reduced morning erections, low libido, erectile dysfunction.** Fatigue, low mood and poor concentration —
the complaints that drive men into clinics — are worthless as discriminators, overlapping with sleep
deprivation, depression, metabolic disease and anaemia.

**Testing: fasted, morning (ideally 8–10 a.m.), not acutely ill, not sleep-deprived, LC-MS/MS if
available.** A low result triggers a *second* draw under identical conditions with **SHBG, LH and FSH**;
free testosterone only when SHBG is abnormal. **Testosterone can fall 20–50% from morning to afternoon**,
and production tracks *sleep duration* more than clock time, rising most steeply across the first three
hours. **Immunoassays overestimate, worst at low concentrations** — re-running stored samples by LC-MS/MS
nearly doubled the proportion below 300 ng/dL, which is why **the Endocrine Society moved the lower limit
from 300 to 264 ng/dL**. Audit: **~25% of new prescriptions had no measurement in the prior year**, nearly
half of those tested had no follow-up draw, and **19.5% did not meet criteria but were prescribed anyway**.

**LH and FSH say where the problem lives.** **Primary** (low T, *high* LH/FSH): Klinefelter, chemotherapy,
mumps orchitis, trauma, chronic alcohol — replacement is usually right. **Secondary** (low T, low or
inappropriately normal LH/FSH): the commonest adult pattern, often responsive to fixing the driver.
**Mixed**: haemochromatosis, advanced liver or kidney disease, advanced HIV. **Drivers with numbers:
visceral adiposity** more than doubles the odds dose-dependently — overweight men **66 ng/dL lower**, men
with obesity **147 ng/dL lower** (MOSH), via adipose aromatase converting testosterone to estradiol
(**~80% of a man's circulating estradiol**), leptin and inflammatory adipokines suppressing GnRH and
Leydig cells, and insulin resistance lowering SHBG. **Sleep restriction: one week at 5 h/night dropped
testosterone 10–15%.** **Obstructive sleep apnoea affects 15–30% of North American men** — the best
clinical argument in the piece, because fragmentation blocks the overnight rise, so the low value is real
but the cause is the apnoea, and **exogenous testosterone relaxes upper airway musculature and worsens
it**; **STOP-BANG ≥3 warrants formal evaluation**. Also chronic opioids (dose-dependent, reversible),
hyperprolactinaemia, haemochromatosis.

**Without a prescription: +84 ng/dL for a 10% body-weight reduction; ~+250 ng/dL after bariatric surgery
at ~30% loss**, with the mechanism of loss mattering less than the amount; **expect 50–150 ng/dL from a
coordinated effort**, reassessed at three to six months. Targets: **waist-to-height below 0.5**,
weight-to-waist near **0.4–0.6 kg/cm**, 7–8 uninterrupted hours of sleep. **Exercise works indirectly
only** — the post-workout spike is small, gone in half an hour, and unrelated to hypertrophy or strength;
their prescription (2–4 sessions/week, **1.4–1.6 g/kg** protein, 3–4 aerobic sessions) is advice, not a
tested protocol. **Supplements: of the 50 most popular "testosterone boosters," 62% had no published data
supporting label claims and 10% of those with data showed the ingredients *lowered* testosterone**; **12%
of muscle-building supplements under FDA enforcement contained undisclosed synthetic steroids**, so a
spiked "natural booster" suppresses production and leaves a man lower than he started. Narrow exceptions:
zinc and vitamin D in confirmed deficiency, ashwagandha modestly.

**TRAVERSE: 5,246 men aged 45–80 with deficiency plus cardiovascular disease or high risk, topical
testosterone, ~two years — MACE 7.0% versus 7.3% placebo.** No benefit, but not the harm signal that
dominated after 2013; higher atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury and pulmonary embolism were
**unadjusted secondary endpoints with multiple comparisons and no dose-response**, and two years cannot
address polycythaemia, prostate trajectories or fertility a decade out. **Costs: HPG suppression in nearly
all men, fertility recovery typically 6–12 months or longer with a meaningful minority never regaining
baseline sperm production**, erythrocytosis, acne, fluid retention, possible worsening apnoea,
gynaecomastia, indefinite monitoring of testosterone, haematocrit, PSA and lipids. SERMs and hCG preserve
fertility, are off-label in the US, and **neither works in primary testicular failure**.

**Higher is not better.** The prostate saturates around **250 ng/dL** — the finding that dismantled the
prostate-cancer fear. For lifters the cleaner argument: **men and women differ 15–20-fold in circulating
testosterone yet gain similar *relative* muscle and strength on matched programmes** (Roberts, Nuckols &
Krieger 2020); testosterone is permissive across most of the physiologic range, and the article concedes
the framework is "more inferred than directly measured" for libido, mood, energy, bone and red cells.
**CAG repeat length in the androgen receptor gene** explains why two men at the same value feel different.
**On the collapse narrative**: the original signal was real (**15–20% lower at the same age** for men born
15 years apart), but a meta-analysis of **1,256 studies and over a million subjects across 1971–2024** puts
the adjusted decline at **~0.56%/year — half the quoted figure — with no decline at all in the LC-MS/MS
subset**, and LH falling in parallel while FSH did not. Two errors compound: **about half of people
classified non-obese by BMI are obese by DXA**, and waist at a given BMI has risen across cohorts, so every
BMI-adjusted study made the same mistake; and comparing immunoassay-era values to modern ones manufactures
a decline. **Remove both — archived NHANES samples run in one lab on one day with waist in the model — and
the decline disappears.** Age-related decline is separate at **1–2%/year after 30**, mostly tracking body
composition, illness and medication. **Their absolute claim that the decline is "almost entirely
measurement error" is stronger than the cited work supports**, and the **GLP-1 testosterone figure (20–30%)
rests on a conference press release**. Routes to coaching and a book preorder, disclosed in an explicit
incentives paragraph.

[The companion piece](low-testosterone-causes-diagnosis-management.md) is the diagnostic logic and **is
unsourced throughout — no numbered citations, no reference list, two inline links that are both tools.**
Its symptom taxonomy inverts the marketing. **Specific**: incomplete or delayed sexual development, loss of
axillary or pubic hair, small testes (**normal 15–35 mL**). **Suggestive**: loss of libido from a normal
baseline, gynaecomastia, infertility, erectile dysfunction, hot flushes, reduced spontaneous erections,
reduced genital sensation. **Non-specific**: depressed mood, poor concentration, low bone density,
unexplained anaemia, disturbed sleep, muscle loss, fat gain, low energy. The worked example: **a man with
poor sleep and rising body fat but normal libido and erectile function is highly unlikely to have
deficiency** and needs a broader workup. **A disease of metabolic health, not of age — well below 0.5%
among lean healthy ageing men, 30–50% among men with obesity and diabetes, obesity given a ~13-fold risk
increase — none of it sourced.** Prescriptions rose **nearly ten-fold between 2000 and 2011**, with **one
in four** treated having no level measured, **one in three** not meeting criteria, **one in two** never
rechecked. **ng/dL ÷ 28.8 = nmol/L.** Two honest admissions: **there is no consensus lower limit of
normal** (guidelines mostly use **300–350 ng/dL**, and no study has found a cutoff separating who benefits
— which sits against the other article's 264), and **repeat measurements are noisy**, hence the BMJ
calculator for telling real change from assay variation. Free testosterone **65–100 pg/mL** by calculation
or equilibrium dialysis, **1.0–1.5 ng/dL (10–15 pg/mL)** by analog/RIA. **~95% of testosterone is Leydig,
~5% adrenal, only 1–4% free.** Nutrition: **very low fat (<25% of calories) lowers it modestly relative to
35–40%**; **severe carbohydrate restriction (<5% of energy)** lowers it, moderate does not consistently;
**three small studies found intakes above 3.4 g/kg/day protein lowered it**, and they recommend
**1.2–2.2 g/kg/day**; **41 studies, over 1,000 men** found no soy effect. **Monitoring is admirably
deflationary: no evidence supports a blood-level target — aim mid-range (300–800 ng/dL) and titrate to
symptoms.** **The under-recognised harm is infertility: sperm production can be fully suppressed in as
little as 10 weeks, and 4–10% never fully recover.** **Flag: "treating true deficiency may actually lower
cardiovascular risk" — the trial evidence supports non-inferiority, not benefit**, and the correlation is
confounded by the metabolic illness the article itself names as the cause. **It defers substantive content
to their paid Testosterone Course roughly ten times** — the physiology, the differential, the forms of
circulating testosterone, the supplements, the non-testosterone agents, most side-effect detail — so read
it as a competent primer that is also a course prospectus. Against its own interest: **it dismisses
testosterone supplements as a category, and they sell supplements.**

[The sleep apnoea guide](basic-guide-sleep-apnea.md) makes the same case in a paragraph: fatigued men
stalling in the gym with low measured testosterone are frequently untreated apnoea cases, and **guidelines
recommend against starting TRT with untreated apnoea**. **STOP-BANG** — snoring, tiredness, observed
apnoea, blood pressure, BMI >35, age >50, neck >40 cm, male sex, one point each, **≥3 warrants formal
testing**. Screening is not diagnosis: confirmation needs polysomnography or a home sleep test, and
**consumer trackers cannot diagnose**. Prevalence **15–30% of men and 10–15% of women in North America**,
over 900 million worldwide. Episodes last **10 seconds or longer**, from a handful a night to hundreds an
hour, and the usually-omitted half is that **carbon dioxide accumulates** because obstruction also blocks
exhalation — which is what he attributes morning headaches to. Risk: neck **>17 in (43 cm) men, >16 in
(40.5 cm) women**, deviated septum, recessed jaw, enlarged tongue or tonsils, **BMI above 35** though
people below it still get it; untreated apnoea causes hypertension, so **difficult-to-treat high blood
pressure** is a reason to be evaluated, and moderate-to-severe disease raises atrial fibrillation, heart
failure, coronary disease and stroke risk. First-line is unglamorous: weight loss (**≥10% of body weight**
for substantial improvement), nasal congestion treatment, cutting alcohol and sedatives — then PAP,
mandibular advancement, surgery. **No reference list and no citations of any kind.** One slip: it calls REM
"deep sleep," which it is not in standard staging (N3 is) — though the real point, that **REM atonia
paralyses the airway musculature**, is right and explains why obstruction clusters there.

## Measuring the body, and the metabolic chain

[BMI's real failure is under-diagnosis](bmi-accurate-bmi.md), not the muscular outlier, and the organising
move is to treat BMI as a **screening test**. Pooling **25 studies and ~32,000 people**, at a cutoff of 30
it runs **~97% specificity and ~42% sensitivity**; in **>13,000 US adults** BMI ≥30 was right in **~95% of
men and ~99% of women** but caught only **36% of men and 49% of women** who genuinely carried excess fat.
**A positive is trustworthy; a negative is not.** In ~5,000 adults, people above BMI 30 carried **the same
cardiometabolic risk whether lean mass was high or low** — the gym's complaint is the small error, settled
in a minute with a tape measure. **BMI survives on outcome data, not precision**: **239 prospective studies,
>10 million people**, mortality lowest at **BMI 20–25**; and in **57 studies / ~900,000 adults**, each
**5-point rise above ~22.5–25 carried ~30% higher all-cause mortality**, **BMI 40–45 costing 8–10 years of
median survival**, comparable to smoking. **The cutoffs are admittedly arbitrary** — the 1995 WHO report
described 25 and 30 as convenient, from largely European-descent populations, and the WHO's own 2004
consultation recommends **23 and 27.5** for South, Southeast and East Asian populations. Sensitivity **falls
to roughly a third over 60**, because sarcopenic obesity is exactly what a normal BMI hides. The
recommendation — **BMI plus waist, or waist-to-height under 0.5, then labs if flags are up** — matches the
**2025 *Lancet Diabetes Endocrinology* Commission** and its clinical/preclinical obesity split (adiposity
assumable above BMI 40). On "BMI is racist": the **weak version** (blame Quetelet's 1832 population
statistic, or Keys' 1972 revival which tested it against other indices in >7,000 men and endorsed it *for
population research*) is a category error; the **strong version** (blame institutions that imposed arbitrary
universal cutoffs on individuals) is accepted, with ethnicity-specific thresholds the remedy already in
place — and the remedy the criticism supports is *pair BMI with waist*, not discard it, since discarding it
catches fewer at-risk people. **Flagged: the personal fat threshold, the visceral/subcutaneous contrast, the
over-60 figure, the 94/80 and 102/88 cm thresholds, the 2023 AMA policy, the waist-to-height superiority
claim and the recommended lab panel (fasting glucose, fasting insulin, lipids, TG:HDL) are all uncited** —
and the uncited material is disproportionately what supports their own workflow. "Neither racist nor sexist"
is rhetoric, and the sexist half is never addressed despite sensitivity differing substantially by sex. Ends
in a coaching funnel.

[Measure the waist at the iliac crest, not the navel](measure-waist-circumference.md). **The landmark is the
whole point**: the cut-points come from **NHANES**, measured at the iliac crest, and a waist at the umbilicus
is a different variable sharing a name. Procedure: palpate the **anterior superior iliac spine**, mark
horizontally on both sides, run the tape flush to the marks, **snug without indenting the skin**, **read to
0.1 cm at the end of a normal exhale**. **Cut-points: >40″ men, >34″ women**, above which he recommends
weight loss straight away, with maintenance calories within about an inch for a novice or reversing a gain
phase for an advanced lifter. **But its own footnote concedes ethnicity-adjusted cut-points are lower
(~37″/34″ for people of Asian descent) and that some national guidelines use 37″/31″, which he says are
"probably better"** — so the bolded 40/34 is the loose version. He also concedes some large datasets do not
report their measurement procedure at all. **No references.**

[The visceral-fat piece](visceral-fat-vs-weight-waist-matters-more.md) is better, and its best-supported
theory undercuts its headline. **Scale weight is a poor instrument for concrete reasons**: glycogen swings
**300–500 g** over 48 hours of eating and each gram carries **3–4 g of water**, so a carb-heavy weekend adds
**2–4 lb** with no fat change; and muscle is denser than fat (**≈1.06 vs 0.9 g/mL**), so a pound of fat takes
**15–20% more space**. **Three theories ranked: (1) overspill/ectopic fat is strongest** — entered into the
same model, **fatty liver predicts metabolic syndrome with an odds ratio above 70 while visceral fat loses
independent significance entirely**, and omentectomy delivers no consistent benefit beyond the weight loss;
**(2) portal theory** — portal IL-6 runs **~50% above systemic**, weakened by the same omentectomy data;
**(3) the aromatase feed-forward loop**, correctly placed as a secondary accelerator. Adipokines: **PAI-1**
(thrombotic), **angiotensinogen** (hypertension), underproduced **adiponectin**.

**The tape is repeatable, not precise**: correlation with imaged visceral fat runs **0.4 to 0.75**, **a
change in waist captures only ~40% of the change in visceral fat**, and waist correlates more strongly with
**subcutaneous** than visceral fat at every site. Three landmarks differ: **NHANES/NIH** (iliac crest at the
midaxillary line) reads ~2 cm higher than WHO in women; **WHO** (midpoint between lowest rib and iliac crest)
has the **best correlation** and underpins the thresholds; **umbilicus** is most practical but migrates
downward and reads **2–4 cm higher** with central fat. **Action Level 1: 94 cm (37 in) men / 80 cm (31.5 in)
women. Action Level 2: 102 cm (40 in) / 88 cm (35 in)** — "a late find." Ethnic adjustments: **85–90 cm men,
75–80 cm women** for East and South Asian populations, tighter for many South Asian groups, and standard
thresholds may *overestimate* risk in people of West African descent. **Waist-to-height below 0.5 (above
0.4)** outperforms BMI across ethnicities — one of their **Vital 5**. The concept carrying most weight is
the **personal fat threshold**: cut-offs are flags, no measurement finds an individual's own threshold in
advance (it announces itself in liver enzymes, fasting glucose, triglycerides, TyG index), and anyone who
already has impaired glucose regulation, dyslipidaemia, hypertension or fatty liver has crossed it. **The
weight-to-waist ratio is the most useful device here: ~0.7 kg/cm general, 0.4–0.6 for well-structured
programmes, and above 1.0 sustained over 8–12 weeks signals lean tissue loss**, calling for **1.6–2.4
g/kg/day protein** and progressive resistance training; women run less favourably early (**~2.8 cm per 3 kg
versus 3.5 cm in men**) because peripheral fat mobilises first, and the article discloses these figures come
primarily from male populations. **Exercise reduces visceral fat without weight loss; diet restriction
largely does not** — **6.1% visceral reduction from exercise alone versus 1.1% from diet alone**, with diet
plus exercise giving **~5.7 cm of waist reduction versus 2.2 cm**, and exercise halving lean-mass loss
(**20–30% of weight lost on diet alone, 10–15% with exercise**). Mechanisms: visceral adipocytes carry more
**beta-3 adrenergic receptors** and fewer braking alpha-2, and contracting muscle releases **IL-6 as a
myokine at 5–30 times resting levels**. Dose **~150 min/week moderate aerobic at 60–80% max heart rate**.
Then the honest caveat: in **300 adults**, doubling prescribed exercise energy expenditure produced **no
additional waist reduction**, though every exercise arm lost about **5 cm** versus non-exercisers — because
the tape reads mostly subcutaneous fat, a real 6% visceral reduction can fail to appear. Testosterone tiers:
**deficient** — restoring physiological levels adds **1–3 kg of lean mass over 6 months**; **eugonadal
(~300–1000 ng/dL)** — no longer limiting; **supraphysiological** — a dose-dependent relationship appears
that is absent within the normal range, and **Bhasin's four-group study** had the no-exercise testosterone
arm gain more lean mass than the exercise-only arm. **Finkelstein showed blocking estradiol conversion in
men with adequate testosterone caused fat gain regardless of testosterone dose**, so aggressive aromatase
suppression on TRT works against the metabolic goal. **No numbered citations, no inline markers — a lumped
list of ten, one of which (a stress-and-adiposity meta-analysis) is never discussed**, and its take-home
recommends the umbilicus while the measurement section names WHO as best-correlated.

[The BRI article](new-number-health-body-roundness-index-replace.md) argues from a formula it describes
incorrectly. It says the calculation uses "height, age, weight, sex, race, and waist circumference."
**BRI, per Thomas 2013 — the paper it cites — is the eccentricity of an ellipse from waist and height only**,
scaling 1 (narrow) to 20 (round). So the closing argument that BRI "would guarantee that people's waist
circumference **and ethnicity** are taken into consideration" does not follow. Dropping weight entirely makes
it **closer to a refined waist-to-height ratio than to "BMI plus waist"** — and the article never asks
whether it beats waist-to-height. The mortality data are worth keeping and undercut "lower is better": in
>30,000 US adults the curve is **U-shaped**, **lowest all-cause mortality at BRI 4.5–5.5**, **below 3.4
~25% higher** (attributed to sarcopenia, frailty or malnutrition — inference) and **above 6.9 ~50% higher**;
population mean **5.62**, risen over 20 years. Useful support: waist correlates well with MRI and CT
abdominal fat, **self-measurement after instruction is accurate and reproducible**, **BMI plus waist
outperforms waist-to-hip, waist-to-height and BMI alone**, and **only 24% of US adults meet both aerobic and
strength guidelines**, so the muscular false-positive is rare by construction. **But the pivotal "BRI beats
BMI" claim rests on weak designs**: of five references, one (Mulligan 2019) is about waist circumference and
**does not involve BRI at all**, two are cross-sectional (which cannot support "predicts"), one is a narrow
subgroup (hypertensives with sleep apnoea), and one is a meta-analysis. **It also contradicts its own
companion** — "a very useful and accurate tool based on a solid scientific foundation" here, versus a recent
arrival without BMI's outcome record in the later BMI article; the later position is better supported and the
difference is never reconciled. It does concede the load-bearing unknown: **nobody knows what change in BRI
corresponds to what change in risk, over what time frame**, making it a stratification number, not a progress
number. No product sold; the calculator recommended is third-party.

**The metabolic chain runs through the personal fat threshold.** [Type 2 diabetes as overflow](know-about-type-2-diabetes-part-1.md):
**roughly 1 in 11 people worldwide**, two failures running together — **beta-cell dysfunction** (loss of the
first-phase insulin response, then cell death) and **tissue insulin resistance**. Taylor's **Personal Fat
Threshold**: subcutaneous fat cells have a storage limit varying by genetics, diet, activity and medication,
and the capacity to make *new* fat cells varies too; exceed your own limit and **where the surplus overflows
is what causes disease, not how much fat you carry**. An insulin-resistant liver stops suppressing glucose
release — **the source of high fasting morning glucose**; fat in muscle blocks uptake — **the source of high
post-meal glucose**, worse when inactive; fat in the pancreas adds glucotoxic injury until beta cells fail.
The extreme case is **lipodystrophy** — inability to store subcutaneous fat producing severe insulin
resistance, diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidaemia in someone with very lean limbs. **Hold it lightly: one
model presented as the model, no competing account, no effect sizes, no designs, no sample sizes.**

[Fatty liver](fatty-liver-disease-causes-symptoms-treatments.md) is the chain's end, reframed as **not a
liver disease that causes metabolic problems but a metabolic disease that manifests in the liver**. MASLD
affects **~25% of adults worldwide**, **5–6%** at the steatohepatitis stage, **7.6% of children generally but
34% of children in paediatric obesity clinics**, ~**$100 billion** annual US cost, and **cardiovascular
disease, not liver failure, is the leading cause of death** in those who have it. Staging: **MASL** (fat in
**>5% of hepatocytes**), **MASH** (plus inflammation and cell death), **fibrosis → cirrhosis** (irreversible).
Alcohol-related disease is separated at **>20 g ethanol/day for women, 30 g/day for men**, or **>14 and 21
drinks/week**. **Fibrosis severity, not fat or inflammation, is the prognostic variable — liver-related death
risk is 50–80× higher with advanced fibrosis than with MASH and no significant fibrosis** — so the workup
targets fibrosis via **FIB-4** and **APRI** (good at *ruling out* at low values), then imaging, with biopsy
the non-routine gold standard. The "two-hit" model is presented then explicitly demoted as incomplete.
**The most actionable sentence: ALT and AST are normal in more than half of MASLD patients, correlate poorly
with severity, and do not rule out MASLD or even advanced fibrosis.** Risks: **up to 80% of people with
obesity may develop it; 60–70% of people with type 2 diabetes have it**, with diabetes **more than doubling**
advanced fibrosis and liver-related death; genetics influences susceptibility in **20–70%** of cases while
known variants explain **10–20%** of heritability; **lean or non-obese MASLD ~19% globally**. Treatment:
**≥5% and preferably ≥10% weight loss — everyone losing ≥10% saw reduced disease activity, 90% achieved MASH
resolution, 45% fibrosis regression**, while **~7% loss cuts liver fat by ~45%**. Diet composition matters
beyond calories: **overfeeding 1,000 kcal/day of saturated fat for three weeks raised liver triglyceride more
than the same surplus from unsaturated fat or simple sugars**, and **isocalorically, 20% of energy from
saturated fat raised liver fat ~40% while 20% from simple sugars did not**; a **28%-of-energy MUFA diet,
half from extra-virgin olive oil, cut liver fat 18% over 12 weeks**; coffee is consistently associated with
lower risk at no established dose. **The best judgement call is declining to turn the isocaloric data into
an anti-sugar claim** — sugar still matters in a hypercaloric context via surplus, de novo lipogenesis and
remnant cholesterol. Exercise benefits the liver **regardless of weight loss**. Drugs: vitamin E for MASH
without diabetes, pioglitazone, the GLP-1 class, and **resmetirom** as the first approved MASH drug for
moderate-to-advanced fibrosis; **bariatric surgery is most potent, improving ~90%**. **Supplements get the
sharpest treatment — against a category they profit from.** **Flagged: 4,175 words with three linked
references, so every number above them is uncited.**

## Getting older

[Sarcopenia has moved off mass onto strength and function](sarcopenia-symptoms-prevent.md). **EWGSOP2 (2019)
put strength at the top with mass confirmatory; the US SDOC, pooling over 18,000 participants, found lean
mass did not predict falls, fractures, mobility limitation or mortality and dropped it from the definition**,
keeping grip and gait speed. Clark & Manini supplied *dynapenia* for the gap. **The rates differ because the
mechanism is neural: mass declines 0.5–1.0%/year after 50 against strength at 1.5–3.0%/year**, power faster
still — selective loss of high-threshold motor neurons, slow through midlife and faster after 60, with
orphaned fibres rescued by sprouting from predominantly low-threshold neurons and converted toward
slow-twitch. A muscle can measure the same size while losing peak force, rate of force development and power,
so **the first tasks to go are the fast ones**, and **lower-body power predicts falls better than leg strength
alone**. Evidence: masters sprinters retain substantially more surviving motor units than sedentary controls;
**single fibres from the oldest-old (mean 89) show preserved, and in some types higher, force per unit size
than healthy twenty-year-olds** — the machinery is fine, less of it is recruited; and a **10-year follow-up
of masters sprinters aged 48–85 at baseline** found **14% slower 60 m, 9–10% loss of jumping power, 21%
decline in knee-extension peak force**, the honest counterweight. Health ABC found muscle *quality* declining
at **~3× the rate of mass**.

**Screen with function, all runnable with a chair, a stopwatch and 4 m of floor:** five-times sit-to-stand
**>15 s**, gait speed **<1.0 m/s** (severe below 0.8), SPPB **≤8**, TUG **>20 s**, 400 m walk unfinished or
**>6 min**. **A man with normal muscle mass who cannot rise from a chair five times in 15 seconds is at real
risk; a man with below-expected mass who is strong is not.** Red flags adding a physician: unexplained **≥5%
weight loss over six months**, recent falls, new difficulty with stairs or groceries. Disease-related
sarcopenia (advanced CKD, heart failure, COPD, cancer, chronic steroids, prolonged hospitalisation) is a
different entity.

**The grip section is the most rigorous thing in the batch**, and a strength company spends its longest
section on why dynamometry is limited. Population value is real — **PURE, 139,691 adults across 17 countries:
each 5 kg lower grip associated with 17% higher cardiovascular and 7% higher non-cardiovascular death**. But
**total lean body mass is the strongest single predictor of absolute grip, stronger than age or height**,
grip is **48–65% heritable**, and **the cutoffs contradict each other — a 30 kg grip is sarcopenic under SDOC
(~35.5 kg for men) and normal under European (27 kg) and Asian (28 kg) criteria: same person, three
answers.** And **it does not scale with training** — a 550 lb deadlift does not produce a 90th-percentile
score, while training grip directly moves the score without changing the biology it is meant to detect, **an
experience claim with no citation that the article does not mark as one**. "Informative at the extremes,
limited in the middle" is the right calibration, and the pivot to **relative grip (grip ÷ body weight)**,
which tracks cardiometabolic outcomes better and removes the obesity paradox, is a real improvement.

**Prescription:** lift **2–3×/week**, compound movements, **2–4 sets of 3–12 reps at ~2–3 reps from failure**,
**moving the concentric as fast as you can** (explosive intent, from de Vos 2005 on chair rise and stairs);
**~1.6 g/kg/day protein**; **3–5 g creatine monohydrate**; treat obesity. A 2025 network meta-analysis found
larger weekly volumes gave larger grip gains to a plateau, **most benefit captured in 2–3 sessions/week at
moderate intensity over 19 weeks**. Creatine plus training in older adults over 8–24 weeks gave **an
additional 1–1.5 kg lean mass beyond training alone**, with the volunteered caveats that some is water,
responses vary, and it does nothing without training. **Two overreaches.** The protein target of **1.6 g/kg**
is said to "track with" **ESPEN, which recommends 1.0–1.2 g/kg for healthy older adults and 1.2–1.5 g/kg with
sarcopenia or chronic illness** — it sits above it. Distribution is handled well (**~3 g leucine, 30–40 g per
meal** short-term, but longer trials show no distribution advantage once total is matched — **hit the total
and stop optimising**). And **"walking is not enough" is the most consequential claim in this area**: the
size-principle reasoning is sound but **no cited study compares walking to resistance training for motor-unit
preservation**, the masters work is cross-sectional and in sprinters, and the FAQ answers flatly "No."
**The powerlifter data is doing more work than it can bear** — men over 69 losing **0.35%/year** against
~1%/year, women over 59 *gaining* **2.5–5%/year**, from a growth model of competition results in people who
chose to compete and kept competing, so healthy-survivor selection is severe and unaddressed. On GLP-1s it
argues no independent sarcopenia (SEMALEAN), with the myosteatosis explanation drawn from a **tirzepatide**
substudy applied to **semaglutide**. Whey, creatine and templates are linked; it still says spend on food and
a gym first.

[Osteoporosis is the opposite extreme: it cites nothing at all](basic-guide-osteoporosis.md). The framing —
**bones are living, remodelling tissue that adapts to load and atrophies without it**, demonstrated by disuse
and spaceflight — is sound. **Osteoporosis is asymptomatic**; aches in bones or joints are not a sign, and
without screening it is found after a fracture. Risk: older age, smoking, alcohol, very low body weight;
women more than men, especially after menopause or earlier loss of ovarian function; **men with testosterone
below 200 ng/dL** or on androgen-deprivation therapy; severe vitamin D deficiency; thyroid and parathyroid
disorders; kidney and intestinal disease; **long-term oral steroids or high-dose inhaled steroids — with the
useful specific that nasal steroid sprays do not cause problems.** **Screening: not everyone. "Most people
are at such low risk that testing is a waste of time and resources"** — restraint from a company selling
fitness services — with **DEXA for all women over 65** standing. Management: healthy dietary pattern with
attention to calcium and protein, stop smoking, cut alcohol, loaded exercise, then **FRAX**, vitamin D and
medication, with the good line that needing medication "should not be viewed as failure, but simply as
another tool." **Load specificity is the practical message**: swimming, aqua-aerobics and cycling will not
build bone the way lifting does, and light-and-high-rep will not match heavier-and-fewer — **asserted with no
evidence**, and best held as mechanism (bone responds to peak strain magnitude and rate, muscle to tension
near failure, so it does not contradict the 5–35 rep hypertrophy finding elsewhere in the library). **The
load-bearing claim is unsupported as written**: "we have studies of high-intensity weightlifting programs in
older patients with osteoporosis showing that they can re-build their bone strength without causing dangerous
fractures during exercise" — **no study named**, and it is the sentence licensing the whole recommendation for
a fearful reader. **"Exercise may be the most important thing people can do" is a ranking, not a measured
comparison** — drugs have fracture-endpoint RCTs, exercise trials here mostly report bone mineral density.
They also push back on **"brittle" and "fragile" as clinical language**, applying their nocebo position to
bone.

[Being strong tracked cancer mortality where reporting that you lift did not](strength-cancer-related-mortality-enough-exercise-actually.md):
**Dankel 2018, 2,773 NHANES participants aged ≥50** (50.4% female, 58% non-Hispanic white), 1999–2002 cycles,
followed via the National Death Index. **Top quartile of knee extensor strength versus the rest: 32/699
(4.5%) cancer deaths against 130/1,944 (6.6%) — a 53% risk reduction minimally adjusted, 50% fully adjusted.
Self-reported strengthening activity gave 6% and 8%, neither significant.** Per unit, **HR 0.95 (95% CI
0.91–0.99) per 15 N in men, HR 0.92 in women**. Excluding the 394 ever diagnosed with cancer, **HR 0.43
(0.22–0.84)**; flipping to the *bottom* quartile against the upper three, **no longer significant**.
**Population attributable fraction 20.9%** — a causal counterfactual computed from observational hazard
ratios. **The thesis — "it is not enough to exercise, you must actually get strong" — is not established by
this design.** Strength came from an isokinetic dynamometer (three warm-ups, three maximal contractions at
60°/s, gravity-corrected peak); activity came from **two NHANES questions about the previous 30 days**, with
**eight or more sessions** counting as engaged and only **382 people (13.8%)** clearing that bar. **Comparing
a precise measurement against a crude one systematically favours the precise one through measurement error
alone**, and the null on activity is badly underpowered; the article lists self-report as a limitation but
never connects it to the conclusion. The background material is solid: **muscle loss is present at all stages
of cancer, not just end-stage cachexia** — **54% of newly diagnosed early-stage colorectal cancer patients
had handgrip below 85% of the age-matched reference**, and **714 newly diagnosed patients carried 0.9 kg less
muscle on average than healthy controls before treatment** — with inflammation, drugs, inactivity and
malnutrition combining into **anabolic resistance**. Baraki is explicit that the mechanisms "remain poorly
understood," that "exactly how strong is strong enough remains unknown," and that **training response varies
widely between people**. Sells nothing. Two blemishes: **the women's result is printed as "HR 0.92, 95% CI
0.86-0.91," an interval excluding its own point estimate**, and the opening cancer-therapy claim cites a paper
absent from the reference list.

## Being pregnant, and the cycle

[Training through pregnancy](exercise-during-pregnancy-safety-training-guide.md) is safe in **uncomplicated,
average-risk** pregnancies, and that qualifier does most of the work. No increased risk of miscarriage, poor
fetal growth, musculoskeletal injury or preterm delivery, resting on the **ACOG 2020 committee opinion** plus
a **2025 BJSM systematic review and meta-analysis of resistance training pooling 47,619 participants** with
no adverse maternal or fetal effects. The rest is **guideline consensus** — the 2019 Canadian guideline, a
2023 scoping review of guidelines from **19 countries** (resistance) and **17** (aerobic), the US Physical
Activity Guidelines — which is expert convergence on an overlapping literature, not replication. The maternal
benefit list (less excessive weight gain, less gestational diabetes and hypertension, less preeclampsia, fewer
perinatal mood disorders, shorter labour, more vaginal births) comes from a **2019 umbrella review** over
heavily observational material, so "associated with" is the right reading; the **epigenetics** paragraph is
weakest though hedged. Active women can usually continue with little modification; inactive women can start.
**Modification, not cessation** — sumo instead of conventional deadlift, wider squat stance, dumbbells rather
than barbells for cleans, standing core work, incline or seated pressing — **all uncited coaching judgement,
undisguised.**

**Two intensity numbers sit here and they do not match.** **Guidelines say RPE 4–6 on a 1–10 scale, 3.0–5.9
METs, heart-rate zone 2, the talk-test point of short broken sentences — at least 150 min/week of moderate
conditioning and resistance training at least two days per week.** **The article prescribes RPE 6–8, roughly
65–85% 1RM, mostly 4–12 reps**, cited to Schoenfeld 2017, **a low- versus high-load meta-analysis in
non-pregnant trainees** — which it states plainly, though that paper does not establish an RPE prescription,
and the closing summary reprints 6–8 without repeating that guidelines say 4–6. **Their argument is honest:
the moderate cap reflects absence of research at higher intensities, not demonstrated harm.** The evidence
offered is ungraded — a retrospective CrossFit cohort with **no sample size, no comparison group and no
outcome numbers**; **"evidence from elite athletes" with no citation at all**; and studies of women lifting at
or near 1RM with no adverse effects noted. **The legacy 140 bpm cap is never engaged** — absent rather than
rebutted.

**The Valsalva fear is answered by two small acute studies.** Meah 2021 found no difference in maternal
cardiac and haemodynamic response with and without a deliberate Valsalva (sample size not reported);
**Gould 2021 monitored placental blood flow by 3D Doppler in 22 pregnant women during near-maximal chest
press and found no reduction versus rest**, with braced lifting described elsewhere as **up to 90% of 10RM**,
plus Moolyk 2024/25 on acute high-intensity responses. **These test the exact mechanism the fear rests on,
and they are surrogate outcomes in small samples** — "no acute drop in placental flow in 22 women" is
narrower than the FAQ's flat "No." **Supine positioning**: after roughly **20 weeks** the gravid uterus can
compress venous return, but the recommendation originated around *sleep* position, does not affect everyone,
and is **symptom-guided rather than a ban**. **Heat**: fever, hot tubs and saunas are associated with
first-trimester neural tube defects; the exercise study (Carmichael 2002) is observational, finding activity
associated with **30–50% lower NTD risk**, and the **90°F/32°C and 60% humidity** thresholds come from a
National Weather Service page attached to no marker. **Diastasis recti**: prevalence **66–100%** to some
degree, but the headline — **90% of non-exercising pregnant women with separation ≥2 cm versus 12.5% of the
exercising group** — comes from **a single 2005 non-randomised study with no reported sample size**, which is
also the sole source for the mechanism and functional-deficit claims. **Falls and environment**: avoid contact
sports, downhill mountain biking, forcible abdominal contact — with credit for saying outright that this is
**anatomical and anecdotal reasoning, not research evidence**. Scuba is out; **altitude above 2,500 m** should
be avoided by women living below it, acclimatised women above **1,800 m** possibly exempt.

**It then fails on its own terms.** The setup is correct — **Meah, Davies & Davenport 2020**, a BJSM
systematic review of evidence of harm subtitled a call to action, found many contraindications rest on
**expert opinion rather than demonstrated harm from exercising or benefit from stopping**. The article writes
"There are a few conditions that emerging evidence and existing guidelines suggest exercise should be stopped
until the condition is managed:" — **and lists nothing.** **There is no warning-signs list anywhere**: no
vaginal bleeding, no amniotic fluid leakage, no regular painful contractions, no chest pain, no dyspnoea
before exertion, no calf pain and swelling — all present in the ACOG opinion it cites throughout. **A reader
using this as their guide learns everything about why restrictions are overstated and nothing about when to
stop.** Its overreach: "it is very rare to have a hidden or undiscovered cause of concern" is broader than
Meah supports, which argued specific contraindications lack evidence, not that undetected risk is rare.
Postpartum: **how you return matters more than when**, sourced to an **international Delphi study** —
structured expert opinion, not data, and the article does not say so. The "fourth trimester" is the first
**12 weeks**, though a review of **22 postpartum guidelines** suggests up to a year, particularly for
breastfeeding women; they push back on the **6-week wait** as not clearly evidence-based, citing ACOG that
activity may resume as soon as medically safe, for some within days. **The FAQ claim that exercise does not
impair milk production is uncited**, one 45-woman treadmill study is described in detail with no reference
marker, and it sells **Coaching** and **dietitian consultations**.

[Cycle-phase periodisation is not supported](women-menstrual-cycle-athletic-exercise-performance.md), and
**the strongest argument is magnitude, not direction.** Estrogen matters for muscle — ovariectomised rats lose
cross-sectional area and protein turnover and recover more slowly after inactivity; postmenopausal women show
higher breakdown with lower mass. But **cycling estradiol runs ~32.7–87.2 pg/mL follicular and 65.4–242.4
pg/mL luteal — about three times the average of the first five post-menopausal years — and the dip lasts days,
not decades.** The mechanistic check: **muscle protein synthesis rates do not differ between phases** (Miller
2006). On the acute side, **Kraemer 1995** (11 women, 4×5) found luteal estradiol **~80–136 pg/mL** against
**27–34** follicular, peaking **16 minutes in** — with the decisive caveat being the article's own: **none of
these studies checked whether hormone changes correlated with any training outcome**, and exercise measurement
is confounded by plasma volume shifts, clearance and posture.

The performance studies are small, mostly single-joint or grip proxies, and disagree. **Phillips 1996** (12
untrained, 10 trained, 5 on contraceptives) found a **~10%** follicular rise, but **estrogen did not correlate
with relative force** and within-cycle variation was **±1.2 lb against a 24 lb mean**. **Sarwar 1996** (20
sedentary women) found mid-cycle quadriceps strength higher by up to **13.5 lb (11.7%)** against a
within-sample SD of about **11.2 lb**, on an **assumed 32-day cycle with ovulation inferred, not measured**.
The two best-controlled studies, using superimposed electrical stimulation to rule out central fatigue with
hormones drawn at every session, found nothing — **Elliott 2003** (7 women, day 2 vs 21) and **Janse de Jonge
2001** (19 women, **no phase differences and no correlation between any performance metric and any hormone
concentration**). **Romero-Moraleda 2019** (13 triathletes, half-squat 1RM then 20/40/60/80% at maximal
velocity) found no differences in power or velocity. **Weidauer 2020** (50 women, hormones measured) found
**no effect of hormone levels on strength** and proposed a non-hormonal cause. **Kishali 2006** found women
medalled regardless of phase **despite reporting feeling significantly better during the first 14 days**. The
confound used best: **Birch & Reilly 2002 found no phase difference in maximal isometric lifting strength but
a ~13 lb difference between 6 am and 6 pm testing.**

**The four periodisation studies share their confounds**: within-subject limb comparisons vulnerable to
contralateral crosstalk, and testing weeks at different training volumes, so a phase effect is
indistinguishable from a taper. **Reis 1995** (7 women, **no p-values reported**) and **Sung 2014** (20 women,
the effect sitting in initial exposure and the taper) carry both; **Sakamaki-Sunaga 2016** (14 women, arms on
opposite schedules, 1RM curl at baseline, 4, 8 and 12 weeks) is the **clean negative** — both arms gained
equally; **Wikström-Frisén 2017** (59 women) is decisively confounded because **each group entered
post-testing at a different training volume** — five sessions that week, one, and three. **None measured
hormone levels**, and most assumed 28 days when cycles run **18–40**. Contraceptives **may slightly reduce
performance, with very small group-level effects and wide individual variation** — individual management, not
a blanket rule — and symptom dulling may improve training tolerance. Reviews (McNulty 2020, Thompson 2020,
Blagrove 2020, Carmichael 2021) agree the evidence is **inconclusive**, McNulty noting most is low quality.
**The recommendation: do not prospectively periodise on cycle phase, do not automatically start the luteal
phase lighter, do not deload by default on symptoms — auto-regulate and monitor actual response.** **Where
they overreach:** "we cannot reject this hypothesis and therefore we accept it" is not how a null works; "weak
but converging evidence pointing to a benefit" is generous given one null and three taper-and-crosstalk
explanations; the relaxin dismissal is asserted; and **the closing nocebo argument — the fourth pillar of the
case — is entirely uncited.** No product is pitched.

## Two loose ends, and one long argument

[Exercise triggers migraine in some but regular training reduces frequency](exercise-affect-headaches.md).
**A new headache starting during exercise is a reason to see a physician, not to self-manage** — guidelines
call for MRI plus MRA, or CT plus CTA, to exclude structural and vascular causes, and a headache cannot be
called primary until they are excluded. **Primary exercise headache** skews young, is slightly more common in
women, is provoked by heat, altitude and sustained strenuous effort, and usually lacks the migraine signature
of nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia and unilateral pain — so a throbbing one-sided headache arriving
with a hard set is more likely migraine provoked by exertion. **Migraine affects 12–15% of adults worldwide
and ranks second only to low back pain as a global cause of disability.** **Exercise is a self-reported trigger
in 22% of patients** (**1,207 patients aged 13–80**). **Despite that, a 2022 systematic review and network
meta-analysis of 21 controlled trials found all exercise interventions reduced migraine frequency**, with the
authors explicit that the **required frequency and intensity are unknown**. Two n=1 **case reports** of
migraine aborted by exercise are treated as curiosities, one of them in *Medical Hypotheses*. The avoidance
pattern is the musculoskeletal one applied to headache — one cited paper is explicitly about **kinesiophobia**
in migraine. **Where it slips:** "all exercise interventions were effective" is stated flatly when the network
meta-analysis carries wide per-modality uncertainty, and aerobic-versus-strength was the source paper's actual
comparison, which the article never reports.

[CBD has good evidence for two rare paediatric epilepsies and almost nothing else](science-cbd-oil-health-performance.md).
**The one real indication is drug-resistant seizures in Lennox-Gastaut and Dravet syndromes**, where
pharmaceutical-grade CBD (Epidiolex, **~$1,300–1,500 per 100 mL bottle**) roughly halved seizure frequency —
the only FDA-approved indication. **For pain the entire human RCT literature was two trials**: Wade 2003
(**n=24 crossover, only 12 completing the full pain assessment**, CBD 54.8 versus placebo 44.5 on a 0–100
scale with **no other outcome differing**) and van de Donk 2019 (**n=20 fibromyalgia, single doses, no
difference on any pain outcome**). Its best section dissects Palmieri 2017 — 12 young women, four dropouts,
**8 analysed against their own baselines with no placebo arm** — and pauses to note the study's own premise (an
HPV-vaccine link) is contradicted by a cohort of ~300,000 vaccinated versus 700,000 unvaccinated girls. **The
anxiety result is one 60-person study with 12 per arm**, and its finding is odd: **300 mg reduced anxiety
during a public-speaking test but 100 mg and 900 mg did not**, only clonazepam sedated, and only the 300 mg arm
raised systolic and diastolic pressure — with the article's own critique unusually complete (underpowered, raw
scores unpublished, no established MCID, healthy participants, fixed dosing in a BMI band of 22–23). **There is
no data at all on exercise performance, recovery or sleep in healthy people** — the title promises "health and
performance" and the performance half is empty. **Product quality is the strongest practical finding: of 84
products from 31 companies, only 31% were accurately labelled for CBD, falling to 12.5% for vaporised
products, with THC in 21% of samples at an average 0.45 mg/mL** — worse than the ~15% contamination the ISSN
cites for supplements generally, and exactly the WADA strict-liability scenario. **Adverse effects are not
trivial**: about a third report a reaction, with diarrhoea **9–20%**, anaemia **30%**, liver injury **8–17%**,
plus interactions. **CBD appears not to bind either cannabinoid receptor directly** while stimulating both by
an unestablished mechanism, and the anti-seizure effect is thought **not** cannabinoid-receptor mediated;
**oral bioavailability ~6%, inhaled ~31%**; **topical CBD has no human absorption data at all** — one mouse and
one rat study, and it says so. **Marijuana-derived CBD remains illegal even below 0.3% THC**, because legality
tracks the source plant. **They do not sell CBD**, criticise a competitor by name, and reach an entirely
negative conclusion about a category they have no stake in. Three description errors: four seizure trials
called double-blind when one is open-label and one is in tuberous sclerosis; the Parkinson's paper listed is a
**case series on REM sleep behaviour disorder**, not the randomised trial described; and **the anxiety trial
the article is built around does not appear in its own reference list**. Currency: October 2020.

[The sex-and-gender article](shades-gray-sex-gender-fairness-sport.md) is 15,000 words and only tangentially
clinical, but it earns a place for two numbers and one methodological lesson. **Its best-supported section is
the history of sex testing**: every method failed on its own terms — pre-1966 medical certificates, 1966–67
compulsory genital inspection, 1967–96 the buccal smear abandoned as unreliable, and 1996 SRY testing, **which
failed 8 of 3,387 women at Atlanta, all of whom were then permitted to compete**, with universal testing
dropped from Sydney 2000 for suspicion-based referral. **The performance number is well sourced: a stable
~10–12% men-versus-women gap in world records since ~1983** (10.0% ± 2.94; top-ten 11.7% from 1982; **5.5%** in
the 800 m freestyle to **18.8%** in the long jump), **above 15% where upper-body power dominates and below 5%
in ultra-endurance swimming**, where women hold the Catalina Channel and Manhattan Island records outright.
Olympic weightlifting **36.8%**; **the 40–70% powerlifting figure is an unpublished federation analysis relayed
by personal communication**, and the article says so. **DSD prevalence roughly 1 per 1,000–2,000 live births,
"neither XX nor XY" about 1 in 1,666**, and **46,XY prevalence in elite female athletes about 7 per 1,000,
roughly 140× the general-population rate** — ~32 of the 4,676 women at London 2012.

**The methodological lesson is the reason to read it.** It grants real differences — men average **10–15 cm
taller**, **30–40% more lean mass**, **~48% and ~34% greater upper- and lower-body strength**, **~12% higher
hemoglobin**, **~5-fold higher testosterone** — then repeatedly reports them shrinking or vanishing **after
normalisation**: lean-mass difference to **~10%** corrected for height and weight; strength differences gone
normalised to muscle cross-sectional area; peak acceleration power in 100 world-class sprinters equivalent per
body mass; Wingate power in 155 untrained students equivalent per kg fat-free mass; fibre type not differing
by sex but tracking training status (**71% type IIa in elite women weightlifters versus 63% in national-level
men**); similar training responsiveness; **50–60%** of muscle and strength variance attributed to
non-sex-specific genetics. **Every one is real, and every one removes the quantity that decides contests.**
Nobody wins a 100 m or a squat by force per square centimetre — and the article never surfaces the distinction.
**Its testosterone argument makes the same move at a larger scale**: papers showing that variation *within* a
sex's range predicts performance poorly are used against testosterone as an explanation for the *between-sex*
gap, a claim they were not designed to address, and the article's own aside acknowledging that is dropped by
the summary. Its sharpest regulatory findings: **101 of 795 elite men (13%) had free testosterone within the
female reference range**, and the IAAF's own advantage data are inconsistent — **2.73% (400 m), 2.78% (400 m
hurdles), 1.78% (800 m), 4.53% (hammer), 2.94% (pole vault)** but **negative in the 100 m (−2.63%), 100 m
hurdles (−1.69%), 200 m (−1.59%) and javelin (−2.53%)**, averaging **0.31% (running) and 1.07% (non-running)**.
**The direct evidence on transitioned athletes is thin and it says so** — four studies as of 2019, the largest
performance dataset being **eight self-selected runners' race times**, and Gooren & Bunck's own conclusion that
post-suppression trans women's mean muscle area stayed significantly greater than untreated trans men's "though
with an overlap that was almost complete." Its critique of Wiik (unmatched comparison groups, possible learning
effect, no individual-level data) is genuinely rigorous. Two later reviews pointing the other way appear as a
**two-sentence post-publication insertion** with the recommendations left unrevised. Its most-quoted line —
**"the phrase 'biological sex' is meaningless and is ill-advised"** — is definitional, not a finding; the
prevalence data falsify a strict genetic binary without making the category meaningless. Its "1–3% precedent"
rests on the 2015 CAS *Chand* award and **never engages the April 2019 CAS award in *Semenya v. IAAF***,
decided months before publication. **Sells nothing.** Currency warning: 2019, and the policy landscape has
moved.

## Weak points

**Four articles carry no meaningful citations.** [Osteoporosis](basic-guide-osteoporosis.md) cites nothing at
all, including for its load-bearing safety claim. [The sleep apnoea guide](basic-guide-sleep-apnea.md) has no
reference list and no citations of any kind. [The shorter testosterone article](low-testosterone-causes-diagnosis-management.md)
has no citations and two inline links that are both tools, so its 13-fold obesity risk, 30–50% prevalence and
4–10% permanent infertility figures are all unsourced. [The waist article](measure-waist-circumference.md)
gives none. Several more have no *numbered* citations and therefore no claim-level attribution:
[Lp(a)](lipoprotein-levels-testing-treatment-heart-disease.md) (seven bare links, one resolving, under
numeric diagnostic thresholds and drug effect sizes); [visceral fat](visceral-fat-vs-weight-waist-matters-more.md)
(a lumped list of ten, one never discussed); [fatty liver](fatty-liver-disease-causes-symptoms-treatments.md)
(three prose links across 4,175 words); [the action plan](lower-heart-disease-risk-action-plan-cholesterol.md)
(no list at all — the four "references" extracted are its Key Principles bullets); plus
[kidney](abnormal-labs-exercise-part-i-kidney-function.md), [type 2 diabetes](know-about-type-2-diabetes-part-1.md),
[the priorities article](where-priorities-improve-health.md) (sixteen links, mostly guidelines not papers) and
[the blood-pressure guide](blood-pressure-causes-prevention-treatment.md), whose named inline links are nearly
as good but whose "WHO 2009" is an uncheckable text string.

**Conflicting thresholds, inside one article.** The liver-enzyme piece gives **>5× ULN**, **>10× ULN** and
**<3× ULN** in three places, plus **10–12 days** versus **one week** versus **7–10 days** for the retest — use
**>5× ULN**. Its sibling puts the transaminase peak at **3–4 days** where it puts it at **4–7 days**, both
citing Pettersson. The action plan's non-HDL-C target reads "<100–130," "<130" and "below 100." The waist
article's bolded **40″/34″** is undercut by its own footnote conceding **37″/31″** screens better.

**Conflicting thresholds across articles.** The waist article's **34″** for women appears nowhere else — the
visceral, BRI and BMI articles all use **94/80 cm (37″/31.5″)** and **102/88 cm (40″/35″)**. The blood-pressure
guide pairs **40 in for a man of European descent** with **31.5 in for a woman of Asian descent**, which is not
a like-for-like pair. Testosterone's lower limit is **264 ng/dL** in one article and **"300–350, no consensus"**
in the other. Protein runs **1.2–1.6** (GLP-1), **1.4–1.6** (long testosterone), **1.6** (priorities,
sarcopenia), **1.2–2.2** (short testosterone) and **1.6–2.4** (visceral fat) — with sarcopenia's 1.6 sitting
*above* the ESPEN range it claims to track. Weight-to-waist warnings differ (**0.6–0.8 good** versus **~0.7
general / 0.4–0.6 well-structured** versus **0.4–0.6**), agreeing only on **>1.0**. And **two of their own
articles contradict each other on BRI**: "a very useful and accurate tool based on a solid scientific
foundation" in [one](new-number-health-body-roundness-index-replace.md), a recent arrival without BMI's outcome
record in [the other](bmi-accurate-bmi.md), unreconciled.

**Uncited load-bearing claims.** "**Lifting weights does not cause hypertension**" — the sentence its audience
most wants — has no citation. "**A 1% reduction in plaque volume is associated with an 18% reduction in major
cardiovascular events**" closes the action plan's case for aggressive therapy with **no source at all**. The
priorities article's "**~90% of what matters**" is invented, as are its sleep-hygiene list, neck thresholds and
social-connection section. Osteoporosis's high-intensity-lifting safety claim names no study. The GLP-1
article's **training prescription** and **weight-to-waist thresholds** carry none. Sarcopenia's "**walking is
not enough**" is size-principle reasoning stated as a finding, and its grip-does-not-scale claim is unmarked
experience. Pregnancy's **"evidence from elite athletes"**, its whole modification list and its milk-production
FAQ are uncited. The menstrual-cycle article's **nocebo pillar** is entirely uncited. The rhabdomyolysis
CK-fall heuristic is uncited, as are the cholesterol basics' 18–20% threshold and its "strong recommendation"
on waist, the visceral article's odds ratio above 70 and IL-6 figures, and most of the BMI article's workflow
support.

**Surrogates doing an endpoint's work.** Naci compares **millimetres of mercury over weeks** against a drug
literature with stroke and mortality endpoints, across populations never randomised against each other.
Osteoporosis ranks exercise above drugs when exercise trials report **bone mineral density** and drug trials
report **fractures**. Lp(a)'s causal claim rests on Mendelian randomisation with **no trial showing that
lowering it lowers events**. The pregnancy Valsalva reassurance rests on **placental blood flow in 22 women**.
The seed-oil rejection rests on null biomarkers plus biomarker-based observational outcomes. The
CAC-in-athletes stabilisation argument rests on a narrative review and two small composition studies.

**Models presented as measurements.** The GLP-1 **Table 1** decomposes 3 kg into compartments its own footnote
calls "bounded estimates," with a **stated total (1.5–3.9 kg) that does not pin to the 3 kg** — the most
quotable claim is the least measured. [Type 2 diabetes](know-about-type-2-diabetes-part-1.md) presents Taylor's
model as *the* model, with no effect sizes or designs. The strength-and-cancer **20.9% attributable fraction**
is a causal counterfactual from observational hazard ratios. BRI's low-end mortality is attributed to
sarcopenia by inference.
