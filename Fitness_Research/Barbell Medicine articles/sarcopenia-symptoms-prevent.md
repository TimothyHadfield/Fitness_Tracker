# Sarcopenia Is a Strength and Function Problem, and It Starts in the Nerves

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Sarcopenia: What It Is, Symptoms, and How to Prevent It" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/sarcopenia-what-it-is-and-how-to-prevent-it/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum, medically reviewed by Austin Baraki · 2026-05-08 · 5,557 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **The diagnosis has moved off muscle mass.** [EWGSOP2 (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1093/ageing/afy169) put muscle *strength* at the top of the algorithm with mass demoted to a confirmatory role; the US [SDOC](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.16372), pooling **more than 18,000 participants**, found lean mass did not predict falls, fractures, mobility limitation or mortality once other factors were accounted for, and dropped mass from the definition entirely, keeping grip strength and usual gait speed.
- **The rates differ because the mechanism is neural, not muscular.** [Mitchell 2012](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2012.00260) puts mass decline at **0.5–1.0%/year** after 50 against strength decline of **1.5–3.0%/year**; power falls faster still. The explanation offered is selective loss of high-threshold motor neurons ([Piasecki 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10522-015-9627-3)) with collateral reinnervation by low-threshold neurons converting rescued fast-twitch fibres to slow.
- **Grip strength is picked apart rather than promoted** — the best section in the article. It is a genuine mortality predictor at population scale but **48–65% heritable** ([Willems 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms16015)), driven mostly by lean mass and build ([Kallman 1990](https://doi.org/10.1093/geronj/45.3.m82)), and the cutoffs disagree: a 30 kg grip is sarcopenic under SDOC (~35.5 kg for men) and normal under European (27 kg) and Asian (28 kg) criteria. Same person, three answers.
- **Screen with function.** Five-times sit-to-stand >15 s, gait speed <1.0 m/s (severe below 0.8), SPPB ≤8, TUG >20 s, 400 m walk unfinished or >6 min. All runnable at home with a chair, a stopwatch and 4 m of floor.
- **The prescription:** lift 2–3×/week, compound movements, 2–4 sets of 3–12 reps at roughly 2–3 reps from failure, **moving the concentric as fast as you can**; ~1.6 g/kg/day protein; 3–5 g creatine monohydrate; treat obesity if present.
- Barbell Medicine sells whey protein, creatine and training templates, all linked from the recommendations. The article nonetheless says the budget goes to food and a gym membership before any supplement.

## The definitional shift, and why it matters clinically

From 1989 until roughly 2010, sarcopenia meant low muscle mass measured against age-matched cutoffs. It failed as a diagnosis because it did not track the outcomes patients care about. The article's summary of the fix is accurate: strength and performance predict falls, fractures, disability and death better than mass does, so both major frameworks reorganised around them, and the American one discarded mass altogether. [Clark & Manini 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/63.8.829) supplied the vocabulary for the gap — *dynapenia*, age-related strength loss not explained by another condition.

The clinical reasoning is worth stating plainly because it is what makes this article more than a training piece: **a man with normal muscle mass who cannot rise from a chair five times in 15 seconds is at real risk; a man with below-expected mass who is strong is not.** That is the whole case for functional screening, and it is why the article's home-test battery is the actionable part.

## The neural mechanism

This is the article's central argument and it is built carefully. Motor units are recruited in size order; high-threshold units running type II fibres contribute only as force or velocity demand rises. Individual motor neurons are lost slowly through midlife and faster after about 60, with type II units affected first ([Piasecki 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10522-015-9627-3)). Orphaned fibres can be rescued by sprouting from neighbouring — predominantly low-threshold — neurons, which converts them toward slow-twitch. Over decades the pool of high-threshold units shrinks. A muscle can therefore measure the same size while losing peak force, rate of force development and power. That explains why the first tasks to go are the fast ones, and why **lower-body power predicts falls better than leg strength alone** ([Skelton 2002](https://doi.org/10.1093/ageing/31.2.119)).

Three lines of evidence are offered that training defends the motor pool:

- **[Power 2010](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181d6f9e9)** — masters sprinters retain substantially more surviving motor units by motor unit number estimate than age-matched sedentary controls, with less of the compensatory reinnervation signature.
- **[Grosicki 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00479.2016)** — single fibres biopsied from the oldest-old (mean age **89**) show preserved, and in some fibre types higher, force per unit size than healthy twenty-year-olds. The contractile machinery is fine; there is less of it recruited.
- **[Hendrickse 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40289323/)** — 10-year follow-up of masters sprinters aged 48–85 at baseline: **14% slower 60 m, 9–10% loss of jumping power, 21% decline in knee-extension peak force.** Slower than the population trajectory, but decline continues even in high performers. The article is right to include this as the counterweight to its own optimism.

**Flag — the powerlifter data is doing more work than it can bear.** [Latella 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01962-6) is quoted twice: men over 69 losing **0.35%/year** against ~1%/year in the general population, women over 59 *gaining* **2.5–5%/year**. This is a longitudinal growth model of competition results in people who chose to compete and kept competing into old age. Healthy-survivor selection is severe and unaddressed; nobody who got weak enough to stop competing stays in the dataset. The direction is almost certainly right, the magnitude is not a training effect estimate.

**Flag — "walking is enough" is the most consequential piece of bad advice in this area.** The claim is that low-intensity activity does not fully recruit high-threshold units and therefore does not protect them. The FAQ answers "Does walking prevent sarcopenia?" with a flat "No." The size-principle reasoning is sound, but **no cited study compares walking to resistance training for motor-unit preservation.** The masters-athlete work is cross-sectional and in sprinters. This is a well-argued mechanistic inference stated as an established finding.

## Grip strength: the most rigorous section

Given how universally grip dynamometry is recommended, it is notable that a strength-training company spends its longest section explaining why it is limited. Four arguments, each supported:

1. **Population value is real.** [PURE](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(14)62000-6) — **139,691 adults, 17 countries**, median four-year follow-up — every 5 kg lower grip associated with **17% higher cardiovascular death** and **7% higher non-cardiovascular death**.
2. **Much of the score is fixed before the clinic visit.** Total lean body mass is the strongest single predictor of absolute grip, stronger than age or height ([Kallman 1990](https://doi.org/10.1093/geronj/45.3.m82)); grip is **48–65% heritable** and shares genetic variance with lean mass ([Willems 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms16015)).
3. **The cutoffs contradict each other** — 35.5 vs 28 vs 27 kg across SDOC, Asian and European frameworks.
4. **It does not scale with training.** A 550 lb deadlift does not produce a 90th-percentile dynamometer score, because almost nobody trains the forearm flexors in the position the dynamometer tests — and training grip directly moves the score without changing the biology it is meant to detect. **This is an experience claim with no citation, and the article does not mark it as one**, but it is the practically important point for the readership.

The summary — "informative at the extremes, limited in the middle" — is the correct calibration, and the pivot to **relative grip** (grip ÷ body weight, [Lee 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0160876)), which tracks cardiometabolic outcomes better and removes the obesity paradox, is a genuine improvement on the standard advice.

## GLP-1s

The article's position: GLP-1s do not independently cause sarcopenia. [SEMALEAN](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41068996/) followed **106 adults with obesity** (mean age 52, mean BMI 46, roughly half sarcopenic at baseline) on max-dose semaglutide for a year — **fat mass −18%, lean mass −5%** and stabilising after month 7, a fat-to-lean loss ratio near **4:1**; sarcopenic obesity prevalence fell **49% → 33%**; handgrip improved by **4.5 kg** in people who were not exercising.

The proposed explanation for strength improving without training is reduced myosteatosis — intramuscular fat that occupies space without producing force — drawing on the [SURPASS-3 MRI substudy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40318682/). **Flag:** that substudy is tirzepatide, not semaglutide, and the inference is cross-study and mechanistic. It is plausible and it is not the same evidential tier as the SEMALEAN numbers.

## The prescription, and where it exceeds its citations

- **Training.** [Ran 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41194011/), a network meta-analysis in sarcopenic older adults, found larger weekly volumes produced larger grip-strength gains up to a plateau, with most benefit captured in **2–3 sessions/week at moderate intensity over 19 weeks**. The article correctly reads frequency as a way of distributing volume rather than a limit.
- **Move the bar fast.** Explosive concentric intent rather than a separate power protocol, from [de Vos 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/60.5.638) on optimal load for power in older adults, on functional endpoints — chair rise, stairs.
- **Protein — recommendation exceeds citation.** The target given is **1.6 g/kg/day**. The cited [ESPEN expert group](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2014.04.007) recommends **1.0–1.2 g/kg** for healthy older adults and **1.2–1.5 g/kg** with sarcopenia or chronic illness. The article says 1.6 "tracks with" this; it sits above it. Distribution is handled well: short-term work supports ~3 g leucine (30–40 g high-quality protein) per meal to overcome anabolic resistance ([Zaromskyte 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2021.685165)), but longer trials have not shown an advantage for any distribution once daily total is matched — **so hit the total and stop optimising.**
- **Creatine.** [Chilibeck 2017](https://doi.org/10.2147/OAJSM.S123529) meta-analysis of creatine plus resistance training in older adults, trials of 8–24 weeks: **an additional 1–1.5 kg lean mass beyond training alone**, plus strength and sit-to-stand improvements. The article volunteers that some of that is water and that responses vary, and that creatine does nothing without training.

**Red flags warranting a physician rather than a programming tweak:** unexplained ≥5% weight loss over six months, five-times sit-to-stand >15 s, gait speed <1.0 m/s, SPPB ≤8, 400 m walk >6 min or not completed, recent falls, new difficulty with stairs or groceries. Separately, disease-related sarcopenia — advanced CKD, heart failure, COPD, cancer, chronic steroids, prolonged hospitalisation — is treated as a different entity requiring a fuller workup. That distinction is the clinical value the source is here to add.

**Commercial note:** the protein section links a whey protein product, the supplements section links their creatine explainer, and the training section links the Beginner Template. None of this is disguised, and the article still tells the reader to spend on food and a gym before supplements.
