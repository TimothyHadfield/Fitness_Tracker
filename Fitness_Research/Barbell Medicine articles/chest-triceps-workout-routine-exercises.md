# Chest and Triceps Together: One Cited Exercise Choice, and a Genuinely Good Section on Form

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Chest and Triceps Workout Routine & Exercises" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-chest-and-triceps-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2023-11-22 · 5,213 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- Five exercises are recommended — bench press, bar dips, standing overhead dumbbell press, close-grip push-up, close-grip bench press. **Exactly one of those five choices has a citation attached to it**, and that citation is a biomechanics/EMG study, not a hypertrophy outcome study. **No exercise in this article is ranked on growth data.**
- The activation citations they do use are used *correctly* — three EMG studies support the claim that chest pressing also activates the triceps, which is precisely what EMG can establish. The problem is elsewhere: the *benefits* of pairing the two muscle groups are supported by studies that do not test pairing.
- **The best part of the article has nothing to do with exercise selection.** Their "R.E.P." critique of "good form" argues that the standard definition of proper technique is circular reasoning, and replaces it with Repeatable / Efficient / Points of Performance. That is the Barbell Medicine clinical position, stated well.
- They also state, unusually for this genre, that there is a **wide "sweet spot" of training stimulus** and that optimising rates of adaptation is neither critical nor achievable. This directly contradicts the volume-maximising framing common elsewhere in this library.
- Heavily monetised: three identical in-body "Find your Hypertrophy Program" calls-to-action with campaign tracking, plus the Powerbuilding II and Bodybuilding I templates and coaching.

## What actually backs the five exercises

| Exercise | Prescription | Rationale given | Cited? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bench press | 3 × 4–6, RPE 7–8 | "A classic", compound | No |
| Bar dips | 3–4 × 6–10, RPE 7–8 | Torso angle claims called "mostly based on speculation" | No |
| Standing overhead DB press | 3–4 × 8–10, RPE 7–8 | Trains the synergists; trunk demand | No |
| — alt: incline DB press | 3–4 × 8–12, RPE 7–8 | 15–30° recommended | No |
| Close-grip push-up | 2–3 × 8–15, RPE 8–9 | "Places greater emphasis on the triceps" | **No** |
| Close-grip bench press | 3–4 × 4–6, RPE 7–8 | Better for triceps than standard grip | Yes — [2] |

The one cited selection claim is that a close grip is better than a standard grip for the triceps, supported by [Larsen 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2020.637066), a biomechanical analysis of wide, medium and narrow grip widths in recreationally trained males during 1RM bench pressing, measuring kinematics, horizontal kinetics and muscle activity around the sticking region. **That is an acute mechanics and activation study in a single maximal lift.** It supports "the triceps do more of the work with a narrow grip" and does not support "the close-grip bench builds bigger triceps." The article states the weaker claim carefully — "could be a better option ... when it comes to working the triceps" — which is defensible, but a reader will take it as a growth ranking.

The close-grip push-up gets the same claim with no citation at all.

Note also that this article is more careful than most about dips: torso lean "is not clear whether this produces different results in terms of strength and size," with a recommendation to try both. That is the right level of confidence, and the same wording appears in their chest hypertrophy article.

## Where the citations do and don't support the argument

The verified mapping makes this checkable sentence by sentence.

**Used correctly.** "Many exercises that load the chest will also activate the triceps to some degree. [3,4,5]" — three EMG studies (bench press vs. dumbbell fly; five bench inclinations; the dumbbell pullover). An activation claim supported by activation data. This is the appropriate use of EMG and it is worth marking, because it is the contrast case for every other place in this library where EMG is asked to do more.

**Overreach — "enhanced muscle synergy."** "Training the two muscle groups together will allow them to grow accustomed to working with each other, establishing better synergy. [11]" The reference is [Kristiansen 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001282), a randomised controlled study of five weeks of bench press training and its effect on EMG-derived muscle synergies. It is a real RCT, but it compares bench training to no bench training — it does not compare training chest and triceps *together* against training them on separate days. The programming conclusion does not come from the study.

**Overreach — "improved physical function."** The claim that training chest and triceps together benefits functional upper-body strength rests on [7] and [8]: a study of the correlation between bench press strength and punch performance in professional boxers, and a study of correlations between golf performance and upper-body power. Both are **cross-sectional correlation studies**. They establish that upper-body pressing strength tracks with some sporting outputs. They say nothing about training organisation, and they cannot support a claim about pairing muscle groups in a session.

**A clear mis-citation.** The anatomy sentence describing the clavicular and sternocostal heads of pectoralis major carries marker [5], which is Lacio 2021, an EMG study of the dumbbell pullover. The article's own parenthetical on that same sentence names the correct source — Solari & Burns, StatPearls — which never made it into the numbered list. The mapping verified overall; this particular attribution is still wrong.

**Three references are never cited in the text at all**: Saeterbakken & Fimland 2013 (body position in shoulder presses), Ogasawara 2012 (time course of arm and chest thickness changes), and the 2018 *Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans*. They sit in the list unused.

**A plain factual error.** "The triceps are more independent than the pectorals and can be isolated and trained directly, without using the chest muscles. An example would be cable flyes." A cable fly is a chest exercise with minimal triceps involvement — the sentence appears to have lost its intended example (a pushdown or extension).

## The section that is worth the read: technique and dosing

Two parts of this article are clinical reasoning rather than exercise ranking, and they are the parts where these authors are actually in their lane.

**On "good form."** They quote the only formal definition they can find in the literature — Colado et al. 2009: proper technique means correct speed and resistance, in the appropriate plane, within the optimal joint range of motion, to maximise adaptations and minimise injury risk — and identify it as circular. It defines proper technique by its outcomes, and would only be informative if the premises (that particular velocities, loads, planes and ranges of motion reduce injury risk) were themselves established. They argue those premises are not established.

Their replacement is the **R.E.P. model**:
- **Repeatable** — ROM, joint angles, velocity and tempo broadly similar rep to rep, with explicit tolerance for variability between people and between reps.
- **Efficient** — minimise muscular force that does not contribute to completing the task.
- **Points of Performance** — the movement meets whatever criteria the exercise was chosen for.

This is the Barbell Medicine position on technique, and it is consistent with the broader pain-science line they argue elsewhere: that "correct form" as the industry uses it is a much weaker predictor of injury than claimed. **Presented as their position, it is well argued.** The supporting claim that "only ONE study has even tried to define" good form is stated more absolutely than is plausible and is uncited; the substantive criticism of the definition survives it.

**On dosing.** Increase training stress too fast and you outpace adaptation, generate excess fatigue and raise injury risk; too slow and adaptation lags. Then the line that separates them from most of this genre: "there is a fairly wide 'sweet spot' of training stimulus. When viewed over a long training career, it is not critically important to 'optimize' rates of adaptation, nor is there any way to ensure such 'optimization' given the number of variables involved."

**This clashes usefully with the optimisation framing running through the YouTube sources in this library**, where marginal differences in volume, rep range or proximity to failure are treated as decisions worth getting exactly right. It is a claim about practical significance rather than about the data, and it is uncited — but it is a defensible clinician's reading of an evidence base full of small, noisy between-group differences.

## The recommendations, marked as recommendations

None of the following is presented with evidence; all of it is coaching advice.

- **Chest before triceps.** Compound before isolation, larger muscle groups first. The one citation here ([Simão 2012](https://doi.org/10.2165/11597240-000000000-00000), a review of exercise order) is on topic. They add the sensible caveat that reversing it is reasonable when triceps growth is the priority, or deliberately to cap the load on the pressing movement.
- **Beginners:** three compound pressing exercises, 3–4 sets each per week, RPE 6–8, per their free Beginner Prescription template.
- **More experienced:** four to six compound pressing exercises per week at 3–5 sets each, RPE 6–8, plus three to six isolation exercises at 3–6 sets each.

**Worth flagging:** RPE 6–8 is meaningfully further from failure than the near-failure prescriptions that dominate the hypertrophy literature and the other sources in this library — it implies roughly 2–4 reps in reserve on most working sets. The article never reconciles this with the standard finding that hypertrophy at moderate loads depends on training close to failure. It is a conservative, fatigue-managing choice consistent with their clinical framing, but it is presented without argument.

**Commercial note:** the "Find your Hypertrophy Program" call-to-action appears three times inside the article body with intro/mid/bottom campaign tracking, and the programming section names Powerbuilding II and Bodybuilding I, both paid templates, as its worked examples. The closing third is a coaching pitch with testimonial framing rather than content. The Beginner Prescription they recommend is free.
