# Enjoyment Predicts Whether People Keep Training, and Fear-Based Coaching Undermines It

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Science of Training Motivation" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-motivation-to-train/)) · Derek Miles · 2021-01-22 · 4,266 words
**Topic:** Evidence & method

## Bottom line
- The featured study ([Rodrigues 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.13617), 575 gym members, 6-month objective attendance tracking) found **enjoyment was the variable with the largest indirect effect on exercise persistence** (β=0.83), with autonomous motivation the largest driver of enjoyment (β=0.85).
- The clinical argument built on it: **coaching that frames movement as dangerous is an autonomy-thwarting behaviour**, and therefore predicts dropout. Cited support is that both people in pain and people without believe round-back lifting is dangerous ([Caneiro 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29982241/), [2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30553986/)), and that physiotherapists hold that bias implicitly.
- Rewards backfire. [Deci 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/) is quoted directly: expected tangible rewards contingent on doing an interesting activity **undermine intrinsic motivation for it**. Miles extends this to passive rehab modalities used for "buy-in".
- The behaviour problem is real in scale: only **18.6% of US adults** meet muscle-strengthening guidelines ([Dankel 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27591819/)); **65% of Europeans** are physically inactive (Eurobarometer 472).
- The article's own strongest move is a concession: Miles says he previously advocated against training modalities he considered ineffective or ridiculous, and no longer does, because almost any active training beats none.

## The theory scaffold, and how much of it is evidence

Self-Determination Theory does the framing (Ryan & Deci 2017). Motivation splits intrinsic (acting to satisfy curiosity or mastery) from extrinsic (socially driven, progressively internalised), and rests on three basic psychological needs ([Cook & Artino 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27628718/)):

- **Autonomy** — feeling in control, supported by offering choice, acknowledging choice, *avoiding judgement*, and encouraging personal responsibility.
- **Competence** — an optimal challenge plus feedback that builds self-efficacy.
- **Relatedness** — an environment of respect and mutual caring.

On top of that sits Vallerand's Hierarchical Model ([1997](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0065-2601(08)60019-2)), which sorts influences into global, contextual and situational levels and splits social factors into a "bright side" that satisfies those needs and a "dark side" that frustrates them. Miles says the model has cross-sectional and longitudinal support ([Standage 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.1.97); [Gillet 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2009.10.004)) — note that this is model-fit evidence, not intervention evidence.

The bridge to practice is stated plainly. Where basic psychological needs are met, people participate because they value the activity (autonomous motivation); where they are frustrated, people participate for reward or from guilt (controlled motivation). Applied to gyms and clinics, messages that a person *needs* a specific technique or they will be injured are the frustrating kind.

Enjoyment gets the strongest prior claim: it drives autonomy and raises intention to keep exercising ([Ntoumanis 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27283879/); [Hagger 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25750803/)), and Ajzen's theory of planned behaviour is cited for enjoyment being the most proximal determinant of continued participation.

## The journal club: what Rodrigues actually did

This is where the article earns its keep, because Miles reports the methods properly rather than the headline.

**Design.** 575 gym members, 18–65 (mean 34.1, SD 11.5), all with at least six months of gym experience, recruited at random times at the reception desk. Self-reported training frequency 3.52 days/week (SD 1.28), sessions averaging 61.5 minutes. They completed questionnaires on motivational determinants, enjoyment and intention, and then **adherence was taken from the gym's electronic attendance records over six months** — an objective outcome, not self-report, which is the design's main strength.

Adherence was binary-coded: 1 if attendance matched the self-reported level, 0 if the participant quit or attended significantly less. Two stated assumptions sit under that: people exercising regularly for over six months have lower dropout intention, and people under six months drop out at roughly 50%.

**Analysis.** Confirmatory factor analysis for the measurement model, structural equation modelling for fit, and a serial mediation analysis following Hayes with separate bright-side and dark-side models. A G*Power analysis (f²=0.01, α=0.05, power 0.95) put the minimum sample at 287, so the study is comfortably powered for what it tested. Participants missing more than 5% of data were excluded.

**Results.** 472 of 575 (82%) were still training at six months, averaging 2.89 days/week (SD 0.98) — note that is below their self-reported baseline of 3.52. The other 103 stopped within two to four months. Bright-side constructs correlated positively with each other, with enjoyment, with intention and with persistence. In the supportive-behaviour model, enjoyment had the largest effect on persistence (β=0.83) and autonomous motivation the largest effect on enjoyment (β=0.85). In the opposing-behaviour model, enjoyment→persistence was again β=0.83 while intention→persistence was β=0.04 — a marked asymmetry, and Miles reads it correctly as lack of enjoyment driving discontinuation. Male and female subgroups fit equally with no difference between them.

**What the design cannot support.** This is a cross-sectional survey with a prospective behavioural outcome and a mediation model. It shows that enjoyment and autonomous motivation predict who keeps turning up; it does not show that manipulating coaching language changes attendance, and the sample was people who had *already* chosen to join a gym and train for six months. Miles acknowledges the last point explicitly.

## The clinical argument, and where it outruns the data

The take-home section makes a stronger claim than the study tested: that fear-based messaging about technique is an autonomy-thwarting behaviour and so contributes to inactivity. Its supports are the Caneiro line of work — implicit associations between back posture and danger in people without pain (2018), and physiotherapists implicitly evaluating round-back lifting as dangerous (2019) — plus a 1950s anchoring literature ([Hovland, Harvey & Sherif](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0048480)) for the idea that setting an unreachable standard ("perfect form from the start") places an anchor too far from the target for progress to be perceived.

That chain is plausible and each link is separately supported, but the chain itself is not. Nobody in the cited work measured attendance after fear-based coaching. Miles writes "it is difficult to be surprised that there is an epidemic of inactivity in society when individuals who are charged with being promoters are advocating that if an individual is moving wrong they will hurt themselves" — a causal claim about population inactivity resting on implicit-association studies. **That is the piece's clearest overreach**, and it is exactly the kind of pain-science claim the reader should hold at arm's length: the evidence establishes the belief is widespread and clinician-transmitted, not that it drives dropout.

The reward argument is on firmer ground and more original. Deci's meta-analysis is quoted verbatim, and the application is sharp: if passive modalities lacking efficacy are used to "bridge the gap" or increase buy-in, they function as contingent rewards and may undermine the intrinsic motivation the clinician is trying to build. Miles then applies the knife to his own side — telling someone they *need* tape, needling, scraping "or glitter" is the same error as telling them they *need* a barbell.

He also gives a usable ladder through extrinsic motivation: **external regulation** (rewards and punishment), **introjected** (pride, guilt avoidance — "feeling bad about not training"), **identified** (the goal is seen as useful), **integrated** (training is part of identity, like putting the coffee on). His framing point is that *movement* is easier to internalise than *exercise*, because exercise already reads as punishment to many people.

**On relatedness**, his best practical observation: resistance training is unusual in that a first-time deadlifter and a 4×-bodyweight lifter can occupy the same room and the same community, which endurance sport largely cannot. Presented as argument, not evidence.

**Marked as a distinction worth keeping:** Miles notes that exercise as a whole does not produce large effect sizes for treating many conditions ([Saragiotto 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26742533/); [Zacharias 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25065642/)) — an unusually candid admission from a source that sells training.

**Things they sell:** none. The article promotes no product, which is worth noting given the source's usual pattern.
