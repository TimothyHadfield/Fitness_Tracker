**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **The damper is not resistance.** Settings 1–10 change the *drag factor* — how fast the flywheel decelerates — by changing airflow through the fan cage. You cannot pre-set a load on a RowErg; the machine measures how hard you accelerate the flywheel, so **the damper does not make the workout harder**.
- Practical default: **damper 5**, with most people effective between **3 and 5**, corresponding to a **drag factor of roughly 100–130**. Drag also varies with altitude, machine condition and dust, so the drag number beats the damper number.
- **Foot strap over the ball of the foot**, shins vertical at the catch, knees under or inside the arms. Lower the footplate if you have long femurs or a large midsection. **Avoid lifting shoes** — a rigid heel-lifted shoe blocks the vertical-shin catch position.
- The stroke sequence is **catch → drive → finish → recovery**, cued as **"legs, body, arms — arms, body, legs."** The recovery reverses the drive, and the knees bend *last*.
- Read **split (time per 500 m)** and **stroke rate (SPM)** together. High stroke rate without power moves nothing. Target a **drive-to-recovery ratio of 2:1 or 3:1** — "row slow to go fast."

It is instructor knowledge, and the author's credentials are the entire evidentiary basis: a Barbell Medicine coach and Master Rowing Instructor for UCanRow2 and Concept2 with ~25 years' experience — **an affiliation with the manufacturer of the machine the article is about**, disclosed in the article itself.

## The damper explanation, which is the article's real contribution
The single most common misunderstanding about the erg is that damper 10 is "heavy" and damper 1 is "light" in the way a loaded barbell is heavy. Niemann's correction is mechanically right and worth internalising: the damper lever controls the volume of air entering the fan cage, which sets the **drag factor** — the flywheel's deceleration rate between strokes. What the monitor actually measures is your ability to accelerate that flywheel against air resistance. The harder you work, the more resistance you feel, **at any damper setting**.

The genuine trade-off is therefore not difficulty but the *shape* of the effort: higher drag means power applied more slowly against a heavier feel; lower drag means power applied faster against a lighter feel. Neither is inherently better, and the choice is subjective — it depends on how well you connect to the machine, your technique, and where you can apply the most force for the duration without form breaking down.

The recommendation to check the **drag factor in the monitor menu rather than trusting the damper number** is the one piece of concrete, machine-level advice that transfers to any erg, since the same damper setting produces different drag on a dusty machine or at altitude.

**The self-experiment** offered is sensible: change the damper every two minutes through a steady-state piece and every round through an interval workout, then compare feel and meters.

## Setup, and where the reasoning is analogy rather than evidence
The foot-stretcher advice — strap across the widest part of the foot, roughly over the shoelaces, pulled tight — exists to allow enough compression at the catch: shins vertical, arms extended, knees under or inside the arms. Two exceptions are given, both anatomical: long femurs driving the knees into the arms or face, and a large anterior midsection (she lists pregnancy among the causes), each solved by lowering the footplate. She borrows the lifting cue about knees tracking over toes for the case where a high footplate makes the knees splay past the elbows.

On shoes, the mechanism given is plausible and untested here: inflexible or heel-lifted shoes force the heels up and change the footstretcher angle, blocking a good catch. The counter-argument that a cushioned running shoe absorbs leg-drive power is dismissed on the grounds that force is submaximal for a lifter using rowing as conditioning — a reasonable judgement, offered without data.

**Sitting** is framed as driving the sitz bones down and sitting tall so the shoulders can get ahead of the hips in the recovery. The supporting claim — **the stroke is "60% legs, 30% hips, 10% arms"** — is presented as fact and is in reality a long-standing rowing coaching heuristic. No source is given, and none exists in the article. Treat it as a teaching device for where to direct effort, not as a measured contribution analysis.

## The sequence, and the deadlift analogy
The stroke is broken into four positions. **Catch:** shins vertical, body hinged forward from the hips (she uses a clock face, 1 o'clock), arms straight toward the flywheel. **Drive:** push the feet into the stretchers and extend the legs with body still hinged and arms still straight; as the legs finish, the body swings open (1 o'clock to 11 o'clock) and only then do the arms pull the handle to the chest. **Finish:** arms in, body open, legs extended. **Recovery:** arms extend, body swings back over to 1 o'clock to clear the knees, and **only then** do the knees bend until the shins are vertical again.

The deadlift comparison is the article's bridge for a lifting audience and it is carefully bounded: she is not claiming the stroke should look like a deadlift or that deadlifts train rowing, only that both move a load with straight arms, a braced midline, leg drive, and force transferred from feet to hands with the lats connecting the load to the trunk. The stated parallel is moving maximal load vertically versus submaximal load horizontally. As an analogy it is useful; as a biomechanical claim it is asserted, not demonstrated.

The drills — **Pick Drill** (arms only, then arms and body, then half-slide, then full strokes) and **Reverse Pick Drill** (legs only, then legs and body, then full) plus a pause-arms-and-body drill — are standard rowing pedagogy and are the most directly usable content for a lifter with no coach.

## Reading the monitor
Split is the time it would take to row 500 m at the current intensity, and functions like a marathon runner's mile pace: lower means more power, and it lets you project longer pieces. Stroke rate is strokes per minute. The point of watching both is that they can move independently — you can raise the rate and lower the power, which is the standard novice failure mode.

Her prescription: exploit the recovery. Because every drive is followed by a genuine rest, slowing the return to the catch lets you apply more pressure on the drive at a lower rate, aiming at a **3:1 or 2:1 drive-to-recovery ratio**. The test piece given is five minutes starting at 28 SPM at full effort, dropping two beats a minute (28/26/24/22/20) while trying to hold the same split — with the rate reduction achieved by decelerating the recovery, not by rowing weakly.
