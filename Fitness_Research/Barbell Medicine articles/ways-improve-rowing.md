**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **The damper is not resistance.** Settings 1–10 change the *drag factor* — how fast the flywheel decelerates — by changing airflow through the fan cage. You cannot pre-set a load on a RowErg; the machine measures how hard you accelerate the flywheel, so **the damper does not make the workout harder**.
- Practical default: **damper 5**, with most people effective between **3 and 5**, corresponding to a **drag factor of roughly 100–130**. Drag also varies with altitude, machine condition and dust, so the drag number beats the damper number.
- **Foot strap over the ball of the foot**, shins vertical at the catch, knees under or inside the arms. Lower the footplate if you have long femurs or a large midsection. **Avoid lifting shoes** — a rigid heel-lifted shoe blocks the vertical-shin catch position.
- The stroke sequence is **catch → drive → finish → recovery**, cued as **"legs, body, arms — arms, body, legs."** The recovery reverses the drive, and the knees bend *last*.
- Read **split (time per 500 m)** and **stroke rate (SPM)** together. High stroke rate without power moves nothing. Target a **drive-to-recovery ratio of 2:1 or 3:1** — "row slow to go fast."

## The damper explanation, which is the article's real contribution
The commonest misunderstanding about the erg is that damper 10 is "heavy" the way a loaded barbell is heavy. Niemann's correction is mechanically right: the damper lever controls the volume of air entering the fan cage, which sets the **drag factor** — the flywheel's deceleration rate between strokes. What the monitor measures is your ability to accelerate that flywheel against air resistance. The harder you work, the more resistance you feel, **at any damper setting**.

The genuine trade-off is therefore not difficulty but the *shape* of the effort: higher drag means power applied more slowly against a heavier feel; lower drag means power applied faster against a lighter feel. Neither is inherently better, and the choice is subjective. The one piece of concrete, machine-level advice that transfers anywhere is to **read the drag factor in the monitor menu rather than trusting the damper number**, since the same setting gives different drag on a dusty machine or at altitude. Her self-experiment is sensible: change the damper every two minutes through a steady-state piece and every round through intervals, then compare feel and meters.

## Setup, and where the reasoning is analogy rather than evidence
The foot-stretcher advice — strap across the widest part of the foot, pulled tight — exists to allow compression at the catch: shins vertical, arms extended, knees under or inside the arms. Two anatomical exceptions get the footplate lowered: long femurs driving the knees into the arms, and a large anterior midsection. On shoes, the mechanism is plausible and untested: inflexible or heel-lifted shoes force the heels up and change the footstretcher angle, blocking the catch.

**Sitting** means driving the sitz bones down and sitting tall so the shoulders can get ahead of the hips in the recovery. The supporting claim — **the stroke is "60% legs, 30% hips, 10% arms"** — is presented as fact and is in reality a long-standing coaching heuristic with no source given. Treat it as a teaching device for where to direct effort, not a measured contribution analysis.

## The sequence, and the deadlift analogy
Four positions. **Catch:** shins vertical, body hinged forward from the hips (a clock face, 1 o'clock), arms straight toward the flywheel. **Drive:** push the feet into the stretchers and extend the legs with body still hinged and arms straight; as the legs finish, the body swings open (1 to 11 o'clock) and only then do the arms pull the handle to the chest. **Finish:** arms in, body open, legs extended. **Recovery:** arms extend, body swings back to 1 o'clock to clear the knees, and **only then** do the knees bend.

The deadlift comparison is carefully bounded: not that the stroke should look like a deadlift or that deadlifts train rowing, but that both move a load with straight arms, a braced midline, leg drive and force transferred from feet to hands through the lats — maximal load vertically versus submaximal load horizontally. Useful as an analogy; asserted, not demonstrated, as biomechanics.

The drills — **Pick Drill** (arms only, then arms and body, then half-slide, then full strokes) and its reverse (legs, then legs and body, then full) — are standard rowing pedagogy and the most directly usable content for a lifter with no coach.

## Reading the monitor
Split is the time it would take to row 500 m at the current intensity, functioning like a runner's mile pace. Stroke rate is strokes per minute. They can move independently — raising the rate while lowering the power is the standard novice failure mode.

Her prescription is to exploit the recovery: because every drive is followed by a genuine rest, slowing the return to the catch lets you apply more pressure at a lower rate, aiming at a **3:1 or 2:1 drive-to-recovery ratio**. The test piece is five minutes starting at 28 SPM at full effort, dropping two beats a minute (28/26/24/22/20) while holding the same split — the rate coming down by decelerating the recovery, not by rowing weakly.
