# A Lifter's "High" Creatinine Is Usually Muscle, Not Kidney — the Number That Changes That Is 1.5 mg/dL

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Abnormal Labs in Exercise Part I: Kidney Function" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/abnormal-labs-in-exercise-part-i-kidney-function/)) · Austin Baraki · 2020-07-31 · 1,902 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **Creatinine is a muscle metabolite that happens to be used as a kidney test.** It is the breakdown product of creatine, which lives in skeletal muscle. Plasma creatinine is stable only while muscle mass, diet and filtration are all stable — and in a lifter one of those is moving.
- **The magnitude a muscular lifter can produce is small.** The article's stated ceiling: "the vast majority of individuals — even those who train — will not exceed **1.5 mg/dL (133 µmol/L)** in the absence of kidney impairment." Values of **1.4–1.5 mg/dL** flagged "out of range" against a typical **0.7–1.2 mg/dL** reference are the realistic muscularity case.
- **Above 1.5 mg/dL, muscularity is not the answer.** Baraki's rule is that levels above this "should **always** be evaluated further, and should only be attributed to muscularity or dietary factors if other causes have been confidently ruled out." Below it, kidney impairment is still possible.
- **The single most useful thing is a prior value.** 1.3 mg/dL today and 1.3 mg/dL five years ago is reassuring. **0.8 → 1.4** warrants evaluation *even if muscle was gained in between* — a trajectory beats a threshold.
- **Cystatin C is the discriminator.** It is a filtration marker not affected by muscle mass, so a muscular lifter with a creatinine-driven "abnormal" eGFR should have a normal Cystatin C, while genuine impairment raises both.
- **The mirror-image trap is worse and gets missed.** Someone with very low muscle mass may sit at a baseline below 0.4 mg/dL. If their kidneys fail and creatinine climbs to **1.0 mg/dL**, that number is inside the reference range and looks fine — while representing a real decline for that person.

## The physiology, briefly

Glomerular filtration rate is the metric of interest: healthy kidneys filter around **125 mL/min**, which puts the roughly 3 L of plasma through the filter about **60 times a day**. A typical normal value is quoted as 120 mL/min/1.73 m² (the 1.73 m² normalising for body size). Direct GFR measurement is impractical, so clinicians infer it from the clearance of a marker — usually creatinine — and feed that into an estimating equation such as **CKD-EPI**.

That inference is where lifters get caught. If muscle mass and diet hold constant, a rise in creatinine means filtration fell. If filtration holds constant and muscle mass rises, creatinine rises anyway. The equation cannot tell the difference; it was fitted on populations, not on individuals with unusual body composition. Baraki is careful that creatinine itself is inert — "a benign metabolic byproduct… not harmful or toxic," valuable only as a signal.

**Creatine supplementation is a second, separate confound.** Metabolising a creatine load raises plasma creatinine without any change in GFR, and the effect is largest when the blood draw sits close to dosing. He is clear that pure creatine has never been shown to damage kidneys, while noting that adulterated products on the market certainly can. Read "never been shown" as absence of evidence of harm across mostly short-to-medium-term trials in healthy people, not as a demonstrated safety profile in kidney disease.

## What to do with an out-of-range result

The order Baraki gives is worth following as written:

1. **Find a prior value** and characterise the direction and the time course, not just the distance from the reference range.
2. **Consider Cystatin C** if the question is genuinely "is this muscle or is this kidney."
3. **Review exposures:** dehydration; new drugs or supplements. He singles out **NSAIDs — ibuprofen, naproxen** — as a common lifter habit that causes kidney problems at high doses over long periods, and adds antibiotics, proton-pump inhibitors and diuretics. Anabolic steroids and tainted supplements can cause outright injury.
4. **Urinalysis with microscopy** is the standard next step. A normal urinalysis in a muscular person with a *stable*, mildly raised creatinine (say 1.3 mg/dL) is reassuring; glucose, protein, blood, crystals, bacteria or casts are not.

The article closes on the point that most matters epidemiologically: **chronic high blood pressure and diabetes are the commonest causes of chronic kidney disease.** A history of either changes the prior on any abnormal value, and should trigger routine monitoring rather than reassurance.

## Where the evidence is thinner than the claim

The very-low-creatinine mortality claim needs its population attached. Baraki writes that levels below **0.4 mg/dL (35 µmol/L)** are associated with increased complications and death, "likely because these numbers often reflect sarcopenia." The hedge is right, but the study he links for it studied **critically ill patients**, where creatinine as a proxy for muscle mass predicts mortality in an ICU cohort. That is a sound illustration of the muscle-mass mechanism and a poor basis for anything about an ambulatory gym-goer. The direction of the argument survives; the generalisation does not.

Otherwise this is a careful piece. The reasoning is explicitly about *interpretation in context* rather than thresholds, the 1.5 mg/dL line is stated as a floor for investigation rather than a licence below it, and the closing framing — decisions made with a clinician, further evaluation beyond the article's scope — is the appropriate limit for a public-facing piece.

**Distinguish evidence from advice.** The physiology (creatinine is muscle-derived; production scales with muscle mass; Cystatin C is muscle-independent) is established. The **1.5 mg/dL action threshold** and the **rest-and-recheck-with-a-prior-value** sequence are clinical judgement — reasonable, conventional, and untested as a pathway.
