# Type 2 Diabetes as Overflow: The Personal Fat Threshold Explained by a Physician

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "What You (And Your Doctor) Should Know About Type 2 Diabetes: Part 1" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/what-you-and-your-doctor-should-know-about-type-2-diabetes-part-1/)) · Austin Baraki · 2023-05-18 · 1,398 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- Type 2 diabetes affects **roughly 1 in 11 people worldwide** ([Roden 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1797-8), named in the sentence).
- **The disease is presented as two failures running together:** beta-cell dysfunction (loss of the first-phase insulin response, then cell death) and tissue insulin resistance. Neither alone is the account.
- **The organising idea is the "Personal Fat Threshold"** ([Taylor 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25515001/)). Subcutaneous fat cells have a storage limit that varies by genetics, diet, activity and medication; the capacity to make *new* fat cells varies too ([Haczeyni 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29243339/)). Exceed your own limit and the surplus overflows.
- **Where it overflows is what causes disease, not how much fat you carry.** Full fat cells become inflamed and stop responding to insulin ([Kim 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4405637/)); glucose and fatty acids then deposit in liver, pancreas and muscle ([Frayn 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12242452/), [Taylor 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3593165/)).
- **This is the model's payoff and the article's best point:** the threshold is personal, so some people become diabetic at low body fat and others store a great deal without immediate harm. The extreme case is **lipodystrophy** — an inability to store subcutaneous fat producing severe insulin resistance, diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidaemia in someone with very lean limbs.
- **Glucotoxicity closes the loop:** very high blood glucose directly injures beta cells, degrading the first-phase insulin response and worsening the glucose that caused it.

## The mechanism, in their order

*Blood sugar regulation.* Pancreatic beta cells sense rising glucose or amino acids and release insulin in two phases — an immediate first-phase response within minutes, then a prolonged second phase. Insulin does two things at once: it moves nutrients into cells and it shuts off release from the liver and fat stores. Doing both is why it lowers glucose so effectively.

*Fat storage.* Surplus energy is stored subcutaneously, also under insulin's direction. When those cells hit their size limit they signal for new fat cells to be made, and people differ in how well they can do that.

*The consequences of overflow.* An insulin-resistant liver stops suppressing glucose release, which is where high *fasting* morning glucose comes from. Fat deposited in muscle stops it taking up glucose, which is where high *post-meal* glucose comes from — worse when the person is inactive. Fat in the pancreas adds to the glucotoxic injury, so insulin must be raised continuously to compensate, until the beta cells fail ([Bergman 2023](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landia/article/PIIS2213-8587(23)00039-6/fulltext)).

## What to hold lightly

**This is one model, presented as the model.** The personal fat threshold and twin-cycle account is Roy Taylor's, it is well supported, and it explains the lean-diabetic and lipodystrophy cases better than a simple adiposity story does. But the article gives no competing account and no uncertainty — no effect sizes, no study designs, no sample sizes. The sources named are reviews and mechanistic work, not trials; the note should be read as a **teaching model with citations attached**, not as a weight of evidence.

**Where the advice will land.** Part 1 promises testing and treatment in later parts and stops before any recommendation, apart from pointing at waist circumference as the practical measure that follows from the model. There is nothing sold in this article.
