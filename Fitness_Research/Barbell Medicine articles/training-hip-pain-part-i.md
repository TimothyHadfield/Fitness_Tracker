# Hip Pain in Lifters: There Is No "Normal" Hip, and the Pelvis Does Not Go "Out"

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Training With Hip Pain Part I" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/training-with-hip-pain-part-i/)) · Derek Miles · 2021-02-26 · 2751 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- This is the theory half of a series and contains **no training advice** — tendinopathy, impingement, muscle injury and osteoarthritis are promised for later parts. Read it for what to stop believing, not for what to do.
- Hip rotation range of motion varies so widely among **pain-free** people that a single "normal" is not clinically usable: in 120 pain-free adults, 47.5% had more external than internal rotation, 13% more internal, 39.5% equal ([Kouyoumdjian 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22227606/)).
- "Weak glutes" and "gluteal amnesia" fail at the measurement step: manual muscle testing grades on an examiner's undefined "moderate" vs "strong" pressure, and functional screens confound strength with **skill at the task being screened**.
- The sacroiliac joint moves under about a degree, and clinicians agree about palpating it roughly half the time. A "rotated" or "out" hemipelvis cannot be reliably detected, and there is nothing to reset.
- Their evidence is almost entirely negative — tests and palpation are unreliable. That does **not** establish the positive claim that these structures never contribute to pain, and in places the article writes as though it does.

## Why hip range of motion varies so much
The reasoning is developmental: most hip morphology is laid down through adolescence under whatever loading that person's sport or life applied. Hockey players and swimmers should not have the same hips, and most lifters start training after the adaptive window has largely closed. Miles states plainly that **there are no data on what "normal" is for a barbell athlete**.

His numbers all come from asymptomatic populations, which is what makes them useful. [Czuppon 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27840297/) found mean internal rotation of 26.9° in male and 34.7° in female college freshman athletes, with a standard deviation near 10° in both — so roughly a 40° spread covers the ordinary middle. Treating that mean as a threshold mistakes a distribution for a cut-off. [Hogg 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29897784/) found the same picture plus systematic between-sport differences, and range of motion is not even a fixed personal trait: collegiate pitchers changed measurably across a season ([Zeppieri 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26346849/), a small uncontrolled pilot).

With no defensible normal, blaming an individual's hip anatomy or squat depth for pain is an inference the measurement cannot support.

## "Weak glutes" is an assessment problem
Both routes used in practice are compromised. Manual muscle testing separates 4/5 from 5/5 by how hard the examiner pushes — and Miles says outright that clinicians vary that pressure to produce the "weak" finding they expect. Functional screens (squat, lunge, jump) assume looking bad means weakness, when an untrained person mostly looks bad from lack of practice.

The Trendelenburg test is his worked example, cited against on [McCarney 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33076947/) and [Kendall 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22797529/). He then cites two studies finding **increased**, not decreased, gluteal activation in people with pain — [Dwyer 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23911109/) in hip osteoarthritis, [Marshall 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21227522/) in low back pain during prolonged standing. Both are small EMG studies in narrow populations, so they undercut the "pain means the glutes shut off" story rather than refuting weakness generally.

The constructive move is his recommendation, not a finding: replace "normal" with **capacity relative to the task** — strong enough to deadlift triple bodyweight and strong enough to run a half marathon are different questions, and a 5-point scale answers neither.

## The pelvis does not move, and palpation cannot find it
This is the best-sourced part of the article. [Goode 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19119382/), a systematic review, found SI joint motion of ~8° (Z), 2.2° (X), 4° (Y) across the literature; Nagamoto 2015 found under a degree; [Tullberg 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9615363/), using roentgen stereophotogrammetry, found under a millimetre of translation and under a degree of rotation **after manipulation** — the specific finding that kills "resetting" the joint. Motion in single-leg stance and active straight-leg raise is likewise under a degree, in patients with real pelvic girdle pain ([Kibsgård 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24602677/), [2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/)).

Reliability is no better: 48% agreement among trained clinicians on SI motion and provocation tests ([Robinson 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16843031/)), and kappa = 0.27 for PSIS palpation in a systematic review ([Cooperstein 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4807681/)). [Laslett 2008](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2582421/) — the paper manual therapists cite most for SI test clusters — itself calls the dysfunction hypothesis's evidential base "flimsy."

Then the asymptomatic-findings argument: [Eno 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26041855/) found SI joint degeneration in up to 65.1% of people with no pelvic or hip pain, 30.5% substantial. Degeneration that common in people who feel fine cannot confidently be named the cause when someone does hurt. Miles closes on pareidolia — clinicians finding the movement they were trained to expect — anchored to a 2001 study where ~30% of undergraduates reported hearing "White Christmas" in pure noise.

## Where the argument outruns its evidence
**First**, the opening claim that imaging "commonly reveals abnormalities in all athletes, not just in those with pain" carries no citation at all; the only asymptomatic-imaging paper cited anywhere is Eno, which is the SI joint, not FAI or CAM morphology. **Second**, "ideas about glutes being weak, 'asleep', or forgetful are not based in scientific plausibility" is stronger than two small EMG studies support — the defensible version is that the assessment methods cannot detect what they claim to. **Third**, Kendall 2013 is titled *Steps toward the validation of the Trendelenburg test* and is cited here as evidence against it, which is at minimum selective.

One sourcing defect: the sentence attributed to "Stovall 2010" on bony landmark asymmetry links to the Cooperstein review, not to Stovall, so that claim is effectively unsourced.
