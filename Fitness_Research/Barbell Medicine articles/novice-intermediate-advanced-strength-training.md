# Novice, Intermediate, Advanced: The Labels Track Measurement Noise, Not Biology

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Novice, Intermediate, and Advanced Strength Training" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum, reviewed by Austin Baraki · 2026-05-07 · 13,383 words
**Topic:** Programming

Part 3 of their Progressive Loading series, and an attack on the framework underneath most strength programming. Marker mapping is verified, so claims below link to the paper cited on that sentence.

## Bottom line
- **The central claim:** the same adaptive systems run at year one and year ten. What changes is the remaining ceiling, the size of the detectable signal, and the width of the noise band. A retrospective of >9,000 powerlifters shows a smooth logarithmic strength curve from year one to year ten with **no inflection at any tier boundary** ([Latella 2023](https://doi.org/10.51224/SRXIV.250)).
- **The load-bearing number:** day-to-day 1RM variation is ~3–5% in most populations ([Grgic 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00260-z)), which scales with absolute strength. A 500 lb squatter's noise band is ±25 lb; monthly gains of 2–3% at that level are 10–15 lb — **smaller than the noise**. That, they argue, is what "advanced" actually names.
- **The strongest supporting comparison:** eight strength athletes and eight non-athletes on identical 21-week programs gained 3.9% and 20.9% respectively ([Ahtiainen 2003](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-003-0833-3)) — but 5 lb on a 135 lb squat is 3.7% and on a 405 lb squat is 1.2%, so most of that gap is denominator arithmetic, not trainability.
- **The clinically useful part** is the tissue-clock argument: neural change in weeks, muscle in weeks-to-months, tendon lagging muscle by 6–8 weeks, bone in 6–12 months. Novice CrossFit injury rates run 9.5/1,000 h versus 0.74–3.3/1,000 h in experienced participants ([Prieto-Gonzalez 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17051737)), which they attribute to that lag.
- **Two citation problems.** The satellite-cell claim is attached to [Morton 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00154.2016), a load-and-hormones study that does not address satellite cells. The 10×5 at 80% 1RM / TMS fatigue experiment is attached to [Skarabot 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-020-04567-3), which is a narrative review, not that experiment. Neither claim is unreasonable; the sourcing is wrong.
- **They sell into this article three times** — a free Training Plateau Action Plan lead magnet (twice), strength templates, and coaching. Two of the article's most practical claims (what counts as a stall; use estimated 1RM for monitoring) are cited to that Action Plan, and the hypertrophy-mechanism claim is cited to their own paid Low Fatigue Training Guide.

## Decision 1: Should a training-age label pick my program?

Their answer is no, and the argument has three parts.

**The framework's premise.** NIA makes three connected claims: the three tiers adapt at categorically different biological rates; that rate difference dictates progression cadence (session / week / block); and it dictates program type. The first claim carries the other two, so the article attacks it across five evidence streams.

**What the dose-response literature shows.** Volume meta-analyses find more volume produces more growth and strength up to a ceiling, with the curve behaving similarly in trained and untrained populations ([Schoenfeld 2017 volume](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197), [Ralston 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0762-7)). Frequency, when volume is equated, has minimal independent effect in either group ([Schoenfeld 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906)). These are **continuous response curves, not tier-specific patterns** — which is the article's cleanest empirical point. What changes with training status is the absolute volume tolerated, the absolute load handled, and proximity to ceiling.

**The practical claim.** The prescription variables are the same for everyone — FITT-VP. Their heuristic is 10–20 working sets per muscle group or movement pattern per week, with ≥70% 1RM for strength and a wider range for hypertrophy ([Schoenfeld 2017 load](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200), [Schoenfeld 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01490-1)). Where an individual sits inside that range is set by tolerance, goals, schedule and demonstrated response — none of which a tier label reports.

They also press the framework on its own vagueness: asked what an "advanced program" contains beyond volume, the answer drifts to "more complexity" and "more variation," with no operationalised definition. That is a fair criticism, though an argument from absence rather than a refutation.

**Minimum effective dose and MRV.** They accept the dose-response direction — more advanced lifters generally need more work for the same result — but reject the boundaries: no data identify where the need changes, and between-individual differences at the same experience level are large. Maximum recoverable volume they treat as conceptually equivalent to overreaching and **dissociated from the volume that optimises hypertrophy**, and they note no study has volume-matched weekly set escalation against holding sets constant, so proactive within-mesocycle volume progression is unsupported rather than disproven ([Minor 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000581)).

## Decision 2: When do I add weight?

Their replacement is reactive loading: the weight goes up when the lifter has demonstrated capacity, not when the calendar says so. This is **their recommendation**, derived from the biology argument rather than tested head-to-head — no cited study compares reactive to scheduled loading.

**The within-session signal is the warm-up.** Lighter and faster than expected → push the top sets. About the same → repeat the load. Noticeably slower or heavier → preserve or reduce. The reasoning is that warm-ups are the one repeated reference a lifter has week over week. This is coaching heuristic, not a cited finding.

**Why they say a schedule cannot work.** Two independent variances sit under any prescription. First, when adaptation becomes detectable: a systematic review of 40 controlled training studies found mean time to first detectable 1RM/MVC increase of **4.3 weeks, range one to twelve** ([Lambrianides 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004168)) — and the article correctly notes part of that spread is a testing-frequency artefact. Second, day-to-day performance variation. Competition CV runs 0.4% in elite skeleton ([Bullock 2009](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640410902763401)) to ~39% in elite surfing ([Mendez-Villanueva 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19924011/)); across 154 elite Olympic weightlifters over 18 months, snatch CV was 2.7% (men) and 3.7% (women) ([McGuigan 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15320680/)).

The illustrative case series is small but vivid: seven physically active subjects performed a true bench press 1RM daily for 38 days (Cox 2025). All improved, averaging ~30%. One was weaker in week two than week one and still finished +23%. The biggest absolute gainer (+70 lb to peak) tested 20 lb below peak on the final day. **n = 7, uncontrolled** — a demonstration of variance, not evidence for a loading method, and the article says so.

**The important counterweight, which they do include:** Lambrianides also found studies that adjusted intensity through the program kept gaining past 8–12 weeks, while studies holding a fixed percentage of an old 1RM plateaued. So reactive loading is not permission to coast — if the load never moves, the relative stimulus drifts below the ~70% 1RM threshold that drives strength adaptation.

**The stall definition.** Three to four weeks without demonstrable improvement in a priority lift, with consistent training, sleep, protein and manageable life stress, compared against the best performance of the past six to eight weeks rather than a lifetime best. Monitoring by estimated 1RM from consistent rep-range/RPE sets, or triangulation (same lift at RPE 7, 8 and 9 at block start and end). **Both are cited to Barbell Medicine's own Training Plateau Action Plan**, not to literature.

Their diagnostic split is sound clinical reasoning: fresh, unsore, motivated but flat → under-stimulated, add load usually via volume or frequency; sore, grinding, declining motivation, more joint discomfort → over-reached, reduce load while preserving the movement pattern. Session RPE consistently below 6–7 suggests too little, consistently above 8 with flat performance too much. Subjective wellness questionnaires outperform most objective monitoring tools longitudinally ([Saw 2016](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094758)).

## Decision 3: How long before each tissue actually changes?

This is the article's most clinically useful section, and it is the part a lifter should act on regardless of what they think of the NIA argument.

**Nervous system — fastest.** Firing rate rises and recruitment thresholds fall within four weeks in untrained subjects on high-density EMG ([Del Vecchio 2019](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP277250)), but adaptation does not stop there: disynaptic reciprocal inhibition — the spinal circuit that silences the antagonist when the prime mover fires — is greater and faster in veteran strength athletes than in recreationally active people ([Lecce 2025](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP289716)), and takes years. The article is disciplined here: four-year-trained subjects showed 44% greater agonist EMG at maximal effort than untrained ([Balshaw 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.13331)), **but** normalised to the muscle's own maximal signal and controlled for size, that difference largely disappears ([Skarabot 2021](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00148.2021)) — so long-term neural adaptation is real but "more modest than raw EMG comparisons imply."

**Muscle — weeks to months, and the early signal is partly fake.** Mechanical tension is the driver; metabolic stress and damage are downstream correlates rather than independent causes ([Roberts 2023](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00039.2022)); post-exercise testosterone, GH and IGF-1 spikes do not predict hypertrophy or strength ([Morton 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00154.2016)). At early timepoints protein synthesis correlated with damage markers rather than eventual growth, and only after the damage response settled did cumulative synthesis correlate with growth (r = 0.94) ([Damas 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3792-9)); imaging showed thigh CSA up 3–4% at week three, **much of it edema**, with true growth of 7–10% by week ten ([Damas 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-015-3243-4), [DeFreitas 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-011-1905-4)). In trained lifters, twelve-week changes below 3–5% may not clear measurement noise at all.

**Tendon — lags muscle by 6–8 weeks.** Peritendinous collagen synthesis was still climbing 72 hours after a 36 km run ([Langberg 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7793.1999.00299.x)) and still building at eleven weeks of training ([Langberg 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7793.2001.00297.x)). A meta-analysis of 61 studies and 763 participants found high-strain protocols — ~85–90% of maximum held about three seconds — produced significantly larger stiffness gains than light loading, needing at least twelve weeks; its three well-trained-athlete studies showed **no stiffness change at all** ([Lazarczuk 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01698-9)). Achilles stiffness takes 6–8 weeks of heavy loading to change measurably in untrained adults ([Kubo 2010](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181c865e2)); in a longitudinal isometric calf study, strength rose significantly by two months while tendon stiffness did not reach significance until three months, then +50% from baseline ([Kubo 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-011-2248-x)).

**Bone — slowest.** A remodelling cycle runs three to six months; DXA-detectable BMD change typically needs 6–12 months ([Kistler-Fischbacher 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bone.2020.115696)). The best result in the literature is LIFTMOR: eight months of twice-weekly heavy resistance and impact training in postmenopausal women with low bone mass produced ~4.1% lumbar spine and 2.2% femoral neck BMD versus controls ([Watson 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/jbmr.3284)). Eight months of hard work for a few percentage points is the right way to set expectations, and their point that a 12-week DXA is dominated by scanner variation is correct.

**The injury argument that follows.** Resistance training injury rates are low: powerlifting 1.0–4.4 per 1,000 training hours across experience levels ([Aasa 2017](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096037), [Keogh 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0575-0)) versus ~10+ for recreational running. Within a sport, though, novices are hurt far more often, and they skew toward acute injury while experienced lifters skew toward overuse. Average symptom duration is under two weeks ([Raske 2002](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465020300021701)), which they read as evidence that most gym injuries are adaptive-budget overextension rather than structural failure. The extreme illustration: of 22 tendon ruptures in weightlifters, 19 occurred in anabolic steroid users ([Kanayama 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546515602010)) — "the engine became too powerful for the chassis."

**Flag:** the injury argument is the article's biggest inferential leap. The novice-versus-experienced injury gap is consistent with a connective-tissue lag, and equally consistent with worse technique, worse load selection, and survivorship bias in the experienced cohort. No cited study establishes the tendon lag as the causal mechanism. The lag physiology is well documented; the attribution is reasoning stated more confidently than the citations support.

## Decision 4: Is my flat month a stall, or is it noise?

Their answer to the fatigue-and-CNS folklore is the most useful debunking in the piece.

**Fatigue is at least four things**, not one ([Enoka & Duchateau 2016](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000929), [Behrens 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01748-2), [Meeusen 2013](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e318279a10a)): performance fatigability (measured force/power decline), perceived fatigability (effort sensation), trait/state fatigue (task-independent tiredness), and recovery state on the overreaching-to-overtraining continuum. This predicts both "feel bad, lift well" and "feel fresh, lift poorly"; a model treating fatigue as one thing does not.

**"CNS fatigue" from heavy work does not hold up.** After heavy resistance training (10×5 back squat at 80% 1RM), jumps and sprints in trained men, assessed with femoral nerve stimulation and TMS, all three produced fatigue needing up to 72 hours to resolve — but **the etiology was predominantly peripheral; cortical output never showed the predicted deficit**. Within a session, low-load sets to failure produce greater discomfort, perceived fatigue, HRV depression and force decrement than heavy low-rep work kept below failure ([Garcia-Ramos 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2400798)). Their conclusion — failure, not heaviness, drives disproportionate fatigue — is well supported. **But the 10×5/TMS study is cited to [Skarabot 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-020-04567-3), a narrative review that is not that experiment.**

**"Novice recovery" lasts about six weeks.** Untrained subjects show pronounced soreness, larger CK rises and slower force restoration after a first novel bout, attenuating after a few exposures; within roughly two to six weeks of consistent training, recovery kinetics resemble trained populations for that stimulus ([McHugh 2003](https://doi.org/10.1034/j.1600-0838.2003.02477.x)). Programs prescribing categorically different recovery architecture for novices are treating a six-week phase as a permanent condition.

**RPE is a skill, not a training-age privilege.** Lifters across experience levels under-predict reps to failure by about 2–5 reps, with only a tendency toward better accuracy in more experienced trainees ([Halperin 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x)); accuracy is anchored to recent experience with failure ([Halperin & Emanuel 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01229-z)), so a trained lifter six months removed from a near-failure set is also inaccurate. A head-to-head trained-versus-untrained comparison on Smith-machine back squat found **no significant difference in RIR accuracy** ([Malleron 2025](https://doi.org/10.5114/jhk/183040)).

**Flag:** calling the "novices can't use RPE" position "unserious" is rhetoric, and the supporting argument — that novices on NIA programming start artificially light then add weight too fast — is an assertion about typical practice, not a cited comparison. The evidence supports "novices can learn RPE about as well," not "the objection is unserious."

**Why the 1RM is a noisy instrument.** ICCs of 0.90–0.99 and ~3–5% day-to-day variation, but in 34% of analyses across 32 reliability studies retest values were systematically higher than initial, averaging ~4–12 lb upper body and 10–12 lb lower body ([Grgic 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00260-z)) — the test improving with repetition independent of physiology. Untrained men randomised to eight weeks of traditional training versus simply repeating the 1RM test up to five attempts per session showed more volume and more growth in the training group but **no significant difference in 1RM or force production** ([Mattocks 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001300)); a counter-reading is that repeated maximal effort is itself low-volume high-intensity training ([Taber 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01107-8)), and the article presents both fairly. Hypertrophy explained roughly 25% of individual variance in isometric strength gain and less in 1RM gain over 12 weeks in 33 men ([Erskine 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-014-2855-4)).

## Decision 5: What happens if I stop for a while?

Strength is more durable than lifters expect, and this section is well sourced. Two weeks of complete cessation in resistance-trained men produces no measurable strength loss ([Hwang 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001807)); a meta-analysis of >100 detraining studies found loss minor or absent for approximately 28 days ([Bosquet 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12047)). The neural layer decays first — maximal EMG falls before morphological change ([Häkkinen 1985](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1748-1716.1985.tb07759.x)) — while rate of force development is maintained ([Grgic 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000737)), so the decay applies to maximal strength expression rather than all neural adaptations. The striking datum: older adults who trained two years then stopped for three were still 14% (leg press) and 9% (bench) above baseline five years after starting ([Smith 2003](https://doi.org/10.1139/h03-035)). Older adults lose faster and need about two sessions per week to maintain; younger adults can hold strength for weeks on one session per week and one set per exercise **provided relative intensity is preserved** ([Spiering 2021](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003964)).

## What to check before you accept the argument

- **Self-citation on the practical claims.** Reference 10 (mechanical tension as primary hypertrophy driver) is their own *Low Fatigue Training Guide*, a paid product; reference 47 (the stall window and the estimated-1RM monitoring recommendation) is their own *Training Plateau Action Plan*. Two of the most actionable claims in the article, neither carrying a literature citation.
- **Two mis-attributions**, both listed above: satellite cells → Morton 2016; the 10×5/TMS fatigue experiment → Skarabot 2021 review.
- **The direct comparison is never made.** No cited study tests reactive loading against scheduled linear progression on strength outcomes. The case against NIA is mechanism plus dose-response shape; the case for the alternative is inference from that, plus practice.
- **The key null result is underpowered**, and they say so — Lambrianides' trained subgroup was too small to rule out modest differences in time to first detectable gain, yet "the timelines are the same" rests on it.
- **Commercial disclosure.** Three calls to action: the free Training Plateau Action Plan (offered twice), Barbell Medicine strength templates, and their coaching service. An argument that no template should be chosen by tier label routes naturally to "buy our templates, which react to what you demonstrate" — coherent, not contradictory, but the reader should see the funnel.
- **Where they are unusually rigorous:** the EMG normalisation caveat, the edema-versus-growth distinction, presenting both readings of Mattocks, flagging the testing-frequency artefact in the 4.3-week figure, and crediting Mike Tuchscherer's Reactive Training Systems for the RPE tools they advocate.
