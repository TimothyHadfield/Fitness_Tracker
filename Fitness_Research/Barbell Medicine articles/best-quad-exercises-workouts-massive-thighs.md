# Seven Quad Exercises, Ranked on Nothing — the Prescription Is the Content

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Best Quad Exercises & Workouts For Massive Thighs" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-quad-exercises-for-bigger-stronger-thighs/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-03-19 · 7,552 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **Not one of the seven exercises is ranked above another on measured muscle growth.** Squats, pendulum squats, leg press, hack squats, split squats, step-ups and leg extensions are presented as a menu, with the explicit instruction to pick 3–4 based on preference and equipment. Where the article does compare, it compares *within* an exercise — foot position, stride length, torso angle — and every one of those comparisons rests on surface EMG or motion capture, not on hypertrophy.
- To their credit, **they flag this themselves**, repeatedly. On step-ups: "as with all surface EMG studies, it's not clear that higher levels necessarily produce greater gains in strength or size." On leg press foot placement and split squat stride: whether different styles produce different outcomes "is mostly speculation."
- The **only two claims in the article supported by training outcomes** are that machine-based squat training produces similar strength and size to free weights when strength is tested in the trained movement (free weights may be better for power), and that bodyweight and barbell back squats raised quad strength similarly in sedentary young women on a leg-extension test. Both are small, short and in relatively untrained populations.
- The **real content is the prescription**: 3–4 exercises, novices 6–10 sets per week across them, advanced lifters 12–20, compounds before isolation, with per-exercise RPE targets rising as the exercise gets more machine-supported (squat RPE 6–8, leg press 8–9, leg extension 8–10). That gradient is sound reasoning about fatigue and miss cost, but it is **their recommendation, not a finding**.
- The **leg extension defence is the clinical highlight**: they trace "open-chain exercise hurts knees" to mechanistic post-ACL-repair research and say current evidence does not support the harm claim. That is this source doing what it exists to do — though the accompanying "numerous studies illustrate the efficacy" is thinly supported by the list.
- One **internal contradiction**: the article concludes hypertrophy is "probably a prerequisite for strength growth" while citing work arguing that exercise-induced size changes do not contribute to exercise-induced strength changes.

## The programme they actually recommend

The prescription is the part a reader can use, and it is more specific than most articles of this type:

- **Barbell squat** — primary: 3–4 × 3–5 at RPE 6–8 (2–4 reps in reserve); secondary: 2–4 × 6–10
- **Pendulum squat** — 3–4 × 4–6 at RPE 7–9; secondary 2–4 × 6–10
- **Leg press or hack squat** — 3–4 × 4–6 at RPE 8–9; secondary 2–4 × 6–10
- **Split squat** — 2–4 × 8–12 per side at RPE 7–8
- **Step-up** — 2–4 × 8–12 per side at RPE 7–8
- **Leg extension** — 2–4 × 10–15 at RPE 8–10

Weekly volume: **6–10 sets for newer lifters, 12–20 for advanced**, across three exercises for beginners and roughly double the exercise count for advanced lifters. Order: larger muscles first, compounds before isolation. Body-part split or full body is "mostly personal preference."

The RPE gradient is the interesting design decision and it goes unexplained: the further an exercise is from a free-weight barbell squat, the closer to failure they take it. The implicit logic is that a missed rep on a machine costs nothing while a missed barbell squat is a real event, and that systemic fatigue per set is lower on supported movements. That is defensible coaching reasoning and it is **not evidence-backed in this article** — no fatigue-cost comparison is cited.

Their volume numbers do not conflict with the training-variable material elsewhere in this library, though they are noticeably conservative at the low end for advanced lifters.

## Every "targets this more" claim, and what backs it

**Squat variations.** Different stance widths and bar positions change how the lifter moves, and training to different depths produces effects specific to the range trained. Both are cited to biomechanics and activation work in the list (a stance-width motion-and-loading analysis, and an EMG study of the loaded free barbell squat). The article's own summary is that **any squat variant is likely to train the quadriceps similarly** despite differing knee and hip flexion — asserted, with no supporting comparison trial.

**Squat vs bodyweight squat — outcome data.** The one training study described in enough detail to identify is a 2023 *Scientific Reports* trial in sedentary young women comparing progressive bodyweight squats with barbell back squats: quadriceps strength, measured by max-effort leg extension, improved similarly. The article uses it to make a specificity point — strength is measured in a context — but it doubles as the article's only head-to-head growth-relevant comparison between two quad exercises, and it found no difference.

**Leg press foot placement — activation only.** Lower foot position increases knee flexion and "potentially" quad loading; higher placement "may" increase glute max loading. Both hedged, both from EMG. Explicitly labelled speculation as to outcomes. Their default recommendation is a relatively low, shoulder-width, toes-out position that reproduces squat-like mechanics.

**Machine vs free-weight squats — outcome data.** Machine-based squat training produces similar improvements in strength and size *provided strength is tested in the trained movement*, while free weights may be better for high-velocity strength and power. Traceable to a 2019 comparison in recreationally-active women during an initial training phase — a small, short study in a specific population, so the confidence the article places in it is a little high.

**Split squat stride length — biomechanics only.** Longer stride tilts the torso forward and "potentially" shifts emphasis to glutes and hamstrings; shorter stride keeps the torso vertical and loads the quads more. Sourced to a lower-extremity biomechanics analysis. The article again says it is not clear this changes strength or size outcomes.

**Step-ups requiring more glute activity than squats and deadlifts — surface EMG, and they say so in the same sentence.** The source is a systematic review of gluteus maximus activation across common exercises. The article's caveat is the correct one and it is unusually explicit.

**Step-up and torso angle** — stay vertical for quads, lean forward for glutes; box just under knee height; minimise trail-foot push-off. Coaching advice, no citation.

**Leg extension** — an open-chain isolation exercise loading the quads through a shorter range about the knee than the compounds. Two claims follow, of different quality. The first is that the reduced range might concern people chasing hypertrophy but that the exercise seems "relatively low fatigue, perhaps making it possible to train with higher volume and a closer proximity to failure" — **uncited, and hedged twice**. The second is the clinical one, below.

The net position for this library: **the article's exercise ranking is not a ranking**, the within-exercise targeting claims are all activation or motion analysis, and the authors label them as such more consistently than most sources here. That is better practice than the underlying evidence deserves credit for, but it does not change the fact that a reader looking for "which quad exercise grows the most quad" will not find an answer, because the article does not have one.

## The leg extension and the knee — the clinical section

This is where the source's professional standing shows. The belief that open-chain knee exercises are dangerous because of shear forces is traced to its origin: mechanistic research around surgical ACL repair suggesting open-chain work might stress a repaired ligament. The article's position is that this historical reasoning has not been borne out, and **current evidence does not suggest leg extensions harm knees**.

The reasoning is right — a mechanistic concern about a healing graft is not evidence that a healthy person's knee is damaged by knee extension — but the claim that "numerous studies have illustrated the efficacy of using exercises like leg extensions in the rehabilitation process, such as ACL reconstruction" is supported in the reading list mainly by one prospective matched follow-up study from 2000 comparing closed-chain-only against combined open- and closed-chain quadriceps strengthening after ACL reconstruction, plus two EMG studies of leg-extension mechanics. **"Numerous" overstates the list.** The safe reading is: closed-chain-only rehabilitation is not obviously superior, and the blanket prohibition on open-chain work in healthy trainees is not supported.

They also note leg extensions and open-chain work are standard in physiotherapy for patellofemoral pain and patellar maltracking — used, not proven, in this article.

## The benefits section, in brief

Long and broadly conventional. Hypertrophy is defined as an increase in muscle mass, with fibre number essentially fixed after the first year of life and hyperplasia treated as debated but marginal; growth comes from fibre size when protein synthesis exceeds breakdown over time, and hypertrophy "lags until the individual adapts" — with the caveat that if the training dose is far too high, it may not happen at all.

Strength is treated as context-specific throughout: adaptations are specific to muscle lengths, velocities and contraction types, and transfer scales with movement similarity.

The **strength–size relationship** is where the article argues with itself. It cites fibre-level work showing hypertrophied fibres produce more force, then cites the position that exercise-induced size changes do not *cause* exercise-induced strength changes — both being downstream of the same loading — and then concludes that "muscle hypertrophy is probably a prerequisite for strength growth." The first and third statements sit awkwardly with the second, and the article does not resolve it.

**Concurrent training** gets a fair treatment: the interference effect traces to Hickson's 1980 study, but an updated systematic review and meta-analysis found combining aerobic and resistance training does not negate maximal strength or hypertrophy gains, with age, aerobic modality and fitness level having little influence. One 7-week study is quoted where endurance athletes adding resistance training gained **12.9% in VO2 max against 6.8%** for endurance training alone.

**Weight management** repeats the debunk found in the chest article: a kilogram of muscle costs about **13 kcal/day**, ten kilograms takes years and buys "just over 100 calories more per day," and EPOC's contribution to total daily energy expenditure is small if it exists at all. Lifting improves body composition through mechanisms other than resting metabolic rate.

Also covered, each with a single supporting reference in the list: reduced risk of type 2 diabetes, cancer and cardiovascular disease; anxiety and depression meta-analyses where the effect appears independent of whether the trainee got stronger; sleep quality; injury prevention; bone mineral density in postmenopausal women (the LIFTMOR RCT); and knee osteoarthritis, where resistance training produces clinically significant improvements in strength, function and pain even in advanced disease, with a claim that earlier training "slows or even reverses the disease's progression" — **that last clause outruns a systematic review of resistance training in musculoskeletal rehabilitation and should be read as the strongest available reading of weak evidence.**

**What they sell.** The article routes readers to the Barbell Medicine forum mid-article, and closes with their pre-made training templates and custom programming, followed by a paragraph of credentials. The prescription table above is genuinely usable without buying anything.
