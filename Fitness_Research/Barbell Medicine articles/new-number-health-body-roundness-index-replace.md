# Body Roundness Index: A Better Number Than BMI, Argued From a Formula They Describe Incorrectly

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "A New Number for Health: Should Body Roundness Index Replace BMI?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/should-bri-replace-bmi/)) · Nick Occhipinti · 2025-09-15 · 4,817 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **The article misstates what BRI is made of, and its main argument rests on the error.** It says the calculation uses "height, age, weight, sex, race, and waist circumference." BRI as defined by [Thomas 2013](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.20408) — the paper they cite for it — is the eccentricity of an ellipse computed from **waist circumference and height only**. Age, weight, sex and race are not in the formula. The conclusion that adopting BRI "would guarantee that people's waist circumference **and ethnicity** are taken into consideration" therefore does not follow: BRI encodes waist, not ethnicity.
- **The BMI critique is the same one as their companion article and is correct.** Specificity 95–99%; sensitivity **under 36% in men and 49% in women** ([Romero-Corral 2008](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2008.11)). BMI's error is missing fat, not inventing it — and only **24% of US adults** meet both aerobic and strength guidelines, so the muscular false-positive is rare by construction ([Bhattacharyya 2024](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000039539)).
- **BRI's mortality curve is U-shaped, not linear.** In >30,000 US adults, BRI **4.5–5.5** carried the lowest all-cause mortality; **below 3.4 was associated with ~25% higher** mortality (attributed to sarcopenia, frailty or malnutrition) and **above 6.9 with ~50% higher** ([Zhang 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.15051); [Zhou 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980022001768); [Yang 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-02598-4)). Population mean is 5.62 and has risen over 20 years.
- **The "BRI beats BMI" claim is carried by weak designs.** Five references support it; one ([Mulligan 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-019-1223-z)) is about waist circumference and does not involve BRI at all, and three of the remaining four are cross-sectional or single-comorbidity cohorts. A cross-sectional association cannot support a claim about *predicting* risk.
- **The reference list is badly degraded** — 46 numbered entries containing roughly 20 unique papers, with entries duplicated up to three times and several containing two citations mashed into one string. The marker-to-reference mapping still verified, so the attributions below are sound, but the list itself is not.

## What BRI is, and what the article says it is
The premise is the one Barbell Medicine argues everywhere: BMI captures how much mass, not where it sits, and location is what carries the risk. Visceral fat around the liver, pancreas and intestines is metabolically active, produces inflammatory adipokines, and is far more strongly tied to cardiovascular events, type 2 diabetes and all-cause mortality than the same quantity of fat on arms, legs or buttocks ([Gruzdeva 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12944-018-0856-8); [Antonopoulos 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/cvr/cvx106)).

The established fix is BMI plus a tape measure. Waist circumference correlates well with abdominal fat on MRI and CT ([Grundy 2013](https://doi.org/10.1155/2013/454285)), and self-measurement after instruction is accurate and reproducible ([Rimm 1990](https://doi.org/10.1097/00001648-199011000-00009)) — a genuinely empowering finding they use well. Two thresholds are in general use: **action level 1** at 94 cm (men) / 80 cm (women), where adiposity-related disease risk rises, and **action level 2** at 102 / 88 cm, where fat reduction is likely to improve health ([Jensen 2014 AHA/ACC/TOS](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.cir.0000437739.71477.ee); [Garvey 2016 AACE/ACE](https://doi.org/10.4158/EP161365.GL)). Their clinical framing of action level 2 as "a late find" is sensible and is their own judgement, not a guideline claim. BMI plus waist outperforms waist-to-hip, waist-to-height and BMI alone ([Sommer 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-69498-7)) — at the cost of two numbers to interpret instead of one.

BRI is presented as the collapse of those two numbers into one. Model the body as an ellipse with height as the long axis and waist as the short axis; the eccentricity of that ellipse scales 1 (narrow) to 20 (round). **That is the whole of it — waist and height.** The article's list of six input variables is simply wrong, and it is not a throwaway: the closing argument for adopting BRI is that it would guarantee ethnicity is accounted for, which is only true if race is an input, which it is not. What BRI does inherit is a *sensitivity* to central adiposity that happens to be better calibrated for populations who carry more visceral fat at a given BMI — a real advantage, but a different and weaker claim than the one made.

**Second-order consequence:** because BRI drops weight from the calculation entirely, it is not simply "BMI plus waist." It is closer to a refined waist-to-height ratio. The article never notices this, which is why it never asks whether BRI adds anything over waist-to-height — the obvious comparison, and one Sommer 2020 is already positioned to inform.

## What the evidence actually shows
The strongest material is the mortality data, and it is worth holding onto because it undercuts a naive reading of "lower is better."

The U-shape is the finding. Lowest all-cause mortality sits at BRI **4.5–5.5**, with elevated risk on both sides. The low-end elevation (~25% above BRI 3.4) is attributed to sarcopenia, frailty and malnutrition — the same confounding-by-illness pattern that produces BMI's left-hand tail, and the article names it correctly. The high-end elevation (~50% above BRI 6.9) is attributed to excess adiposity. Note that these are **associations from cross-sectional and observational cohorts**, and the low-end attribution to sarcopenia is inference, not measurement.

The claim that BRI outperforms BMI and waist alone for heart disease, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, reduced kidney function and all-cause mortality — "especially in populations where the standard BMI cutoffs are already known to be off, such as those of Asian descent and/or elderly individuals" — is the article's pivotal claim, and its support does not hold up on inspection:

| Cited | What it is | Fit to the claim |
|---|---|---|
| [Cai 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/10641963.2023.2259132) | Cohort, hypertensive patients with obstructive sleep apnoea | Narrow clinical subgroup |
| [Rico-Martín 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.13023) | Systematic review + meta-analysis, metabolic syndrome | The strongest of the five |
| [Mulligan 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-019-1223-z) | EPIC-Norfolk, waist circumference and mortality | **Does not study BRI** |
| [Zhang 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2023.1148662) | Cross-sectional, eGFR, Chinese population | Cannot support "predicts" |
| [Gao 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12944-023-01814-2) | Cross-sectional, colorectal cancer | Cannot support "predicts" |

One meta-analysis, one cohort in a narrow subgroup, two cross-sectional studies and one paper about a different measure. That is a thin foundation for "more accurate screening tool," and the article states the conclusion without qualification.

## Where this sits against their own companion article
Their [BMI article](bmi-accurate-bmi.md), published later, describes BRI and relative fat mass as "recent arrivals, without the deep record against hard outcomes that BMI has built over decades" — the decisive objection, and one this article never raises. Here BRI is "a very useful and accurate tool based on a solid scientific foundation"; there it is promising but unvalidated. **Both are Barbell Medicine, and the difference is not reconciled.** The later article's position is the better-supported one: BMI's outcome record runs to 239 studies and >10 million people; BRI's runs to a handful of cohorts, mostly since 2020, mostly Chinese or NHANES.

The three practical arguments for BRI adoption are honest about being practical rather than evidential — one number instead of two is easier at scale, and BRI escapes the cultural baggage that makes patients dismiss BMI before the conversation starts. That last point is a real clinical observation about consultation dynamics and the kind of thing only a clinician source contributes.

**Flagged, and the article says it itself:** nobody yet knows **what change in BRI corresponds to what change in risk, over what time frame.** For a metric proposed as a tracking tool, that is the load-bearing unknown, and until it is answered BRI is a stratification number, not a progress number.
