# Measure Your Waist at the Iliac Crest, Not the Navel

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How To Measure Your Waist Circumference (Men and Women)" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-to-measure-your-waist-correctly/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2018-07-20 · 556 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- Waist circumference is the cheap measurement Barbell Medicine uses to decide **who should be gaining weight and who should not**. It is not a body-composition estimate; it is a risk proxy.
- The landmark matters. The navel is the popular site, but the large datasets the risk cut-points come from — chiefly **NHANES** — measure at the **iliac crest**, so that is the site to copy. Measuring somewhere else and reading off a cut-point derived elsewhere is a category error.
- The cut-points quoted: **>40″ for men, >34″ for women**. Above them, Feigenbaum recommends weight loss straight away. Within about an inch of them, maintenance calories for a novice (whose waist tends to fall with training anyway), or reversing a weight-gain phase for a more advanced lifter.
- The article's own footnote quietly undercuts the headline numbers: **ethnicity-adjusted cut-points** are lower (roughly 37″/34″ for people of Asian descent), and some national guidelines use **37″/31″** for screening — which he says are "probably better" for screening purposes. So the 40/34 figures are the loose version, not the best version.
- **No references.** This is a how-to piece with a numbered procedure and zero citations.

## The procedure

Five steps, taken from the NHANES anthropometry protocol:

1. Palpate the front of the hip for the highest bony prominence — the **anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS)**.
2. Mark a horizontal line at that level on both sides with a cosmetic pencil.
3. Run the tape around the waist with its lower edge flush to the marks.
4. Pull snug but **do not indent the skin**.
5. Read to the nearest **0.1 cm at the end of a normal exhale**.

The end-of-expiration detail and the no-compression rule are the two that most people get wrong at home, and both bias the number in predictable directions.

## Why the landmark is the whole point

The reasoning here is methodological rather than physiological, and it is the article's one genuinely useful contribution. Waist circumference is associated with premature death, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and stroke in large cohorts — but a "waist" measured at the umbilicus and a "waist" measured at the iliac crest are different variables that happen to share a name. Since most of the cut-point evidence rides on NHANES, using the NHANES site is the only way the published thresholds mean anything for your number.

He also concedes that **some large datasets do not report their measurement procedure at all**, which is an honest admission that the evidence base here is messier than the confident 40/34 numbers imply.

## Evidence versus recommendation

The claim that waist circumference predicts disease risk is well supported in epidemiology, though the article cites nothing for it. Everything after that — the specific cut-points, "immediate weight loss via calorie restriction", the within-an-inch maintenance rule, the reverse-diet advice for advanced lifters — is **clinical practice advice from one physician**, not a finding. It is reasonable advice; it is stated with more certainty than an uncited article can carry, and the footnote conceding that lower cut-points are probably better for screening sits awkwardly beside the bolded 40/34 in the body text.

Nothing is being sold in this article.
