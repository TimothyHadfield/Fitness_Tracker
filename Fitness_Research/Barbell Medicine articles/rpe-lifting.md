# RPE as Autoregulation: The Internal-Load Argument, and the Accuracy Problem It Sidesteps

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "What is RPE in Lifting? (Rate of Perceived Exertion Explained)" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/autoregulation-and-rpe-part-i/)) · Hassan Mansour · 2020-09-04 · 4,831 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The argument is **theoretical, not empirical**. A 4,831-word article carries **one citation** and no reference list. Everything else is coaching reasoning and worked examples. Read it as a well-constructed rationale, not as evidence that RPE-based programming outperforms percentage-based programming — no such comparison is offered.
- The core claim is a **distinction between external and internal load**: the barbell weight is not the stimulus, it is a strategy for eliciting a response, and the response — modulated by training status, sleep, stress, nutrition, environment — is what drives adaptation. RPE is offered as a composite readout of internal load with all those variables already "baked in." This is a real and useful framing borrowed from endurance sport, and it is the strongest thing in the article.
- **Their answer to the subjectivity objection is not "lifters are accurate" but "you don't need to be."** They argue the useful range of loads is already narrow — nobody who deadlifted 105 kg last week is choosing between 65 and 150 kg — so RPE only has to calibrate within that range. That is a materially weaker and more defensible claim than the one usually made for RPE.
- **The one citation does not support the claim it is attached to.** "Trainees can implement RPE to a reasonable degree of accuracy within the span of a few weeks" links to [Helms 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002097), titled *Self-Rated* Accuracy of RPE-Based Load Prescription in Powerlifters. Self-rated accuracy measures confidence, not accuracy. It is the wrong design for the claim.
- **This clashes with the library's accumulated position on RIR estimation**, which is that people are systematically wrong about proximity to failure — often by a lot. See below; the clash is real but narrower than it first looks.

## The external/internal load distinction
The setup is a scenario every intermediate lifter recognises: a linear progression prescribes 125 kg, the warm-ups feel wrong, the fourth rep is missed, and the session is abandoned in frustration. Mansour's diagnosis is that the prescription was written against the wrong variable.

External load is the absolute physical work — 150 kg for 8, or 5 km in 25 minutes. Internal load is the psychological and physiological response it produces, and that response varies with training status, emotional state, nutritional status, genetics and environment. The same 25-minute 5 km is a different stimulus for a novice and an elite runner, and a different stimulus for the same runner in cool autumn air versus summer humidity. **The claim is that adaptation tracks internal load, so a prescription written in external-load units is prescribing the proxy rather than the thing.**

This is standard in endurance coaching and much less standard in barbell training, and importing it is the article's genuine contribution. It also does real explanatory work: it is offered as the reason for the wide between-person variation in response to identical programmes, which most programme design quietly ignores.

**Flagged:** internal load is presented as an established causal mechanism rather than a model. No study is cited for the claim that adaptation tracks internal load, or that RPE indexes it well. It is plausible physiology stated with the confidence of a finding.

## What RPE is here, and how it is meant to be used
The scale runs 1–10 for lifting, anchored to reps in reserve as a beginner's heuristic: RPE 10 is no reps left, 9 is one, 8 is two. A prescription reads "Back Squat 4 reps @ RPE 8 x 3 sets" and the load floats. For aerobic work the scale starts at 6 and is anchored to talk-test descriptors instead.

The most important procedural point in the article is easy to miss: **the warm-ups do the autoregulating, prospectively.** The lifter is not meant to execute a planned load and then rate it afterwards; they are meant to read the ramp — how 225 lb felt today versus last week — and pick the top-set load from that. The three worked scenarios (feels the same → small increase; feels heavy → drop 10–15 lb; flying → jump 4%) are all decisions made before the working set, not diagnoses made after. Their working rule of thumb is that **±5% load ≈ ±1 RPE at a given rep count**, with the caveat that this varies by person and lift.

Three further practical positions, all coaching judgement rather than evidence:
- **RPE charts and e1RM calculators ballpark only.** They are explicit that a chart's output does not generalise across lifts, across people, or across phases within one person — an e1RM derived from a set of 8 at the end of a high-rep block will overpredict a single in the next block, and that is a context effect, not lost strength. This is unusually honest for a source that uses e1RM in its own templates.
- **RPE and linear progression are not opposed**, provided the linear progress happens without exceeding the prescribed stress.
- **New exercises get worked up to slowly**, never extrapolated from ratios off a related lift.

The failure modes are named symmetrically: chronic underrating buries you in fatigue, chronic overrating leaves progress on the table. The note that habitual overrating often traces to apprehension after a prior injury is the clinician's voice in the article and is worth keeping.

## The subjectivity objection, and where this collides with the rest of the library
The article takes the standard criticism head-on — RPE is subjective, so it licenses slacking — and answers it in two moves.

The first move is good: subjectivity is the feature, because the subjective response *is* the stimulus, and a "myopic focus on performance numbers alone" deliberately discards information. The second move is the interesting one: **accuracy requirements are lower than critics assume, because the search space is small.** A lifter with recent data on a lift is choosing within a narrow band; RPE only has to calibrate within it. "Perfect accuracy is not necessary for it to be a useful tool."

**The clash.** Other material in this library says people are bad at this in ways that ought to matter. [Halperin 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x) finds lifters under-predict reps to failure by **2–5 reps**, with only a tendency toward better accuracy in more experienced trainees; [Barbosa-Netto 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29112055/) found over 40% of lifters got 6–10 extra reps with their self-declared 10-rep weight and 26% got 19–20 — nine-plus reps in reserve while believing they were near failure. Against that, the trials that *verified* RIR accuracy before use found errors under one rep, or a worst case of −1.5 reps on a 3-RIR squat estimate, improving on retest ([the 18-subject failure trial](../House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/hard-should-workout-new-study-epic.md); [the RIR-accuracy trial](../House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/train-easier-size-strength-new-study-interesting.md)).

The reconciliation is that both are true of different populations: **untrained-to-effort lifters are badly wrong; lifters who have recently been to failure and have practised the estimate are close.** Barbell Medicine's position is compatible with this, but only because they never quantify. They assert the skill develops "within the span of a few weeks" and cite a self-rated study for it. A 2–5 rep error is not a rounding error inside a narrow load band — at 4 reps @ RPE 8 it is the difference between the prescription and a warm-up. **The article's own argument would be stronger, not weaker, for saying that RPE requires periodic calibration against true failure**, which is exactly the fix the rest of the library converges on and which this article never mentions.

**Overreach flag, stated once:** the claim that percentage-based prescription "quickly loses validity" is put as a defect of percentages rather than as a tradeoff. Percentages fail by ignoring day-to-day readiness; RPE fails by inheriting the lifter's estimation error. Neither article-side comparison is tested here.

## Commercial note
The article is written by a coach at a company that sells [training templates](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/) built on RPE prescription, and the worked examples run on their own Beginner Prescription programme. The argument is not thereby wrong, but the article is a rationale for the product's central design choice, and it does not consider the case against it beyond the strawman version.
