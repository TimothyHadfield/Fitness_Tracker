# Shoulder Pain: Rehab by Modifying Load, Not by Fixing the Scan

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Barbell Medicine Guide to Shoulder Pain" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/shoulder-pain-guide/)) · Charlie Dickson · 2025-05-05 · 6,710 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **One study carries almost the whole evidential load here.** Barreto 2019 MRI-scanned *both* shoulders of 123 people who had pain in only one. Rotator cuff tears, labral tears and osteoarthritis showed up on the painless side almost as often as the painful side. Full-thickness supraspinatus tears and glenohumeral osteoarthritis were about **10 percentage points more common** on the painful side; most other findings were near-equally distributed.
- That is a **cross-sectional imaging study**. It supports "MRI findings are common in painless shoulders, so a finding is not automatically the cause" — it does not support "structure never matters", and the 10-point gap is itself evidence that some findings do track with pain.
- **The rehab protocol is not evidence-based in the citation sense — it is clinical practice, and the article cites nothing for it.** Every dose, tempo, progression rule and case decision is the author's clinical judgement. Read it as "here is how a physio who works with lifters actually does this", not "here is what trials show".
- Their operating rule: **stop chasing a diagnostic label, find an entry point, load progressively.** Outside a short list of conditions (fracture, instability, frozen shoulder, cervical radiculopathy, cardiac), they argue the label rarely changes what you do.
- **"Tolerable" is defined**, which is the most portable thing in the article: mild-to-moderate discomfort that does not interfere with the movement and does not spike to debilitating levels during the session or in the following **24–48 hours**.
- Passive treatments (ice, massage, dry needling, injections, medication) are described as optional comfort aids that can make training possible — **explicitly "not necessary"** for recovery. That is a stated position, not a cited finding.

## What the article actually establishes with evidence

The whole "your shoulder hurting does not mean it is damaged" argument rests on three linked sources, and no numbered reference list.

**Barreto 2019** is the load-bearing one. Scanning both shoulders in the same person is a strong within-subject design for this question, because it controls for the individual's age, occupation, genetics and training history — the confounders that wreck the usual "scan people with pain, find abnormalities, blame the abnormalities" approach. Finding cuff tears bilaterally in people who only hurt on one side is genuinely hard to explain away. But cross-sectional design means no timeline: it cannot tell you whether a finding preceded, followed or is unrelated to the pain.

The other two supply the "general health affects local pain" claim: **Hsu & Sheu 2016** on diabetes and shoulder disorders, and **Yang & Qu 2018**, a systematic review of hyperlipidaemia and rotator cuff disease. Both are observational associations. The article extends the same claim to **excess body fat and high blood pressure with no citation at all**, and adds inadequate sleep as an injury-risk and pain-intensity factor, also uncited.

**Everything else in 6,710 words is uncited.** "Most cases improve without surgery, injections, or months of inactivity" — no source. The prognosis claims, the screening list, the entire rehab framework, all three case studies: clinical reasoning, not referenced evidence.

## Screening — when to stop self-managing

The article gives a red-flag list before any rehab advice, which is the correct clinical order. See a professional for: severe pain not improving over a few weeks; significant loss of strength or mobility; pain with numbness, tingling or instability; pain caused by a fall, impact or sudden force; and — the one people miss — **chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness, or pain radiating down the left arm**, which is a cardiac screen, not a shoulder screen. Everything downstream is explicitly conditional on none of these being present.

## The rehab model: modify the dose, don't stop

The recommendation set, stated as recommendation:

- **Aim for tolerable, not zero pain.** Some discomfort is expected as the shoulder re-adapts to loading.
- **When pain exceeds tolerance, modify rather than rest.** Their concrete levers: swap barbell overhead press for landmine press; use a pin bench press with the pins set *just above the point where symptoms spike*; cut load and volume temporarily; slow the tempo (3s eccentric, 1s pause) so the same rep scheme requires less weight; add shoulder isolation work as a warm-up that doubles as capacity-building.
- **Vary the movements.** Their claim is that novelty itself sometimes helps calm symptoms — offered as clinical observation, with no mechanism or citation.
- **Anchor RPE to symptom tolerance, not muscular failure.** In case 1 the client is told RPE 10 means his limit for symptoms, not true performance failure. This is the single cleverest transfer in the article: it turns an existing autoregulation tool into a pain-management brake without inventing a new system.

## What the three cases show

All three are drawn from Barbell Medicine's paid Pain & Rehab service, with details altered.

**Jake, 25, anterior pain, diagnosed biceps tendinitis on presentation with no imaging.** Bench, overhead press and low-bar squat setup all painful; had been doing nothing but banded rotations for a month on YouTube advice. The fix is not rest but substitution: pin bench above the painful range plus 1–2 light full-range sets, landmine press for volume overhead work, safety-bar squat so legs keep training, sidelying external rotation and preacher curl as warm-ups. Reps dropped 1–2 every two weeks; **load capped at 10 lb increases per week**. Three ~6-week phases (~18 weeks) ending in new 3-rep maxes.

**Jessica, 32, CrossFit/weightlifting, lateral "deep" pain, MRI-confirmed labral tear plus AC joint degeneration, told to trial physio before surgery.** Symptoms only above roughly **85% of 1RM** on bench, and with overhead work. Modifications: low pin bench, floor press, muscle snatch and muscle clean in place of full snatch/clean & jerk (removing the fast drop under the bar), bottoms-up kettlebell press for light overhead volume, push-up plus and serratus punch work for scapular control, and METCONs replaced with assault-bike intervals driven by the legs. Three ~4-week phases (~12 weeks) back to her previous bench max, **with the labral tear untouched** — which is the article's implicit point about imaging.

**Anthony, 63, retired dentist, posterior pain after three days of cabinet installation, no training history.** Struggling to put on a jacket or seatbelt. His programme is not modified lifting but a first-ever lifting programme: band scaption, cable and sidelying external rotation, prone T and Y, serratus wall slides, landmine press, wall push-ups, rows and pulldowns, ~2 months. This case is really about deconditioning meeting an unaccustomed load, and the article treats it as the same problem with a lower entry point.

## Where it overreaches, and what they sell

**Overreach:** the article opens by asserting pain "does not always mean things are damaged" and immediately generalises from one bilateral-MRI study to shoulder pain as a category. Barreto supports "these findings are frequently asymptomatic"; it does not support the stronger reading that structure is irrelevant, and the article's own reported 10-point gap for full-thickness supraspinatus tears and osteoarthritis quietly cuts against the strong version. The claim that pain "can improve without having to fix the abnormalities" is plausible and consistent with Jessica's case, but no longitudinal or trial evidence is cited for it here.

**Second overreach:** "the exact diagnostic label typically doesn't change the rehab process much" is a defensible clinical heuristic offered with no supporting citation, and it is doing heavy work — it is the premise that licenses skipping imaging.

**Commercial interest, stated plainly:** the case studies are advertisements as well as illustrations. They come from the Barbell Medicine Pain & Rehab coaching service, which is linked at the top and bottom of the article and is a paid product. The advice to skip imaging and self-manage points readers toward a coaching subscription rather than a clinician. The advice itself is conventional in physiotherapy — this is not a case of the evidence being bent to fit the product — but the reader should know the recommendation and the product coincide.
