# The Classic Press: A Technique Story Where Frequency Probably Did the Work

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Press Like A Pro: The Classic Press" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/classicpress/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2017-10-30 · 4,012 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **This article cites nothing.** It is a first-person account of one lifter — the author — taking his competition press from **118 kg to 125 kg in six months at 1 kg lighter bodyweight**. Read it as coaching experience and a technique description, not as evidence.
- **The "Classic Press" is a rack-position and hip-drive change**, not a new lift: bar racked low **on the chest** rather than floating under the chin, grip roughly one finger wider, hips pushed deliberately forward into a layback, then a fast stand-up into the press.
- **The author's own verdict on cause is the interesting part.** He ranks the contributors as technique plus programming ≈ 95% of the improvement, with assistance lifts contributing least *in themselves* — and attributes any benefit from variations to the extra pressing frequency and volume they allowed, not to the variations' unique properties.
- **The programming claim is the generalisable one:** press more often. He ran **six pressing "slots" per week** for years, and argues 1RM strength requires regular exposure to singles — a heavy single you could grind a triple with, not a true max.
- **Barbell Medicine sells training templates, seminars and supplements. This 2017 article promotes none of them.** The only outbound links are to a free blog post of his own, a Starting Strength video, Instagram clips, and a Wikipedia page. There is no product pitch anywhere in it.

## What the technique actually is

The article spends its first third on definitions, and the taxonomy matters because the "Classic Press" only makes sense against it: the **floating rack** (bar held in the air under the chin, touching neither shoulders nor chest); the **military press** (feet together, straight knees, no dynamic start); **Press 1.0** (wider stance, minimal dynamic start, touch-and-go reps); **Press 2.0** (maximal dynamic start, as taught at Starting Strength seminars, no bottom stretch reflex); and **Press 3.0 / Olympic press** (Press 2.0 with an intentional double layback).

The **Classic Press** sits alongside these. The bar comes out of the rack onto the chest with elbows slightly ahead of it; the lifter breathes, braces glutes, low back and abs, deliberately pushes the hips forward into a layback, then stands up fast and drives the bar up and back while cueing "straight up."

The reasoning behind the low rack position is stated as a hypothesis in the author's own words: the further down the chest the bar sits, the more chest musculature can contribute. The provenance is anecdotal and openly so — an unbookmarked old-school lifting blog, a phone call with Mark Rippetoe, and a conversation with Derrick Crass, a 1984 and 1988 US Olympian, who described lifters in the clean-and-press era catching the bar low on the chest with a slight lean-back and bent knees so judges could not red-light them for layback or knee bend. The name is chosen precisely because the technique predates him — he is explicit that he did not invent it.

**The stated drawback is real and specific:** because the bar can be thrown up and back quickly, balance is easy to lose. Historical lifters countered this with bent knees straightening under the bar. That is not available at a USSF meet, where the bar comes out of a rack, so the lifter has to manage balance consciously.

## The programming argument

This is the part most readers can use, and it is stated as opinion backed by coaching experience rather than data.

His training at the time ran **six press-family slots per week** — presses, press variations, bench, incline variations — arranged over four days, each pressing slot built on a top single at RPE 8 followed by back-off sets of 3–5 reps, loaded either by percentage or by RPE. Squats and deadlifts occupied the other slots on each day.

The core claim: **improving a 1RM requires regular exposure to singles.** The failure mode he describes is a lifter whose 5RM has climbed but whose 1RM has not kept pace — strength has developed, but the ability to display it as a single has not been trained. His fix is a submaximal single most sessions: a weight you could grind a triple with, which does double duty as a load barometer for the day's volume work and as technical practice.

He also describes **daily maxing** in a handful of advanced lifters — a heavy single every day for 2–3 weeks before a contest or test, never a true max, sometimes substituting a paused variant. Reported results: one lifter added 30 lb to a lifetime-best squat, another 15 lb to a lifetime-best bench, both within two weeks. **This is an uncontrolled n-of-a-few coaching anecdote**, presented as such, with the correct caveat attached — it is not universally useful and should be reserved for lifters with a large base of prior exposures.

His summary of the drivers of 1RM progress: **volume, frequency of exposures, and doing singles regularly.**

## Assistance work, and why he thinks it barely mattered

The honesty here is the article's best feature. He states flatly that none of the variations were life-changing and that he does not believe any uniquely improved his press. The proposed value of variations is threefold. **Fatigue control**: his worked example compares a belted press to a shoulder-height pin press at matched relative intensity and volume (1 @ 8, then 4 @ 9 × 3) — the belted press uses heavier weight and permits the dynamic start, recruiting more muscle mass, so a coach can dial fatigue down with the pin press without dropping the exposure. **Motivation**: a lifter watching a stalled competition lift three or four times a week has historical numbers to feel bad against, where a variation has no training history and early progress on it is a morale gain. **Technique**, mostly through position and bar-path constraints.

His running notes: **shoulder-level pin press** (his favourite — forces strict pressing and bar-path attention); **above-forehead pin press** (overload, may teach the second layback, stressful on elbows and low back); **press with chains** (disliked; the chains swung too much to work as overload); **seated and Z-press** (better for high reps than low-rep strength work); **2-count paused press** (awkward, but credited with helping him press through the sticking point). Incline work he judges to have contributed nothing directly, serving as developmental pressing slots that spared his elbows from six weekly presses.

## For the lifter, for the coach

The prescriptions at the end are triaged by what the lifter is already doing:

- **Novice** with good coach-verified technique, not underweight, on the programme — may try the Classic Press technique.
- **Intermediate/advanced** who is *not* using a floating rack, or who is but presses fewer than twice a week, with stalled progress — change **programming first**: more frequency and volume at appropriate intensities.
- **Intermediate/advanced** using a floating rack *and* already pressing more than twice a week, still stalled — then change **technique** before programming.

For coaches, three variables: programming, technique, fatigue management. Two statements in this section deserve flags.

**Flagged as overreach.** "There is overwhelming evidence to suggest that increasing volume appropriately produces better outcomes with respect to strength, hypertrophy" — the direction is broadly consistent with the volume-response literature elsewhere in this library, but the word *overwhelming* is doing work no citation supports in an article that cites nothing at all.

**Flagged as an evidence-free safety claim.** "There is insufficient data to suggest the Classic Press' rack position and mechanics increase the likelihood of upper limb or lower back pain or injuries." Absence of data is stated correctly, but in context it functions as reassurance about a technique nobody has studied. The accompanying note — that pain and injury are two different things not necessarily related — is the Barbell Medicine pain-science position, stated here without support.

His final claim on fatigue management is more defensible: a lifter with compromised recovery (losing weight, poor sleep, high stress) should not be held on a low-volume, high-intensity programme for long, because they detrain readily. And his practical observation, offered as experience, is that the press tolerates more frequency, more volume and slightly higher relative intensity than the squat or deadlift, with advanced lifters pressing more than four times a week not being unusual.
