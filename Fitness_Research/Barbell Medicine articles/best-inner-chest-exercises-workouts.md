# "Inner Chest": An Article That Debunks Its Own Title and Then Keeps Selling It

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "5 Best Inner Chest Exercises & Workouts (With Samples)" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-inner-chest-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine (no named author) · 2024-01-30 · 3,103 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **The article says in its first paragraph that the premise is wrong**, and repeats it later: "the inner chest isn't a separate muscle — it's part of your pecs as a whole," and isolating a portion of it is "unlikely." Whether regional hypertrophy happens at all is left as "up for debate." That is the correct answer and it is creditable to state it above the fold in an article whose title promises otherwise.
- **Nothing in the reference list is about regional hypertrophy of the pectoralis major.** Not one of the ten references measures growth in a sub-region of any muscle. The list contains EMG comparisons, a single- vs multi-joint review, respiratory-function studies in smokers and COPD patients, a golf correlation, a push-up cohort study and a cardiovascular-risk trial. There is no evidence here for inner-chest targeting because none was cited.
- **Anatomically there is no inner head.** The pectoralis major has a clavicular and a sternocostal head (with an abdominal portion sometimes described), divided by origin. "Inner chest" names the sternal end of a muscle near where it originates, not a distinct compartment with its own nerve supply or its own line of pull. The article's own statement is the accurate one.
- **Then it contradicts itself twice.** The high-to-low cable fly is introduced as "an isolation exercise… to improve the **lower portion** of your pectoral muscles," a regional claim asserted with no support; and the dumbbell hex press is included specifically because you are "squeezing the muscles together," which is the inner-chest idea the opening paragraph rejects.
- **The actual programming advice is fine and generic:** train through a relatively large range of motion, load the muscle at varied angles, work near failure, progress the load, and start around **10–15 sets per week for chest**. This is their standard hypertrophy guidance and does not depend on the inner-chest frame at all — which the article admits.
- **This reads as SEO content, not as the clinicians' work.** It is bylined "Barbell Medicine" with no named author, sits in a cluster of nine near-identical chest articles that link to each other, and is markedly weaker than the Feigenbaum, Baraki and Inglis pieces elsewhere in this library.

## The five exercises, as prescribed

|---|---|---|---|
| Incline bench press | Primary | 3–4 × 5–8 | RPE 7–8 (2–3 reps in reserve) |
| Flat dumbbell bench press | Secondary | 3 × 10 | RPE 7–8 |
| Dumbbell flye | Tertiary | 2–4 × 10–15 | RPE 8–9 (1–2 in reserve) |
| High-to-low cable flye | Tertiary | 2–4 × 10–15 | RPE 8–9 |
| Dumbbell hex press | Tertiary | 2–4 × 10–15 | RPE 8–9 |

The one specific technique claim worth recording is the incline angle: **~15° for strength carryover, 45–70° if the goal is transfer to an overhead press.** No source is given for either figure. The written how-to sets the bench at 15–30°.

The RPE prescriptions are internally consistent with Barbell Medicine's autoregulation material, which is linked five times, and the effort gradient — compounds further from failure, isolation closer — is a defensible default.

## What the article claims for chest training, and what would be needed to support it

Stripped of the numbers, the claimed benefits are: builds muscle and several kinds of strength; may improve respiratory capacity; is versatile because pressing recruits anterior deltoid and triceps and flyes recruit biceps; may transfer to sport; improves cardiovascular health.

Two of these are worth flagging as evidence-type mismatches.

**Respiratory capacity.** The supporting work is a study of upper-body resistance training in sedentary male smokers and an RCT of strength training in COPD patients. Both are clinical populations with impaired baseline function. Reporting them under "benefits of training the inner chest" for a general reader takes a finding about restoring function in disease and presents it as a benefit of chest exercises for everyone. The COPD claim itself — that resistance training lowers inflammatory markers and improves strength, endurance, function and quality of life in those patients — is reasonable; its relevance to a hypertrophy article is not. (The article also misspells the condition as "COPS.")

**Cardiovascular health.** The push-up study cited is an occupational cohort of active adult men followed for future cardiovascular events — an association between a fitness measure and later events, in a self-selected population. The article's framing, that performing these exercises "can greatly improve your cardiovascular health," converts a cross-sectional-style association into a causal claim about the exercises listed above it. The separate statement that resistance training improves blood pressure, body composition, insulin sensitivity and lipids is hedged correctly ("is assumed to") and is supported by the trial in the list.

**Flagged, and this is the sharpest one:** the dumbbell flye is credited with acting "as a chest opener, which helps reduce back pain, boost range of motion, and lessen stiffness in the upper body area." That claim carries **no citation at all**, and it runs directly against Barbell Medicine's own pain-science position elsewhere in this library — that tightness and posture predict pain poorly, and that stretching a muscle open is not a mechanism for reducing back pain. Their clinical writers would not have let this sentence through. It is the clearest sign that this article was not produced by the same people as the rest of the source.

Two smaller errors: the hex press is described as "an isometric training exercise," which it is not — it is a dynamic press with a sustained isometric adduction component; and the dumbbell flye how-to instructs the reader to lie on an incline bench while the equipment list for the same exercise calls for a flat bench.

## Where it is honest

For all of that, the article does three things right, and they are worth separating from the rest.

1. **It refuses the premise.** Twice, unprompted, in an article optimised for a search term that depends on the premise being true. Most publishers monetising "inner chest" do not.
2. **It says the answer does not change either way.** "Whether differences in regional hypertrophy from different exercises are possible or not doesn't really change our recommendations." That is the practically important point: variety of angles, long ranges of motion, proximity to failure and progressive loading are what you would do regardless.
3. **It tells readers not to chase exercises.** "There are no super-secret or special exercises that have to be done… Don't feel pressured to copy what influencers are doing." Picking exercises by equipment access and enjoyment is named as the biggest factor.

**Sells:** their training programmes and personalised online fitness coaching, both linked in the conclusion, plus nine internal links to sibling chest articles and five to their own RPE and progressive-loading posts. Nothing is claimed for a product, but the article exists to route search traffic into the programme and coaching pages.
