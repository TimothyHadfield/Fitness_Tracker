# Range of Motion: Full Is the Default, and Partials Have Four Different Jobs

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Range of Motion Considerations" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/range-of-motion-considerations/)) · Ben Inglis · 2022-10-03 · 3,430 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- The article refuses to answer "deep squats or 90 degrees?" as one question. It splits it into four — strength, hypertrophy, pain, health — and gets a different answer in each. That framing is the most useful thing in it.
- **Strength is joint-angle specific**, so the answer depends entirely on the test. Full ROM squat training improved full, parallel *and* half squat maxes (Pallarés 2019); quarter squats beat full squats for sprint speed and jump power in college athletes (Rhea 2016). Neither result is wrong; they measured different things.
- **For hypertrophy the article backs larger ROM as default**, and the trials it lists are one-sided in that direction: Pinto 2012 (biceps, 0–130° beat 50–100°), McMahon 2014 (quads), Bloomquist 2013 (0–120° beat 0–60°), Kubo 2019 (adductors and glutes bigger with full depth — **but quads identical**, 4.9% vs 4.6%).
- **On lengthened partials it lands almost exactly where this library has since settled.** Writing in 2022, before the trained-subject trials, Inglis concludes partials at long muscle lengths "may produce similar results compared to full ROM" and advises against using them for a significant portion of training given the thin evidence. That is *equal, not better* — the library's current verdict — reached three years early and for the right reason.
- **For health he assumes the null and says so.** There is no trial comparing ROMs on longitudinal health outcomes, so he declines to claim one. That is the most disciplined paragraph in the piece.

## Strength: the answer is whatever you are testing

The article's opening premise is that strength adaptations are specific to trained joint angle, and it anchors that on the isometric literature — Kitai & Sale 1989, Thepaut-Mathieu 1988, Lindh 1979, Weiss 2000. From there the squat-depth trials stop looking contradictory.

For **full ROM**: Bloomquist 2013 found deep squatting improved both shallow and deep squat performance while shallow squatting improved only shallow. Pallarés 2019 found full squats produced greater gains in full, parallel and half squat 1RM than either partial variant. Hartmann 2012 found quarter squats produced no significant vertical jump improvement while deep back and front squats did.

For **partial ROM**: Rhea 2016 found quarter squats produced greater improvements in maximal speed and jumping power in highly trained athletes. Kubo 2019 found half-squat training was better for half-squat 1RM and full-squat training better for full-squat 1RM — the specificity result stated plainly.

Inglis then does something worth noting: rather than declaring one study right, he goes after **why Pallarés and Rhea disagree despite similar methods**. Two candidate explanations, both his own reasoning rather than cited findings. First, loading: Pallarés ran 4×8 at 60% progressing to 5×4 at 80%; Rhea ran daily undulating 8RM→2RM. Both are low-velocity, high-proximity-to-failure schemes, and neither is well suited to developing power — so their conclusions about ROM and power are weakened by the loading choice, not the depth choice. The relevant load for power output in the squat is around **30–70% 1RM** (Soriano 2015 meta-analysis), with sets kept short enough to limit velocity loss (Weakley 2019). Second, training status: Rhea's athletes squatted **1.39× bodyweight**, Pallarés's **1.14×**, and less-trained subjects respond to almost any stimulus (Ahtiainen 2003), to the point that untrained people gain muscle from walking (Konopka 2014). He finally shrugs and attributes it to interindividual differences — an honest but unsatisfying landing.

## Hypertrophy: where it meets the library's adjudicated position

**The library's settled position is that lengthened partials come out equal to full ROM in trained lifters — not better.** This article is compatible with it, and arrived there independently.

The full-ROM evidence he lists is genuinely one-directional, and the Kubo 2019 detail is the one most people skip: full-depth squatting produced significantly more **adductor and glute maximus** volume, but quadriceps growth was **statistically indistinguishable** (4.9 ± 2.6% vs 4.6 ± 3.1%). The depth advantage in that trial was a hip-musculature advantage, not a quad one.

Against that he sets three partial-ROM findings: Oranchuk 2018 (a systematic review of isometric training — long-length isometrics grew muscle at **1.16%/week** above 70° versus **0.47%/week** at ≤60°), Pedrosa 2021 (long-length partial leg extensions beat both full ROM and short-length partials, with more growth in distal regions), and Goto 2019 (mid-range partial lying triceps extensions, **48.7 ± 14.5%** growth versus **28.2 ± 10.9%** full ROM).

His conclusion is the careful one: partials at long muscle lengths *likely* beat partials at short lengths and *may* match full ROM, "given the paucity of evidence we advise against utilizing partial ROMs for a significant portion of training." That is a recommendation, not an evidence claim, and he marks it as such. It has aged well — the trained-subject work that arrived after publication moved the field toward *equal*, and the confident 5–10% superiority claims that circulated in 2023 did not survive.

One thing to flag: the Goto 2019 result is a **48.7% vs 28.2%** hypertrophy difference from mid-range partials, an effect size that should trigger scepticism, and the article passes it along without comment. Its proposed mechanism was sustained intramuscular hypoxia, not muscle length, so it does not actually belong in the "long muscle lengths" argument at all.

## Pain and health: the clinical sections

The pain section is short and is the one place the article speaks as a clinic. The stated position is standard modern pain science — pain is a complex experience involving far more than tissue status (Cohen 2018), shaped by biopsychosocial factors (Moseley 2007). The practical rule follows: pick exercises that are "somewhat threatening, but tolerable," and progress the ROM back. The worked example is a lifter whose floor deadlift hurts but whose block pull from below the knee does not — start there, lower the blocks over time, manage the load so symptoms stay tolerable. Partial ROM here is a **temporary modification to keep training, not a training method**, and the stated goal is always to work back toward the full range.

The health section is the most restrained. Resistance training's benefits are well supported — the strength-mortality association is carried by five cohort and pooled analyses (Volaklis 2015, Stamatakis 2018, Ortega 2012 in one million adolescents, Ling 2010, Li 2018) — but on ROM specifically he writes that no evidence directly examines different ranges of motion against longitudinal health outcomes, "so for now we should probably assume the null." The pro-full-ROM case is then explicitly argument rather than evidence: dynamic training improved bone mineral density where isometric did not (Neves 2021, in hemodialysis patients — a narrow population to generalise from), muscle mass associates with health (Wang 2019), more degrees of freedom means more physical independence with age. Against that, one point for partials: adherence improves with self-selected exercise (Baz-Valle 2019), so a ROM someone will actually keep doing beats one they won't.

**Flagged:** the overuse-injury claim — "building proficiency in a wider variety of tasks reduces overuse injury risk," supported by a single reference (Campbell 2021) — is stated as fact and is a good deal stronger than one citation supports.

**Sells:** nothing. No template, seminar or supplement is recommended anywhere in the article.
