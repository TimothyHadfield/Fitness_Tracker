# Programming, effort and recovery

Thirty-five notes on how Barbell Medicine tell people to structure training: how much, how hard, how
often, how to progress, and what to do when it stops working. The recovery material — detraining,
overtraining, HRV — is among the best-sourced writing in this library; the programming material runs
from well-evidenced to entirely uncited to product catalogue, sometimes inside one article. The one
thing to know before anything else: **their effort prescription sits below every other source here,
and it is asserted rather than cited.**

---

## The frame: most programming variables are levers on time, not on growth

Their organising claim is that the variables lifters argue about change *how fast* you get somewhere,
not *whether* — and that the sweet spot for each is wide. [How many chest
exercises](many-chest-exercises-per-workout.md) says volume sets the dose and how you carve it into
sessions "doesn't seem to matter." [Progressive loading](progressive-loading.md) says outright that
"over a sufficiently long training career it is not critically important to optimize rates of
adaptation." [What drives hypertrophy](drives-hypertrophy-muscle-growth-works.md) makes the same case
for growth — tension, volume, effort, fatigue management and consistency interacting, exercise choice
demoted — **without a single citation**.

Two strands are properly evidenced. **Volume has a graded dose-response; frequency does not, once
weekly volume is equated** — Grgic 2018 (22 studies) and Thomas & Burns 2016, who trained chest press
and hack squat **1×/week versus 3×/week at matched volume** for the same result
([how many chest exercises](many-chest-exercises-per-workout.md)). And **load is dose-specific for
strength but not for size**, from Schoenfeld's 2017 low- versus high-load meta-analysis, which they
cite in at least six of these articles and read correctly each time ([strength vs
hypertrophy](strength-vs-hypertrophy-training-similarities-differences.md), [strength vs
bodybuilding](strength-training-vs-bodybuilding-similarities-differences.md), [beyond progressive
overload](beyond-progressive-overload-work-smarter-not-harder.md)).

What is a stance is the numbers. Almost every actionable figure here — set counts, RPE bands, rest
windows, block lengths — is house prescription. Sometimes they say so; often they don't.

## How much volume

**The house number is 10–20 hard sets per muscle group or movement pattern per week**, in [the
strength training guide](strength-training-guide-get-stronger.md) and
[novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md) — **uncited in
both**. The strength guide's supporting sentence is attached to Pelland 2024, a *methods paper on
reporting proximity to failure*, which cannot carry it.

The narrower versions vary and none is sourced. [Strength vs
bodybuilding](strength-training-vs-bodybuilding-similarities-differences.md): **8–12 sets per week per
movement pattern**, ~80% at 3–8 reps in the 65–85% 1RM band. [How many chest
exercises](many-chest-exercises-per-workout.md): **1–3 exercises per muscle, 2–3 sets of 3–20 reps,
2–3 sessions per muscle per week** — and it contradicts itself three times on exercise count in one
article. [The beginner prescription](beginner-prescription.md): **2+ sessions/week, 2–3 hard sets per
exercise**, with its "more exercise leads to greater improvements" claim linked to two
low-versus-high-*load* meta-analyses that say nothing about volume.

The ceiling argument is better. Volume has a dose-response "but only to a point," the point being
individual tolerance. [Novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md)
is usefully sceptical of maximum recoverable volume: they treat it as equivalent to overreaching,
**dissociated from the volume that optimises hypertrophy**, and note no study has volume-matched
weekly set escalation against holding sets constant — so proactively ramping volume within a
mesocycle is unsupported rather than disproven.

Their instrument for finding the ceiling is session RPE, from [intermediate powerlifting
programs](intermediate-powerlifting-programs-increase-strength-size.md): most sessions at
**sRPE 6–8**; persistent **sRPE >8 with soreness and no progress = too much**; persistent **sRPE <5
with no soreness and no progress = too little**. Autoregulation substituting for a number they concede
they do not have, presented as such.

The minimum dose is the best-evidenced part. [When time is short](exercise-have-no-time.md): **one to
two sessions a week** associates with lower mortality (observational); **a single set per week
improves strength**, more sets do more (Krieger 2009 meta-regression); and for a powerlifter,
**3–6 sets each of squat, bench and deadlift per week, 1–5 reps, above 80% 1RM** maintains 1RM
(Androulakis-Korakakis 2021).

## How hard — the prescription that breaks with the library

**RPE 6–8 on compound lifts and RPE 8–10 on isolation, even inside a hypertrophy programme.** That is
**2–4 reps in reserve on the main lifts**. It appears in [strength vs
hypertrophy](strength-vs-hypertrophy-training-similarities-differences.md), [strength vs
bodybuilding](strength-training-vs-bodybuilding-similarities-differences.md) and [intermediate
powerlifting programs](intermediate-powerlifting-programs-increase-strength-size.md), and the same
band is what [the beginner prescription](beginner-prescription.md) and [the strength training
guide](strength-training-guide-get-stronger.md) give beginners.

**The split is uncited.** No paper anywhere in this corpus supports treating compounds and isolation
differently on effort. The implicit logic is fatigue budgeting — compounds expensive in fatigue and
cheap in per-muscle stimulus, isolation the reverse — coherent, consistent with their [arms for
GPP](train-arms-gpp.md) and [GPP](general-physical-preparedness-training-lifters.md) reasoning, and
never argued.

**They are inconsistent with themselves.** Their chest-and-triceps article gives RPE 6–8 across the
board while their shoulder article gives RPE 8–10 for the same class of isolation movement. [How many
chest exercises](many-chest-exercises-per-workout.md) argues for **~4–5 reps from failure** and calls
the Physical Activity Guidelines' "within 1 repetition of failure" instruction **"made up"** — correct
about the guideline, but a third number. [Training at home](exercise-home.md) contains the
contradiction inside one article: **"within about 5 repetitions of failure"** in one section,
**"within 2–3 reps (RPE 8)"** in the next.

**The strength half is well supported; the hypertrophy half is not.** "Stay away from failure for
strength" rests on **two Pareja-Blanco velocity-loss trials** (~8 weeks, professional soccer players
and resistance-trained men) where the lower-velocity-loss groups matched or beat the higher-loss
groups with less fatigue. Those are training-outcome studies with a measurable proxy — a much better
class of evidence than the activation data under their exercise-selection content. But they are **two
papers from one lab**, and [strength vs
bodybuilding](strength-training-vs-bodybuilding-similarities-differences.md) treats the conclusion as
settled on that basis. Nothing comparable supports extending the band to hypertrophy work.

The fatigue evidence they lean on is real: failure costs more recovery time at matched volume
(Morán-Navarro 2017; Santos 2021), and **low-load sets to failure produce more discomfort, perceived
fatigue, HRV depression and force decrement than heavy low-rep work kept short of failure**
(Garcia-Ramos 2024, in
[novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md)). Their reading —
**failure, not heaviness, drives disproportionate fatigue** — holds. It justifies *some* distance from
failure. It does not pick RPE 6–8, and no cited study tests that band against a closer one for growth.

One 2022 sentence to quote carefully: [progressive loading](progressive-loading.md) states flatly that
outside untrained people doing isolation work, failure produces no more growth than 2–4 RIR. Its two
citations are Carroll 2019 (small, fibre-level, underpowered) and **Helms 2018, which compared RPE
against percentage loading and did not test failure at all**. The direction survives; the absoluteness
does not.

## RPE and autoregulation: their signature contribution

RPE is how they prescribe load, diagnose stalls, cap volume, scale rehab and programme for youth,
endurance athletes, home trainees and time-crunched adults. It is also the design principle of the
templates they sell.

**What it means here.** A **0–10 scale anchored to reps in reserve** — 10 no reps left, 9 one, 8 two
([returning to the gym](returning-gym.md) gives the house definition; below 5 is rarely prescribed).
A prescription reads "back squat 4 reps @ RPE 8 × 3 sets" and **the load floats**. The history is
credited accurately in [the intermediate/advanced
template](general-strength-training-template-intermediate-advanced.md): Borg's 6–20 scale, adapted for
lifting by **Mike Tuchscherer**, whom Feigenbaum names as his own former coach. The RIR anchoring
traces to Zourdos 2016, cited correctly in [the beginner prescription](beginner-prescription.md),
[youth resistance training](recommendations-youth-resistance-training.md) and [the endurance-athlete
article](strength-training-endurance-athlete-part-iii-programming.md).

**The theoretical case**, from [what is RPE in lifting](rpe-lifting.md), is the **external versus
internal load** distinction imported from endurance coaching: the barbell weight is not the stimulus
but a strategy for producing a response, and the response — modulated by training status, sleep,
stress, nutrition, environment — is what drives adaptation. Genuinely useful, and **stated as
established mechanism with nothing cited**: the whole 4,831-word article carries **one citation and no
reference list**.

**How it is actually operated** is the part people miss: the warm-ups autoregulate *prospectively*.
You read the ramp — how 225 lb felt today against last week — and pick the top set from that.
[What is RPE](rpe-lifting.md): feels the same → small increase; feels heavy → drop **10–15 lb**;
flying → jump **4%**. Their conversion is **±5% load ≈ ±1 RPE** at a given rep count. [The
intermediate/advanced template](general-strength-training-template-intermediate-advanced.md) works it
on a 500 lb squatter — bar through 425×1 @ RPE 6, 445×1 @ 7, 460×1 @ 8, then sets of 5 at 405 climbing
RPE 8 → 8.5 → 9, fifth set scrapped — and, on the paused bench, *abandons* an attempt to predict load
from RPE charts and an estimated 1RM in favour of ramping until a set lands on target. That is the
most honest paragraph in the corpus about what RPE charts are worth; the same article notes e1RM does
not generalise across lifts, people or phases.

They are candid about failure modes — **chronic underrating buries you in fatigue, chronic overrating
leaves progress on the table**, and habitual overrating often traces to apprehension after an injury.

**The reliability evidence is the weak point.** [What is RPE](rpe-lifting.md) answers the subjectivity
objection not by claiming accuracy but by claiming **you don't need to be accurate**, because the
useful load band is already narrow. That is a defensibly modest claim. Its single citation is **Helms
2017, *Self-Rated* Accuracy of RPE-Based Load Prescription in Powerlifters**, attached to "trainees can
implement RPE to a reasonable degree of accuracy within a few weeks." **Self-rated accuracy measures
confidence, not accuracy** — wrong design for the claim. [Training at home](exercise-home.md) makes
the same assertion with no study named at all.

Against it: **Halperin 2022 finds under-prediction of reps to failure by 2–5 reps** across experience
levels, and **Barbosa-Netto 2021 found over 40% of lifters got 6–10 extra reps at their self-declared
10-rep weight, 26% got 19–20**. At 4 reps @ RPE 8, a 2–5 rep error is the difference between the
prescription and a warm-up. The library's fix is **periodic calibration against true failure**, and it
appears nowhere in this corpus — even though
[novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md) cites Halperin
correctly and adds **Malleron 2025, finding no trained-versus-untrained difference in RIR accuracy**.
The corpus contains the correction to its own RPE article, unreconciled.

The usable summary: **RPE is a good instrument for adjusting a load you already roughly know on a day
whose readiness you don't. It is a poor instrument for setting absolute proximity to failure in
someone who has not recently been there.**

## How to progress: load follows adaptation

**The redefinition.** [Beyond progressive
overload](beyond-progressive-overload-work-smarter-not-harder.md) argues overload does not add stress
— it adds **stimulus to hold stress constant** as fitness rises. They reject the ACSM definition for
conflating stimulus with stress and the NSCA one for describing periodisation, framing it on
Impellizzeri 2019's internal/external load split and DeLorme's original rule. The corollary: **if
fitness drops, lower the stimulus.**

**The rule.** [Progressive loading](progressive-loading.md): **"the weight on the bar should be
reactive, not proactive."** Its worked example is the best teaching device in the corpus — a lifter
squats 5 @ 315 lb at RPE 8 and next week feels the same. **Full Send** (320 lb, comes back RPE 9)
demonstrates nothing but extra fatigue; **Repeater** (315 lb, RPE 8) is correct; **Undershoot** (305
lb, RPE 7) is wrong but harmless. The diagnostic: **someone genuinely stronger adds weight without the
RPE rising.** Both errors miss; only one costs anything, so **prefer undershooting over a career**.

**Why a schedule cannot work.** **Lambrianides 2022: a mean of 4.3 weeks to a demonstrable strength
increase, range 1 to 12** — so weekly load increases outrun adaptation. (The 2025 article calls that
paper "a review of 40 studies"; the title indicates an original investigation. Verify before
repeating.) On top of that sits **~3–5% day-to-day 1RM variation** (Grgic 2020, 32 reliability
studies) and a case series where **seven subjects did a true bench 1RM daily for 38 days**, all
improving ~30% — one weaker in week two than week one and still +23%
([novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md)).

**The counterweight they include:** Lambrianides also found studies that adjusted intensity kept
gaining past 8–12 weeks while studies on a fixed percentage of an old 1RM plateaued. Reactive loading
is not permission to coast — if the load never moves, the relative stimulus drifts below the **~70%
1RM** strength threshold.

**Three things count as progression** ([the strength training
guide](strength-training-guide-get-stronger.md)): more weight at the same reps and RPE, more reps at
the same weight and RPE, or **the same work at a lower RPE and higher bar speed**. That third is why
[understanding stalls](training-expectations-understanding-stalls.md) insists bar weight alone is
inadequate: **315 × 1 @ RPE 9 followed by 315 × 1 @ RPE 8 is progress; 135 × 1 @ RPE 8 followed by
145 × 1 @ RPE 10 is not.**

**Intensity floors drift unexplained** between the two articles — **65–75% and >85–90%** in 2022,
**~70% and ~85%** in 2025 — and the singles floor rests on **Androulakis-Korakakis 2018, a pilot study
in a handful of powerlifters**, reused for the same claim in at least four articles. **Double
progression** is cited to Plotkin 2022, which showed reps are a legitimate progression variable but
did not test the scheme.

**The gap that matters most: no cited study compares reactive loading against scheduled progression.**

## Load, reps, and set-extension methods

For **strength**, loads above **60–70% 1RM** beat lighter, with the productive band at **65–85%** and
singles at **85–93%** ([strength vs
bodybuilding](strength-training-vs-bodybuilding-similarities-differences.md)) — the windows are house
numbers, the direction is Schoenfeld 2017. For **hypertrophy**, a wide band: **3–20 reps for compounds
and 6–20+ for isolation** ([strength vs
hypertrophy](strength-vs-hypertrophy-training-similarities-differences.md)).

The best conceptual frame in the corpus explains why the same lifts are used differently: **strength
training is constrained and hypertrophy training is not.** A powerlifter must train the competition
lifts at competition loads and speeds; someone chasing size has no test to prepare for, so exercise,
load and rep scheme are free. Higher hypertrophy volume is a *consequence of the format*, not a
requirement of the goal. [Strength vs
bodybuilding](strength-training-vs-bodybuilding-similarities-differences.md) adds the point most
content skips: **bigger does not automatically mean stronger** — the relationship is cross-sectional
and causally unsettled (Reggiani & Schiaffino 2020).

[Specificity](pendulum-specificity-application-part-i.md) takes that apart. Specificity is three
things — **energy system, muscle group and skill** — and the muscle-group part extends to
**contraction type, velocity, joint angle and muscle length**. Its heretical exhibit is **Rhea 2016:
28 subjects, 16 weeks, three squat depths; strength improved at the depth trained, and quarter squats
transferred best to vertical jump and 40-yard sprint.** Its real thesis is *anti*-specificity: push
SAID to its end and a golfer should never squat, so there is an **inflection point** past which
general strength work pays more than technical practice — reasoning, and marked as such. It also
introduces the **Repeated Bout Effect** as the mechanism by which a fixed programme stops working: the
same squat accrues less damage over time, so a static input becomes a smaller stimulus.

[Myo-reps](myo-reps.md): **one activation set of ~12–30 reps to RPE 8, then 3–5 rep back-off sets on
the same load with 20–30 second rests**, stopping when you cannot complete 3; target failure within
about **five back-off sets**; 10–12 reps ≈ **62–68% 1RM**, 12–15 ≈ **58–62%**. A large activation-to-
back-off gap (14@8 → 4@10) buys more total volume further from failure; a small gap (10@8 → 5@10) the
reverse. Framed honestly as **time-efficient volume on a lower-priority lift, not superior**, and not
for competition lifts. Its stimulus story runs on **muscle damage and metabolic stress**, both largely
retired elsewhere in this library, and its motor-unit claim is cited to a **computational model**.
**No comparison against volume-matched straight sets is cited.**

[Blood flow restriction](introduction-blood-flow-restriction-training.md): cuffs at **20–30% 1RM**,
**one set of 30 then 3 × 15 with 30–60 seconds rest**, tightness at a perceived **7/10** because
required pressure varies with blood pressure, limb size and cuff width. **Weaker than conventional
training alone** (Loenneke 2012), **equal or better for strength when combined with it** (Luebbers
2014; Yamanaka 2012). The rehab case is strongest — Takarada 2000 found **passive occlusion with no
exercise reduced disuse atrophy**. It grades its mechanisms honestly and **carries affiliate-tagged
links to two cuff products**.

## How long to rest

**2–5 minutes for strength, 2–4 for hypertrophy**, top of range for heavy compounds
([the rest-periods guide](ultimate-guide-rest-periods-maximize-workouts-strength.md)). Both are stated
**before** the evidence review and **neither upper bound is tested by anything cited**.

The evidence: **Schoenfeld 2016** — 23 trained men, 8 weeks, **3 min beat 1 min**, squat 1RM
**+15% vs +7.5%**, bench **+12.7% vs +4%**. **de Salles 2010** — 36 men, 16 weeks, leg press
**+22/+34/+42%** at 1/3/5 min, but **volume was not equated** and they say so. **Robinson 1995** —
squat 1RM **+7/+6/+2%** at 180/90/30 s, so the 90-second group got nearly the full benefit at half the
time cost, which they flag and then ignore. **González-Hernández 2023** — no difference between 3 and
5 minutes. **Ahtiainen 2005** — 6-month crossover, 2 versus 5 minutes, no difference.

Defensible synthesis: **more than 2–3 minutes beats less for trained lifters, mostly via preserved
volume; 3 versus 5 is unresolved.** The mechanism section is careful — PCr falls ~80% in a single hard
effort and takes about **8 minutes** to restore, while ATP barely moves.

Three problems. The 2025 article rests its hypertrophy case on **Grgic 2017, six studies in mostly
untrained men**, and never engages the 2024 network meta-analysis in which **every rest band's
credible intervals overlapped**. The **novice-needs-longer-rest** claim is sourced to a sprint-running
review and a study of enzyme activities in children. And the **2-minute isolation floor** contradicts
Nippard head-on with no study on either side.

The best-sourced section is **supersets** (Zhang 2025): **agonist–agonist** costs volume and force, so
it is acceptable for hypertrophy only if volume is maintained and poor for strength;
**agonist–antagonist** preserves both and is the best time-saver; **alternate-peripheral** holds up at
RPE 6–8 but taxes cardiorespiratory capacity. [When time is short](exercise-have-no-time.md) repeats
that finding **with no citation anywhere in the piece**, and lands on **at least 2 minutes, with
diminishing returns past 3**.

## Shaping the week: frequency, order, periodisation

**Frequency is a distribution device, not a dose.** Weekly volume matters; how you cut it up is
preference ([how many chest exercises](many-chest-exercises-per-workout.md)), and they flag their own
gap — that literature covers sessions under ~10–12 sets per muscle. Their argument for spreading
volume is fatigue, not adaptation. **2–4 sessions a week** is the house range ([the strength training
guide](strength-training-guide-get-stronger.md)). **Rest days are not a physiological requirement**:
three consecutive training days matched spaced sessions (Yang 2018), and small studies of daily
training still showed gains ([when time is short](exercise-have-no-time.md)) — schedule around your
life.

Two placement rules worth keeping. From [the intermediate/advanced
template](general-strength-training-template-intermediate-advanced.md): in a developmental block each
competition lift leads its own session and the deadlift is spaced from the squat; **approaching a
meet, reverse both deliberately** to condition the lifter to the meet's fatigue sequence. From [twelve
ways to skin the Texas Method](ways-skin-texas-method.md): **move the deadlift off the end of a long
volume day and open the light day with it** — volume unchanged, placement moves — and put conditioning
between days one and two or after day three, preserving a full rest day.

On periodisation they are sceptical of the theory. [The overtraining
article](overtrained-evidence-shows.md) cites **Kiely 2018** for the position that periodisation
imported supercompensation without foundation, and argues that because neural, muscular and connective
adaptations run on different clocks **there is no single crest to time a session to**. The operational
version, from [intermediate powerlifting
programs](intermediate-powerlifting-programs-increase-strength-size.md): run a **mesocycle of 4–8
weeks** while it works, change variables when it stops, "this is also not an exact science," with
variation **high for untrained lifters and reduced for 2–4 weeks near a test**.

The one worked scheme is [twelve ways to skin the Texas Method](ways-skin-texas-method.md), fix #2:
when weekly progression dies, **wave the reps down over eight weeks** — volume day 5×5 → 4×5 → 3×5 →
3×4, intensity day 5RM → 4RM → 3RM → five singles, adding **2.5–5 lb upper / 5–10 lb lower** — then
**take 10–15% off and restart**. It also converts volume day to EMOM work at **70–80%** for lifters
with compromised recovery. It cites nothing.

For a written-out RPE block, [the intermediate/advanced
template](general-strength-training-template-intermediate-advanced.md): three days, each opening with
**a single at RPE 8** (which rehearses first-rep mechanics and calibrates the day), then **sets of 5 at
RPE 8**, then a supplemental at ascending **RPE 7/8/9**, then a lighter one at **RPE 6/7/8**, plus 1–2
**time-capped** GPP days. Set *ranges* are deliberate: take the extra sets on a good day, not on a bad
one.

The two "best programme" articles are catalogues.
[Five intermediate powerlifting programmes](intermediate-powerlifting-programs-increase-strength-size.md)
ranks five of their own templates with **no comparative data of any kind**, and its table contradicts
its own title. [Two best Olympic weightlifting
programmes](best-olympic-weightlifting-programs-maximum-performance.md) does the same with two more,
with numbering broken badly enough that no figure in it can be checked.

## Deloads, stalls, and when to change something

**Deloads are the conspicuous gap.** No article argues for a deload schedule, prescribes a structure,
or reviews planned versus reactive unloading. What exists is scattered: the **10–15% reset** in [the
Texas Method article](ways-skin-texas-method.md); a **deload week used to set a baseline** in [the HRV
article](understanding-heart-rate-variability-strength-training.md); "poor inputs mean deload,
adequate inputs mean reformulate" in [beyond progressive
overload](beyond-progressive-overload-work-smarter-not-harder.md). Their implicit position is that
**proper autoregulation already deloads you** — bad days produce lighter loads at the same RPE. That
is coherent and never stated as an argument.

**What counts as a stall.** [Understanding
stalls](training-expectations-understanding-stalls.md): a **trend, not a session** — **at least three
weeks**, with a "regression" stipulated at roughly **>15%** and openly labelled arbitrary.
[Novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md) says **three to four
weeks** measured against **the best performance of the past six to eight weeks**, and cites that
definition **to their own paid Training Plateau Action Plan**. [The strength training
guide](strength-training-guide-get-stronger.md) says four to six weeks. Their threshold for a real
change is **~5%**, their own judgement, matching the **~2–5% between-day noise floor** established
independently in [how fast do you lose muscle](fast-lose-muscle-strength.md).

**The diagnostic fork.** Fresh, unsore, motivated but flat → **under-stimulated**, add work via volume
or frequency. Sore, grinding, losing motivation → **over-reached**, reduce load while keeping the
movement pattern. [Beyond progressive
overload](beyond-progressive-overload-work-smarter-not-harder.md) adds the branch that matters: **if
it was working and stopped, add volume**; **if it never worked, check sleep and nutrition first.**

**The five-question audit** ([understanding
stalls](training-expectations-understanding-stalls.md)): review the block's goal; widen the window to
three or four weeks; compare like with like; account for movement changes; assess life context. Its
companion point is that **changing the standard resets the baseline** — full depth instead of
above-parallel means you changed the test.

## Fatigue, overtraining and monitoring

**Fatigue is at least four things**, from
[novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md): **performance
fatigability** (measured decline), **perceived fatigability** (effort sensation), **trait/state
fatigue**, and **recovery state** on the overreaching continuum. That model predicts both "feel bad,
lift well" and "feel fresh, lift poorly." **"CNS fatigue" does not hold up**: after 10×5 back squats at
80% 1RM, fatigue took up to **72 hours** to resolve but **the aetiology was predominantly peripheral
and cortical output never showed the predicted deficit** (though that experiment is cited to a
narrative review that does not report it).

**Nobody has produced overtraining syndrome in a lab from lifting.** [Are you really
overtrained](overtrained-evidence-shows.md) is the best-argued article here. A systematic review of
**22 overload studies found 10 reported zero performance decline** and no reliable biomarker (Grandou
2020). The failed attempts are the substance: **three athletes squatted a true 1RM daily for 30 days
and all improved** (473→500, 275→303, 484→530 lb); **Raastad 2001's daily submaximal leg work produced
+6% 1RM**; **Dankel 2017's 21 consecutive days** gained ~2 kg in both arms; **Coleman 2024's
supervised 9 weeks at ~90 sets per week** produced adaptation and no OTS. Only **Fry 1994 — 11 men, 10
sets at 1RM daily for 14 days** — dropped 1RM ~12 kg, and recovery took 2–8 weeks, short of the months
OTS requires. Their inference: **intensity, not volume, is the necessary ingredient.**

The categories cannot be told apart in real time — functional overreaching resolves in days to ~2
weeks, OTS in months to years, so **the label is assigned after recovery, not at presentation**. **No
biomarker works**: self-report beat hormones, resting heart rate and HRV across 56 studies (Saw 2016),
and the testosterone-to-cortisol ratio moved **opposite** to prediction (Fry 1998). They also note
**low energy availability co-occurred in 86% of OTS cases**, and that a 2024 review found **nocebo
effects in sport roughly twice the size of placebo effects** — so telling a lifter their watch says
they are overtrained is not neutral. Their most consequential practical claim: **overtraining and
undertraining present identically and demand opposite interventions**, and undertraining is the
commoner error.

**HRV does not track recovery from lifting.** [Understanding
HRV](understanding-heart-rate-variability-strength-training.md) is the most rigorous piece here. The
**two head-to-head resistance-training trials both came back null** — 21 older women over 7 weeks, 22
young men racing to 20 sessions — no difference in strength, hypertrophy, function or non-responder
count **despite the HRV groups training more** (27 of 35 sessions versus 21). The decisive datum is
**Chen 2011: in elite weightlifters, 1RM performance returned to baseline at ~30 hours while HRV took
~60** — waiting for HRV means waiting past readiness. High HRV is not simply better (atrial
fibrillation, on Mendelian randomisation), and **under 5% of the parameters wearables report have been
validated**: the Polar H10 holds against EKG, **Apple Watch and WHOOP underestimate HRV**. Their own
recommendation — a **5–7 day rolling average used only to *add* a session, never to skip one** — is
coaching judgement on a null result, labelled experimental. **What they endorse instead is session
RPE**: a rising sRPE trend across **two or more weeks at constant relative load**.

## Layoffs and coming back

[How fast do you lose muscle and strength](fast-lose-muscle-strength.md) is the best-sourced article
in this batch, and its organising idea is portable: **the reason you stopped predicts the damage
better than the number of days.** Missing sessions, immobilisation, bed rest and critical illness are
four different situations, and nearly every frightening statistic comes from the last two.

The reference number is **Wall 2014: two weeks in a full leg cast cost 8.4% of cross-sectional area
and 22.9% of strength** — strength falling close to three times faster than size, under the most
extreme unloading a healthy person experiences. Skipping the gym is not that stimulus. Short-term
disuse atrophy runs almost entirely through **a fall in muscle protein synthesis**, not a rise in
breakdown.

**Order of decay**: strength stamina, then maximal strength, then power and rate of force development
(largely *preserved* over short layoffs), then size, then tendon and bone. A heavy first session back
is **mostly lost skill, not lost muscle** — and treating it otherwise drives the second mistake,
coming back too fast.

Against a **~2–5%** noise floor: three weeks off in adolescent athletes, and **two weeks off in
powerlifters and D1 footballers**, produced no significant loss; four weeks cost resistance-trained
older men **~17%**; two to three months costs **7–12%**. The five-year illustration: older adults who
trained two years then stopped for three still finished **14% above baseline on leg press and 9% on
bench**, against **+82% and +34%** for continuers. [When time is short](exercise-have-no-time.md)
gives the same picture from different papers, with roughly **10%** lost over two to three months.

**Maintenance and return advice is entirely uncited** — sections one to seven of that article are
dense with citations and the maintenance and return sections carry **none**: one to two sessions a
week to hold gains, re-entry at **1–3 weeks of 2–3 sets at RPE 6–7**. That is exactly where the Time
Crunch template sits. [Novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md)
does have a citation for the maintenance dose: **younger adults hold strength on one session and one
set per exercise per week if relative intensity is preserved**, older adults need about two.

[Returning to the gym](returning-gym.md) is the practical version, and the author states outright that
the recommendations "are not based on direct evidence from the literature." **Undershoot on purpose**,
for a behavioural reason: overshooting produces DOMS, DOMS makes you skip sessions, and **frequency
early matters more than the quality of any one session**. The better half is the follow-up — **if you
are unusually sore, go back on schedule anyway** and moderate within the session. The three-week ramp
caps everything at **RPE 8**: week 1 ascending sets at RPE 5/6/7, week 2 at 6/7/8 with one repeat set,
week 3 with two repeat sets on the main lifts. **The intensity ceiling rises once and then holds**;
everything after is added volume at that ceiling.

## Cardio and concurrent training

[Concurrent training](concurrent-training-interference-effect-cardio-kill-gains.md) is one of their
better articles. The interference effect traces to **Hickson 1980 in untrained men**, and three later
meta-analyses — **Petré 2021, Schumann 2022, Lundberg 2022** — found **no significant compromise to
strength or hypertrophy**. They demote the mTOR/AMPK story explicitly because all three signalling
citations are **acute single-session studies**, refusing to let a mechanism outrank an outcome. Their
reframing: interference is **a total-workload problem, not a pathway problem** — adding 150 minutes of
conditioning to a five-day lifting week is a large block of volume, and the same would be true if you
added it as lifting.

**Explosive strength is the live exception**; they quote Schumann's caveat against themselves
("especially when… performed in the same session"), set a 2025 null trial against it, and leave it
unresolved. **On modality they conclude there is no basis to choose**, citing two reviews pointing
opposite ways — a better account than the received view that running interferes most. Their **ordering
recommendation is conditioning after lifting on shared days**, which is where the library pushes back:
a 2021 meta-analysis found strength gains lowered by same-session but not separate-session cardio, and
that is very likely Petré 2021, which they cite for the null only.

Dosing: **untrained beginners** do both at **60–80% max HR, ≥150 min/week**; **competitors** taper
conditioning over the final **4–6 weeks** (200 → 30 min/week across five weeks, uncited);
**intermediates** ramp to 150 min/week over six weeks (also uncited).

[GPP for lifters](general-physical-preparedness-training-lifters.md) is the fuller article. Its
distinctive call is **LISS before HIIT for anyone with no aerobic base**. Anchors: **RPE 6–7 or ~70%
max HR** is roughly the aerobic ceiling; LISS at **3–5.9 METs**, starting ~20 minutes and adding 5 every
3–4 weeks to 30 before raising intensity; HIIT starting at **5–7 intervals of 20–30 s all-out with
90–100 s complete rest**, ceiling ~15 rounds. The transferable part is their **modality criteria** —
low risk, low skill, sustains 20–30 s of hard cardiorespiratory work, low residual fatigue — landing on
**cycling and rowing**, not running, kettlebells or barbell complexes (complexes hit local failure
before the cardiorespiratory system loads; grip work bleeds into other training). They also give the
provenance of their own rule of thumb: the 220-age formula came from a 1970s review including cardiac
patients.

[HIIT](high-intensity-interval-training.md): above **80% max HR**, SIT above **90%**; **intervals
longer than 2 minutes beat shorter ones** for VO2max; **80% of aerobic work in zones 1–2, 20% high
intensity**; **"all out" is a mistake, not a goal**; floor of **15-minute sessions three times weekly
for six weeks**. **None of those claims is sourced** — the article notes most HIIT research is 6–8
weeks in untrained people, never applies that caveat, and **sells the cited version behind a
membership**. On fat loss they are correct and blunt: **exercise alone has very little direct effect on
weight**, and HIIT and moderate continuous training do not differ.

[Five ways to improve your rowing](ways-improve-rowing.md) is erg-specific and mechanically right:
**the damper is not resistance** — it changes airflow and therefore the **drag factor**. Default
**damper 5**, most people **3–5**, **drag factor ~100–130**, and read the drag rather than the damper.
**Foot strap over the ball of the foot, shins vertical at the catch**; sequence **catch → drive →
finish → recovery** cued "legs, body, arms — arms, body, legs" with the knees bending **last**; target
a **2:1 or 3:1 drive-to-recovery ratio**. It cites nothing, and the author's affiliation is with the
machine's manufacturer, disclosed in the article.

## Warm-ups

No dedicated warm-up article exists here, and what there is is uncited — but consistent, and different
from the rest of the library. **Their position is that the warm-up is a load-selection instrument
first.** [What is RPE](rpe-lifting.md) makes it the mechanism of autoregulation;
[progressive loading](progressive-loading.md) and
[novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md) turn it into the
rule that **lighter and faster than expected means push, heavier means preserve**.

**On mobility they are dismissive**: [the intermediate/advanced
template](general-strength-training-template-intermediate-advanced.md) says that if you can reach the
required positions, **skip the foam rolling and start squatting with the empty bar**, since squatting
prepares you for squatting better than stretching does — offered as opinion, correctly. That is
materially less warm-up than the three-phase protocol elsewhere in this library, and neither side
cites a trial in lifters.

The structure they do give, from [training at home](exercise-home.md): multiple sets of the same
exercise at rising difficulty, **2–3 minutes rest**, working up to the target RPE set, with
overshooting explicitly fine. [When time is short](exercise-have-no-time.md) compresses it: **rest only
as long as it takes to change plates, and cut reps on later warm-up sets**. And the operative rule for
RPE users, from the worked ramp: **warm up *through* RPE 7 and 8**, because that is what makes the top
set findable. [Youth resistance training](recommendations-youth-resistance-training.md) adds the only
population-specific version — a non-specific warm-up aimed at physical literacy rather than the day's
lift.

## Novice to advanced: how the prescription changes

Their answer is that it barely does, and this is the argument they defend hardest.
[Novice, intermediate, advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md) is the strongest
version: a retrospective of **>9,000 powerlifters shows a smooth logarithmic strength curve from year
one to year ten with no inflection at any tier boundary** (Latella 2023), and **day-to-day 1RM
variation of ~3–5%** scales with absolute strength, so a 500 lb squatter's noise band is **±25 lb**
against monthly gains of 10–15 lb. That, they argue, is what "advanced" names — not different biology
but **a shorter detection window**, which is exactly how [the strength training
guide](strength-training-guide-get-stronger.md) redefines the tiers.

**The denominator argument** does much of the work. Ahtiainen 2003 — eight strength athletes and eight
non-athletes on identical 21-week programmes gaining **3.9% versus 20.9%** — looks like a trainability
gap, but 5 lb on a 135 lb squat is 3.7% and on a 405 lb squat is 1.2%. [Strength
standards](strength-standards-bodyweight-age-sex.md) makes the same point and then overreaches,
claiming the adaptive processes **do not change rate at any point in a career** — unsupported by
anything in that article.

What genuinely is training-age dependent is physiological.
[Novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md)'s **tissue clock** is
the most clinically useful section in the corpus: neural change within **four weeks**; muscle in weeks
to months (**thigh CSA up 3–4% at week three, much of it edema**, true growth 7–10% by week ten);
**tendon lagging muscle by 6–8 weeks** (61 studies, 763 participants — high-strain protocols at
~85–90% held ~3 s needed at least **twelve weeks**, with **no stiffness change at all** in the three
well-trained cohorts); **bone slowest**, needing **6–12 months** for DXA-detectable change. And
**"novice recovery" lasts about six weeks** — programmes prescribing categorically different recovery
architecture for novices are treating a six-week phase as permanent. The same clock is why [the
strength training guide](strength-training-guide-get-stronger.md) insists **four adaptations run in
parallel from session one** — neural, hypertrophy, tendon, bone — rather than in phases.

**What actually changes:** more absolute volume, more variety early and less near a test, more
customisation for advanced lifters, a longer detection window and a longer stall window. Load bands,
rep ranges, effort targets and progression logic stay the same.

Two definitions worth keeping. [The intermediate/advanced
template](general-strength-training-template-intermediate-advanced.md): **a novice is defined by the
sustainable rate of load increase, not by how much they lift** — someone who can still add weight
every session — with the corollary that if you have never run a linear progression you are a novice
until proven otherwise. [Strength standards](strength-standards-bodyweight-age-sex.md) reports their
preferred split as simply **binary: untrained (under about a year) and trained**. [Five intermediate
powerlifting programmes](intermediate-powerlifting-programs-increase-strength-size.md) gives the
sharpest critique — the tiers "have thus far used arbitrary criteria that have not been validated in
any way" — and then **adopts an arbitrary definition anyway**, flagging it as arbitrary.

For expectations, [strength standards](strength-standards-bodyweight-age-sex.md) has the best numbers,
correctly framed as **descriptive, not prescriptive**, from **809,986 drug-tested unequipped
competition entries**. **Recreational lifters after one year**: squat **1–1.5× bodyweight**, bench
**0.7–1.1×**, deadlift **1.25–1.75×** — roughly the **10th percentile of competitive powerlifters**
(uncited, and a useful calibration). Career shape: **~7.5–12.5% in the first year of competing, ~20%
cumulative by ten years**, measured from a lifter's first meet, not from untrained. Age bites far less
in trained people: sedentary strength falls **1–3% per year after 40**, while masters powerlifting data
show **women over 59 still gaining ~2.5–5% per year and men over 69 losing ~0.35%**. And the health
threshold sits far below the performance one — in the Aerobics Center Longitudinal Study (**8,762 men,
mean 18.9 years follow-up**) the middle third of strength was ~28% less likely to die than the weakest
and the strongest third ~23%, so **the return is in leaving the bottom third, not reaching the top**.
Hold that article's attributions loosely; its numbering is broken.

## Special cases

**Youth.** [Recommendations for youth resistance
training](recommendations-youth-resistance-training.md) rests its whole dose on one meta-analysis
(Lesinski 2016: **≥23 weeks, 5 sets of 6–8 at 80–89% 1RM, 3–4 minutes rest**) and then says it is **not
executable as written** — the inference being that these youth were so untrained their measured 1RMs
are not comparable to a trained adult's. That is a critique of the construct, not the arithmetic, and
it is rare. The translation is **sets of 6–8 at RPE 7–9**. Also: **duration beats intensity** — an
8-week block does not produce durable gains, so keep training present most of the year, **up to 4
days/week off-season dropping to 1–2 in season to maintain** — and **strength before power**. Two
sloppinesses: the RPE conversion silently changes "5 sets" to "**5 exercises**," and rest is quoted as
3–4 minutes in one place and 2–3 in another.

**Endurance athletes.** [Part III of the endurance
series](strength-training-endurance-athlete-part-iii-programming.md) diagnoses the real problem: their
programmes prescribe sets and reps but not intensity or rest, so nothing progresses — **300 reps across
24 sets with no intensity prescribed** against **85 reps across 14 sets** with intensity on three of
four exercises. The dose is **4–5 exercises, 3–5 sets, 4–10 reps, >70% 1RM, 2–3 minutes rest**, and the
rest requirement forces the shape: at 2–3 minutes you cannot run a circuit inside an hour. The **6 ×
400 m analogy** is the best coaching writing in the corpus — a 50-second 400 m runner doing 6 × 400 at
80% on 3 minutes rest calls that hard; cut the rest to 30 seconds and it becomes a 1.5-mile run. The
squat equivalent is **6 × 4 at RPE 8 with 3 minutes rest**. **Nordic hamstring curls are the one
exercise here with strong injury-prevention evidence**: a meta-analysis of **8,459 athletes** found
they **halve hamstring injury rates**, with a **number needed to treat of 3** for recurrence, over
protocols of at least six weeks, reversing when the programme stops. And the contraindication section
is why this source exists: **fewer than 9 menses in 12 months, a stress-fracture history, or BMI <17.5**
needs medical evaluation before any added stress, and **>5 points on the De Souza stratification means
resistance training is contraindicated until energy balance is restored**. It sells nothing.

**Time-crunched and home.** [When time is short](exercise-have-no-time.md) reframes the accounting —
count volume over a **1–2 month window** — and gives an uncited toolkit: **pair exercises by setup**,
**time caps** (20 minutes on the main lift, 10–15 on a compound supplemental, 10 on isolation),
**E3MOM for 4–6 reps at RPE 6–7 on compounds, E2MOM for 6–10 on isolation**. [Training at
home](exercise-home.md) prescribes **three slots at 8–12, 12–15 and 15–25 reps, each to RPE 8**, over a
four-week block of **3 strength and 3 conditioning days**, progressing **1–5% per week**. Its
transferable idea is the **exercise-selection ladder**: with no weights, manipulate difficulty by load,
unilateral variation, a pause at a disadvantageous position, more range, slower tempo or a plyometric
concentric — bodyweight squat → loaded backpack → split squat → single-leg squat → single-leg squat
with a two-count pause. It is unusually honest that the programme **is not designed to maximise 1RM
strength, hypertrophy or athletic development**.

**Sport crossover.** [Olympic lifting vs powerlifting](olympic-lifting-vs-powerlifting-differences.md)
concedes, from its own key citation (Morris 2022), that **weightlifting training builds no more
strength than ordinary strength training** and that **plyometrics produce roughly equal power, sprint
and change-of-direction gains** — honest, in an article partly written to sell an Olympic template.
[The Olympic programmes article](best-olympic-weightlifting-programs-maximum-performance.md) adds that
weightlifters show **13–36% more lower-body power** on jump tests, that the lifts are programmed with
**fewer reps, lower volume and higher loads** for technical reasons, that Olympic lifting is a **poor
hypertrophy choice**, and — creditably — that the **~67% fast-twitch fibre proportion in elite
weightlifters cannot separate training from self-selection**. It then abandons that reasoning to claim
causally that Olympic lifting raises bone density, on a **cross-sectional comparison of three sports**.
[How to incorporate the snatch and clean](incorporate-snatch-clean-into-program.md) goes further: they
are **poor assistance exercises for the powerlifts**, and the justification is **adherence, not
adaptation**. The mechanism is a **swap, not an addition** — take the upper-back or ab slot on a GPP
day — with **low reps, low RPE, many sets** (power snatch **2 reps @ RPE 6–7 × 6–8 sets**; power clean
worked to 2 @ RPE 6–7 then a **10-minute EMOM of 2**), pauses and complexes used to cap the load.

**Accessory work.** [Arms for GPP](train-arms-gpp.md) is the purest statement of the fatigue-budget
logic: select for direct targeting, **large range with both phases**, and **low fatigue at volume**;
prescribe **3–6 sets of 10–20 at RPE 7–9**, or an AMRAP in which **every set is capped at RPE 6–7** so
acute fatigue does not limit accumulated volume. They are honest about the stakes — "it is unlikely
that your athletic development is being held back by a lack of arm work."
[GPP for lifters](general-physical-preparedness-training-lifters.md) covers the rest: **two or more GPP
sessions weekly with two to four conditioning bouts**, upper back at **3–5 × 12–15 at RPE 7–8**, trunk
work at **1–2 exercises per week rotated every 3–5 weeks**. Its selection call is **lat pulldowns over
weighted chins** for upper-back hypertrophy, on a volume-per-unit-fatigue argument stated as principle,
and direct ab work is defended as *cheap* rather than necessary — a prone bridge gets similar abdominal
activation to a 6RM squat for far less fatigue (both citations acute EMG, and the note says so).

## Where this departs from the rest of the library

- **Effort — the real clash.** **RPE 6–8 on compounds even for hypertrophy, RPE 8–10 on isolation** is
  2–4 RIR on the main lifts: **below the 1–3 RIR band this library adjudicated**, above Menno
  Henselmans' argument that 4–6 RIR can match 0 RIR **when total reps are equated**, and the split is
  used by nobody else here. It is **asserted, not argued** — no citation supports it anywhere. And
  Henselmans only rescues RPE 6–8 if you add sets to equate reps, which they never require. Their own
  [how many chest exercises](many-chest-exercises-per-workout.md) gives a third figure (~4–5 reps from
  failure) and [training at home](exercise-home.md) contradicts itself inside one article.
- **Rest periods.** The 2025 article rests its hypertrophy case on a **six-study 2017 review in mostly
  untrained men** and never engages the 2024 network meta-analysis in which every rest band's credible
  intervals overlapped. Presenting a 2–4 minute floor as firmer than that is the clearest case here of
  a recommendation outrunning the literature, and their **2-minute isolation floor** contradicts
  Nippard's 1–2 minutes with no study on either side.
- **Cardio ordering.** They put conditioning **after lifting in the same session**; the library's other
  sources weight a 2021 meta-analysis showing strength gains lowered by same-session but not
  separate-session cardio, and their own quoted Schumann caveat points the same way. They are
  **better than the library on modality**, though — two reviews pointing opposite ways means nobody
  should assert running interferes most.
- **Overtraining and HRV.** Four direct contradictions with Nippard: the **2–3 week definition**
  (functional overreaching on the consensus definition), **supercompensation itself** (via Kiely 2018),
  the **volume-versus-intensity attribution**, and **whether HRV is worth tracking**. Both cite **Fry
  1994 for opposite conclusions**, and both cite Saw 2016 — which Nippard uses before recommending HRV
  as "the more objective marker."
- **Rep ranges.** **3–20 for compounds, 6–20+ for isolation** — narrower at the top than the library's
  5–35 band, looser at the bottom than House of Hypertrophy's advice to stay above 5 reps. Neither
  difference is argued.
- **Warm-ups.** "Skip the mobility work, start with the empty bar" is materially less than the
  three-phase protocol elsewhere here, and it is opinion rather than argument. What they add that
  nobody else does is treating the warm-up ramp as the load-selection instrument.
- **Regional hypertrophy.** They argue — correctly, and against most of the library — that **incline
  bench is not shown to preferentially build the upper chest**. Then several of their own listicles
  assume the opposite.
- **Set-extension mechanism.** [Myo-reps](myo-reps.md) explains its stimulus through muscle damage and
  metabolic stress, both largely retired in this library's House of Hypertrophy material. The author
  hedges it as theoretical, so this is dated framing rather than error.
- **Optimisation itself.** Where the video sources rank exercises and chase optimal set counts, Barbell
  Medicine's position is that the sweet spot is wide and optimising is neither critical nor achievable
  over a career. That is the most anti-perfectionist message in the library, and on the evidence they
  cite for it, it holds up better than most of the numbers it displaces.
