# A Physician's Starter Guide to Lifting: Clearance, Dose, and What Beginners Actually Need

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Beginner Prescription" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-beginner-prescription-blog/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2019-05-09 · 4,889 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **Most people do not need medical clearance to start.** Following ACSM's 2018 screening logic, clearance is reserved for currently inactive people with known cardiovascular, metabolic or kidney disease, or anyone with active symptoms. Age and body weight alone are not reasons to see a doctor first.
- **The prescription is deliberately loose:** 2+ sessions/week, 2–3 hard sets per exercise, most work at RPE 6–8, 2–4 minutes rest, add 1–5% load *after* performance improves. They argue schedule details matter far less than consistency.
- **Machines are not a downgrade.** They cite a 2023 RCT (Hernández-Belmonte) in which machine training matched free weights for strength (~11% gains) and hypertrophy. This is **hypertrophy and strength outcome data, not EMG activation data** — unusual for this genre and worth noting.
- **Their injury model is load management, not technique.** Injury is framed as exceeding current capacity ("overdraft"), with technique judged by a Repeatable/Efficient/Points-of-performance test rather than against an ideal. The claim is presented as settled; the injury-rate figures behind it are uncited.
- **This article is a funnel.** Four free 5-week templates are offered repeatedly, and the closing section sells the paid 15-week Barbell Medicine Beginner Template, plus HSA/FSA-eligible coaching and physician consultations. The advice is defensible on its own, but the article is structured around the sale.

## The case for lifting: what is actually behind each claim
This is the bulk of the article, and the quality of support varies a lot by claim.

**Blood pressure — strongest.** Naci 2019, a network meta-analysis of **391 randomised trials**, is cited for resistance training lowering resting systolic BP by **7.83 mmHg** in hypertensive people, against **8.69 mmHg** for aerobic training, and **13.5 mmHg** for the two combined. That paper's actual purpose was comparing exercise with antihypertensive drugs, and the honest reading is that exercise is competitive with medication in *hypertensive* populations — not a substitute in general.

**Type 2 diabetes.** The FORTE trial (Yang 2017, RCT) is cited for 16 weeks of resistance training lowering HbA1c, with the article reporting that 72% of exercisers reduced their medication dose versus controls. Presented as if this is a general result; it is one trial.

**Obesity.** Handled unusually well for a training site. Franz 2007 (meta-analysis, ≥1 year follow-up) is cited for exercise alone producing only modest weight loss; Lopez 2022 (Obes Rev meta-analysis) for resistance training shifting loss toward fat rather than lean tissue; Jakicic 2019 for activity predicting *maintenance*; Caruso 2023 (a narrative review) for exercise improving satiety signalling — the weakest of the four, and the appetite claim leans on it harder than a review supports.

**Sarcopenia and bone.** The interesting move is redefining sarcopenia as **dynapenia** — strength loss, not mass loss. Their stated figures: mass falls ~1%/year after 50, strength roughly three times faster, driven by alpha motor neuron loss (Aare 2016, an animal/mechanistic paper on failed reinnervation). "Walking is not enough; you must load the tissue" is a *recommendation* built on that mechanism, not a trial result. The 60–80% 1RM bone-loading range is given without citation.

**Osteoarthritis.** They reject the wear-and-tear model and prescribe graded exposure. Tanaka 2013 (meta-analysis of RCTs) supports exercise for knee OA pain. The "STEP 9 trial" they cite is **Bliddal 2024 — semaglutide in obesity with knee OA**, a drug trial with no resistance training in it. It supports "weight loss reduces OA pain"; it does nothing for the sentence that follows it about resistance training being the gold standard.

**Mental health.** Gordon 2018 (JAMA Psychiatry meta-analysis of RCTs) is cited for resistance training reducing depressive symptoms. The article then says "the same meta-analysis" showed anxiety reduction — Gordon's anxiety meta-analysis is a *separate* paper, and only the depression one is linked. Dementia: Hamer & Chida 2009 for a 45% lower Alzheimer's risk, correctly attributed, but that is prospective observational data being reported next to a BDNF mechanism as though the mechanism were established in humans. Migraine: Woldeamanuel 2022 network meta-analysis, strength training reducing monthly migraine days by **3.55** versus **2.18** for moderate aerobic exercise, with no difference by muscle group trained.

## The prescription itself
Organised as **FITT-VP** (Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type, Volume, Progression).

- **Frequency:** at least 2 sessions/week; they explicitly say weekly total matters more than distribution, and that both compressed and spread schedules work. Stated as a research finding without a citation.
- **Intensity:** RPE 6–8 for most work, with RPE framed as reps-in-reserve. They acknowledge the objection that novices rate effort poorly (Glass & Stanton 2004, self-selected intensity in novices) and answer it with Zourdos 2016 and Helms 2016 — RPE as a *learnable skill* that improves with instruction. Note that Helms 2016 is a review/application paper, not evidence of novice accuracy improving.
- **Time:** 30–60 minute sessions; 2–5 minutes rest on compounds, 2–4 on isolation, reasoning that shorter rest forces load or volume reductions. No citation.
- **Type:** movement patterns (squat, hinge, push, pull) over specific lifts, plus 150–300 min/week moderate conditioning (Piercy 2018, the US Physical Activity Guidelines), and Saint-Maurice 2018 for short bouts counting as much as continuous sessions.
- **Volume:** 2–3 challenging sets per exercise, with a "Goldilocks zone" framing. **The two links behind "more exercise leads to greater improvements" are Schoenfeld 2017 and Carvalho 2022 — both are low- vs high-*load* meta-analyses, not volume dose-response studies.** They cannot support the volume claim they are attached to; this is the clearest citation-to-claim mismatch in the article.
- **Progression:** their most distinctive point. Adaptation precedes load increase, not the reverse — add weight when warm-ups feel lighter, and hold or reduce load on bad days. Framed as autoregulation rather than linear progression, and offered as clinical reasoning, not as a tested protocol.

## Where the argument outruns the evidence
The injury hierarchy (bodybuilding ~0.2–1, powerlifting ~1–4, running 10+, contact sports 15–80+ injuries per 1,000 hours) carries no citation, and the conclusion drawn from it — that technique is largely irrelevant and load management is the whole story — is stronger than "technique predicts injury poorly." Similarly, "elite lifters show rep-to-rep variability, therefore variability is a strategy rather than an error" is an inference, not a finding. The pain-science position here is the house view of a clinician group; it is well-supported in direction and overstated in degree.

## What they sell
Four free 5-week templates (Express, Foundational, Traditional, Muscle Preservation for 60+) are linked at least six times; the article closes by upselling the paid 15-week Beginner Template as the "gold standard," and separately promotes HSA/FSA-eligible coaching, paid online physician consultations, a calorie calculator, a macro calculator, the podcast and the newsletter.
