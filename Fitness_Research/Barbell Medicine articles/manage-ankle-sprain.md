# Ankle Sprains: Rule Out Fracture, Then Keep Loading

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How-To Manage an Ankle Sprain" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-to-manage-an-ankle-sprain/)) · Derek Miles · 2022-10-27 · 2816 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- The only early decision that matters is **fracture or no fracture**. The Ottawa Ankle Rules screen for bony injury; they are not a severity grade, and mild/moderate/severe grading changes nothing you do.
- Lay distillation: **if you cannot bear weight, get evaluated.** If you can, and are not tender over the named bony landmarks, conservative care plus continued training is likely appropriate.
- **Rest and immobilisation give worse outcomes than continued activity with functional support.** Ice and compression are symptom relief only — no good evidence they speed healing or return to sport.
- Kinesiotaping, cupping, scraping, dry needling and laser have no good evidence here, and the mechanisms claimed for them are not what is happening.
- Rehab runs in three phases: restore weight-bearing → reload the bar through tolerable range of motion → reintroduce speed, landing and change of direction. The article recommends Barbell Medicine's own paid pain-and-rehab consultation and programming service.

## The injury and the fracture screen
A sprain is a ligament stretched past capacity, from micro-tearing to full rupture. Return timing is set by what the person needs to do — a few weeks for recreational activity, months for peak performance — not by a grade. **Lateral / inversion sprains are 85–90% of the total**: ATFL, CFL and/or PTFL, the ATFL most often, described as the most common skeletal injury in physically active populations. Pain typically drops off quickly over the first two weeks. **Medial / eversion** sprains hit the deltoid ligament; isolated deltoid injury is uncommon and warrants evaluation for fracture. **High / syndesmotic** sprains (extreme dorsiflexion plus external rotation; football, rugby, skiing) take longest and may require a non-weight-bearing period.

The Ottawa Ankle Rules exist to catch fractures that would change management. Criteria as given: four steps **both immediately after injury and now**; bony tenderness along the distal 6 cm of the posterior fibula; the same on the posterior tibia / medial malleolar tip; tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal; tenderness at the navicular. The author is candid that acutely everything hurts and palpating correctly needs anatomy, so the rules are best applied by a clinician. He calls the system validated with predictive value but gives no sensitivity, specificity or study data.

## What the evidence supports, and where the advice runs past it
Evidence-backed here: rest/immobilisation losing to functional treatment; ice and compression unsupported beyond symptoms; NSAIDs reducing pain without increased harms versus placebo **short-term (<14 days)**, against a *theoretical* concern that suppressing inflammation delays healing; kinesiotaping failing to improve ankle function or performance; semi-rigid or lace-up bracing preferable to elastic bandaging.

The bracing passage is the piece's most rigorous moment: the author volunteers that most bracing trials compare bracing to **immobilisation**, so what they show is that bracing beats rest, probably because the braced person keeps moving. He then states flatly that bracing "is not necessary to return to sport or to reduce the risk of further injury" — stronger than a comparison-limited evidence base carries. Same overreach in "there is no evidence that any modality speeds up the healing process": absence of evidence is stated as a proven negative.

Recommendation rather than evidence: the phase progression, the RPE prescriptions, the exercise selections, and the balance argument — explicit specificity reasoning (to be hard to knock over on one leg, train on one leg), flagged by the author as opinion. Nothing is cited for balance training reducing recurrence, a real gap given how much that has been studied, and no sample sizes, durations or effect sizes appear anywhere in the article.

## The progression
**Phase I — walking, stairs, weight-bearing.** Crutches if needed, weight-bear as tolerated, wean once the limp is gone. Single-leg stance, curtsy lunge, single-leg cone touches, calf raises, step-ups/downs. Squats and deadlifts are out; machines and upper-body work maintain training.

**Phase II — slow athletic movement and balance.** Back to the bar via pin squats and block pulls, which give two axes of progression: range of motion and load. Symptoms govern, not load. Dedicated calf work bodyweight → deficit → machine, plus box step-ups and rear-foot-elevated split squats loading the injured side.

**Phase III — speed and change of direction.** Jumping and sprinting return with the emphasis on **landing and deceleration**. Max effort is RPE 9/10, so volume stays low with near-full recovery — 3–10 max-effort jump reps, or 10–20 second AMRAP sets — plus reactive cutting drills.
