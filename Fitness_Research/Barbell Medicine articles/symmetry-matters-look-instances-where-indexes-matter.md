# Asymmetry Rarely Matters, But After Some Injuries It Does — And Almost Nobody Measures It Properly

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "When Symmetry Matters: A Look At Instances in Where Indexes Matter After Injury" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/when-symmetry-matters-a-look-at-instances-in-where-indexes-matter-after-injury/)) · Derek Miles · 2020-11-13 · 6,158 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- Two claims, and they cut in opposite directions. For untrained and generally healthy people, hunting for asymmetries is a waste of attention — meeting physical activity guidelines matters far more. **After specific injuries — hamstring strain, ACL reconstruction — side-to-side strength deficits do predict reinjury**, and there the asymmetry is worth measuring and worth training.
- The measurement most clinicians actually use cannot detect the deficits that matter. Manual muscle testing tops out at "resists strong pressure", which is bounded by *the clinician's* strength. Surveys found **56% of physical therapists and 60% of sports-medicine physicians used MMT alone** to clear athletes after ACL reconstruction ([Greenberg 2018](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2018.8264), [Greenberg 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967119839041)).
- The risk numbers come from prospective observational cohorts, not trials. Hamstring limb symmetry index below 85% was associated with **4× injury risk** in 100 professional footballers ([Fousekis 2011](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.2010.077560)) and **2.4×** in rugby union, rising to **3.4× below 80%** ([Bourne 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546515599633)). These are risk markers; no cited study shows that *raising* LSI lowers reinjury.
- LSI is a ratio, so it lies when the uninvolved limb also detrains ([Wellsandt 2017](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2017.7285)). Miles argues for pairing it with peak torque to bodyweight — a genuinely useful correction, and one he demonstrates in his own case data.
- **The practical recommendation is his, not the literature's.** His gym-floor substitute for a dynamometer — 3×6 at a prescribed RPE on a knee extension or hamstring curl machine, computed as a ratio — he states plainly has no peer-reviewed support. Under the brief's distinction: evidence for "LSI predicts reinjury", clinical judgement for "here is how to measure it without a Biodex".

## The measurement problem, which is most of the article

Miles is a physical therapist, and the piece is structured as a scoping review of when limb symmetry indexes earn their keep. He opens by conceding the general case away: asymmetry is normal and often *sport-produced*. Youth baseball pitchers show side-to-side differences in shoulder and hip range of motion ([Oliver 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000000796)); a sweep rower who has rowed starboard for a decade should not be symmetrical. For the general population, "getting people to move confers far greater benefit than whether their quadriceps index is greater than 90%."

He then adopts Bohannon's definition of strength — the maximum voluntary output muscles can produce *under a specified set of test conditions* ([Bohannon 1987](https://doi.org/10.1177/026921558700100103)) — and Bohannon's four criteria: a strength measure must be sensitive enough to separate sub-normal from normal, precise enough to document change in both directions, reliable, and predictive of something that matters. Manual muscle testing fails at least the first two.

The MMT scale (Kendall 1993) runs 0–5. Three of five is "moves through range against gravity"; above that the grades are "slight", "moderate" and "strong" pressure. Miles's objection is structural rather than rhetorical: the ceiling of the scale is *the tester's* force output, so grading presupposes the clinician is stronger than the athlete. His worked example: an athlete kicking 150 lb on one leg and 120 lb on the other has an 80% LSI, and a clinician would have to reliably perceive a 30 lb difference at forces above their own capacity to catch it. At 200 lb versus 140 lb — a 70% index — it is hopeless. Both athletes grade 5/5.

He is caustic about the downstream narratives this produces. "Telling an athlete who squats 2.5 times their bodyweight to full depth that their glutes are 'weak' is asinine." He also argues that "weak glutes" and "glutes not firing" as folk diagnoses are artefacts of test mechanics — a hip abduction break test is easy to overcome, a knee extension break test is not, so hips get labelled weak. And he rejects two adjacent constructs outright: the hamstring-to-quadriceps ratio is "an antiquated, unsubstantiated metric" (asserted, with no citation attached), and single-muscle tests for gluteus medius or vastus medialis are not valid because those muscles cannot be isolated by a test.

The stated alternative is dynamometry — isokinetic (Biodex-type, resisting at a fixed angular velocity, typically tested at 60/180/300°·s⁻¹) or handheld isometric, with the caveat that handheld devices reinherit the clinician-strength problem unless anchored to something ([Osternig 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3525192/)).

The survey data are the sharpest material here. Greenberg's two studies asked clinicians what they use to clear athletes after ACL reconstruction. Among physical therapists in the orthopaedic and sports sections, 80% named strength as a criterion — but 56% of those used MMT alone. Among physicians in the PRiSM society, 100% named strength: 60% MMT, 28% isokinetic, 12% isometric. So the majority of clinicians who bother to test strength before return-to-sport clearance are using an instrument that cannot resolve the deficits the literature says are dangerous.

## Where the asymmetry actually predicts something

Miles restricts the "it matters" case to post-injury and post-surgical populations, and gives the base rates: reinjury runs 10.1% for hip flexor and quadriceps strains ([Eckard 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546517716179)), up to 34% for hamstrings ([Schmitt 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22666648/)), and 23% after ACL reconstruction in younger athletes ([Wiggins 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546515621554), systematic review and meta-analysis).

For hamstrings, [Sanfilippo 2013](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3182776eff) used MRI plus isokinetic testing and found that at return to sport the injured limb had a **20.4% smaller cross-sectional area** but only a **9.6% peak torque deficit**, which had resolved by six months. Miles draws the right inference: strength and cross-sectional area are not 1:1, and a deficit persists at the moment athletes are typically cleared. The risk estimates come from the two prospective cohorts already noted — Fousekis in 100 professional footballers (isokinetic, <85% LSI → 4× hamstring strain risk) and Bourne in rugby union (custom Nordic curl rig with a load cell, <85% → 2.4×, <80% → 3.4×).

For ACL reconstruction, the 90% LSI benchmark is conventional. [Grindem 2016](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096031) (Delaware-Oslo cohort) found athletes failing a return-to-sport test battery were roughly 4× more likely to reinjure, with strength assessed isokinetically. [Rambaud 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-098602), a scoping review, reports a 70% LSI threshold before return to running; [Myer 2008](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e31816a86cd) proposes peak torque to bodyweight of 1.6 (men) and 1.5 (women) before sport-specific work. And [Lepley 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/1941738115578112) — cited in the text as "Kepley", a typo — found average side-to-side quadriceps deficits of **23% at six months** after ACLR.

Two counterweights he raises to his own case are the best part of the article. First, Goodhart's law: hop distance symmetry, a standard clearance test, shows only weak association with normal landing biomechanics ([Wren 2018](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2018.7817)), and any skill-based test can be trained toward without the underlying capacity changing. Second, LSI is a ratio and inflates when the uninvolved limb also weakens — the specific finding of [Wellsandt 2017](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2017.7285), which is why he insists on carrying PT/BW alongside.

## The case study, and what it is worth

A 220 lb 29-year-old man herniated an L3 disc and presented with right-leg radicular symptoms — with, unusually, isokinetic data from four months *before* the injury. Right knee extensor peak torque fell from 205.4 to 68 ft·lb. Pre-injury LSI was 87.4%; three weeks post-injury, 28.6%. At four months he was at 140 ft·lb, LSI 54%, PT/BW up from 31% to 64%. At eighteen months, LSI 83.3% and PT/BW 82.3% — still asymmetrical, and Miles's answer to "does that matter" is "possibly, for a sprinter or competitive powerlifter; absolutely not for the average trainee".

Management was continued training with constraints, not rest: five days a week throughout, squats and deadlifts at sharply reduced absolute load with RPE caps, plus isolated quadriceps work on a knee extension machine three times a week programmed *to failure* — his rationale being that at that level of capacity it took little load or volume to fatigue the muscle. He notes he would have used 1–2 reps in reserve instead after a muscle injury or ACLR. He frames the whole progression through [Dye 1996](https://doi.org/10.1097/00003086-199604000-00003) — the envelope of function — and cites [Chiu 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269215514540919), a systematic review of spontaneous lumbar disc regression, for the point that herniations resolve without waiting.

Worth noting for the library: this is a rehabilitation context recommending **machine isolation work and sets to failure**, which sits against the general-training material elsewhere here that treats failure as unnecessary for hypertrophy. Miles's justification is specificity and load tolerance, not a growth argument, so the two are not really in conflict — but the recommendation reads differently out of context.

**Where the argument outruns the evidence.** The cited studies establish that low LSI *predicts* reinjury in observational cohorts; none tests whether correcting LSI reduces it, yet the article's practical advice treats the threshold as a causal target — the same Goodhart problem he identifies in hop testing. The 85% figure comes from hamstring cohorts in soccer and rugby and is then described as the "normal recommendation for return to sport after the injuries listed above", which is broader than the source data. Grindem's headline "84% reinjury reduction" is a cohort-derived association with a decision rule, not a trial of that rule. And the gym proxy — 3×6 at a set RPE, converted to an index — is offered honestly as unvalidated ("this metric does not have peer-reviewed literature, but I would be comfortable arguing it is a better standard than a manual muscle test"), which is exactly the right framing but should not be read as evidence.

**Commercial note:** the article makes "the case promoted at Barbell Medicine that a general strengthening program should serve as a foundation" — Barbell Medicine sells training templates and rehabilitation programming. The claim is defensible on its own terms and there is no product pitch in the text, but the conclusion coincides with what they sell.
