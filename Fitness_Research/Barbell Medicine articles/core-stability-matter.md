# "Core Stability" Has No Agreed Definition, and the Real Objection Is the Language

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Core Stability: Does it Matter?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/core-stability-does-it-matter/)) · Charlie Dickson · 2021-02-19 · 974 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **There is no consensus definition of "core stability."** It varies by author and by which muscles are being studied, which makes it hard to measure and therefore hard to link to injury risk or performance.
- The conventional split is **global** (rectus abdominis, obliquus externus, thoracic lumbar iliocostalis — affect trunk orientation without attaching directly to the spine) versus **local** (multifidus, transversus abdominis, obliquus internus — attached directly to the spine).
- The practice built on that split — **"selectively activating" the local system without the global one**, using unloaded bodyweight work to avoid "overactivation" and compensatory movement patterns — has, per Wirth 2017, no scientific support.
- **They are not against the exercises; they are against the pitch.** Lumbar stabilisation work is called "an excellent way to introduce movement", useful for short-term symptom reduction and confidence in fearful patients. The objection is telling someone their core or spine is weak.
- Barbell Medicine still recommend trunk training as part of general resistance training. **That is a preference, not a finding** — they present no evidence that it does anything specific, and by their own argument it does not need to.

## The argument, and where it strains
The honest move in this short article is that Dickson presents the trials that go against him. **Inani 2013** and **Moon 2013** are randomised controlled trials favouring lumbar stabilisation over "traditional strengthening" for pain and function in non-specific low back pain. His rebuttal is methodological rather than dismissive: what these authors call traditional strengthening is very low-level movement. That makes them a weak-comparator problem — evidence that stabilisation beats a token control, not that it is special. Both trials are also small.

The consequences of the language are argued from qualitative and observational work. Darlow 2013 (*Ann Fam Med*) interviewed people about what clinicians told them; the article quotes a woman repeatedly tested and told she was "ridiculously weak", who terminated a pregnancy in part because she did not believe her body could carry it. Dickson labels this extreme himself, which is the right handling — it is one interview quote, not a rate. Bunzli 2017 supplies the mechanism: pain-related fear reduces activity. For athletes the cost is more mundane — time spent on fifteen stabilisation drills with no demonstrated effect on injury risk or performance.

Two closing numbers. **Owen 2020** (network meta-analysis, *BJSM*) is cited for the claim that low back pain improves over time regardless of treatment. **Flagged:** that is a lossy read — a network meta-analysis exists precisely to rank modes against each other, and Owen's did find differences between exercise modes. **Steffens 2016** (*JAMA Intern Med*) is cited for exercise plus education reducing future low back pain by **45%**; there the effect is carried by exercise, so the bundled phrasing flatters the education half.

**Flagged — the strongest sentence is the weakest supported.** "There is absolutely no scientific evidence to support those types of claims" rests on a single narrative critical analysis (Wirth 2017), which is also the sole source for the parallel claim in their sciatica article. The defensible version is that the selective-activation rationale is unvalidated and poorly defined, which is not the same as proven to be nothing. Absence of a definition is a measurement problem, not a disproof.

**They sell things:** the article closes by linking Barbell Medicine's low back rehab programmes and paid Pain & Rehab consultations.
