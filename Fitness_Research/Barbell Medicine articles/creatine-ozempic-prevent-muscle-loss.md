# Creatine on a GLP-1: A Small Multiplier on Training, Not a Muscle-Preserving Drug

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Creatine on Ozempic: Does It Prevent Muscle Loss?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/creatine-on-ozempic-does-it-prevent-muscle-loss/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum, medically reviewed by Austin Baraki · 2026-04-29 · 2,573 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **No trial has tested creatine during GLP-1 therapy.** Anyone claiming creatine preserves muscle specifically on semaglutide or tirzepatide is ahead of the evidence, and the article says so in exactly those terms.
- The effect is **mediated entirely by training**. Creatine plus resistance training beats training alone by roughly **1–2 kg of lean mass and 5–10% strength over 8–12 weeks**; in older adults (closest to the average GLP-1 patient) the meta-analytic figure is about **1 kg over 10–12 weeks**. Creatine without training does close to nothing for body composition.
- They take apart the marketing syllogism link by link: the muscle loss is **overstated** (DXA "lean mass" includes water, glycogen, organs and connective tissue, and the SURPASS-3 MRI substudy showed a substantial fraction of tirzepatide "lean mass" loss was **intramuscular fat**); creatine is **not standalone**; and nothing about the mechanism is **GLP-1-specific**.
- Dose: **3–5 g/day (0.03–0.05 g/kg), any time; skip the loading phase.** Monohydrate only — HCl, ethyl ester and buffered forms cost more with no reliable advantage. A 1–2 kg scale increase in the first weeks is intramuscular water, not fat.
- The named target for gendered creatine gummies and drink mixes is sharp and, unusually for a supplement-selling company, argues *against* a product category: a "pink tax" on inferior forms at low doses.
- **Commercial disclosure:** Barbell Medicine sell both a whey isolate and a creatine product. This article does **not** push their creatine — it recommends any plain third-party-tested monohydrate — but it does link their **Whey Rx** whey isolate in the protein paragraph, plus their coaching and beginner template.

## What creatine does, and why the training caveat is load-bearing
The mechanism section is conventional and correctly hedged. Supplementation raises total muscle creatine by roughly 20%, enlarging the phosphocreatine pool that regenerates ATP in the first seconds of a hard set. Two secondary mechanisms are offered: cell swelling from intracellular water acting as an anabolic signal, and increased satellite cell activation and myonuclear addition **when combined with resistance training**.

That conditional is the whole argument. The per-set energy advantage buys slightly more work; the cellular responses amplify adaptations *to that work*. Remove the training and there is no work to amplify — hence the article's repeated line that without a training stimulus, no trial has shown creatine meaningfully protects lean mass in someone losing weight. They call the effect sizes "real, but not life-changing", and flag that the deficit literature specifically is **thinner than the marketing suggests**, since most creatine trials run in weight-stable or weight-gaining trained populations rather than in people actively dieting.

## The three broken links in the GLP-1 creatine pitch
**Link one — "people on Ozempic lose muscle."** Partly true, badly measured. DXA lean mass is a compartment, not a tissue. The article leans on the SURPASS-3 MRI substudy, which it names in the sentence itself, to argue that much of the apparent lean loss on tirzepatide was intramuscular fat. This is the most substantive evidence claim in the piece and it is a **post-hoc analysis of an open-label phase 3 trial**, not a purpose-built study.

**Link two — "creatine prevents muscle loss."** Not on its own. The preservation effect in the literature is mediated by lifting.

**Link three — "therefore GLP-1 users specifically need it."** No. If creatine helps during GLP-1-assisted weight loss it helps for the same reason it helps during diet-only weight loss or post-bariatric recovery. The drug changes nothing about how creatine works.

This is a clean piece of reasoning and it is the article's main value. It is also, notably, an argument that reduces the case for a supplement they sell.

## Cognition, and the appropriate uncertainty
In rested, well-nourished adults, most trials find no benefit or a small task-specific one. The effect is more consistent under physiological stress — a 2024 crossover trial using a single large dose reported improved reaction time and working memory after sleep restriction. Their verdict: a possible secondary upside if you already take it, **not** a reason to start, and "TBD pending larger trials." That is the right confidence level for one crossover study.

## Where it sits against the library, and what to watch
The **priority order for protecting body composition on a GLP-1** is: resistance training, then protein, then sleep, then creatine — "a nice-to-have, not a must-have."

The protein figure in that list is **"around 1.6 g/kg of target body weight"**. Two things to flag. First, *target* body weight is a different and smaller denominator than current body weight for someone with obesity, so the absolute gram target is lower than it looks. Second, their companion article on protein during GLP-1 use gives **1.4–1.6 g/kg** — the lowest figure anywhere in this library, below the library's adjudicated **1.6–1.8 g/kg** and below what the ISSN and Henselmans propose. The two Barbell Medicine articles are not using the same denominator or the same number, which is worth knowing before treating either as their settled position.

Minor overreach: creatine is described as "one of the safest supplements studied at scale" and the FAQ answers "is creatine safe with GLP-1 medications?" with a flat **yes**. There is no interaction study; the claim is an absence of any known mechanism plus creatine's general safety record. Reasonable clinical inference, but it is inference.
