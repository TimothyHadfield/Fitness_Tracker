# Statins and Muscle: Real Mechanisms, Small Real Incidence, and a Reference List With Errors

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Statins and Muscle: Do They Affect Strength or Growth?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/statins-muscle-strength/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2026-04-07 · 4,951 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **Statins do not appear to blunt strength gains in people who tolerate them.** The anchor is [STOMP](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23183941/) [9]: 420 statin-naive adults randomised to atorvastatin 80 mg or placebo for six months, no meaningful strength difference, CK about 21 U/L higher on statin.
- **True drug-caused muscle symptoms are 1–5% above placebo in controlled trials** [27], against 10–20% reported in clinic. The gap is expectation, and [SAMSON](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34531021/) [8] is the cleanest demonstration: 60 patients who had previously quit statins, symptoms tracked daily across statin, placebo and no-tablet periods, **90% of the symptom burden occurred equally in all three**; half then restarted successfully.
- **Three mechanisms are real and are amplified by exercise**: CoQ10 depletion [22,23], isoprenoid depletion weakening membrane resilience against eccentric load [25], and ryanodine-receptor calcium leak [26]. The article's argument is that these run at low level in nearly everyone and only cross into symptoms when several risk factors stack.
- **The 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidaemia guideline names vigorous exercise as a SAMS risk factor for the first time** [1] — the single most useful new fact in the article for a lifter.
- **Practical test they recommend:** CK while symptomatic, 5–7 days off training, repeat. Normalises on rest → training. Stays up → statin may be contributing. This is their clinical heuristic, not a validated protocol, and they present it as advice.
- **CoQ10 supplementation is not supported** — meta-analyses do not show it reduces SAMS, and the 2026 guideline does not recommend it [1]. They then add that trying it does "little harm," which is preference, not evidence.

## The mechanisms, and why exercise matters

Statins block HMG-CoA reductase, which makes cholesterol but also CoQ10 and isoprenoids [3]. Three consequences follow, each cited to a specific paper:

**CoQ10 depletion** — plasma CoQ10 falls 16–54% across studies; the largest randomised trial reported 38% with atorvastatin and 27% with lovastatin [22]. They are careful that **intramuscular** data is less consistent than circulating data [23], which is the right caveat: the mechanism is established in blood, inferred in muscle.

**Isoprenoid depletion** — the structural molecules that keep the sarcolemma resilient. The cited work [25] found that restoring isoprenoids reverses statin-induced muscle cell death while CoQ10 alone does not, which is an elegant argument against the popular supplement fix. Their clinical inference — that this is why statin myopathy shows up when someone *starts* a new programme, because novel training is eccentric-heavy — is reasoning, not a cited finding.

**Ryanodine-receptor calcium leak** [5,26] — statin binding destabilises the calcium gate, calcium leaks, proteolytic enzymes and oxidative stress follow, and exercise-generated reactive species compound it.

Their honest summary of who gets in trouble: dose, drug interaction, an SLCO1B1 variant [14], age over 65, hypothyroidism, kidney disease and exercise, stacked [3]. The illustrative case [2] is a 43-year-old on fenofibrate plus atorvastatin who started a home dumbbell programme and reached a **CK of 18,979 U/L**, was hospitalised for two days with normal kidney function, and later resumed atorvastatin monotherapy and training without recurrence. A single case report, used correctly — as an illustration of a mechanism, not as an incidence estimate.

## Where the article overstates its own evidence

**STOMP is described as more than it was.** The article says the 420 participants were "put through a standardized six-month resistance training program," and the key-takeaway box claims "no difference in strength gains **or muscle growth (hypertrophy)**." STOMP as published (Parker 2013, *Circulation*) tested atorvastatin 80 mg versus placebo in statin-naive adults and measured muscle strength, endurance and exercise performance — it was a drug trial with muscle outcomes, not a supervised hypertrophy trial, and **it did not measure muscle size**. The strength conclusion stands; the hypertrophy conclusion is not supported by the trial cited for it, and the article's own title asks about growth. Worth verifying against the paper before this note's claim is reused.

**Reference [24] does not support the sentence it sits under.** It is cited for the claim that CoQ10 depletion affects fast-twitch fibres most. Allard 2021 is a trial showing moderate-intensity exercise training **improves** muscle performance in symptomatic and asymptomatic statin users — a finding that, if anything, cuts against the article's caution about training on statins.

**SAMSON is misattributed.** The text calls it "published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2020," but reference [8] is Howard et al., *JACC* 2021;78:1210-1222. The design is also compressed: SAMSON randomised the order of twelve one-month periods (four statin, four placebo, four no-tablet), not three four-month blocks. The 90% nocebo figure and the restart rate are reported correctly.

**"Neither answer is complete, and neither is wrong"** — the article's framing of the doctor-versus-forum disagreement — is rhetorical balance. The article then spends its length showing that the forum answer is substantially wrong.

## The endurance-athlete detour

A long section on coronary artery calcium in high-volume endurance athletes. Marathon runners show CAC above 100 in ~36% of cases versus ~22% in the general population [17]; the effect scales with training volume in men and not in women [18]; and the most active people still have the lowest all-cause mortality [19]. Their explanation is plaque composition — athletes accumulate harder, more calcified plaque and fewer mixed plaques [20] — with the honest counterweight that Masters@Heart found *more* mixed plaque in lifelong athletes [21], and that favourable composition seems to apply to athletes who started with clean arteries. Atrial fibrillation risk rises above roughly 2,000 lifetime hours of vigorous endurance training [28]. They state plainly this is irrelevant to the average lifter. This section is well-hedged and is the most balanced part of the article.

## Reference-list quality — a caution

The marker-to-reference mapping passed its offset check, so the claim-level attributions above are reliable. **The reference list itself is not.** At least seven entries carry a PMID, DOI or author that points at a different paper than the one described:

- [10] links to PMID 9346087, which is an injury-prevention editorial, not Thompson's lovastatin study.
- [12] links to PMID 28265005, a paper on antibiotic resistance.
- [13] gives a DOI that resolves to a coronary-stenting outcomes paper.
- [27] links to PMID 27199463, an antimicrobial-stewardship editorial, not Thompson's 2016 JACC side-effects review.
- [23] is printed as "Sarter B, PPM 2002" but links to Deichmann, Lavie & Andrews, *Ochsner J* 2010.
- [25] is printed as "Hanai JI 2007" but links to Cao et al., *FASEB J* 2009.
- [26] is attributed in both the body text and the list to "Lahiri S"; the paper at that DOI is Lotteau et al.

None of this changes the article's conclusions, but anyone chasing a citation from this piece should follow the DOI and check what arrives.
