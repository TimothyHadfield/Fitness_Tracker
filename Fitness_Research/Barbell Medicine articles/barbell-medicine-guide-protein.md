# Barbell Medicine's Protein Guide: A Wide Range Whose Top End Rests on One Review

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Barbell Medicine Guide to Protein" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/barbell-medicine-protein-recommendations/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2021-05-28 · 3,348 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **The headline recommendation is 1.3–3.1 g/kg/day**, split three ways: 1.3–2.2 g/kg is "more than adequate" for most people gaining or maintaining; 2.3–3.1 g/kg for those who are lean *and* actively dieting, training hard 5+ days a week, or anabolically resistant. The opening Key Points state the range as 1.6–3.1.
- **The bottom of that range is well founded and the top is not.** Morton 2018 — 49 studies, 1,863 subjects — is cited correctly for a **1.6 g/kg breakpoint** above which resistance-training outcomes stop improving. That is the library's number. The 2.3–3.1 g/kg figure comes from a single source, **Helms 2014**, a systematic review, seconded by the ISSN's 2018 review.
- **Against the library's position:** the library holds **1.6–1.8 g/kg**, with everything above 2.0 classified as insurance rather than effect, and specifically flags that *nobody has run 1.6 against 2.4 in a deficit* — the cutting trials always compared adequate against inadequate. This article's 2.3–3.1 g/kg recommendation is exactly the claim that flag was aimed at. It is a defensible asymmetric-risk judgement; it is not backed by a head-to-head trial, and the article does not say so.
- **On the per-meal ceiling they are ahead of their era and still short of the library.** They give **0.25–0.6 g/kg or ~20–45 g per meal** — an upper bound well above the old 20–30 g "ceiling," so they had already abandoned the absorption myth in 2021. But the library's position is that there is **no ceiling at all**, and stating a per-meal band still implies one. Their meal-spacing advice (every 3–5 hours) is immediately downgraded: "less important than the total daily intake."
- **Protein source does not matter at adequate intakes**, and the trial evidence here is the strongest part of the article.
- **Barbell Medicine sell protein powder.** Their WheyRx isolate is named and linked mid-article, in a paragraph about protein content by weight, with the note that it is ~90% protein. Recommendation 8 — that supplements be CGMP-compliant and third-party tested by NSF, Informed Choice or USP — describes their own product's positioning. The advice is sound; the reader should know who is giving it.

## How this article cites

The article carries a **28-item numbered reference list at the bottom that is never called from the text** — no markers, no numbering in the body. Claim-level attribution instead comes from inline hyperlinks with the author and year written into the sentence ("Morton 2018 found…"), 26 of them. So numbered attribution is not available and I have not used it; where I name a paper below, the article named it in that sentence. The textbook source (Gropper & Smith, *Advanced Nutrition and Human Metabolism*) is cited by bare name with no link at all.

One citation error worth recording: the link on the **Groen 2015** sentence points to PMID 21525248, which is the **Leenders 2011** leucine paper cited two sentences later, not Groen. The Groen paper is real and is in the bottom list; the hyperlink is simply wrong.

## What the evidence actually supports

**The 1.6 g/kg number.** Morton 2018 is a systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of protein supplementation across 49 studies and 1,863 healthy adults, and it found intakes around 1.6 g/kg optimised hypertrophy and 1RM gains. This is the same paper the rest of the library converges on, and it is co-authored by Henselmans and Helms — two names that recur elsewhere in this collection. Two independent lines agree: a 2019 review recommends **1.4 g/kg** for endurance athletes (Vitale 2019), and the **2019 IAAF consensus statement** recommends **1.3–1.7 g/kg** for athletes maintaining or gaining weight (Burke 2019). Three sources, three overlapping bands, all landing between 1.3 and 1.7.

**Source does not matter when the dose is adequate.** Four trials, and the article reports their protein intakes, which is the detail that makes the argument work:
- **Banaszek 2019** — whey vs pea, 8 weeks, no difference in strength, power, performance, lean mass or body composition. Intakes were **1.8 and 1.7 g/kg** — the article correctly notes the dose was high enough to swamp any bioavailability difference.
- **Joy 2013** — 24 college-aged men, 8 weeks, **48 g/day** of whey or rice protein. No differences in bench or leg press 1RM, Wingate peak power, or perceived recovery and soreness.
- **Babault 2015** — 161 men, 12 weeks, 25 g twice daily of whey, pea or placebo. Both protein groups beat placebo for strength and hypertrophy; **no difference between whey and pea**.
- **Hartman 2007** — 56 young men, 12 weeks, milk vs soy vs sugar after training. All groups gained strength with no between-protein difference; milk showed a small hypertrophy edge that was **barely significant**, and the article notes the confound honestly: the soy group ate 1.2 g/kg and the milk group 1.4 g/kg.

The acute mechanism is not disputed — animal proteins raise blood EAAs faster and higher, whey faster than casein (Boirie 1997), and 25 g of whey (2.7 g leucine, 10.9 g EAAs) near-maximally stimulates muscle protein synthesis where you would need **31 g of corn protein or 105 g of hemp protein** for the same doses (Gorissen 2018). The article's own conclusion is the right one: those acute differences do not translate into long-term training outcomes once total intake is adequate.

**PDCAAS numbers** are given for whey 1.0, soy 0.99, pea 0.893, wheat gluten 0.25 (Rutherfurd 2015 — note this is a **growing-rat** study, which the article does not mention). Animal proteins run 90–99% digestible, plant 70–90%.

## Where the recommendations outrun the evidence

**The 2.3–3.1 g/kg tier.** The stated basis is Helms 2014, a systematic review titled, unambiguously, "A Case for Higher Intakes," concerning lean resistance-trained athletes in caloric restriction, with the ISSN's 2018 review (Kerksick 2018) agreeing. The endpoint that generates the number is **minimising lean mass loss / avoiding negative nitrogen balance** — not hypertrophy, not strength, not performance. The article states this correctly. What it does not say is that the underlying trials compare higher protein against **inadequate** protein, so they establish that too little is bad, not that 3.1 beats 1.8. This is the specific gap the library records, and it stands here.

**The safety claim is stated well.** Intakes above the RDA appear safe in people without kidney disease (Antonio 2014 — the 4.4 g/kg trial; Poortmans 2000; Brändle 1996; Wu 2016). The clinical turn is the interesting one and is properly hedged: they cite the **Cochrane review** (Hahn 2020) plus the KNOW-CKD cohort (Lee 2019) to say it is *unclear* whether restricting protein to ~0.6 g/kg in existing kidney disease preserves function, prevents death or improves quality of life. That is a physician reading a Cochrane review accurately rather than reaching past it, and it is the most careful passage in the article.

**Recommendation 4** deserves credit for arguing against interest: intakes up to 4.4 g/kg are safe but "has not shown to improve strength, hypertrophy, body composition, or performance outcomes." A supplement company saying more protein does nothing above a threshold is worth noticing.

**Flagged:** the RDA is given as "0.8–1.0 g/kg"; the actual US RDA is 0.8 g/kg. Minor, but it inflates the reference point the recommendations are measured against. And the claim that BCAAs alone cannot enhance muscle protein synthesis when other EAAs are limiting is attached to Leenders 2011 — a chronic leucine supplementation trial in **elderly type 2 diabetic men**, which is suggestive but is not an acute MPS study in the relevant population.
