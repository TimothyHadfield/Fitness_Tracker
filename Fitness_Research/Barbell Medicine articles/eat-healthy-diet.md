# How to Eat a Healthy Diet: The Targets, and Which Ones Have a Paper Behind Them

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How To Eat A Healthy Diet" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-to-eat-a-healthy-diet/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2025-04-07 · 5,010 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

> Record note: the stored transcript's header carries the wrong title ("Understanding Heart Rate Variability for Strength Training"). The URL, body and reference list are all the healthy-diet article; the title line is a scraping artifact.

## Bottom line
- **The organising idea is the dietary pattern, not the nutrient** — habitual average intake over long periods [1,2]. Everything else in the article is downstream of that, and it is the part they defend best.
- **No named diet is superior.** [Ge 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.m696) [11], a BMJ network meta-analysis of 14 popular named diet programmes across 121 randomised trials, is the citation. It compared **weight and cardiovascular risk factors** over roughly 6–12 months. The article generalises it to "health or performance"; the trial evidence does not cover performance or hard endpoints.
- **Protein: 1.6 g/kg/day.** This traces to [Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608) [9], a meta-analysis and meta-regression whose breakpoint for resistance-training gains sits at ~1.62 g/kg/day. The number is used correctly. The higher figure they give for physique athletes in a deficit — **up to 3.1 g/kg/day — carries no citation at all**.
- **Fibre 25–30 g/day** from [Veronese 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqx082) (an umbrella review) [12] and the [Reynolds 2019 Lancet](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31809-9) series [22]. They are careful to say fibre "may not be entirely causal" and that the benefit likely comes from the whole pattern of fibre-rich foods. That hedge is correct and is more than most sources give.
- **Saturated fat ≤10% of Calories is recommended with no citation on the sentence.** The one saturated-fat paper in the reference list — Li 2015, a large prospective cohort [25] — is **never cited anywhere in the article**. It is an orphan reference sitting under an uncited recommendation.
- **They sell what they are writing about.** The article states that ~20% of supplements are likely contaminated and 30–50% mislabelled [28,29], that most people do not need supplements, and that vitamin D, fish oil and multivitamins do not help most people [30,31,32] — then links twice to their own whey protein product and once to their Peri-Rx supplement, and four times to their coaching service.

## What the evidence supports

**Dietary patterns.** The framing is defined from the 2025 Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee report [2], with the AHA's 2021 dietary guidance statement [3] for the fibre-and-added-sugar claim and Cena & Calder [1] for pattern change as a public health goal. This is guideline-and-review sourcing, appropriate for a framing section.

**Appetite is not primarily under conscious control** [13,14,15]. This is the same argument they make at length in the weight-regain article, and it is the article's most consequential claim because it justifies the "change the food environment" advice. Two of the three citations fit: Stevenson's review of the psychological basis of hunger [14] and Emilien & Hollis on determinants of adult eating behaviour [15]. The third, Westbury 2023 [13], is a review of **obesity stigma** — relevant to the surrounding argument, but not a source on appetite regulation. Under-supported by one of its three references.

**Ultra-processed food drives spontaneous overeating** [16]. This is [Hall 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2019.05.008), the inpatient crossover RCT — and it is the strongest causal evidence anywhere in the article. **The article gives none of its numbers.** For the record: 20 adults, two weeks on each diet, matched for presented macronutrients and energy density, ad libitum intake; participants ate roughly 500 kcal/day more on the ultra-processed arm and gained about 0.9 kg, versus losing about 0.9 kg on the unprocessed arm. Small, tightly controlled, and it does the work the article asks of it — but n=20 for two weeks is worth knowing.

**Plant-based patterns and lower intake** [17]. The "over 600 fewer Calories per day" figure comes from Clarys 2014, a **cross-sectional comparison of self-reported diet quality** across vegan, vegetarian, semi-vegetarian, pesco-vegetarian and omnivorous eaters. The article says "some studies suggest," which is the right hedge for a cross-sectional design, and it explicitly declines to recommend vegetarian or vegan eating to everyone.

**Food environment** [18]. Mattes & Foster is a short commentary in *Obesity*, not a study. It is used to support a fairly assertive claim — that tasty ultra-processed options "tend to win out despite our best efforts and intentions" even when healthy options are equally available. That claim is plausible and consistent with Hall 2019, but the citation attached to it is an opinion piece.

**Macronutrient intake data** [7]. Shan 2019 in *JAMA*, NHANES 1999–2016, is a solid national-average source for the 81 g protein / 252 g carbohydrate / 74 g fat figures.

**Protein source.** For plant versus animal equivalence they cite two small RCTs: Babault 2015 [19], pea versus whey, and Joy 2013 [20], rice versus whey. Both found no meaningful difference at adequate intakes. **Neither tested a protein-restricted condition**, so the second half of the sentence — that below 1 g/kg/day animal sources may produce better outcomes — has no supporting study attached to it. That half is clinical inference, and the article does not mark it as such.

**Supplements.** This section is the article's most rigorous, and it argues against the category. Supplement use figures from Kantor 2016 [26] and a trade-association press release [27]; the contamination and mislabelling figures from Mathews 2018 [28] and Tucker 2018 [29]; and three negative outcome citations — Barbarawi 2019 [30], a meta-analysis of 21 RCTs in more than 83,000 people finding no cardiovascular benefit from vitamin D; the AHA science advisory on fish oil [31]; and the USPSTF evidence report on vitamins and minerals for primary prevention [32]. Protein powder is the exception they carve out, cited to Pasiakos 2015 [33], and framed narrowly as useful for people who would not otherwise reach their protein target.

## Where recommendation outruns evidence

The article is generally good about saying "we recommend" rather than "the evidence shows." Several specific numbers still arrive without support:

- **3.1 g/kg/day protein for physique athletes in a deficit** — no citation.
- **3 g/kg/day carbohydrate minimum for "performance-minded folks"** — no citation. It is a reasonable practitioner figure; it is not sourced here.
- **Saturated fat ≤10% of total Calories** — no citation on the sentence, with an unused cohort study [25] in the reference list that would have partly supported it.
- **The entire essential-fatty-acid-deficiency section** — the disease list, the malabsorption conditions, the paediatric consequences — is uncited.
- **Low-carbohydrate diets and training.** The claim is that low carbohydrate intake "may impair muscle mass gain along with both strength and endurance performance, particularly in trained individuals" [23,24]. Burke 2017 [23] is a well-conducted study in **21 elite race walkers over three weeks** showing impaired exercise economy on a low-carb high-fat diet — an endurance-economy finding, not a hypertrophy or strength finding. Paoli 2019 [24] is a **narrative review** of ketogenic diets and hypertrophy, not a trial. So the endurance half of the claim has a study; the muscle and strength half rests on a review. Their conclusion — do not use very low carbohydrate diets in trained lifters chasing hypertrophy or strength, but it may be fine for beginners losing weight — is a defensible clinical position drawn from thin evidence, and they do label it "based on the existing evidence."
- **The Twinkie Diet.** Mark Haub's 10-week self-experiment is used to illustrate that energy deficit drives weight and lipid change regardless of macronutrient composition. It is n=1, uncontrolled and uncited. It illustrates; it does not demonstrate.

**Flagged as the clearest overreach:** "there is no good scientific evidence showing clear superiority of any particular diet for health or performance" [11]. Ge 2020 is a strong network meta-analysis, but it tested named diet programmes against each other for **weight loss and cardiovascular risk factors in trials mostly under a year**. It says nothing about performance and nothing about long-term disease endpoints. The claim as written is broader than the paper.

## Where this sits against the rest of the library

Consistent, not contradictory. The 1.6 g/kg/day protein target and its Morton 2018 source match the protein material elsewhere in this library. The plant-versus-animal-equivalence-at-adequate-intake position matches. The "appetite is not primarily conscious" argument is the same one the weight-regain article builds on, sourced to the same kind of literature. The "no best diet" conclusion agrees with the general position of the YouTube evidence-review sources, and here it has a stronger citation behind it than those usually carry.

## The commercial layer

Worth stating plainly, because this article is unusually dense with it. In roughly 5,000 words the reader is directed to: a free Calorie calculator, a free macronutrient calculator, their whey protein product page (twice), their Peri-Rx performance supplement, and their coaching service (four separate links, including one immediately after the "putting this into practice" box and one closing the article).

The supplement section is where this matters most. They correctly report that a fifth of supplements are likely contaminated and up to half mislabelled, and that most people need none — and then recommend their own two. The advice they give with it is the right advice (buy GMP-certified, third-party tested products) and it is a standard the reader can apply to any brand, including theirs. None of the nutritional recommendations appear bent to fit a product. But the "Peri-Rx" link is broken: it points at the whey protein isolate product page rather than the supplement it names.
