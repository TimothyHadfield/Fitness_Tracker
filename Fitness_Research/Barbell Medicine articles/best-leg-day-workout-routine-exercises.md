# Build a Leg Day From Movement Slots, Not From an Exercise List

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Best Leg Day Workout Routine & Exercises" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-leg-day-workout-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine (unattributed) · 2024-04-04 · 10,637 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **The usable content is a slot template, not the exercise list.** One primary squat and one primary hinge, one secondary squat and hinge, one tertiary or isolation movement, calves anywhere. Everything else in the article is filling those slots.
- **Prescribed proximity to failure scales inversely with how systemically taxing the movement is** — RPE 6–8 on barbell squats and deadlifts, 7–8 on leg press and RDL, 8–9 on curls and calves, 9–10 on leg extensions. The article never states this as a principle, but it is the most transferable thing in it.
- **Frequency is a scheduling variable, not a training variable.** A meta-analysis of 22 studies found the apparent dose-response of frequency on strength vanished once weekly volume was equated ([Grgic 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x)), and a separate trial found 1×/week and 3×/week produced the same hypertrophy and strength at matched volume ([Lambrianides 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004300)). This agrees with the volume-first position already in this library.
- **Hip thrust versus squat for glutes: the EMG says one thing and the growth data says another.** Hip thrusts show higher glute excitation than back squats ([Delgado 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003290)), but a direct training comparison found similar gluteal hypertrophy and similar transfer to the deadlift. Their recommendation — do both — outruns that evidence in the harmless direction.
- **The citation quality is poor for a Barbell Medicine article.** The marker-to-list mapping is technically verified, but the article's own list attaches plainly wrong papers to at least six claims, and four consecutive protein recommendations all point at the same review. Details below.
- **Three in-article CTAs sell a hypertrophy programme**, plus a template quiz, injury-rehab coaching, and — immediately after debunking the supplement category — their own pre-workout and whey protein.

## Decision 1: What actually has to be in a leg day

The template, stripped of the sales copy:

- one **primary** squat and one **primary** hinge
- one **secondary** squat and one **secondary** hinge
- one **tertiary** or isolation movement for quads or hamstrings
- calves appended to any session

For a single day, they suggest alternating priority: on a squat/quad day run primary squat → secondary hinge → tertiary squat → quad isolation → calves; on a hinge/hamstring day, the mirror. Novices take the lower end of the set ranges, experienced lifters the higher end, and additional volume goes in via extra exercises or extra sets on existing ones.

The one supporting citation for training legs at all is [Bartolomei 2018](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001639), which found some carry-over from lower-body resistance training to upper-body strength adaptation in trained men. It is a single small trial doing a lot of rhetorical work for a claim ("train your legs") that nobody disputes.

They also explicitly kill the hormone argument for squats: yes, squats raise testosterone and growth hormone acutely, so does essentially all exercise, the magnitude does not correlate with size or strength gains, and the hormone hypothesis is not supported. **This claim carries no citation in the article** — they link a podcast episode instead — but it matches their own testosterone article and the library's existing position. Squats are one good way to load a lot of muscle over a long range of motion, not a hormonal event.

## Decision 2: Which primary movement

**Back squat.** High bar versus low bar: same muscles, more upright torso on high bar, more horizontal on low bar, similar training effects, choose by preference. Depth below parallel for most people, with the explicit concession that higher or lower is fine outside powerlifting, "based on their current abilities." No citation on any of it — this is coaching judgement presented as settled.

**Safety squat bar.** Positioned as a substitute where holding a straight bar hurts — upper-body pain or mobility restriction — and as a way to load thoracic extension. Mechanically it sits between a high-bar squat and a front squat in torso angle ([Hecker 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002912)). This is the clearest example in the article of their general approach to technique: the bar is a tool for accommodating a person, not a standard the person must meet.

**Conventional deadlift.** They are careful that a deadlift is not a squat — it starts with a concentric contraction, uses a more horizontal torso and less knee flexion, and therefore is not interchangeable with squatting. Hence one squat *and* one hinge, not two of either.

The clinical claim attached to it is their signature position: rather than avoiding the deadlift because it loads the low back, **regular dynamic loading of the low back may help desensitise a person with low back pain and improve function**, citing [Cai 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001396). That study compared lower-limb and back exercise programmes in runners with chronic low back pain. It is a reasonable piece of evidence for load-based management of low back pain in general; **it is not a study of deadlifts**, and the article presents it as though it were. The position is mainstream in physiotherapy and correct in direction — the citation just does not reach as far as the sentence.

**Sumo versus conventional.** Wider stance puts the grip inside the legs and produces a more vertical torso, which "may load the back more comfortably for some" ([Belcher 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000298); [Cholewicki 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/) — the latter measured lumbar spine loads during competition-standard maximal lifts and is the actual biomechanical source). Trade-off: sumo demands more hip mobility. Their recommendation is that most people train both; for powerlifters, use whichever you are stronger at, and if you have never trained the other, run both in one block to find out; for weightlifting, CrossFit and strongman, conventional, on specificity grounds. That is coaching advice, and they present it as such.

## Decision 3: Filling the secondary and isolation slots

**Leg press** (secondary squat, 2–3 × 6–10, RPE 7–8). Loads quads, glutes and hamstrings ([Martín-Fuentes 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17134626), EMG systematic review). Their honesty here is worth crediting: they say the leg press *likely* produces less fatigue than the squat because less muscle mass and no balance demand, then immediately add "though this is mostly speculation." The citation attached to the "good for beginners and veterans alike" line ([Walker & Häkkinen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000000381)) is about differing neuromuscular adaptations in young versus older men, not about the leg press or training status.

**Romanian deadlift** (secondary hinge, 2–3 × 6–10, RPE 7–8). Not a straight-leg deadlift — knees unlock, bar traces the legs, stop a few inches below the knee. Their handling of the EMG evidence is exactly right: surface EMG shows more hamstring electrical activity in the RDL, "however, it is unknown whether these differences manifest as more or less improvement in strength and/or hypertrophy." That is the correct caveat on every activation study in this genre, and they apply it consistently — including to their own hip thrust argument below.

**Split squat** (tertiary squat, 2–3 × 8–12 per side, RPE 7–8). Unilateral, adds a balance demand ([Marchetti 2018](https://doi.org/10.1515/hukin-2017-0174), comparing in-line and traditional lunges). Practical note: slower tempo, or hold a support with one hand and a dumbbell in the other, to strip out the balance limitation.

**Hip thrust** (tertiary hinge, 2–3 × 8–12, RPE 7–8). The interesting slot. The claim that the hip thrust is uniquely good for glutes has EMG support — higher glute excitation than back squat ([Delgado 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003290)) — but the training comparison found the squat and hip thrust produced **relatively similar hypertrophy and similar transfer to the deadlift.** That comparison is the strongest evidence in the section and it is a **preprint** (Plotkin et al., bioRxiv 2023, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.06.21.545949)), which the article does not flag. Their conclusion — train the glutes with several exercises, hip thrust *and* squat — is a recommendation rather than a finding, and a defensible one.

**Leg extension** (quad isolation, 2–4 × 10–15, RPE 9–10). This is where the clinicians speak. The fear that open-chain knee exercise causes harm via shear force derives from mechanistic research on ACL reconstruction, and "the current evidence does not suggest leg extensions 'hurt the knees' or are otherwise harmful." **This paragraph carries no citation at all**, in an article that cites 58 things, and it is precisely the paragraph where their clinical authority is doing the work. The position is well supported in the rehabilitation literature and heterodox in the gym — but a reader cannot check it here.

**Lying hamstring curl** (2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9) and **calf raise** (2–5 × 10–15, RPE 8–9). Both are attached to injury-prevention claims — "strengthening the legs through exercise reduces the risk of injury in sports," and the same for the triceps surae. The direction of that claim is well supported in the wider literature. The evidence *they* cite is not: a review of recurrent hamstring injury risk factors, a 1981 strength-journal column, a hamstring-to-quadriceps ratio review, a qualitative interview study of 20 expert clinicians, a balance-training cohort in community-dwelling older adults, and a book chapter. **Right conclusion, wrong evidence.**

## Decision 4: How much, how often, how heavy

**Frequency.** Their answer is the one this library already holds. [Grgic 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x) pooled 22 studies and found an apparent dose-dependent frequency effect on strength that disappeared once volume was equated; [Lambrianides 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004300) found the same hypertrophy and strength from once-weekly and thrice-weekly training at matched weekly volume. Practically: one or two leg days a week inside a PPL split should get you the same place as full-body or body-part splits. The accompanying claim that volume *is* dose-dependent for both strength and hypertrophy is stated without any citation.

**Load selection.** RPE and reps-in-reserve, chosen during warm-ups to match the day's capacity, with velocity and heart rate as alternatives. Their framing is unusually relaxed: "there's a lot of wiggle room in load selection for strength and hypertrophy improvements. As long as you're in the ballpark, you're likely fine." That is consistent with the load-equivalence evidence elsewhere in this library, and it is stated as opinion.

**Range of motion.** Train through a relatively large ROM; small increases or decreases are unlikely to change outcomes. No citation, and it is a slightly weaker claim than the current literature would support — the ROM evidence generally favours long muscle lengths more strongly than "relatively large is fine."

## Decision 5: What to eat around it

**Protein.** Around **1.6 g/kg body weight per day** is enough for most people to maximise training results and body-composition change versus lower intakes ([Antonio 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-015-0100-0); [Tagawa 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-022-00508-w), dose-response meta-analysis of RCTs). Higher is fine, especially when losing weight, very lean, or training >10 h/week; competitive physique athletes 2.3–3.1 g/kg; spread across 3–5 meals 3–5 hours apart; source only matters if you are protein-restricted below ~1 g/kg, in which case prefer animal sources. **All four of those bullets cite the same paper** ([Stokes 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu10020180)) under four different reference numbers, which makes the evidence base look four times deeper than it is.

**Carbohydrate.** Starting point **3–5 g/kg/day**, from fruit, vegetables, rice, quinoa, oats, beans and potatoes. Mechanistic case: falling blood glucose impairs force production, glycogen below roughly **70 mmol/kg** is associated with weakness and limited force output ([Nielsen 2011](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2010.204487)), and intra-workout carbohydrate may help sessions over 90 minutes.

Their read on low-carbohydrate diets is the honest part: the strength data are "relatively small, of short duration, and in untrained individuals" ([Henselmans 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14040856), systematic review), and low-carbohydrate approaches remain reasonable for a beginner whose priority is fat loss. The hypertrophy claim is not honest by accident — see the citation problems below.

**Supplements.** They lead with a debunking: vitamin D, fish oil and multivitamins do not reduce cardiovascular disease, most cancers, or type 2 diabetes risk — and here the citations are genuinely strong ([Barbarawi 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2019.1870), 21 RCTs and >83,000 participants; [Siscovick 2017](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000482), AHA science advisory; [O'Connor 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.15650), USPSTF evidence report). They then recommend **their own caffeine-free pre-workout and their own whey protein isolate**, with no evidence offered for either. Stated plainly because it is worth weighing: the article debunks the supplement category, then sells two products from it, in consecutive paragraphs.

## Decision 6: When to expect results

Strength: an average time to measurable improvement of about **4 weeks**, with large individual variation. Hypertrophy: longer, roughly **4–6 weeks or more**, depending on the person and on how muscle size is measured ([DeFreitas 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-011-1905-4)). The surrounding discussion of non-response is good — group means hide individuals who respond strongly, weakly, or not at all.

## The citation problem

The marker-to-reference mapping passed its offset check, so each `[n]` in the text does point at list entry *n*. The problem is that Barbell Medicine's own list is wrong in places. Where a claim and its assigned reference plainly do not match, I have not attributed:

- **[2]** glute anatomy → an EMG systematic review of the deadlift.
- **[22]** RDL hamstring sEMG → a clinical review of hamstring injury diagnosis and return to play, not an EMG study.
- **[26]** "the squat and hip thrust produce relatively similar hypertrophy" → a ketogenic-diet and hypertrophy review. The study that actually reports this comparison sits in the same list at **#57** (Plotkin et al., hip thrust and back squat, bioRxiv preprint).
- **[41]/[42]** appear swapped — "carbohydrates provide energy" points at a gluconeogenesis chapter, and "this process is called gluconeogenesis" points at a carbohydrate-supplementation paper.
- **[50]** "low- and very-low-carbohydrate diets don't do as well from a hypertrophy perspective" → [Burke 2017](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP273230), a study of exercise economy and race performance in elite race walkers. That paper says nothing about hypertrophy. This one matters, because the hypertrophy claim is the reason a lifter would change their diet.
- **[57]** "a meta-analysis of 40 studies… average time to improvement about 4 weeks" → the Plotkin hip thrust preprint again.

Add the four-numbers-one-paper protein block and the StatPearls-heavy anatomy section, and the apparent 58-reference depth is considerably thinner than it looks. Nothing here suggests bad faith — this is the signature of an SEO listicle assembled to a template, published without a named author, rather than the carefully-cited clinical writing the same site produces elsewhere.
