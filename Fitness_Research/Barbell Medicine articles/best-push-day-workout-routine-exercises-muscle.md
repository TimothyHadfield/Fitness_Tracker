# A Push Day: Five Exercises, and Which Parts of the Case Are Actually Cited

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Best Push Day Workout Routine & Exercises for Muscle Growth" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-push-day-workout-routine/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-01-26 · 8,974 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- The recommended session is **bench press 5×4–6 @ RPE 7–8, overhead press 3–5×6–10 @ RPE 7–8, then dumbbell flye, lateral raise and overhead triceps extension at 2–4×10–15 @ RPE 8–10**. **Not one set, rep or RPE number in that prescription carries a citation.** It is house practice, and should be read as such.
- The general shape — one primary compound, one secondary compound, two isolation movements, everything in a wide rep range 2–5 reps from failure — is consistent with the loading literature they cite elsewhere ([Schoenfeld 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports9020032), viable hypertrophy range ~4–25 reps). The specific numbers are preference.
- Their own framing undercuts exercise-choice anxiety: the alternatives section says swapping dumbbell for barbell, cable for dumbbell "won't provide variety in how the muscles are targeted and will mostly yield similar results." Then two paragraphs later they cite an EMG study to distinguish barbell from dumbbell bench. Both claims are in the article; the first is the better-supported one.
- **The nutrition half is more heavily cited than the training half** — protein 1.6 g/kg/day ([Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608)), up to 2.2 g/kg for dieting physique athletes, carbohydrate 3–5 g/kg as a starting point ([ISSN 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-018-0242-y)) — and it ends with a link to their own whey isolate in their own shop.
- **Two citation errors survive inside an otherwise clean reference list.** A "meta-analysis of 89 articles" is attributed to [17], which is Antonio's 2015 single trial of 3.4 g/kg; and the muscle-protein-synthesis time-course sentence carries marker [40] (a 2022 strength-adaptation study) when the intended source is plainly Atherton & Smith, which sits in the list twice, at 13 and 41, and is never cited under 13.

## Decision 1: is a push day worth running?

The article's argument for a push/pull/legs split is organisational, not physiological: grouping chest, shoulders and triceps into one session lets you fatigue a small set of muscles together without carrying that fatigue into the next day, which uses mostly different ones. No evidence is offered for the split outperforming any other arrangement, and none is claimed. This is the honest version of the answer — the split question is a scheduling question.

Worth reading alongside their companion piece on chest exercises, which cites the frequency meta-analyses directly and concludes that how you distribute the same weekly volume across days does not measurably change strength or hypertrophy outcomes. If that is right, the push day is a convenience, and the article treats it that way.

## Decision 2: which five exercises, and on what basis

**Bench press (primary).** The claim that two to three months of bench training produces substantial muscle-thickness gains even in untrained people is supported by three studies at once — [Brandão 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003550) on exercise-order effects, [Chaves 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32922646/) on horizontal versus incline bench in untrained young men, and [Kikuchi 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesf.2017.06.003), which found low-load bench press and push-ups produced similar hypertrophy and strength gain. That last one quietly makes the point that the bar is not doing anything magical.

The genuinely useful specific: from bench pressing alone, the **triceps grow more slowly than the pecs, and what growth there is concentrates in the lateral head** ([Ogasawara 2012](https://doi.org/10.1556/IMAS.4.2012.4.7), a muscle-thickness time course). That is the actual justification for adding an overhead triceps extension later in the session, and the article makes the connection.

**Overhead press (secondary).** The claim that all push muscles show higher activity in the standing than the seated variation is **uncited**. So is the assertion that the standing version's stability demand "allows for more activation." Treat as opinion.

**Dumbbell flye, lateral raise, overhead triceps extension (tertiary).** All three are recommended with no supporting citation. The overhead extension is justified mechanistically — shoulder in extension puts the triceps at near-maximal length — which is a plausible rationale and a fashionable one, but the article does not cite the lengthened-position literature to support it.

## Decision 3: what changes if you substitute

The stated substitutions are dumbbell bench for barbell bench, high-incline bench for overhead press, cable crossover for dumbbell flye, cable lateral raise for dumbbell lateral raise. The framing is explicit and, in my view, the most useful sentence in this section: these swaps "won't provide variety in how the muscles are targeted and will mostly yield similar results."

Two claims are then made that sit awkwardly against that:

- Dumbbells allow a larger range of motion, which "could be useful if you're training for hypertrophy," cited to [Schoenfeld & Grgic 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/2050312120901559). That review is about range of motion generally, not about dumbbells; the step from "longer ROM helps" to "dumbbells give longer ROM" is the authors' inference, not the paper's finding.
- [Farias 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001651) found barbell bench elicited higher triceps activity and dumbbell bench higher pectoral activity. This is acute surface EMG across multiple sets. It is an activation finding, not a growth finding, and the article presents it as a "key difference in the results produced by the two types of equipment," which overstates it.

The high-incline (45–70°) claim — more deltoid contribution, some upper-pec involvement — is uncited here, and their chest-exercise article argues the opposite direction on incline angle and upper-pec emphasis. Worth knowing they are not fully consistent between articles.

## Decision 4: what to eat around it

This section is a compressed version of Barbell Medicine's standing nutrition position, and it is where the referencing is densest.

**Protein.** Baseline recommendation is **1.6 g/kg total body weight per day**, higher for people in a deficit or with anabolic-resistance risk factors ([Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608)), up to **2.2 g/kg** for competitive physique athletes preserving mass while dieting. Above roughly 1 g/kg, plant and animal sources appear equivalent for strength and hypertrophy; below that, prioritising animal sources may matter ([Stokes 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu10020180)). Spacing protein feedings three to five hours apart keeps muscle protein synthesis elevated across a 12-hour window (same source). Insufficient protein while lifting means less muscle and strength gained ([Castaneda 2001](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-135-11-200112040-00008), an RCT in chronic renal insufficiency; [Campbell 2018](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2017-0389), 8 weeks in aspiring female physique athletes).

Note the population mismatch in that last pair: a renal-insufficiency trial and a small physique-athlete trial are being used to support a general claim about protein sufficiency in lifters. The claim is almost certainly true; the specific evidence cited for it is not the strongest available.

**The mis-citation.** The article reports "a meta-analysis of 89 articles" finding that strength rises **0.72% per 0.1 g/kg/day increase in protein**, and that protein without resistance training does nothing for strength. Marker [17] points to [Antonio 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-015-0100-0) — a single follow-up trial of a 3.4 g/kg diet, not a meta-analysis of 89 articles. The preceding sentence about a "2015 study" of high versus normal protein points to [16], which resolves to Kerksick & Leutholtz 2005, a review. Both attributions are wrong. The 0.72% figure is real and comes from the dose-response protein meta-analysis literature, but it is not in this article's reference list at all.

**Carbohydrate.** Recommendation is **3–5 g/kg/day** as a starting point ([ISSN 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-018-0242-y)). They are candid that the evidence is weak: a review of 49 studies found little relationship between carbohydrate intake and strength performance ([Henselmans 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14040856)), and they list the reasons those studies are unpersuasive — short, nutritionally uncontrolled, untrained subjects, low training volumes. The mechanistic case for carbohydrate is fatigue-specific: low muscle glycogen impairs force production by altering calcium release ([Gejl 2014](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000132); [Nielsen 2011](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2010.204487)), with a threshold thought to sit around **70 mmol/kg** ([Murray & Rosenbloom 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuy001)).

**Flagged:** they then advise against very low-carbohydrate diets for trained lifters pursuing hypertrophy or strength, citing [Burke 2017](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP273230) — elite race walkers, endurance economy — and [Paoli 2019](https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2019-0071), a narrative review. Neither is a hypertrophy trial in trained lifters. The recommendation is defensible; the evidence stated for it does not reach the specificity of the claim, and they do soften it appropriately for beginners losing weight.

**Supplements, and the sell.** They open by noting ~75% of Americans took supplements in the past year against a $35bn market ([Kantor 2016](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.14403)), then dispatch vitamin D, fish oil and multivitamins as showing no cardiovascular or cancer benefit for most people ([Barbarawi 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2019.1870); [Siscovick 2017](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000482); [USPSTF/O'Connor 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.15650)). That is a genuinely deflationary supplement section from a company with a supplement line.

**They then recommend one product: their own whey isolate**, linked twice into their shop, once with tracking parameters attached, cited to [Pasiakos 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-014-0242-2). The caveat they attach is the correct one — protein supplements help people who would not otherwise hit the intake target — but the reader should note that the single supplement surviving their own scepticism is the one they sell.

## Decision 5: how long before anything happens

Their answer is **at least four weeks**, and this is the most carefully handled claim in the article. In an analysis of **40 resistance-training studies that tested strength repeatedly rather than only at the endpoints**, the average time to a meaningful strength gain was **4.3 weeks**, with a range across studies of **1 to 12 weeks**, and squat 1RM improvements ranging **3% to 40%** ([Lambrianides 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004300)). They add that responses are non-uniform — non-responders at one end, extreme responders at the other — and that day-to-day performance is noisy for reasons that have nothing to do with the programme.

This is the right way to answer a "when will I see results" question and it is unusually well sourced for a listicle.

## What the article does not support

- **The programme itself.** Every set, rep and RPE figure is uncited. So is the "one primary, one secondary, two isolation" formula, and the beginner prescription (1–3 exercises per muscle group, 2–3 sets of 3–20 reps, ~4–5 reps from failure, 2–3 sessions per week). These are recommendations, not findings, and the article does not mark the difference.
- **Injury avoidance.** The safety section is entirely uncited, but it is worth reading because it states their technique position plainly: any repeatable, efficient self-selected technique that meets the exercise's points of performance is fine, technique should be expected to change over a career, and lifters should not be rigidly attached to one movement pattern. That is the pain-science-adjacent house view, and here it is presented as advice rather than as evidence — correctly.
- **"Track your progress."** Promises three methods, delivers one (keep a journal). The section is unfinished.

**Commercial disclosure:** the article links to Barbell Medicine's strength and hypertrophy programme catalogue, their online fitness and nutrition coaching, and their whey protein isolate. None of it is disguised, but the supplement recommendation sits directly after a section arguing most supplements are useless.
