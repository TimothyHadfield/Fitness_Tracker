# Five "Best" Intermediate Powerlifting Programmes, All of Them Their Own

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "5 Intermediate Powerlifting Programs To Increase Strength & Size" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-intermediate-powerlifting-programs/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-07-15 · 6,772 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **All five "best" programmes are Barbell Medicine's own paid templates** — Strength I, Strength/Powerlifting II, Strength III, Powerbuilding II, Powerbuilding III. No third-party programme is considered, and **no comparative data of any kind is offered**: not between their templates, and not against 5/3/1, Texas Method, Sheiko or anything else. The ranking is a product catalogue.
- The article's best passage argues that **novice/intermediate/advanced classifications are made up** — arbitrary, unvalidated, and useless for choosing a programme — and then admits it is using "another made up definition" (6–9 months of regular Big Three training). That self-awareness is genuine and rare.
- It also **contradicts itself immediately**: the comparison table places Strength III and Powerbuilding III under "Advanced Lifters (12+ months)" and "9+ months" in an article about intermediates, and the Strength II section is written throughout about "the Powerlifting II Program".
- **Sex is deliberately absent from the modifiable/non-modifiable factors list.** Their position: men and women gain the same *relative* strength at the same *rate*, so programming should be individualised by response, not by sex. Drawn from a 15-year analysis of 1,271 male and 626 female powerlifters.
- **Injury framing:** all resistance training averages roughly **1–4 injuries per 1,000 participation hours**; average powerlifting injury symptoms last **12 days**; comparable to walking (0.19–1.2 or 0.5–2) and below track and field (~3.6).
- Two claims outrun their sources: that lifting **"delays and even reverses the course of" osteoarthritis**, and that stronger people get greater health benefits than weak ones (observational, confounded by everything that makes people strong).

## The five programmes

| Template | Weeks | Lifting days/wk | Stated audience |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength I | 13 | 3 | ">3–9 months" |
| Strength II (text calls it Powerlifting II) | 13 | 3–4 (text says four) | 6–9 months |
| Strength III | 13 | 4 | "Advanced, 12+ months" |
| Powerbuilding II | 10 | 4 | 6–9 months |
| Powerbuilding III | 14 | 4 | "Advanced, >9 months" |

Every template adds **two GPP days** — conditioning plus direct arm, upper back and core work — which may be run after lifting sessions or separately. Each builds to a testing week or a meet. The differences described are volume (Powerbuilding II adds a fourth session and more sets and reps than Powerbuilding I) and **customisation**: Strength III lets the user select all their own lifts, including substituting the overhead press for the bench in preparation for a strengthlifting meet, and Powerbuilding III is "the most customizable", with adjustable volume, intensity and exercise order and a taper.

**What backs the recommendations:** nothing empirical. The rationale for each is a description of its structure and audience. This is not an evidence ranking and should not be read as one.

## Their strongest argument: lifter classification does not work

Whether someone has lifted for one year or two, or squats 400 or 500, "doesn't tell a coach how to program for an individual." The adaptation-rate alternative — novice improves session to session, intermediate week to week, advanced slower — fails because adaptation rates vary wildly between people and within a person over time: a beginner may not add weight every session, an advanced lifter may go on a run of session-to-session increases before a meet. Their conclusion is that these categories "have thus far used arbitrary criteria that have not been validated in any way."

This is a real critique and it is aimed squarely at the framework the article's own title depends on. **They then adopt an arbitrary definition anyway** (6–9 months on the Big Three), flagging it as arbitrary. Marking the difference between the argument and the marketing constraint is the honest thing to do, and they do it.

The framing number: in a study of nearly 7,000 competitive powerlifters, lifters typically added **7.5–12.5% to their total in the first year of competing**, against roughly **20% total over the following ten years** (Latella, in prose). That is the shape of the whole career, and it is more informative than any classification scheme.

## The training-variable positions

**Intensity.** They set a **minimum load threshold for strength**: roughly **65–75% 1RM for multi-rep work** and **>85–90% 1RM for singles**, prose-linked to Schoenfeld 2017, Mangine 2015 and Androulakis-Korakakis 2018. *(Note: the Mangine and Androulakis-Korakakis links in the article both point to the same MDPI URL, so one of the two is broken.)*

**This is where the article collides with other material in this library.** Schoenfeld's 2017 low-versus-high-load meta-analysis is used elsewhere in these notes to establish that loads from roughly 30% to 85% 1RM produce **similar hypertrophy** when sets are taken to failure. Barbell Medicine use the same body of work to argue for a **65–75% floor**. Both readings can stand, because they are about different outcomes — the meta separates strength (load-specific, favours heavy) from hypertrophy (load-tolerant). But a reader who holds both notes should see that the same paper is doing opposite work in each, and that the difference is entirely in the outcome measured.

**Proximity to failure.** Compound strength work at **RPE 6–8 / 2–4 RIR**; isolation hypertrophy work and short peaking phases at **RPE 8–10 / 0–2 RIR**. This is a recommendation, presented without citation.

**Volume.** Higher volumes generally produce greater strength gains — a "graded dose-response" — **provided** intensity, proximity to failure and exercise selection match how strength will be tested and the total stress suits the individual. Volumes exceeding current capacity produce *worse* results. They state plainly that the optimum is unknown and that minimum-dose research is ambiguous. Their practical instrument is **session RPE**: most sessions should land at sRPE 6–8; persistent sRPE >8 with soreness and no progress means too much, persistent sRPE <5 with no soreness and no progress means too little. Autoregulation as a substitute for a number they concede they do not have — a reasonable clinical move, and explicitly a recommendation rather than a finding.

**Progressive loading, not progressive overload.** Their distinctive framing: training does not have to get harder to force adaptation; it has to **keep pace** with fitness so that it stays the same subjective difficulty. "We get bigger, faster, and stronger and are THEN able to lift more weight." Load is *matched* to performance potential using RPE and RIR, rather than pushed ahead of it.

**Periodization.** Run a mesocycle (4–8 weeks) while it works; change variables when progress stalls or the lifter goes stale. "This is also not an exact science."

**Exercise selection.** SAID and transference: the more alike two movements, the more carryover. Variation should be high for untrained lifters (motor learning) and **reduced for 2–4 weeks near a competition or test**. Adherence improves when lifters choose their own exercises within the constraints of the goal — which is also the argument for their most customisable templates being their most advanced.

## Injury risk

Rates per 1,000 participation hours, as tabulated: bodybuilding **0.24–1.0**, powerlifting **1–4.4**, weightlifting **2.9–3.3**, CrossFit **2.3–3.1**, strongman **4.5–6.1**, Highland Games **7.5**. Comparators: walking and cycling **0.19–1.2 or 0.5–2** depending on method, track and field **~3.6**. Severity: the average powerlifting injury's symptoms last **12 days**, and resistance training injuries generally resolve inside two weeks.

They also make a methodological point worth keeping — that injury rates should be expressed **per participation hour** rather than per athlete or per season, because exposure differs. That is the correct convention and most gym discourse ignores it.

**This is their house position and it is well supported by the epidemiological literature they list.** It is prevalence and incidence data from surveys and cross-sectional studies, not prospective trials, so it describes what happens in these sports rather than proving that lifting is protective.

## Strength and size are not the same adaptation

The article states that larger fibres produce more force, then immediately qualifies: increases in size are "unlikely to be the sole *cause*" of increases in strength, "rather it's one of many adaptations the muscle *can* use to increase force production." The reading list includes Loenneke's argument that exercise-induced changes in muscle size do not contribute to exercise-induced changes in strength — a heterodox position they present in its softened form. They also warn that low transference means you can gain size without the strength you wanted, or strength without size. **This is one of the more careful passages in the article** and it is the reason "powerbuilding" is offered as a separate track rather than assumed to fall out of powerlifting.

## Weight management, usefully deflated

Resistance training reduces waist circumference and abdominal fat; regular exercise appears to improve satiety awareness. Then the deflation, which is the valuable part:

- **EPOC** rises with volume, intensity and muscle mass involved, and is higher in untrained than trained people — but whether it meaningfully raises total daily energy expenditure "hasn't been thoroughly studied", and the available research suggests it contributes **very little**.
- **The metabolism myth:** a kilogram of muscle burns roughly **13 kcal/day**, a kilogram of fat about **4.5 kcal/day**. Gaining **10 kg of muscle** — years of work — raises resting metabolic rate by about **130 kcal/day**. "A rather modest difference."

Both points cut against claims their own industry makes routinely.

## Health claims, and two overreaches

Supported and appropriately hedged: regular exercise lowers risk of type 2 diabetes and some cancers; resistance training improves bone mineral density in postmenopausal women with osteopenia and osteoporosis (the LIFTMOR RCT is in the reading list); it produces clinically significant improvements in strength, function and pain in knee osteoarthritis; resistance training reduces anxiety and depressive symptoms in meta-analyses of RCTs, and the benefit appears not to depend on getting stronger, "through some not-yet-understood mechanism."

**Two claims outrun the cited work:**
1. "Starting to lift earlier not only lessens the strength loss caused by osteoarthritis but also **delays and even reverses the course of the condition**." The reading list's support here is a systematic review of resistance training in musculoskeletal rehabilitation. Reversing structural disease progression is a much stronger claim than improving symptoms and function, and it is not established.
2. "**Stronger persons have greater health benefits than lifters with minimal strength gains.**" This is observational; strength is a marker of a great deal else, and the article does not say so.

One transparency note: the UpToDate chapter cited for the general health benefits is authored by **Sullivan, Feigenbaum and Baraki** — Barbell Medicine's own principals citing themselves in a reference list.

## What they sell
The entire article. Five product pages, five "GET PROGRAM" buttons, a sample PDF, and a closing section headed "Why Train With Barbell Medicine?" advertising bespoke coaching. The programming theory in the middle is substantive and largely stands on its own, but the article's answer to "what are the best intermediate powerlifting programs?" is, in every case, one of theirs.
