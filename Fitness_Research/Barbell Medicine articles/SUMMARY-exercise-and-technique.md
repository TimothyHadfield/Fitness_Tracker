# Exercise selection, technique and equipment

Forty notes on which exercise to pick, how to perform it, and whether the equipment does anything.
They are organised below by the decision you are making, not by article, because a large fraction of
these articles are the same article wearing different equipment. Read the first section before
anything else: it says how little of this corpus is backed by measured muscle growth, and the rest
of the file assumes you know that.

---

## Start here: three proven claims, and everything else

**Across all forty of these articles, exactly three exercise-selection claims are backed by measured
hypertrophy.**

1. **Bench angle does not matter.** [Chaves 2020](bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md)
   trained untrained young men for **8 weeks** on flat only, incline only, or both, and found **no
   meaningful difference in pectoralis major hypertrophy or pressing strength**. This is a null, and
   it is the only bench-angle outcome trial anywhere in the corpus. It appears in four articles and
   is used correctly in all of them.
2. **The seated hamstring curl beats the lying and standing versions.**
   [Maeo 2021](best-glute-hamstring-exercises.md) — **12 weeks, MRI-measured muscle volume**, long
   versus short hamstring lengths — found greater hamstring growth training at long muscle lengths.
   Note what that is evidence *for*: muscle length during the movement, not the seated machine as an
   object.
3. **Overhead triceps extension beats the pushdown.**
   [Maeo 2022](best-triceps-exercises-cable.md) measured triceps brachii hypertrophy directly and
   found it **"substantially greater"** after elbow extension trained in the overhead
   (shoulder-flexed) position than in the neutral arm position.

That is the list. Every other ranking in these forty articles — every tier list, every "targets the
upper pecs", every ordering of six rear delt exercises — rests on **surface EMG, moment arms, bare
anatomy, or nothing at all**.

What makes this damning rather than merely disappointing is that Barbell Medicine *know*. They cite
[Vigotsky 2017/2018](weightlifting-belt-they-work.md) — the papers arguing that greater
electromyographic responses do not imply greater motor unit recruitment and that hypertrophic
potential cannot be inferred from EMG amplitude — in at least six articles, and then rank exercises
on EMG a few paragraphs later in the same document. The instances are catalogued in **Weak points**
at the foot of this file.

**Practical consequence.** When someone asks "which exercise is best for X", the honest answer from
this source is the one it gives itself in
[10 best chest exercises](best-chest-exercises-muscle-strength.md): *most exercises in a category
produce similar results provided they work the muscle through a relatively large range of motion,
using a load that gets you within 3 to 4 repetitions of failure within roughly 3 to 20 reps.* That
sentence is a claim about load, effort and range, not about exercise identity, and it is the single
most transferable line in the corpus.

## The equivalence results — the genuinely useful findings

Where an outcome trial exists here, the result is almost always **equivalence**, not a ranking.
Collected in one place, because these are the findings worth carrying:

- **Push-ups match the bench press.** [Kikuchi & Nakazato 2017](best-chest-exercises-muscle-strength.md):
  18 men with at least a year of training, split 9/9, bench at **40% of 1RM** or kneeling push-ups,
  3 sets to failure twice weekly. Both gained about **3 mm of muscle thickness** at pecs and triceps,
  no significant difference. [Kotarsky 2018](best-bodyweight-chest-workout-without-equipment.md): 4
  weeks, 3x/week, progressive calisthenic push-ups versus bench press, similar gains in **bench 1RM
  and chest thickness**. Both are small and short — but this is why
  [ten push-up variations](best-bodyweight-chest-workout-without-equipment.md) is the only
  bodyweight-chest article with real data under its headline.
- **Machines match free weights.** [Hernández-Belmonte 2023](best-bench-press-alternatives-build-chest.md)
  randomised free-weight against machine-based training and found **strength and hypertrophy
  comparable**, with the crucial caveat that **strength gains are specific to the mode trained**.
  The same finding is reported in [10 best chest exercises](best-chest-exercises-muscle-strength.md).
- **Cable and barbell preacher curls grow the biceps equally.**
  [Nunes 2020](best-chest-biceps-workout.md) randomised two opposite torque-emphasis profiles and
  found **hypertrophy essentially equal**, with a strength advantage to the cable version only when
  tested at long muscle lengths. Also cited in
  [the arms and shoulders day](arms-shoulder-workout-bigger-biceps-shoulders.md), where it is the
  *only* training-outcome study behind any exercise choice.
- **Hip thrust and back squat produce similar gluteal hypertrophy.**
  [Plotkin 2023](best-leg-day-workout-routine-exercises.md) — and similar transfer to the deadlift.
  It was a **bioRxiv preprint** when cited and neither article flags that. It is also filed against
  the wrong claim in [the glute and hamstring article](best-glute-hamstring-exercises.md), where it
  sits under back extensions rather than under the hip-thrust paragraph it actually bears on.
- **Bodyweight and barbell squats raise quad strength similarly.** Wei 2023, in sedentary young
  women, measured by max-effort leg extension
  ([quad exercises](best-quad-exercises-workouts-massive-thighs.md)).
- **Exercise order barely matters for size.** [Brandão 2020](best-triceps-exercises-cable.md)
  randomised **43 men over 10 weeks** to bench-then-triceps or triceps-then-bench and found similar
  1RM bench improvement either way. [Nunes 2021's meta-analysis](best-chest-biceps-workout.md): the
  exercise done earlier gains more *strength*; for hypertrophy there is no clear signal.
- **Frequency is a scheduling variable.** [Grgic 2018](best-chest-shoulder-workouts.md) pooled 22
  studies and found the apparent frequency dose-response for strength **vanished once weekly volume
  was equated**; Thomas & Burns 2016 and [Lambrianides 2022](best-leg-day-workout-routine-exercises.md)
  found 1x/week and 3x/week produced the same hypertrophy and strength at matched volume.
- **Supersets save time without costing adaptations.** [Weakley 2020 and Burke's "Less time, same
  gains"](exercises-chest-back-workout-day.md) support pairing chest with back as agonist-antagonist
  supersets. This is the only evidenced claim in that entire article, and it is about session
  structure rather than exercise choice.
- **Single-joint work is not inferior.** [Gentil 2015](best-rear-delt-exercises-workouts.md)
  concludes the choice should turn on preference, equipment and time; Brigatto 2020 found similar
  strength increases; Mannarino 2021 found **single-joint produced more elbow flexor hypertrophy**
  than multi-joint after eight weeks.

**How long until anything happens.** The best-sourced number in the corpus, appearing in three
articles: across **40 resistance-training studies that measured strength repeatedly rather than only
at the endpoints**, the first demonstrable strength gain took **4.3 weeks on average, range 1 to 12
weeks**, with squat 1RM improvements from **3% to 40%**
([Lambrianides 2022](best-pull-day-workout-routine-exercises.md)). And response is enormous in its
spread: [Ahtiainen 2016](bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) followed **287 adults on
the same 6-month programme** and saw muscle size change anywhere from **−11% to +30%**, with age and
sex explaining little.

## Two kinds of article, and how to tell them apart

The corpus splits cleanly, and knowing which kind you are quoting is the single most useful filter.

**Bylined clinician essays.** Feigenbaum on [lifting belts](weightlifting-belt-they-work.md),
[bar placement in the squat](bar-placement-squat-matter.md) and [the grip problem](grip-problem.md);
Ben Inglis on [range of motion](range-motion-considerations.md); Claire Zai on
[the squat](squat-correctly-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) and
[the bench press](bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md). These argue positions, quote
sources against their own interest, flag their own speculation, and mostly sell nothing. The belt
article separates what is shown from what is assumed better than anything else in this library. The
range-of-motion article refuses "how deep" as a single question. The bar-placement article is a
lecture on why its own evidence is inadequate.

**Unbylined "best X exercises" listicles**, mostly the 2023–24 cluster. These are search-optimised
content with a reference list bolted on. The tells are structural: reference lists that duplicate
entries wholesale (17 numbers resolving to 13 papers in [wrist wraps](wrist-wraps.md), 22 to 18 in
[chest hypertrophy](best-chest-exercises-hypertrophy.md), 12 to 6 in
[kettlebell chest](best-kettlebell-chest-workout-exercises.md), 6 to 4 in
[cable chest](best-cable-chest-workout-exercises.md), one protein review under four numbers in
[leg day](best-leg-day-workout-routine-exercises.md)); citations landing on the wrong papers; and
technique instructions no coach would write.

**Eight of them are, for practical purposes, one article.**
[Chest and shoulder workouts](best-chest-shoulder-workouts.md),
[chest with dumbbells](best-chest-workout-dumbbells.md),
[kettlebell chest](best-kettlebell-chest-workout-exercises.md),
[back and shoulder](back-shoulder-workout-exercises-v-shaped-body.md),
[pull-up alternatives](best-pull-alternatives.md), [chin-up alternatives](chin-alternatives.md),
[hack squat alternatives](hack-squat-alternatives-similar-results.md) and
[plyometrics](best-plyometric-exercises-speed-agility-power.md) recycle the same handful of EMG
papers behind different equipment framings, with the same anatomy tour, the same
primary/secondary/tertiary labels, and the same commercial close. Read one and you have read the set.

## Technique, lift by lift

### The squat

[Claire Zai's guide](squat-correctly-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) is craft rather than
evidence — 11 references, only six on a specific sentence, and none of them on the form advice.
Stance shoulder-width to start, **toes out 15–30°**, high bar versus low bar "similar… mostly up to
personal preference", grip on the power-bar score marks, elbows tucked down, weight over midfoot,
vertical bar path. All uncited.

**On depth she is unusually careful.** Aim below parallel (hip crease under the top of the knee) —
then: no unique health or hypertrophy benefit to going *well* below parallel; likely little
difference between parallel, just-above and just-below; strength gains are specific to the depth
trained; and **the real argument for fixing a depth is consistency of measurement** — otherwise you
cannot tell whether you got stronger or just shortened the movement. "Squatting above parallel is
certainly better than not squatting at all." Her five causes of a shallow squat: awareness, an
over-vertical torso, wrong stance width, knees caving, and simple lack of strength — with the useful
framing that if you can bodyweight-squat below parallel but not with a bar, the problem is load, not
mobility. On **knees past the toes** she says there is no evidence forward knee travel raises injury
risk versus other squat styles — an absence-of-evidence claim, probably true — and then converts it
to "a specific squat form is not correlated with injury or pain", **uncited, in an article that cites
eleven papers elsewhere**.

**Front versus back squat.** [Feigenbaum's analysis](bar-placement-squat-matter.md) of Yavuz 2015 —
**12 healthy men**, 1RM front and back squat, seven-muscle sEMG normalised to MVIC. **The only
statistically significant EMG difference was vastus medialis** (front **55.4%** vs back **48.8%** of
MVIC); the only kinematic difference was hip angle (front **95.9°** vs back **79.5°**). Erectors,
glute max and both hamstrings did not differ. Front squat 1RM averaged **85.0 ± 15.7 kg** against
**109.2 ± 25.5 kg** — near-identical excitation at roughly **28% less absolute load**, which is his
headline point: the number on the bar is not the stimulus. His conclusion is deliberately weak —
*the absence of a large activation difference makes a large outcome difference unlikely* — and that
is the correct form of the claim. Two caveats travel with it: the squats were **above parallel**
(depth set at a 90° knee angle), and the comparison was front to **high bar**, so the article's
low-bar framing is asserted, not measured.

**Bar-position mechanics.** [The 2012 moment-arm article](barbell-forces-levers-moments-torque.md)
gets the useful part right: a front squat lengthens the **distal femoral** moment arm and loads the
knees; a low-bar back squat forces a more horizontal torso and lengthens the **spinal and proximal
femoral** moment arms, loading the hips. Moment arms grow through the descent, which is why the
bottom is hardest. **Its central argument is then backwards** — see Weak points.

**Squat substitutes.** [Twelve hack squat alternatives](hack-squat-alternatives-similar-results.md)
works as a substitution map by constraint, not a ranking: no rack or spotter → leg press, pendulum,
belt squat, landmine squat; free weights only → Hatfield, goblet, split squat variants; **lower back
currently unhappy → belt squat, leg press, goblet squat**. The belt-squat recommendation (same
lower-body training, less spinal loading, better tolerated returning from injury) is the most useful
thing in it and carries **no citation at all**. Coaching notes: **~2 s per phase** on split squats to
make the balance demand manageable, and a **10–12"** rear-foot surface for Bulgarians rather than a
standard 14–18" bench.

### The bench press

[Claire Zai's bench guide](bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) is the best technique
writing in the corpus, largely because two of its decisions come with a **self-test** rather than a
cue.

**Grip width** is defined against the bar: knurling begins **16 in (40.64 cm)** apart, score marks
sit **31 in (78.74 cm)** apart — the powerlifting maximum. Index fingers at the knurling is a close
grip; on the marks is wide. Their starting point is **pinkies on the marks**. The test: **at the
bottom the forearms should be vertical from the side and from the front**. Angled back toward the
head means the touch point is too high for that grip; toward the feet, too low. Angled inward means
too much elbow tuck; outward, not enough.

**Bar in the hand:** high in the palm across the thenar and hypothenar eminences so the wrist stacks
over the radius (the "bulldog" grip); a thumbless grip is discouraged. From the side the bar should
look stacked over the forearm, not hanging behind the wrist. **Bar path:** down and slightly forward
on the descent, **up and back** on the ascent, finishing over the shoulder joint — at the bottom the
bar sits a few inches in front of the shoulder regardless of grip, so a straight vertical press keeps
it forward of the shoulder longer than necessary and adds shoulder torque. **The arch** is four
components: thoracic extension, scapular retraction and depression, lumbar extension, and a Valsalva
hold. **Leg drive** is cued as pushing the feet *backward along the floor* toward the rack, not
straight down, peaking as the bar leaves the chest, and is distinguished from hip thrusting.
**Spotting:** the spotter's job is the lift-off and the clear miss — "just enough" assistance is not
quantifiable and therefore not a reliable stimulus, so if reps are being ground out with help, drop
the load.

Their position that **arching is not dangerous** is argued from mechanism only (compressive spinal
load while supine is low, so the standing-lift intuition does not transfer), with no evidence cited
on either side. The modifications offered — plates or small boxes under the feet, or the feet-up
bench press — are sensible load modification for anyone who does not tolerate repeated lumbar
extension at volume.

The short [bench press for hypertrophy](bench-press-hypertrophy-technique-programming.md) page adds
exactly one thing: the **programming role**. Use the bench as the trackable heavy movement, then buy
additional chest volume more cheaply with machine presses and flyes. It cites nothing and deflates
grip width and bar path as unlikely to change hypertrophy outcomes; its own recommendation is a
**moderate grip, roughly the width that leaves the forearms vertical at the bottom**.

**Substitutes.** [Seven bench press alternatives](best-bench-press-alternatives-build-chest.md) is
organised on **strength specificity, not growth**: pick the substitute closest to the lift you want
to improve. A **~15° incline transfers better to flat bench, 45–70° better to the overhead press**.
Close-grip bench, floor press, incline, overhead press, machine chest press, dip, push-up — all at
2–4 sets of 4–8 at RPE 6–8. It refuses the "incline builds upper pecs" claim, calls the dip
lean-angle distinction speculative, and sells nothing.
[The chest-and-triceps article](chest-triceps-workout-routine-exercises.md) holds the corpus's only
cited close-grip claim (Larsen 2021, sticking-region biomechanics at 1RM): it supports *"the triceps
do more of the work with a narrow grip"*, not *"the close-grip bench builds bigger triceps"*, and the
article words it carefully enough to stay honest.

### The deadlift and the grip

[The Grip Problem](grip-problem.md) is 4,600 words with **two citations, both on one sentence**.
**The algorithm:** double overhand for as long as it holds, then hook grip and actually train it,
then alternate grip if hook is not viable for your hands or bar; **chalk always**; straps instead of
alternate grip if you will never compete. Switch to your competition grip **no later than your last
warm-up set**.

**Grip is limited by friction**, not just squeeze — set by hand tension, knurl pitch and tooth depth,
bar diameter, chalk, and hand-on-hand contact in the hook and alternate grips. Feigenbaum's own
numbers in one lifter: **220 kg double overhand** on a stiff 29 mm bar, **265 kg hook** on the same
bar, **280 kg hook** on a 27 mm whippy bar, **320 kg alternate** on a 28.5 mm Texas Power Bar,
**330 kg alternate** on the 27 mm bar — roughly **110 kg of spread from grip and bar choice alone**.
Dry skin on steel measures **0.5–0.75** coefficient of friction, the highest of any body region
tested; he could find no experimental data for athletic tape and says so.

**The clinical core is the alternate grip's asymmetry.** In strength sports biceps ruptures occur
**distally at the radial tuberosity**; in the general population **90–97% of biceps tears are
proximal long-head tears**. The mechanism is rapid tension onset on a supinated arm with a slightly
flexed elbow. His mitigation is coaching, not avoidance: get tight, pull with a fully extended elbow,
do not jerk the bar. Supination also produces slight humeral external rotation, altering the lat's
line of pull, which plausibly explains **"windmilling"** — the bar drifting out on the supinated
side. He labels the follow-on speculation (that lumbar tweaks cluster on that side) as speculation.

**Sumo versus conventional**, from [leg day](best-leg-day-workout-routine-exercises.md): wider
stance, grip inside the legs, more vertical torso, "may load the back more comfortably for some",
more hip mobility required. Train both; powerlifters use whichever they are stronger at;
weightlifting, CrossFit and strongman default to conventional on specificity grounds.

### The overhead press

[Press Like A Pro](press-like-pro-classic-press.md) **cites nothing** and is a first-person account
of one lifter taking his competition press from **118 kg to 125 kg in six months** at 1 kg lighter
bodyweight. The "Classic Press" is a rack-position change, not a new lift: bar racked low **on the
chest** rather than floating under the chin, grip about one finger wider, hips deliberately pushed
forward into a layback, then a fast stand-up into the press. The stated drawback is real — the bar
can be thrown up and back quickly, so balance is easy to lose.

**The author's own verdict is the interesting part**: technique plus programming at roughly **95% of
the improvement**, with whatever the assistance lifts contributed attributed to the extra pressing
frequency and volume they allowed rather than to any unique property. He ran **six pressing slots per
week** across four days, each built on a **top single at RPE 8** plus back-off sets of 3–5. His core
claim: **improving a 1RM requires regular exposure to singles** — the failure mode is a lifter whose
5RM has climbed but whose 1RM has not, because displaying strength as a single is itself untrained.
His triage: if you are not already pressing more than twice a week, **change programming before
technique**.

### Rows and pulls

There is no real technique content here, only selection. [Ten pull-up substitutes](best-pull-alternatives.md)
and [ten chin-up substitutes](chin-alternatives.md) are near-identical lists of the same eight rows
plus a lat pulldown and a dumbbell pullover, with **two citations between them** — a practitioner
column on the lat pulldown, and Fenwick 2009, a **trunk-muscle and spine-loading study**, cited for a
list of back prime movers it was not designed to adjudicate.

What is useful in both is **difficulty scaling**: the ring row and inverted row are continuously
adjustable by body angle and anchor height (rings at hip height to start, ~70° to the floor is easy,
closer to horizontal is harder); the lat pulldown by moving a pin; the **lever row** — a one-arm
barbell row gripping near the collar — solves the problem of running out of dumbbell. The chin-up
article opens by naming what is *not* on the list and is usually the right answer: for a first
chin-up or more reps, use **band-assisted chins or an assisted machine**, because those train the
actual pattern. Both articles' real recommendation is **additive, not substitutive** — pair a
vertical pull with a horizontal pull — and beyond **10–12 consecutive chin-ups**, load the movement
rather than adding reps. Buried in the chin-up article is the line that undercuts the genre: *"it's
difficult to predict that a different grip or ROM would produce a different result in regional muscle
growth… the choice of a specific variation should mainly reflect the individual's preferences."*

The **lat pulldown grip-width literature contradicts itself**, and
[the rear delt article](best-rear-delt-exercises-workouts.md) lays it out honestly: Lusk 2010 found
**similar** activation of lats, mid-traps and biceps between narrow and wide; Signorile 2002 found
**higher** lat activity wide at 10RM; Andersen 2014 found **no major differences** across medium,
narrow and wide at 6RM — and the 6RM load itself was **lower** with the wide grip. Pick by
preference. Note that [the pull day article](best-pull-day-workout-routine-exercises.md) cites that
same Andersen paper for "small differences in **muscle development**", which it does not measure.

## Range of motion and depth

[Ben Inglis 2022](range-motion-considerations.md) is the best article in this domain and the only one
that refuses "how deep?" as a single question, splitting it into strength, hypertrophy, pain and
health.

**Strength is joint-angle specific**, so the answer depends on the test. Bloomquist 2013: deep
squatting improved both shallow and deep squat performance, shallow squatting improved only shallow.
Pallarés 2019: full squats produced greater gains in **full, parallel and half** squat 1RM than
either partial. Hartmann 2012: quarter squats gave no significant vertical jump improvement while
deep back and front squats did. Against that, Rhea 2016: **quarter squats produced greater
improvements in maximal speed and jumping power** in highly trained athletes. Inglis does not declare
a winner — he attacks *why* Pallarés and Rhea disagree, noting both used low-velocity,
high-proximity-to-failure loading badly suited to developing power (the relevant load for lower-body
power output is around **30–70% 1RM**), and that Rhea's athletes squatted **1.39× bodyweight**
against Pallarés's **1.14×**.

**For hypertrophy, larger ROM is the default** and the trials point one way: Pinto 2012 (biceps,
0–130° beat 50–100°), McMahon 2014 (quads), Bloomquist 2013 (0–120° beat 0–60°). The detail most
people skip is **Kubo 2019**: full-depth squatting produced significantly more **adductor and glute
maximus** volume, but quadriceps growth was statistically indistinguishable — **4.9 ± 2.6% vs
4.6 ± 3.1%**. That depth advantage was a hip advantage, not a quad one.

**On lengthened partials he lands where the library has since settled.** Writing in 2022, before the
trained-subject trials, he concludes partials at long muscle lengths *"may produce similar results
compared to full ROM"* and advises against using them for a significant portion of training given the
thin evidence — *equal, not better*, reached three years early. His supporting evidence: Oranchuk
2018 (long-length isometrics grew muscle at **1.16%/week** above 70° versus **0.47%/week** at ≤60°),
Pedrosa 2021 (long-length partial leg extensions beat both full ROM and short-length partials), and
Goto 2019 (**48.7 ± 14.5%** vs **28.2 ± 10.9%** for mid-range partial triceps extensions) — the last
passed on uncritically despite a proposed mechanism, sustained intramuscular hypoxia, that does not
belong in a muscle-length argument.

**For pain**, partial ROM is a **temporary modification to keep training, not a training method**:
the lifter whose floor deadlift hurts but whose block pull from below the knee does not starts there,
lowers the blocks over time, and works back toward the full range. **For health he assumes the null
and says so** — no trial has compared ranges of motion on longitudinal health outcomes, so he
declines to claim one. That is the most disciplined paragraph in the corpus.

## Equipment

### Lifting belts — the good article

[What lifting belts do](weightlifting-belt-they-work.md) pulled **every** study comparing belted to
unbelted — **36 in total, 20 occupational and 16 in the gym** — and sorted them properly. That
accounting, not any one finding, is what makes it the best equipment article here.

**Shown.** Belts raise intra-abdominal pressure in **5 of 7** lifting studies (the two nulls were
isometric tasks): Harman 1989, **175 mmHg belted vs 156 beltless** at 90% 1RM deadlift; Lander 1992,
**25–40% higher** at an 8RM squat. The effect is **additive with a Valsalva** (baseline for scale is
roughly **20 mmHg standing, ~65 mmHg during a Valsalva**). Belts do **not** meaningfully alter trunk
sEMG — McGill's participants never exceeded **15% of an isometric MVC** either way, and Escamilla
2002 found obliques at **62% MVC beltless vs 53% belted** with rectus abdominis at **63% belted vs
56% beltless**, a swap rather than a suppression. Belts do **not** decondition the trunk:
construction workers wearing lumbar belts for two to three months got **stronger** on direct trunk
strength and endurance testing.

**Failed.** Belts do not reduce low-back injury rates, lost work days or compensation claims across
large occupational trials (Wassell 2000 in *JAMA*; Martimo 2008 in *BMJ*; Reddell 1992; Mitchell 1994
across 1,000+ employees). About **90% of belt-wearing gym-goers say they use one to prevent injury** —
that is the belief the data do not carry. Feigenbaum then quotes NIOSH against his own interest: the
studies "cannot be used to either support or refute" the effect, which is *insufficient evidence*, a
weaker claim than his own preceding sentence. All of it is occupational, and the transfer to barbell
lifting is asserted rather than argued.

**Assumed and never measured.** Trunk stiffness, the proposed mediator, is not measured in any cited
study, and he concedes outright: **"there are no studies looking directly at whether or not people
can lift more weight with a belt."** The performance case is proxies — **15.5% faster concentric** at
90% 1RM squat, faster final reps of an 8RM, **RPE two points lower** on an 80% 1RM deadlift
(observational and unblindable) — plus one null. He names **placebo** as part of the mechanism.

**Buying and using (advice, not evidence).** Leather between two suede layers; plastic or
artificial-leather cores never break in. **4 inches wide** (IPF maximum 10 cm; IWF allows 12 cm),
**10 mm thick for general strength training, 13 mm for powerlifting, 6.5 mm full leather for
weightlifting**. Size so your measured waist falls mid-range for that manufacturer; hole spacing
**1 inch centre-to-centre**, wider spacing signalling weaker material. Closure type is preference.
Usage: belt the heaviest weekly exposure of your priority lifts from the last warm-up onward, go
beltless otherwise to manage absolute-load fatigue — he calls that "just a theory" and it is. Someone
**with** back pain may reasonably use a belt more liberally, not because it treats pain but because
staying active matters more than the belt does. **Disclosed conflict:** Pioneer, one of three brands
recommended, sponsors the Barbell Medicine podcast, and he says so.

### Wrist wraps

[The wrist wrap article](wrist-wraps.md) is the same shape, thinner. **No reliable change in grip
strength** across four studies, whatever the material. **No 1RM change** in the only head-to-head:
**9 men and 9 women**, bench 1RM with no wrap, flexible wrap and stiff wrap, all null. A survey of
**70 competitive powerlifters** found **71% wrap for squats, 89% for bench**, with **no correlation
between wrap use and strength**. What *does* show up consistently is **subjective stability**, plus a
randomised trial finding external supports improved **wrist joint position sense**. **No study has
tested wrist wraps against injury rates in any barbell sport**; their conclusion — wraps for people
who **already have** wrist pain, not as prophylaxis — rests on an adjacent RCT of carpal-stabilising
**taping**, and they say so. The "wraps weaken your wrists" myth is killed on reasoning rather than
evidence: the tissues are still loaded, and no data show wrap users have weaker grips.

Crucially the article **separates wraps from straps**: the two ergogenic findings in its own list —
more weight moved, more reps when grip is the limiter — are about **straps**, and it does not smuggle
them across. Picks: a **medium 60 cm flexible wrap** as the do-it-all; stiff wraps for competitive
powerlifters; an **18" Rogue** for CrossFit/weightlifting; a small flexible wrap for managing pain.
Put them on near the end of warm-ups. Their own verdict: **accessories are a relatively unimportant
part of training**.

### Everything else

**Knee sleeves, shoes and chalk get no treatment at all.**
[The squat guide](squat-correctly-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) lists shoes, wrist wraps,
belt, chalk and sleeves in one uncited paragraph and says **none of it is essential**. That is the
whole of the corpus on those items. **Straps** appear twice as coaching advice:
[chin-up alternatives](chin-alternatives.md) recommends them for T-bar and chest-supported rows so
grip fatigue does not cap the working set, while [the grip article](grip-problem.md) allows them for
anyone who will never compete.

**Cables.** [Five cable chest exercises](best-cable-chest-workout-exercises.md) makes two honest
practical arguments — **angle freedom** and **easier isolation** (a fixed path removes stabilisation
effort) — and one claim to discard: *"for some, this may produce greater muscle hypertrophy than free
weights"*, cited to an EMG study that **did not include free weights and did not measure
hypertrophy**. Better lines from it: seated versus standing cable press "will mostly be similar, so
choose on personal preference", and feeling a chest fly in the delts is normal, not a fault.
On **attachments**, [the cable triceps article](best-triceps-exercises-cable.md) says outright that
**no attachment is demonstrably superior** — use what you prefer. (Its supporting references there
are ergonomics studies of **screwdriver torque and cylindrical handle diameter**.)

**Kettlebells.** [The kettlebell chest article](best-kettlebell-chest-workout-exercises.md) undercuts
its own premise: **"when it comes to hypertrophy, kettlebells don't really provide any advantages
that other free weights don't."** What is genuinely useful is handling — the centre of mass hangs
**below** the hand, so load the web space between thumb and index finger rather than the middle of
the palm (palm loading forces the wrist into extension when the bell rotates over, the "broken wrist
grip"), and grip lightly while the bell is moving. Plus the right answer to fixed-weight jumps:
**double progression** — add reps within a range at a fixed weight, then move up a bell and reset.

**Dumbbells versus barbells.** Saeterbakken 2011, in
[chest and shoulder workouts](best-chest-shoulder-workouts.md), found **dumbbell 1RM roughly 17%
lower than barbell** on a chest press, correctly attributed to the extra degrees of freedom — and the
same article adds that both "likely produce similar results in terms of muscle growth."
[The push day article](best-push-day-workout-routine-exercises-muscle.md) says swapping dumbbell for
barbell or cable for dumbbell "won't provide variety in how the muscles are targeted and will mostly
yield similar results" — then two paragraphs later cites Farias 2017 (EMG) to distinguish barbell
from dumbbell bench. The first claim is the better-supported one.

## The ranking articles, muscle by muscle

### Chest — nine articles, one real finding

Of the nine chest articles, the only outcome comparisons available anywhere are **angle-vs-angle
(null)**, **push-up-vs-bench (null)** and **machine-vs-free-weight (equivalent, with mode-specific
strength transfer)**.

[Ten chest exercises for muscle and strength](best-chest-exercises-muscle-strength.md) is the best of
them and is honest in its second sentence that the "10 best" is not a ranking. It concedes that
claims of differential regional growth are *"either fabricated or speculation based on mechanistic
data"* — the exact criticism this library levels at the genre, made by the source itself.
[Best chest exercises for hypertrophy](best-chest-exercises-hypertrophy.md) states the thesis that
there is **no best chest exercise** and then ranks eight anyway, only two with any citation on the
selection rationale; its intensity section is worth keeping — above roughly **60–70% 1RM** beats
lower loads for strength, and **for hypertrophy the usable range is roughly 30–90% 1RM provided sets
go close to failure**.

[Seven dumbbell chest exercises](best-chest-workout-dumbbells.md) contributes the two best caveats in
the cluster. On bench angle: **"sEMG differences using different bench angles don't reliably predict
actual outcomes."** And on prescriptions — it quotes the 2018 *Physical Activity Guidelines for
Americans* (2×/week, 1–3 sets of 8–12, within 1 rep of failure) and dismantles it: the 2–3 set figure
**"is actually not backed by any experimental evidence"**, and the 1-rep-from-failure instruction is
**"completely made up."** It then cites velocity-loss work indicating similar strength and
hypertrophy at **4–5 reps short of failure**.
[Five exercises for a chest and back day](exercises-chest-back-workout-day.md) has no evidence for
its five picks and real evidence for its **supersets**, plus two restrained passages: dip torso angle
is unclear, and on the mind–muscle connection, *"the muscles are being active whether you're
consciously trying to squeeze them or not… most of these claims are mostly speculative"* — noticeably
more conservative than the internal-focus position elsewhere in this library.

**Regional chest claims are the worst material in the corpus.** No article here measures a region of
the pectoralis major, before or after training.

- [Upper chest](best-upper-chest-exercises-bigger-pecs.md) states the correct caveat twice in prose
  while its **title, structure, exercise descriptions and conclusion assume the opposite throughout**.
  Three of its six exercises carry no citation at all; the rest carry acute EMG or kinetics —
  including Ebben 2011, a **ground-reaction-force** study stretched into a claim that the decline
  push-up "allows most of the mechanical load to be put on the upper pectorals."
- [Lower chest](best-lower-chest-workout-exercises-massive-pecs.md) cites Vigotsky's paper **by its
  own title** — *hypertrophic potential cannot be inferred* — and then ranks ten exercises on EMG.
  Its only genuine regional-growth citations, **Ema 2013 and Earp 2015, are about the quadriceps**:
  that makes regional growth possible in principle, not evidence that a decline press grows the lower
  pec. Four of its ten exercises have no supporting study of any kind, including the "jackhammer
  pushdown" claimed to give "one of the best possible angles to isolate your lower chest."
- [Inner chest](best-inner-chest-exercises-workouts.md) says in its first paragraph that the inner
  chest **is not a separate muscle** and isolating it is unlikely — then sells a high-to-low cable
  fly for "the lower portion" and a hex press for "squeezing the muscles together". Its saving grace
  is that the answer does not change either way: *"whether differences in regional hypertrophy from
  different exercises are possible or not doesn't really change our recommendations."*

### Shoulders, rear delts and traps

[Six shoulder exercises](best-shoulder-exercises.md) — overhead press, high-incline dumbbell bench,
upright row, one-arm cable lateral raise, bent-over rear delt fly, face pull — rests **entirely on
EMG or anatomy**: at least eight distinct EMG papers and no growth comparison. It does refuse a claim
it could easily have sold: *"there is no data to support a specific 'push to pull' ratio or to
confirm that face pulls are uniquely beneficial."*

[Six rear delt exercises](best-rear-delt-exercises-workouts.md) is the clearest case in the corpus.
It states, better than any other article here, that EMG differences *"shouldn't be taken as gospel"*
and that existing data *"doesn't show that the differences are predictive of actual outcomes"* — then
ranks six exercises with **not one posterior-deltoid growth study in its list**. Half the list is
back training that happens to involve the rear delts. The two with the strongest activation case are
the **reverse pec deck** and the **bent-over dumbbell fly**: Botton 2013 and Campos 2020 both found
the reverse pec deck produced the highest posterior deltoid activity of the exercises tested. That is
an acute measurement, and it is the strongest statement the evidence permits. One usable technique
detail (Schoenfeld 2013): during horizontal abduction a **neutral grip** produced higher posterior
deltoid and infraspinatus EMG.

[Five trap exercises](best-trap-exercises-workouts-bigger-back.md) concedes its own basis in one
sentence: **"there is little data on what exercises cause the greatest improvements in hypertrophy of
the trapezius."** The deadlift stays the primary movement even though the article admits **shrugs
produce higher trapezius excitation**, and it then claims "solid growth" from the deadlift on the
grounds that the traps work **isometrically** to transmit force — not an established hypertrophy
stimulus. Its fatigue reasoning is good: face pulls earn their place partly because light loads over
a small range across little muscle mass cost almost no systemic fatigue.

**Upright rows and impingement** is the clinical content in both the shoulder and trap articles. The
classical model was mechanical — rotator cuff or subacromial bursa irritated between acromion and
humeral head, with the upright row's internally rotated, elevated arm singled out. Their response is
the current physiotherapy position: **"impingement" now denotes a spectrum of clinical findings
involving shoulder pain and dysfunction, not damage to an identified structure.** If the label names
a symptom cluster, "this exercise causes impingement" is a category error. Practical conclusion: load
management rather than avoidance — start conservatively, progress over time. **The reframing is well
founded; the slide to "not uniquely injurious in practice" is an injury-incidence claim with no
injury data behind it.**

[The back and shoulder day](back-shoulder-workout-exercises-v-shaped-body.md) is the purest case of
selection by anatomy: of fourteen references, three sit near the exercise selection, and the stated
method is to pick movements where the back muscles are prime movers and the shoulder muscles are
synergists. Its own caveat is the most valuable sentence in it: *"It's not clear that the anatomy
related here translates perfectly, as there is a lot of interindividual variation in training
response."* Its grip line is worth keeping too — pull-up and chin-up grip variations "will load the
muscles slightly differently but likely impart a similar overall training effect."

### Arms

[The arms and shoulders day](arms-shoulder-workout-bigger-biceps-shoulders.md): 8,349 words, 25
distinct references, and **only seven of them touch exercise selection at all**. Four of the seven
prescribed exercises carry no citation whatsoever, and neither do the sets, reps, RPE targets, order,
progression or timeline. Its best-cited item is the **close-grip incline bench press** (three acute
EMG studies), and it correctly calls the hypertrophy implication "mostly speculative".
**It gets the preacher curl backwards**, claiming the bench "produces flexion in the shoulders, which
trains the biceps at a lengthened range of motion", better for the long head. **Shoulder flexion
shortens the biceps long head**; the lengthened position is shoulder *extension*, i.e. the incline
curl. The same error appears in [the pull day article](best-pull-day-workout-routine-exercises.md).

[Six cable triceps exercises](best-triceps-exercises-cable.md) holds the corpus's third proven claim.
Its organising idea is anatomically sound and untested as a prescription: the medial and lateral
heads arise from the humerus and do not cross the shoulder, while the long head originates on the
scapula and does — so shoulder position changes the long head's length while leaving the other heads
alone. **The one recommendation that survives on growth evidence: include at least one overhead
(shoulder-flexed) triceps exercise.** Everything downstream — cable versus dumbbell, rope versus bar,
one arm versus two — is activation, mechanism and preference.

[The chest and biceps day](best-chest-biceps-workout.md) contributes the only genuinely useful idea
in the pairing genre: chest and biceps are an **"alternate-peripheral" pair** whose exercises barely
overlap, so fatigue carryover is smaller than for chest+triceps or chest+shoulders — and they are
explicit that whether this produces *more growth* than another pairing is "up for debate". Its
concentration-curl ranking rests on a single **non-peer-reviewed American Council on Exercise PDF**.

### Legs

[Seven quad exercises](best-quad-exercises-workouts-massive-thighs.md) ranks nothing above anything
else on growth and says so repeatedly — on step-ups, "as with all surface EMG studies, it's not clear
that higher levels necessarily produce greater gains in strength or size." Its real content is the
**prescription**: 3–4 exercises, **6–10 sets/week for newer lifters, 12–20 for advanced**, compounds
before isolation. Its **leg extension defence** is the clinical highlight: the belief that open-chain
knee work is dangerous traces to mechanistic research around **ACL graft repair**, and current
evidence does not suggest leg extensions harm healthy knees. (The accompanying "numerous studies
illustrate the efficacy" is supported mainly by **one** matched follow-up study from 2000.) Its
within-exercise claims — leg press foot placement, split squat stride length, step-up torso angle —
are all EMG or motion capture, labelled as speculation more consistently than most sources here.

[Eight glute and hamstring exercises](best-glute-hamstring-exercises.md) tiers rather than ranks:
primary squat and deadlift, secondary hip thrust and RDL, tertiary step-up, split squat, hamstring
curl and hip extension. Pick 4–5 per week at RPE 7–8, 8–9 for curls. **Only the seated hamstring curl
is backed by measured growth**; five of the eight are justified with activation papers, in an article
whose own "How to Choose the Right Exercises" section states that EMG "doesn't correlate well with
strength and hypertrophy results."

[Leg day](best-leg-day-workout-routine-exercises.md) is the most useful leg article because what it
delivers is a **slot template**, not an exercise list: one primary squat and one primary hinge, one
secondary squat and hinge, one tertiary or isolation movement, calves anywhere. It is careful that a
deadlift is **not** a squat — concentric start, more horizontal torso, less knee flexion — hence one
squat *and* one hinge. It also kills the hormone argument for squats: yes, squats acutely raise
testosterone and growth hormone, so does essentially all exercise, and the magnitude does not
correlate with size or strength gains. The **safety squat bar** is positioned as an accommodation for
people whose shoulders or mobility will not tolerate a straight bar, sitting mechanically between a
high-bar squat and a front squat in torso angle.

### Plyometrics

[The plyometrics article](best-plyometric-exercises-speed-agility-power.md) argues against its own
topic, which is rare and creditable: plyometrics **"aren't absolutely necessary for most people"**,
and they are a **poor hypertrophy tool** — reduced eccentric loading, short time under tension. Where
outcome data appears it is at the level of the **modality, not the movement**: meta-analyses support
plyometric training improving vertical jump, sprint time and power; a 2023 meta-analysis in highly
trained youth soccer players supports **combined strength-plus-plyometric** training over either
alone; a systematic review of cluster-randomised trials supports plyometric-containing programmes
reducing **ACL injury incidence**. The eight exercise picks are backed by neither hypertrophy nor
activation data — two rest on studies **validating the movement as a test** (a rotational throw
predicting bat speed is evidence about a test, not a training effect), and the drop-jump section
prescribes sandy surfaces and drop heights above **50 cm** on a muscle-pre-activation rationale with
no jump or sprint outcome behind it.

## The programming defaults that recur

House conventions, not findings, but they appear in almost every listicle and are worth knowing.

- **Movement tiers.** Primary / secondary / tertiary — roughly heavy-barbell-high-skill /
  loadable-lower-cost / accessory-isolation. Compounds first, isolation last, larger muscles first.
- **The RPE gradient.** Proximity to failure scales *inversely* with how systemically taxing the
  movement is. From [leg day](best-leg-day-workout-routine-exercises.md): **RPE 6–8 on barbell squats
  and deadlifts, 7–8 on leg press and RDL, 8–9 on curls and calves, 9–10 on leg extensions.** The
  logic — never stated as a principle, never cited — is that a missed rep on a machine costs nothing
  while a missed barbell squat is a real event. It is the most transferable idea in the listicles.
- **Volume.** Typically **6–10 sets per muscle per week for newer lifters, 12–20 for advanced**;
  10–15 sets/week for chest; **6–12 direct triceps sets/week for beginners, 15–20+ for the
  experienced**. **Rest** at least two to three minutes between sets (two Grgic 2017 reviews).
- **"Progressive loading", not "progressive overload"**
  ([cable triceps](best-triceps-exercises-cable.md)). Training should not get progressively *harder*
  over a career; it should stay the same subjective difficulty while the load underneath rises. An
  RPE 8 at 40 is an RPE 8 at 20. Definitional position, not a finding — and the justification for the
  RPE-based prescriptions throughout.
- **The R.E.P. model of "good form"** ([chest and triceps](chest-triceps-workout-routine-exercises.md),
  [glutes and hamstrings](best-glute-hamstring-exercises.md)). They quote the only formal definition
  of proper technique in the literature (Colado 2009) and correctly call it **circular**: it defines
  correct technique by injury reduction while the premises about velocity, plane and range are
  unestablished. Their replacement: **Repeatable** (ROM, joint angles, velocity and tempo broadly
  similar rep to rep, with tolerance for real variability), **Efficient** (no force production that
  does not move the bar), and meeting the exercise's **Points of performance**. It explicitly
  declines to prescribe neutral spine or knees behind toes. **A reasoned position, not a demonstrated
  result** — nothing cited tests whether coaching it lowers injury rates.
- **Exercise choice by fatigue cost.** [How to choose exercises](choose-exercises-hypertrophy.md) —
  uncited throughout — replaces "which exercise is best?" with four screens: does it load the target
  muscle through a meaningful range; can you repeat it; **what does it cost in fatigue**; does it fit
  the rest of the programme. Machines are neutral, not inferior, and **switching exercises too often**
  is named as a mistake because it destroys your ability to track progress.
- **The sweet spot.** From [chest and triceps](chest-triceps-workout-routine-exercises.md): *"there
  is a fairly wide 'sweet spot' of training stimulus… it is not critically important to 'optimize'
  rates of adaptation, nor is there any way to ensure such 'optimization'."* Uncited, and the
  clearest statement of why this source disagrees with the optimisation framing of the video sources
  in this library. Note the matching inconsistency: that article prescribes **RPE 6–8 across the
  board** while [shoulders](best-shoulder-exercises.md) prescribes **RPE 8–10** for the same kind of
  isolation work, and neither argues for its choice.

## Weak points

**EMG-after-Vigotsky, instance by instance.** The belt article sets the standard: EMG cannot be used
to infer motor unit recruitment, and more excitation meaning more growth "remains yet to be proven"
([belts](weightlifting-belt-they-work.md)). Then:

- [Best chest exercises for hypertrophy](best-chest-exercises-hypertrophy.md) cites Vigotsky under
  the incline dumbbell press to say excitation differences do not predict outcomes — then, roughly
  6,000 characters later, justifies a **15-degree decline dumbbell press on Glass & Armstrong 1997**,
  a pure EMG study of bench angles. The two sections are irreconcilable as written.
- [10 best chest exercises](best-chest-exercises-muscle-strength.md) cites two **null** EMG studies
  plus Vigotsky, then asserts **uncited** that pec excitation rises as the bench steepens and
  lower-pec excitation rises as it declines — the regional rule a reader will actually carry away.
- [Best lower chest exercises](best-lower-chest-workout-exercises-massive-pecs.md) cites Vigotsky's
  paper by its own title and then ranks ten exercises on EMG.
- [Best upper chest exercises](best-upper-chest-exercises-bigger-pecs.md) states the caveat twice in
  prose while everything structural assumes the opposite.
- [Best glute and hamstring exercises](best-glute-hamstring-exercises.md) writes the caveat cleanly,
  then justifies five of eight entries with activation papers.
- [Best rear delt exercises](best-rear-delt-exercises-workouts.md) puts it in the clearest terms of
  any article here and ranks six exercises with **no posterior-deltoid growth study at all**.
- [Best shoulder exercises](best-shoulder-exercises.md) drops the caution entirely — same authors,
  eight EMG papers, no caveat anywhere.
- [Chest and shoulder workouts](best-chest-shoulder-workouts.md) claims "research suggests that the
  incline press allows for much more significant hypertrophy" with **no incline-versus-flat
  hypertrophy trial in its list**, then contradicts itself twelve paragraphs later by admitting the
  outcome question is "unclear".

[Feigenbaum's front-vs-back squat piece](bar-placement-squat-matter.md) is the honourable exception:
he frames sEMG as a **surrogate endpoint**, shows it failing in one direction (Jenkins 2015 found
higher EMG at 80% than 30% 1RM, yet Schoenfeld 2017's meta-analysis of 21 studies found equal
hypertrophy) and holding in another (rectus femoris EMG far higher in leg extension than leg press,
and the growth data agree), and concludes that **only large EMG differences are worth taking
seriously**.

**The horse.** [The chest and biceps article](best-chest-biceps-workout.md) supports a biceps
antagonist claim with **Watson & Wilson 2006, "Muscle architecture of biceps brachii, triceps brachii
and supraspinatus in the horse."** In the same list: a golf club-head-speed claim cited to
**Smereck's emergency-medicine case report of a young adult with acute chest pain after benching**,
and an incline-press "isolates the clavicular head" claim cited to **Kotarsky's push-up training
trial**.

**Duplicated reference entries** — the reliable tell for the SEO cluster. [Wrist wraps](wrist-wraps.md):
17 numbers → 13 papers. [Chest hypertrophy](best-chest-exercises-hypertrophy.md): 22 → 18.
[Kettlebell chest](best-kettlebell-chest-workout-exercises.md): 12 → 6.
[Cable chest](best-cable-chest-workout-exercises.md): 6 → 4 (Signorile 2017 three times).
[Upper chest](best-upper-chest-exercises-bigger-pecs.md): the same Rodríguez-Ridao EMG paper at 1, 5
and 9. [Arms and shoulders](arms-shoulder-workout-bigger-biceps-shoulders.md): 28 → 25.
[Hack squat alternatives](hack-squat-alternatives-similar-results.md): Swinton 2012 as both [3] and
[5], for a **goblet squat** claim in a paper that never studied a goblet squat.
[Dumbbell chest](best-chest-workout-dumbbells.md) is the worst: references **15, 16 and 17 are
verbatim duplicates of 3, 4 and 5**, cited for a resistance-training injury-rate claim none of them
addresses, while the paper that does (Aasa) sits at [8] attached to a proximity-to-failure claim it
has nothing to do with. [Plyometrics](best-plyometric-exercises-speed-agility-power.md) duplicates
two entries and drops one, so its numbering drifts from reference 33 onward.
[Leg day](best-leg-day-workout-routine-exercises.md) cites one protein review under **four
consecutive reference numbers**, making the evidence base look four times deeper than it is.
[The quad article](best-quad-exercises-workouts-massive-thighs.md) has entry 29 duplicating 28
verbatim and markers running past the end of the list. And [bar placement](bar-placement-squat-matter.md)
— the best-argued article in the batch — **does not list Yavuz 2015, the study the entire article
analyses**, along with three other inline citations, while listing Evans 2002 twice.

**Technique instructions no coach would write.**
[Lower chest](best-lower-chest-workout-exercises-massive-pecs.md) tells you to reverse a dumbbell fly
"when the dumbbells touch your chest" (in a fly they do not), to "sit on the edge of the decline
bench" for a fly, and gives the medicine ball push-up the pro tip "make sure you do an equal number
of reps with each hand on the ball", which does not describe the exercise as instructed.
[Chest and shoulder workouts](best-chest-shoulder-workouts.md) gives "keep your feet slightly off the
floor" as a general bench instruction, defines shoulder flexion and extension with **the same words
for both**, and numbers its safety sections 1, 2, 3, 5, 4.
[Inner chest](best-inner-chest-exercises-workouts.md) calls the hex press "an isometric training
exercise", specifies an incline bench for an exercise whose own equipment list says flat, and — the
sharpest one — credits the dumbbell flye with acting as **"a chest opener, which helps reduce back
pain"**, uncited and directly against Barbell Medicine's own pain-science position.
[Chest and triceps](chest-triceps-workout-routine-exercises.md) offers **cable flyes** as its example
of isolating the triceps "without using the chest muscles".
[Plyometrics](best-plyometric-exercises-speed-agility-power.md) answers "what should I do to jump
higher?" with a list including the **bent-over row**.
[Upper chest](best-upper-chest-exercises-bigger-pecs.md) names its six exercises differently in the
introduction and conclusion than in the numbered list, swapping **close-grip** for **wide-grip**
bench — movements with opposite intent.
[Cable chest](best-cable-chest-workout-exercises.md) has an exercise heading that contradicts its own
body text and a closing recap listing an exercise the article never covered.
[Chest and back](exercises-chest-back-workout-day.md) puts the trapezius "from the base of the neck
to the waist" and supports a claim about the trapezius's origin with a **fibre-type histochemistry
paper**.

**The physics error.** [Barbell Forces: Levers, Moments, and Torque](barbell-forces-levers-moments-torque.md)
(2012) defines moment arm and torque correctly and then argues **backwards**, repeatedly claiming
that lengthening these levers *helps* the lifter — "a longer lever will increase the torque and thus
allow for heavier weights to be moved," complete with an Archimedes quote. These are **resistance**
moment arms; lengthening one increases the torque the muscles must overcome, making the lift harder.
Mechanical advantage comes from the *muscle's* moment arm, never mentioned. It cites nothing.

**Safety claims stated more absolutely than anything cited.**
[The bench guide](bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) calls the cardiovascular risks of
the Valsalva "unsubstantiated" on a citation that only shows people reflexively breath-hold and raise
intra-abdominal pressure. [The squat guide](squat-correctly-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md)
asserts that "a specific squat form is not correlated with injury or pain" with no citation at all.
[Arms and shoulders](arms-shoulder-workout-bigger-biceps-shoulders.md) calls the shoulder-damage
claim about overhead work "made-up" and avoiding lockout "wholly made up and has no basis in reality"
— on citations covering **injury base rates**, not those claims.
[Press like a pro](press-like-pro-classic-press.md) states there is "insufficient data" that the
Classic Press raises injury risk, which is true and functions as reassurance about a technique nobody
has studied. [The pull day article](best-pull-day-workout-routine-exercises.md) asserts, uncited,
that "most gym accidents are due to faulty equipment, not trainees doing the exercise wrong." And an
"osteoarthritis progression is slowed or completely stopped" sentence appears near-verbatim across at
least four articles ([shoulders](best-shoulder-exercises.md),
[rear delts](best-rear-delt-exercises-workouts.md), [cable triceps](best-triceps-exercises-cable.md),
[bodyweight chest](best-bodyweight-chest-workout-without-equipment.md)) — a house sentence, reused,
unsupported every time; the papers behind it cover **pain and physical function** in knee
osteoarthritis, not structural disease modification.

**The bone-density heading.** [Ten push-up variations](best-bodyweight-chest-workout-without-equipment.md)
has a section headed "Improved Bone Density" whose **two citations are a knee-osteoarthritis dosing
review and a musculoskeletal-rehabilitation review**. Neither is about bone.
[Lower chest](best-lower-chest-workout-exercises-massive-pecs.md) claims the chest fly machine boosts
bone density with **no bone-density study anywhere in its thirty references**, and claims lifting
reduces cancer and type II diabetes risk with no epidemiological study of either in the list — while
its sign-off boasts that "our methods are backed by the most up-to-date research in sports medicine,
so you'll never have to worry about snake oil salesmen here."
[The back and shoulder day](back-shoulder-workout-exercises-v-shaped-body.md) props a bone-density
claim on a **case study of two powerlifters (n = 2)**, cites a **VO₂max study for blood pressure and
cholesterol**, conflates osteoarthritis with osteoporosis, and marks its final two claims with a
superscript reference series **that does not exist in its list**.

**A recurring irrelevant citation.** Biressi 2007, a developmental-biology paper on cellular
heterogeneity in vertebrate skeletal muscle development, is attached to a training-dose claim in at
least four articles ([chest and shoulder](best-chest-shoulder-workouts.md),
[lower chest](best-lower-chest-workout-exercises-massive-pecs.md),
[chest and biceps](best-chest-biceps-workout.md), [cable triceps](best-triceps-exercises-cable.md)),
always beside the same unsupported mechanism — that too high a training dose produces "too much
muscle protein breakdown" to build size. Training-induced net atrophy in a fed lifter is not an
established outcome.

**One dated contradiction the catalogue never resolves.** [The Grip Problem](grip-problem.md) (2017,
edited by Mark Rippetoe) makes a strong form-determines-adaptation argument: deadlifting with a
rounded back means the erectors "weren't required to do their job today", so they will not be able to
tomorrow. [The chest-and-back article](exercises-chest-back-workout-day.md) (2024) says that as long
as an exercise is performed repeatedly with an efficient movement pattern, "your unique technique
will work just fine". Both remain live. Read the 2017 piece as a Starting Strength artefact; its grip
mechanics stand independently of its technique philosophy.
