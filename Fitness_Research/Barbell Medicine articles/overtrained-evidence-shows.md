# Nobody Has Produced Overtraining Syndrome in a Lab From Lifting

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Are You Really Overtrained? What the Evidence Shows" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/overtraining-syndrome/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2026-03-31 · 6,236 words
**Topic:** Volume & recovery

## Bottom line
- **Overtraining syndrome (OTS) is a diagnosis of exclusion, defined retrospectively**, and no controlled resistance-training study has ever produced it. A systematic review of 22 resistance-training overload studies found **10 reported zero performance decline** and identified no reliable biomarker ([Grandou 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31820373/)).
- The categories cannot be told apart in real time. Functional overreaching resolves in days to ~2 weeks, non-functional overreaching in weeks to months, OTS runs months to years — **you assign the label after recovery happens, not at presentation** ([ECSS/ACSM consensus, Meeusen 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/)).
- Their replacement concept: a mismatch between **training load and available resources**, where "resources" includes sleep, energy intake and life stress. This is a coaching framework, not a diagnosis, and they are reasonably clear about the difference.
- **No biomarker works.** Subjective self-report beat hormones, resting heart rate and HRV across 56 studies ([Saw 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4789708/)). The testosterone-to-cortisol ratio moved in the *opposite* direction to the model's prediction under genuine resistance-training overload ([Fry 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9843563/)).
- **The most consequential misread is overtraining versus undertraining** — same presentation, opposite interventions — and in their experience undertraining is the more common error.
- **This contradicts a Jeff Nippard note already in this library on four specific points**, including the direction of the volume-versus-intensity claim and the usefulness of heart-rate monitoring. See below.

## The construct and where it came from
The article's most interesting move is historical. Overtraining descends from Hans Selye's 1936 General Adaptation Syndrome, which became the supercompensation model: stress, recover, adapt above baseline, time the next session to that peak. Their objection is that the body is not one system adapting in one wave — neural adaptations take hours to days, hypertrophy weeks to months, connective tissue months to years — so there is no single crest to hit. They cite [Kiely 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29189930/) directly: periodization theory imported supercompensation without adequate foundation.

The practical consequence they draw is sharp and worth keeping. Believing in a supercompensation window pushes lifters to hold intensity high (a maximal stimulus is needed to trigger it) while cutting volume at the first stall (a failure to improve must mean insufficient recovery). The result is **simultaneously overloaded on intensity and underloaded on volume** — the worst combination for long-term strength.

They also argue the word itself does four incompatible jobs: a deliberate stimulus in coaching texts, a dangerous state to avoid in the same texts, whatever a wearable's algorithm was trained on, and a specific exclusion diagnosis in sports medicine. This matters because language has physiological effects — a 2024 review found **nocebo effects in sport roughly twice the magnitude of placebo effects** across 20 studies ([Chhabra & Szabo 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38999724/)). Telling a lifter their watch says they are overtrained is not a neutral act. This is the same pain-science reasoning they apply to "your spine is degenerating," and they say so explicitly.

## The resistance-training studies
This is the section that earns the article. It is a parade of attempts to break trained lifters that mostly failed:

- **[Coleman 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/)** — supervised 9 weeks, ~90 working sets per week, 5 sets of 8–12 to failure with 2 minutes rest. Most subjects adapted and improved; nobody met OTS criteria.
- **[Zourdos 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27238810/)** — three competitive strength athletes squatted a true 1RM every day for **30 consecutive days** plus submaximal volume. All improved (473→500 lb, 275→303 lb, weightlifter 484→530 lb). A case series of three, and they say so.
- **Daily bench press for 38 days**, 7 subjects, all improved, average ~+30%. The instructive detail: one subject was weaker in week two than week one — a coach at day 14 would have called it overtraining — and finished +23%. The biggest gainer (+50 lb) tested 20 lb below his peak on the final day. The strongest subject had the *most* day-to-day variability, which cuts against the assumption that training history stabilises performance.
- **[Dankel 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27875635/)** — 21 consecutive days of daily arm training, one arm doing a 1RM per session and the other higher volume. Both arms gained ~2 kg of strength; only the volume arm grew.
- **[Fry 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7808252/)** — the one that worked, sort of. 11 weight-trained men, 10 sets at 1RM daily for 14 days (~140 maximal singles). 1RM fell ~12 kg, and electrically stimulated force fell too, ruling out a purely voluntary explanation. But recovery took 2–8 weeks with no follow-up past that window, so OTS — defined by months of impairment — was never established. And **testosterone rose while cortisol fell** ([Fry 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9843563/)), the reverse of what the T:C model predicts.
- **[Raastad 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11394254/)** — daily submaximal leg training for two weeks produced a **+6% 1RM increase**. Their inference: intensity, not frequency or volume as such, is the necessary ingredient for overreaching in lifting.
- **[Margonis 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17697935/)** — 12 men, 12 weeks, frequency climbing 2→6 days/week and intensity 70→100% 1RM. The authors called the result an overtraining response; Barbell Medicine points out that the hang clean peaked during the high-volume phase and never fell below baseline, so the criterion of persistent decline was not met, and attributes the post-rest decline to skill decay.

Their explanation for why OTS does not appear in lifters is a hypothesis, and flagged as one: **an overuse injury such as tendinopathy intervenes first**, before load can accumulate far enough to produce a syndrome.

## Mechanisms and biomarkers, all of which fail
Five hypotheses get walked through and dismissed: glycogen depletion (OTS persists through adequate carbohydrate intake), the serotonin/BCAA central fatigue theory (BCAA supplementation does not prevent it, serotonin varies widely, and it is reductionist), autonomic imbalance (changes are downstream, not predictive of performance), the cytokine cascade (does not replicate; some of the same cytokines mediate favourable adaptations), and HPA axis dysregulation.

The HPA section is the article's most rigorous passage. The EROS study ([Cadegiani & Kater 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5722782/)) used insulin tolerance testing and found **85% of OTS athletes produced peak cortisol below the 18 μg/dL adrenal-insufficiency threshold — but so did 75% of non-exercising controls**. On blunted ACTH, 57.1% of OTS subjects fell below 35 pg/mL versus 33.3% of controls, with the authors claiming 80% accuracy. Barbell Medicine's rebuttal is precise: the study was retrospective, had no pre-OTS baseline test, never showed that restoring ACTH resolves the condition, was small, and classified athletes on symptom criteria that overlap with HPA dysfunction by design. They also correct the "adrenal fatigue" framing — the adrenals are intact; the upstream signal changes.

On monitoring: HRV correlates with training status in endurance athletes ([Flatt 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36135035/)) and falls transiently after rapid load increases ([Baumert 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17016118/)), but none of that work validates HRV against performance recovery. In Olympic weightlifters, **strength recovered at ~30 hours while HRV did not normalise until ~60** ([Chen 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21273908/)) — so HRV would have pulled an athlete from a session they were ready for. What they endorse instead is **session RPE** ([Foster 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9662690/), [Halson 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4213373/)): a rising sRPE trend across two or more weeks at a constant relative load. One bad session is nothing; one bad week probably is too.

## Prevalence, and what is really going on
The ~20% figure for elite endurance athletes ([Halson & Jeukendrup 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15571428/)) gets treated sceptically because many prevalence studies never measure performance, substituting "staleness" or "training distress." Four confounders they list are rarely controlled: aging out of sport, **PED cessation** (which produces the identical picture — blunted HPA axis, mood disturbance, performance collapse), **low energy availability, which co-occurred in 86% of OTS cases** ([Stellingwerff 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34181189/)), and psychiatric illness overlapping heavily with major depression.

Exercise Hypogonadal Male Condition is used as the worked example ([Hackney 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7005256/), [Lane & Hackney 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29657961/)): highly trained men with testosterone comparable to sedentary seventy-year-olds, reduced LH pulsatility centrally, and **15–40% less testosterone production even when the testes are stimulated directly with hCG**. The separator from OTS is performance — EHMC athletes keep performing. Their line is quotable and correct: if your labs look alarming but your training is going well, you are not overtrained.

## Where this clashes with the library
**Against Jeff Nippard, *[Overtraining vs Overreaching](../Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/maximize-gains-not-overtrain.md)* (2017), on four points:**

1. **Definition.** Nippard sets overtraining at a performance drop persisting beyond **2–3 weeks**. On the consensus definition Barbell Medicine uses, 2–3 weeks is functional or at most non-functional overreaching — OTS requires months. Nippard's threshold would label as overtraining what the literature calls a normal training response.
2. **Supercompensation.** Nippard's whole framework is overreach → recover → supercompensate. Barbell Medicine, via Kiely 2018, argues that model was imported without foundation and generates exactly the anxious programming logic Nippard's advice encodes.
3. **Volume versus intensity — a direct reversal.** Nippard cites a 2010 Schoenfeld review to conclude overtraining "results more from excessive volume than from intensity." Barbell Medicine reads Raastad 2001 (daily submaximal volume, +6% 1RM) and Fry 1994 (daily maximal singles, −12 kg) as pointing the other way: **intensity is the necessary ingredient**. Same phenomenon, opposite causal attribution.
4. **Monitoring.** Nippard recommends HRV or morning heart rate with a >10% deviation triggering 1–2 weeks of reduced load. Barbell Medicine's Chen 2011 finding is the specific counter-case. Note that **both cite Saw 2016**, which concluded subjective measures beat objective ones — Nippard cites it for his four-question self-survey, then recommends HRV as "the more objective marker" in the next breath, which is at odds with the paper he just used.

They also use **the same Fry 1994 study for opposite purposes**: Nippard offers it as proof overtraining is real; Barbell Medicine offers it as the strongest attempt on record that still failed to meet OTS criteria. Worth noticing, because it shows how much of this disagreement is definitional rather than empirical.

**Alongside Menno Henselmans, *[CNS Fatigue Is Local, Not Central](../Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/cns-fatigue-not-central.md)*:** the practical advice converges — audit your performance, not your nervous system — but the physiology does not quite. Barbell Medicine treats central fatigue as a real category throughout (their own rest-periods article details serotonin, dopamine and group III/IV afferent mechanisms), while rejecting the serotonin theory of OTS specifically. Henselmans argues the central category is largely misnamed for lifting. They are not contradicting each other so much as drawing the line in different places.

## Evidence versus recommendation, and what they sell
The evidence claims are well supported and unusually well hedged — retrospective diagnosis, absent biomarkers, small studies named as small, hypotheses labelled as hypotheses. The recommendations go further than the evidence in the ordinary clinical way: the five-step framework (align testing with the program, fix energy intake, fix sleep, track sRPE trend, medical workup at 4–6 weeks) has no trial behind it, and the ferritin floor of **30–50 ng/mL rather than the standard 15** is a clinical opinion presented as a correction to lab reference ranges. The claim that OTS "almost always turns out to be an unaddressed life variable" is explicitly flagged by them as their own experience.

Commercially, this article sells: a training consultation is pitched near the top, an online medical consultation in the workup section, and the framework routes twice to their **Training Plateau Action Plan**. The advice is reasonable and the products are the natural next step from it — the reader can weigh that.
