# A Seven-Exercise Arms and Shoulders Day — and How Little of It Is Actually Cited

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "7 Arms and Shoulder Workout For Bigger Biceps and Shoulders" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-arm-and-shoulder-workout/)) · Barbell Medicine (no named author) · 2024-07-12 · 8,349 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- 8,349 words, 25 distinct references, and **only seven of them touch exercise selection at all** — three EMG studies of the bench press, one EMG study of lateral raise variants, one EMG study of curl shoulder positions, and one training trial comparing cable to barbell preacher curls. Everything else cited is nutrition, supplements or injury base rates.
- **Four of the seven prescribed exercises carry no citation whatsoever**, and neither do the sets, reps, RPE targets, exercise order, progression scheme or the "results in 4–6 weeks" timeline. This is a programming article wearing a reference list.
- **They get the preacher curl backwards.** They say the bench "produces flexion in the shoulders, which trains the biceps at a lengthened range of motion," better for the long head. Shoulder flexion *shortens* the biceps long head. The library's note on [Kassiano 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39809454/) (n=63, 8 weeks, direct comparison) has the **incline** curl — shoulder extended — favouring the biceps, with the preacher favouring the more distal elbow flexors.
- Where they are careful, they are genuinely careful: they say outright that whether bench-press variations change *relative growth* of any muscle "is mostly speculative," and that EMG differences between lateral raise styles may not translate to strength or size.
- The safety section states two pain-science positions — that overhead work damaging shoulders is "made-up," and that avoiding lockout is "wholly made up and has no basis in reality" — **more absolutely than anything they cite**. Their citations cover injury base rates (1–4 injuries per 1,000 training hours), not those two claims.
- They sell into it heavily: three inline CTAs to a hypertrophy programme quiz, six named training templates, coaching, and three of their own supplements — recommended in the same section that argues supplements are "generally not necessary."

## What the session is

Seven exercises in a fixed order, tiered primary → secondary → tertiary:

| Exercise | Tier | Sets × reps | Proximity to failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overhead press (barbell, standing) | Primary | 3–4 × 5–8 | RPE 7–8, 2–3 reps in reserve |
| Close-grip incline bench press | Secondary | 2–4 × 6–10 | RPE 7–8 |
| 1-arm cable lateral raise | Tertiary | 2–5 × 10–15 | RPE 8–10, 0–2 in reserve |
| Overhead triceps cable extension | Tertiary | 3–5 × 8–12 | RPE 8–10 |
| Preacher curl (EZ-bar) | Tertiary | 3–5 × 8–12 | RPE 8–10 |
| Triceps press-down (rope) | Tertiary | 3–5 × 10–15 | RPE 8–10 |
| 1-arm concentration curl | Tertiary | 3–5 × 10–15 | RPE 8–10 |

Newer lifters take the bottom of each set range, more advanced the top. Compounds first, isolation last, "as it is generally a good idea to train larger muscle groups and more complex movements first."

**None of that table carries a citation.** Not the rep ranges, not the RPE targets, not the volume progression by training age, not the ordering rule. It is defensible, conventional programming, and it is presented as *their* approach — the article opens by saying there is "no one optimal way" and closes by saying you can swap in similar variations. Read as clinical judgement, it is unobjectionable. Read as an evidence-based prescription, which is how the framing invites, it is unsupported.

One structural oddity: they describe the overhead press as loading the anterior, lateral **and posterior** deltoid heads, then include no rear-delt work in a day billed as covering the shoulders.

## Which exercises, and how much of the reasoning is evidence

**Close-grip incline bench press** is the best-cited item. The incline recruits the anterior deltoid more, the close grip asks more of the triceps than a normal grip ([Saeterbakken 2017](https://doi.org/10.1515/hukin-2017-0047), [Saeterbakken 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18126444), [Rodríguez-Ridao 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17197339)). All three are **acute surface-EMG studies** — activation, not growth. They then say the hypertrophy implication is "mostly speculative," which is exactly the right hedge and rarer in this genre than it should be.

**1-arm cable lateral raise.** They prefer the cable because its resistance curve loads the stretched initial range more. That preference is **uncited**. Their one citation here ([Coratella 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17176015), EMG in competitive bodybuilders) is used to say the opposite of a sales pitch — that the modest activation differences between raise styles may not change strength or growth. **Library check:** the House of Hypertrophy note on 3D shoulders records the same cable-versus-dumbbell stretch argument, then reports that a later trial in trained lifters found **similar side-delt growth** between the two. So the preference is speculation that has since been tested and not supported. It is also a preference, not a prescription, and they say so.

**Preacher curl — flagged, this is an error.** They justify it as training the biceps at a lengthened range of motion because the bench flexes the shoulder, and claim this produces better hypertrophy "especially in the long head." Shoulder flexion shortens the biceps long head; the lengthened position is shoulder *extension*, which is the incline curl. Their citation for the sentence, [Oliveira 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24150552/), is an EMG study of how shoulder position changes biceps activation in dumbbell curls — it does not establish which position is lengthened, nor anything about growth. **Library conflict:** Kassiano 2025 (63 previously untrained women, incline vs preacher dumbbell curl, 3 × 8–12 to failure, 2×/week, 8 weeks) found the 50% upper-arm site — biceps only — favoured the **incline** curl, with the 70% site favouring the preacher. The direct-comparison evidence points the other way from Barbell Medicine's rationale.

Their second preacher-curl claim is better supported: EZ-bar, dumbbell and cable versions produce similar hypertrophy, with the EZ-bar possibly better tolerated ([Nunes 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17165859) — the only actual training study cited anywhere in the exercise sections).

**Overhead press, overhead triceps extension, triceps press-down, concentration curl** are described entirely from anatomy and preference. The triceps long head crosses the shoulder and needs shoulder extension to be trained at length; the biceps should be trained with and without shoulder flexion; the concentration curl is chosen for large range of motion and minimal shoulder involvement. That reasoning is standard and plausible. It carries **zero references**, and the article does not say so.

**Library check on the overhead press:** their claim that it loads all three deltoid heads sits against moment-arm data summarised in the House of Hypertrophy shoulder note, where a narrow-to-moderate-grip vertical press is predominantly *shoulder flexion* and the front delt's leverage is three to four times the side delt's, with the rear delt barely involved. Neither source has a hypertrophy outcome trial; the disagreement is between two uncited claims and a modelling argument.

## Volume, frequency and how to progress

The honest core of the article is that it refuses to give a number. Response varies — hyper-responders, non-responders, everyone in between — and "there's no real way to tell how much is enough in advance." Newer lifters need less. Their heuristic for building the day: two compound shoulder movements, then two biceps and two triceps exercises picked so that one of each puts the shoulder in a neutral position and the other flexed (biceps) or extended (triceps), then optionally one shoulder isolation.

On progression they give progressive loading via RPE/RIR autoregulation, plus double progression (add reps to the top of the range, then add load). Their framing of overload is unusually careful and worth recording: training should not get progressively *harder*, it should stay the same subjective difficulty while your capacity rises, and the stimulus should track the rate of adaptation rather than the rate you want. They also correctly attribute the term to DeLorme's 1946 rehabilitation work. **All of this is uncited** — including the range-of-motion section, which asserts that muscles trained at longer lengths grow more, a claim with a substantial literature behind it that they simply do not reference.

Timelines given: noticeable strength change in 1–12 weeks, often 2–4; measurable size change around 4–6 weeks. **Uncited.**

## Where the citations actually are: nutrition, supplements, injury

Nearly all the referencing sits in sections that have nothing to do with arms and shoulders.

**Protein.** 1.4–1.6 g/kg/day, with a recommendation of ~1.6 g/kg on training and non-training days, on the basis that added protein raises strength gains dose-dependently up to that point ([Tagawa 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-022-00508-w), dose-response meta-analysis of RCTs) and that timing matters far less than total daily dose ([Kerksick 2005](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-2-1-50)). Dieters and those with anabolic resistance may need more ([Stokes 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu10020180)); physique competitors up to 2.3–3.1 g/kg. **Mild library clash:** the ISSN 2017 protein stand treats 1.4–2.0 g/kg as a *minimum* and puts the dieting range at ≥1.6–2.4 g/kg, where this article says there is "not really any benefit" above ~1.4–1.6 for most people. The claim that higher protein aids fat loss by displacing ultra-processed food is asserted without citation.

**Carbohydrate.** 3–5 g/kg unless training over an hour; low-carbohydrate diets discouraged for maximal performance. The glycogen-threshold claim (performance falls below ~70 mmol/kg) is supported by [Mul 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.pmbts.2015.07.020), [Gejl 2014](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000132) and [Henselmans 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14040856) — note Gejl is elite endurance athletes, a long way from an arms day.

**Supplements — flagged for the conflict.** The section argues supplements are "generally not necessary," and cites three strong sources to show fish oil and multivitamins do not reduce cardiovascular disease or cancer risk ([Barbarawi 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2019.1870), 83,000 participants across 21 RCTs; [Siscovick 2017](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000482); [O'Connor 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35727272/), the USPSTF evidence report). It then recommends three Barbell Medicine products — Peri-RX with caffeine, Peri-RX watermelon, and Whey Rx isolate — with **no citations at all** for any of them. Stating that plainly: the debunking is well sourced, the sales list is not.

**Injury.** Resistance training runs 2–4 injuries per 1,000 participation hours ([Bukhary 2023](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.49759), cross-sectional), and 1–4 per 1,000 hours across modalities ([Aasa 2016](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096037), systematic review; [Strömbäck 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967118771016), cross-sectional). The walking (0.19–1.2) and cycling (0.5–2.0) comparators are uncited.

**Flagged overreach.** Their two heterodox safety claims are stated at maximum strength with no supporting citation: that overhead work harming shoulders is "made-up and not supported by scientific evidence," and that avoiding lockout "is wholly made up and has no basis in reality." The defensible versions — that gym-goers do not show clearly higher rates of shoulder pain than non-lifters, and that no evidence shows stopping short of lockout reduces injury — are what the cited injury-rate literature can carry. "No evidence for" is not "disproven," and the article does not make that distinction. To their credit, the accompanying advice is the good clinical version: find a variation you can train pain-free now and progress back toward full overhead pressing.

## A mapping caveat, and what they sell

The marker-to-reference mapping for this article passed its offset check, but **two places in the article have markers pointing at the wrong papers**. The "Why Should You Train Your Arms and Shoulders Together?" section attaches [2] and [3] — the bench grip-width and bench-inclination EMG studies — to a sample full-body programme, and the "Weight Management" section attaches [4] and [5] — bench inclination and lateral raise EMG — to a claim about exercise reducing weight regain. Meanwhile the three papers that obviously belong to that claim (Miller 1997, Vidanage 2023, Johns 2014) are cited at no marker at all. Those claims are left unattributed here rather than repaired by guessing. The reference list also duplicates three entries (3=4, 8=10, 14=16), which is why 28 numbered items resolve to 25 distinct papers.

**Products.** The article carries the same "Find your Hypertrophy Program" call-to-action three times (intro, middle, end), names six paid templates — Strength I, Powerbuilding I, Bodybuilding II, General Strength & Conditioning I and II, Hypertrophy I — pitches one-to-one coaching, and recommends three own-brand supplements. That is not disqualifying, and the training advice itself is conservative and sane, but this is a commercial listicle with a research veneer rather than an evidence review.
