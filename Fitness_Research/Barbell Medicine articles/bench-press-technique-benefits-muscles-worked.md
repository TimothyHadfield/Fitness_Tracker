# The Bench Press, Set Up From First Principles — and the Angle Debate Settled by Outcome Data

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How-To Bench Press: Technique, Benefits, and Muscles Worked" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-to-bench-press/)) · Claire Zai · 2024-04-22 · 7,347 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- The technique half of this article is craft, not evidence: nine setup decisions with recommendations and self-check tests, almost none of it cited. It is good craft, and worth having, but it is **expert opinion presented as instruction**.
- The **bench angle question is the one place they bring outcome data**, and they win the argument on it. Flat vs incline vs decline produced no meaningful difference in pec major hypertrophy or pressing strength over 8 weeks ([Chaves 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32922646/)), and push-ups matched bench press for both 1RM and chest growth over 4 weeks ([Kotarsky 2018](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002345)). The null EMG studies are supporting evidence, not the basis.
- They cite [Vigotsky 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001249) explicitly to say that EMG differences do not license inferences about recruitment or growth potential — **this source knows the activation-vs-outcome distinction and states it**, which is more than most of this library's exercise-ranking material does.
- **Arching is not dangerous** is their position, and it is reasoning rather than evidence: compressive spinal load while lying supine is low, so the standing-lift intuition does not transfer. No study is cited for it. The advice that follows (feet on plates, feet-up variation for those who don't tolerate lumbar extension) is sensible load modification.
- Their **Valsalva defence is stated more strongly than the citation supports**. The one reference on the sentence ([Hemborg 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3159082/)) shows people reflexively breath-hold and raise intra-abdominal pressure when lifting. It does not establish that the cardiovascular risks are "unsubstantiated" — that broader claim is uncited.
- The health-benefits section is well-referenced but **oversells one number**: the 23% all-cause mortality reduction is described as coming from "a large review" when [Dankel 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.acvd.2016.04.005) is a single prospective cohort.

## The setup, decision by decision

The article walks the lift as a sequence of eight setup decisions plus spotting, each with a named recommendation. Two of them are unusually good because they come with a self-test rather than a cue.

**Grip width** is defined against the bar itself: on a standard power bar the knurling begins 16 inches (40.64 cm) apart and the score marks sit 31 inches (78.74 cm) apart, the maximum grip legal in powerlifting. Index fingers at the knurling is a close (roughly shoulder-width) grip; index fingers on the marks is a wide one. Their starting recommendation is pinkies on the marks. The self-test is the useful part: **at the bottom of the rep the forearms should be vertical from the side and from the front**. Angled back toward the head means the touch point is too high for that grip; angled toward the feet means too low. Angled inward means too much elbow tuck; outward means not enough. This converts "find your grip width" from taste into a checkable geometric condition.

**Bar in the hand.** The bar should sit high in the palm across the thenar and hypothenar eminences so the wrist stacks over the radius — the "bulldog" or compression grip. A false (thumbless) grip is discouraged because it makes that position harder to hold and raises the risk of the bar rolling. Visual check from the side: the bar should look stacked over the forearm, not hanging behind the wrist.

**Bar path.** Down and slightly forward on the descent, up and back on the ascent, finishing over the shoulder joint. At the bottom the bar sits a few inches in front of the shoulder regardless of grip width, so a straight vertical press from the chest leaves the bar forward of the shoulder for longer than necessary and adds shoulder torque.

**The arch** is built from four components: thoracic extension, scapular retraction and depression, lumbar extension, and a Valsalva breath hold. The stated purposes are a stable base, a shortened range of motion, and a better shoulder position for force production.

**Leg drive** is force from the legs into the trunk, cued as pushing the feet backward along the floor toward the rack rather than straight down, peaking as the bar leaves the chest. The distinction they draw is with hip thrusting, which lifts the hips off the bench and is not leg drive.

**Spotting** gets a clinical-sounding recommendation worth repeating: the spotter's job is the lift-off and the clear miss, and "just enough" assistance is not quantifiable, so it does not provide a reliable stimulus. If reps are being ground out with help, reduce the load and complete the planned work.

**Judgment, not evidence.** None of the above is cited. It is competent, internally consistent coaching from people who compete and coach the lift, and the article's own closing line — technique need not be perfect, only repeatable and sustainable — is the honest framing.

## Arching, breathing, and where the position outruns the citation

Two claims in the technique section are contested in the wider industry, and both are argued from mechanism rather than from studies.

On the **arch**, the reasoning is that spinal loading while supine on a bench is very different from a standing barbell lift, with minimal compressive load through the spine, so the danger claim imports an intuition from squats and deadlifts where it does not apply. They concede that some individuals will not tolerate repeated lumbar extension at high volumes and give two modifications — plates or small boxes under the feet, and the feet-up bench press — as temporary or permanent options. This is the load-modification model this source applies everywhere, and it is applied well here. But **no evidence is cited on either side**, so "the arch is not the major injury risk it is made out to be online" is a clinical opinion, defensible and probably right, not a finding.

On **breathing**, they reject the advice to exhale on the way up, on two grounds: it is difficult or impossible during hard efforts, and the risks attributed to the Valsalva manoeuvre — centred on a transient rise in systolic blood pressure — "remain unsubstantiated." The single citation on that passage, Hemborg 1985, is attached to the descriptive sentence that people reflexively breath-hold and raise intra-abdominal pressure when lifting, which it does support. **The stronger claim about risk carries no citation.** That is the pattern the brief warns about: the pain-and-safety position stated more absolutely than the cited work reaches.

## Bench variations: the one ranking backed by growth data

This is the section that matters for the library, and it is the counterexample to almost every other exercise ranking here.

The question is whether bench angle changes what you build. Their answer is no, and they arrive at it in the right order.

**The activation evidence is null to begin with.** [Saeterbakken 2017](https://doi.org/10.1515/hukin-2017-0047) found no significant differences in pectoralis major, anterior or posterior deltoid, or latissimus dorsi excitation during 6RM efforts on flat, incline and decline bench. [Christian 2023](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004250) likewise found no statistically significant differences in pec major, anterior or lateral deltoid excitation across flat, decline and incline, with dumbbells or barbells.

**Then they disqualify the method rather than lean on it.** Where other studies do find EMG differences by bench angle, those differences "are not really predictive of actual outcomes," cited to Vigotsky 2017 — the methodological paper arguing that greater electromyographic responses do not imply greater motor unit recruitment and that hypertrophic potential cannot be inferred from them. This is the correct move and it is rare in this genre.

**The ranking claim then rests on training outcomes.** Chaves 2020 trained untrained young men for 8 weeks on flat only, incline only, or both, and found no meaningful differences in pec major hypertrophy or pressing strength. Kotarsky 2018 compared 4 weeks of push-ups against bench press three times a week and found similar improvements in both 1RM bench press strength and chest hypertrophy.

**So: outcome-backed, not activation-backed.** The conclusion — most pressing exercises produce similar hypertrophy in the pecs, anterior delts and triceps, so use a variety through long ranges of motion — follows from two small, short training studies in largely untrained or lightly trained people, plus two null activation studies and a methods critique. That is thin but it is the right *kind* of evidence, and the recommendation is appropriately weak ("use a variety") rather than a ranking.

The assistance-exercise section that follows returns to opinion: direct assistance (paused, close- or wide-grip, pin press, board press, floor press, slingshot, feet-up) alters one variable while staying specific; indirect assistance builds pressing muscle mass generally. Their closing line there is the specificity principle applied honestly — the bench press remains the most specific way to improve the bench press.

## The health case, and its numbers

The benefits section is the best-referenced part of the article, and mostly reports its numbers properly.

Resistance training 2–3 times per week is associated with a **23% reduction in all-cause mortality** (Dankel 2016). The article calls this "a large review"; it is a prospective cohort study of a national US sample, which supports association, not effect. Greater volume is associated with greater benefit ([Figueiredo 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0793-0)).

**Response varies enormously.** In [Ahtiainen 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-015-9870-1), 287 adults on the same 6-month programme changed muscle size by anywhere from **−11% to +30%**, and age and sex did not predict who responded. The article also reports a review of 40 studies where strength was measured repeatedly rather than only pre and post: the first demonstrable strength increase took **4.3 weeks on average, ranging from 1 to 12 weeks**, with squat 1RM gains across studies ranging from **~3% to 40%**. That passage carries reference marker 12, but reference 12 is the Lauersen injury-prevention meta-analysis used correctly two sections later — **the article's own numbering double-uses it, so this striking finding is effectively uncited** and I could not identify the review.

Injury risk: [Lauersen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092538), a meta-analysis of RCTs of exercise interventions for injury prevention. Bone: [O'Bryan 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01675-2). Blood pressure: [Naci 2019](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099921), a network meta-analysis of 391 RCTs, in people with resting SBP above 140 mmHg — endurance exercise −8.69 mmHg, resistance training −7.83 mmHg, combined −13.5 mmHg. Metabolic: a **17% lower risk of developing diabetes** in lifters ([Momma 2022](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061)), and in type 2 diabetes, those with larger 1RM improvements saw larger HbA1c improvements ([Jansson 2022](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdrc-2021-002595)).

The **athlete's heart** passage is the most careful writing in the article. The Morganroth hypothesis — that endurance training causes eccentric left ventricular hypertrophy and resistance training concentric hypertrophy — is set out, then dismantled: the eccentric adaptation occurs in both, endurance training produces the larger wall thickening, chamber size scales proportionally, and the wall-thickness-to-chamber ratio does not meaningfully differ between modes ([Williams 2007](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.107.185214); [Spence 2011](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2011.217125), a prospective randomised longitudinal MRI study; [Naylor 2008](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200838010-00006)). The summary claim that resistance training "does not negatively affect heart function" is stated absolutely on the strength of one small MRI trial, a narrative appraisal and two AHA position statements — reasonable, but stronger than the design of the evidence.

**What they sell.** The article promotes their own Bench Press Prescription video, and closes with three calls to action: training programmes, strength-training resources, and personalised coaching. Nothing in the technique advice depends on buying any of it.
