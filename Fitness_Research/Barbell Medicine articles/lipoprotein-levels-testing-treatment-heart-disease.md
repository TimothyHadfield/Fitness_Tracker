# Lipoprotein(a): A Genetic Risk Factor You Can Measure Once and Cannot Yet Treat

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Lipoprotein A: Levels, Testing, and Treatment For Heart Disease" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/lipoprotein-a-testing-and-treatment/)) · Austin Baraki · 2025-08-05 · 3,045 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **Lp(a) is an LDL particle carrying an extra molecule, apolipoprotein(a).** That one addition makes it a preferential carrier of oxidised phospholipids and gives it pro-thrombotic and anti-fibrinolytic properties on top of its cholesterol content. Per particle it is more atherogenic than LDL; in aggregate LDL still does more damage, because there is far more of it.
- **Over 90% of the variance in Lp(a) is attributable to the *LPA* gene.** Levels are essentially set by age 5 and stay stable for life. This is the single most consequential fact in the article: **diet and exercise do not meaningfully move it.**
- **Test once, in every adult** — the position of the National Lipid Association and the European Atherosclerosis Society, ideally alongside a first lipid panel. US ACC/AHA guidance remains more conservative, treating elevated Lp(a) as a "risk-enhancing factor" for selected patients rather than a universal screen. The article sides with the broader recommendation.
- **Thresholds:** rule *out* significant Lp(a)-mediated risk below **30 mg/dL (75 nmol/L)**; rule it *in* above **50 mg/dL (125 nmol/L)**; **30–50 mg/dL** is a grey zone interpreted against other risk factors. Risk is a continuous gradient — the cut-offs are clinical convenience, and the article says so.
- **Never convert between units with a fixed factor.** Multiplying mg/dL by 2.0–2.5 misclassifies risk, because apo(a) isoform size varies and mass does not track particle number. nmol/L is the preferred unit.
- **There is no approved therapy that lowers Lp(a) and has been shown to reduce events.** Management is entirely compensatory: drive every other modifiable risk factor harder.
- **Commercial note:** the article links twice to Barbell Medicine's **paid online physician consultation**, including in its closing sentence.

## How the article cites, and why that is a problem here

This article uses **no in-text citation markers at all.** It ends with a bare list of seven links — two conference/news pages, two society consensus documents, an NLA PDF, and two PubMed entries — with nothing tying any of them to any sentence. **There is no numbering to be broken; there simply is no claim-level attribution.**

For a technical training article that would be a minor complaint. For a **clinical** article stating numeric diagnostic thresholds, drug effect sizes, and guideline positions, it is a genuine weakness. Every quantitative claim below — the 10–20% statin effect, the 20–30% PCSK9 inhibitor effect, the 30/50 mg/dL cut-offs, the "one in five" prevalence — is asserted without a traceable source. The figures are consistent with the published Lp(a) literature as far as one can tell, and the one resolvable reference (Tsimikas 2017, a *JACC* "Test in Context" review) plausibly underlies much of the piece. But a reader cannot check any specific number against any specific source, and should not treat the figures as verified.

## The clinical reasoning, which is the article's real contribution

Baraki is a physician and the strongest section is the one addressing the obvious objection: **why test for something you cannot treat?**

His answer is **reclassification**. A patient who looks low- or moderate-risk on conventional scoring may in fact sit in a higher-risk band once Lp(a) is known. That changes the aggressiveness of everything else — how low you push apoB, how tightly you control blood pressure, how hard you push on glycaemia and smoking. The term he uses is **"compensatory risk reduction."** The test does not produce a treatment for Lp(a); it produces a different treatment intensity for everything else.

He is explicit that this is a contested position ("some have argued that we should not bother testing"), and states his own side of it. That is the right handling — **"we recommend testing" is presented as a judgement call, not a settled fact.**

A second piece of good clinical reasoning: **elevated Lp(a) with no other risk factors may carry lower short-term absolute risk than normal Lp(a) with a heavy conventional risk burden.** Relative risk from a genetic marker is not the same as absolute risk in a person, and the article does not let the reader confuse them.

## What the treatments actually do

**Lifestyle:** no meaningful effect. The article makes a point worth keeping — a low-carbohydrate, high-saturated-fat diet may modestly lower Lp(a), but raises LDL-c enough that net risk goes up. **Lowering a marker is not the same as lowering risk.** Exercise shows no consistent relationship with Lp(a) in observational data and is recommended anyway on its own merits.

**Statins:** raise Lp(a) by roughly **10–20%** on average, with variable individual response. Net effect on cardiovascular risk remains clearly beneficial because the LDL particle reduction is much larger in absolute terms. A useful practical note: if LDL-c fails to respond adequately to an adherent patient's statin, high Lp(a) may be part of the explanation, since Lp(a)-carried cholesterol is counted in the measured LDL-c on most panels.

**PCSK9 inhibitors** (evolocumab, alirocumab): lower Lp(a) by about **20–30%**. FOURIER and ODYSSEY OUTCOMES both showed greater absolute risk reduction in patients with higher baseline Lp(a). The article's phrasing — "suggesting that the Lp(a) reduction *may* contribute" — is appropriately hedged. These were subgroup findings, not the trials' primary questions.

**Aspirin:** mechanistically attractive because Lp(a) is pro-thrombotic. A MESA analysis suggested benefit in high-Lp(a) individuals **alongside increased major bleeding**. No consensus; the article correctly frames this as a shared-decision-making conversation rather than a recommendation.

**Niacin:** lowers Lp(a) by **20–30%** and has repeatedly failed to add outcome benefit on top of statins in large trials. The article's proposed explanation — that the absolute reduction achieved was too small to matter over trial timeframes — is speculative, and flagged as such by the reasoning that follows it.

**Lipoprotein apheresis:** effective, invasive, several hours per session every 1–2 weeks, expensive, reserved for very high-risk patients.

**Emerging drugs:** several in late-stage trials, testing the **"Lp(a) hypothesis"** — that specifically lowering Lp(a) reduces events. Genetic estimates suggest reductions of **>50–100 mg/dL (>100–200 nmol/L)** may be needed for a clinically detectable effect within a 3–5 year trial. That is a large absolute drop and a good calibration of how far short current therapies fall.

## Where the confidence outruns the evidence

**Flagged:** the article states early that Lp(a) is an "independent **causal** risk factor," flatly and without qualification. That causal claim rests on Mendelian randomisation and genetic epidemiology — strong evidence of a causal relationship, but **not on any trial showing that lowering Lp(a) lowers events, because none exists yet.** The article resolves this itself several sections later when it introduces the Lp(a) hypothesis as something still to be tested, and closes by saying research "still needs to determine whether direct Lp(a)-lowering therapies are safe and effective." The two statements sit in tension; the later one is the accurate one.

A smaller point: "approximately one in five individuals worldwide have elevated Lp(a)" is a widely quoted figure whose value depends entirely on which threshold and which population you use — and the article uses it without saying which.

**Commercial note, plainly.** Two links go to Barbell Medicine's paid online consultation service, one mid-article ("Consulting with a physician familiar with Lp(a)...") and one as the final line ("We at Barbell Medicine are available and happy to help"). This is a clinical article that ends by recommending the authors' own clinical service. Nothing in the medical content depends on that, and the recommendation to see a knowledgeable physician about a borderline result is ordinary good advice — but the reader should see where the link goes.

**Genuinely useful, non-commercial resource:** the article twice points to **lpaclinicalguidance.com**, an interactive tool for visualising how a given Lp(a) level shifts long-term risk and how much LDL-c or systolic blood pressure reduction would offset it. That is the most actionable thing in the piece and it costs nothing.
