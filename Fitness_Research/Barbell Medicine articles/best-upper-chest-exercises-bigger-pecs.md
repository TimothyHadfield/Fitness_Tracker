# The Upper-Chest Article Argues Against Itself, and the Disclaimer Is the Correct Half

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "6 Best Upper Chest Exercises for Bigger Pecs" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-upper-chest-exercises-for-bigger-pecs/)) · Barbell Medicine (unattributed staff) · 2023-12-22 · 3,650 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **This article both contradicts and agrees with Barbell Medicine's own position on incline bench.** Elsewhere in this corpus ([their chest-hypertrophy article](best-chest-exercises-hypertrophy.md)) they write that "there isn't enough evidence to support" the claim that incline presses preferentially target the upper pecs. Here, the *prose* says the same thing twice — and the *title, structure, exercise descriptions and conclusion* assume the opposite throughout.
- **No cited study measured regional growth.** A regional-growth claim needs regional measurement: ultrasound or MRI of the clavicular head versus the sternocostal head after a training block. Of the eleven unique papers cited, six are surface EMG or biomechanics, one is an anatomy textbook chapter, and the three training-outcome papers are about volume and repetition duration in general. **Zero measured a region of the pectoralis major before and after training.** Zero measured any of the six exercises against another for size.
- **The ranking is activation-based where it is based on anything at all.** The only exercise-specific citations in the whole list are EMG and kinetics studies. There is no hypertrophy outcome data behind the ordering of the six.
- The mapping is **verified**, so the mismatches below are the article's, not an extraction artefact. The clearest is citing a meta-analysis of *repetition duration* to support a claim about *rep ranges*.
- The article recommends Barbell Medicine's coaching service and its hypertrophy and bodybuilding templates — while conceding, to its credit, that none of the templates is aimed at the upper chest.

## The self-contradiction, stated precisely

The opening paragraph is the honest one. Whether different chest exercises produce differential growth in specific parts of the muscle "is an area of active debate"; EMG studies of flat, incline and decline bench have produced "mixed results"; and those excitation differences "are not really predictive of actual outcomes, e.g., differences in muscle size and strength." The disclaimer returns in the "Find the Right Incline for You" section: as the bench moves toward horizontal, pec excitation rises and anterior-deltoid excitation falls [9] — "however, this doesn't mean a lower or higher incline is better or worse for targeting the upper pectoralis major, as excitation levels don't necessarily correlate to training outcomes."

That is the same position as their chest-hypertrophy article, and it is the defensible one.

Everything structural argues the other way. The title promises six upper-chest exercises. The incline dumbbell fly section opens "you know that incline bench exercises reign supreme when it comes to upper-chest activation." The incline bench section states that "the clavicular head is more isolated," citing a surface-EMG study [1]. The wide-grip bench section says a wide grip is "likely putting more stress on its clavicular head." The "Combining Exercises" section says the six will let you "emphasize the upper pectorals." The conclusion says the upper pecs "are probably the most important muscles to train to get that full, ripped-chest look."

The net effect: a reader who reads only the exercise sections gets the industry claim, and a reader who reads the two hedged paragraphs gets the evidence-based one. **They are not consistent, and the article never reconciles them.** Anyone using this library should treat the hedge as Barbell Medicine's actual position and the rest as convention.

## What the cited evidence is, exercise by exercise

| Exercise | Cited support | Kind of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Incline dumbbell fly | None | — |
| Incline bench press | Rodríguez-Ridao 2020 [1] | Surface EMG, five bench inclinations |
| Wide-grip bench press | Kim 2016, Larsen 2021 [2,3] | EMG (push-up hand width); kinematics + EMG at the sticking region of a 1RM bench |
| Low-to-high cable fly | None | — |
| Decline push-up | Ebben 2011 [4] | Kinetics — ground reaction force across push-up variants, not muscle |
| Bar dips | None | — |

Three of the six exercises carry no citation whatsoever. The three that do carry acute laboratory measurements. Ebben 2011 is worth singling out: it is a **kinetic** analysis measuring how much bodyweight each push-up variant loads through the hands. It supports "a decline push-up is harder than a flat push-up." The article stretches it to "the decline push-up is a more efficient exercise for training the chest," which the paper does not test, and to the uncited claim that the decline "allows most of the mechanical load to be put on the upper pectorals" — a regional claim from a study with no EMG and no imaging.

The set and rep prescriptions (2–4 × 10–15 at RPE 8–9 for flyes and cable work, 3–4 × 5–8 at RPE 7–8 for incline bench, and so on) are uncited. They are house programming convention, and the article is explicit that they are recommendations.

## The supporting arguments, and where they outrun the citations

**Volume.** "Muscular hypertrophy and strength have been shown to have a dose-dependent relationship with training volume" [10,11] — Figueiredo 2018 and Schoenfeld 2019. This is the strongest evidence in the article and it is used correctly, including the qualifier that the dose-response holds "only if the individual can tolerate it." Note that Schoenfeld 2019 found volume enhanced hypertrophy but **not** strength in trained men, which the article's "hypertrophy *and* strength" wording glosses over. Neither paper is chest-specific or region-specific.

**Rep ranges.** "Most rep ranges have similar effects on hypertrophy" [12] is cited to Schoenfeld 2015, a meta-analysis of **repetition duration** — how fast each rep is performed. That is a different variable. The conclusion happens to be broadly supported elsewhere in the literature; the citation attached to it does not support it.

**Shoulder strength transfer.** "Most lifts rely on shoulder strength to execute, so any exercise that builds strength in your shoulders can greatly improve the quality of your lifts" [7] is cited to Schütz 2022, which measured joint movement and loading at the shoulder, elbow and wrist during chest exercises. It describes what happens during the lift; it says nothing about transfer to other lifts. The anatomical claim that follows — that the clavicular head flexes an extended arm and this matters for many lifts [8] — is cited to a StatPearls anatomy chapter, which is an appropriate source for the anatomy and no evidence at all for the training inference.

**Mind-muscle connection.** Snyder & Fry 2012 showed verbal instruction changes EMG amplitude during the bench press [13]. The article's phrasing — attentional focus "may increase muscle excitation levels, **potentially** leading to greater results" — is correctly hedged. This is activation data and the article does not overclaim on it.

## Sloppiness worth knowing about

The article's list of six is inconsistent with itself. The introduction and the conclusion both name "the incline dumbbell fly, incline bench press, **close-grip bench press, cable crossover**, decline push-up and bar dips." The numbered list in between contains "**wide-grip bench press**" and "**low-to-high cable fly**." Close-grip and wide-grip bench are not variations on a theme — close-grip shifts demand toward the triceps, which is the opposite of the article's stated intent. The reference list also carries the same Rodríguez-Ridao EMG paper three times under different numbers (1, 5 and 9).

## What they sell

The programming section links Barbell Medicine's beginner template, and the closing section pitches personalised coaching ("get in touch with us today to get a personalized training program") plus the hypertrophy and bodybuilding templates. They do note that **none of their templates is specifically aimed at the upper chest**, which is a more honest close than most articles of this genre manage.

**Flagged:** the article asserts throughout that specific exercises emphasise a specific region of the pectoralis major, while the only evidence it cites for regional emphasis is surface EMG — and it says in its own words, twice, that surface EMG does not predict growth. That is the overreach, and it is one the article itself identifies without acting on.
