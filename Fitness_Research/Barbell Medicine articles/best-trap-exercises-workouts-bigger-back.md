# Five Trap Exercises, Ranked by EMG Because Nobody Has Measured Trap Growth

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Best Trap Exercises & Workouts for a Bigger Back" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-trap-exercises-for-a-bigger-stronger-back/)) · Barbell Medicine (uncredited) · 2024-03-19 · 4,718 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **They concede the whole basis of the article in one sentence:** "there is little data on what exercises cause the greatest improvements in hypertrophy of the trapezius." Every ranking here rests on **surface EMG activation as a percentage of maximum voluntary isometric contraction** — an acute surrogate, not a growth outcome. This library's standing position on EMG-to-hypertrophy inference (from the Nippard and House of Hypertrophy material) is that activation rankings are a first approximation at best; nothing here changes that.
- **The prescription:** deadlift 3–4 × 3–5 @ RPE 6–8 (their only "primary"); barbell/Pendlay row 2–4 × 8–12 @ RPE 7–8; shrug 2–4 × 10–15 @ RPE 8–9; upright row 2–4 × 10–15 @ RPE 7–8; face pull 2–4 × 10–15 @ RPE 7–8. Programming rule: pick **1–2 priority and 1–2 tertiary** trap exercises per week.
- **The best content is the upright row / impingement section**, and it is the clinical voice the rest of the library lacks. Their position: "shoulder impingement" is no longer a specific structural injury but **a spectrum of clinical findings involving shoulder pain and dysfunction**, and upright rows "do not appear to be uniquely injurious to the shoulders in practice." The first half is mainstream shoulder-pain research. The second half is asserted without a single injury-incidence study.
- **The second-best content debunks their own industry.** They calculate that 1 kg of muscle burns **~13 kcal/day** against 1 kg of fat at ~4.5, so gaining **10 kg of muscle — a matter of years — would add under 100 kcal/day** to total expenditure, and they say EPOC's contribution to daily energy expenditure is likely minimal. That is a fitness company dismantling the "muscle stokes your metabolism" claim on its own page.
- **Heavily commercial.** A coaching pitch is embedded inside the shoulder-pain paragraph, the article closes with programme and coaching links plus an email-list offer, and one of its references is the authors' own UpToDate chapter.

## The exercise selection, and what it is actually built on
The framing is reasonable: rows and pulldowns are not obviously sufficient for the trapezius, which spans from the occiput and C7–T12 to the clavicle, acromion and scapular spine, with upper fibres elevating and upwardly rotating the scapula, middle fibres retracting, and lower fibres depressing while assisting upward rotation.

The evidence problem starts immediately. The deadlift is argued in on the grounds that EMG work shows relatively light (12RM) sumo and conventional deadlifts drive high trapezius activation as a percentage of MVIC — and the article immediately concedes that **shrugs produce higher excitation still**. It then keeps the deadlift as the primary movement anyway, on the strength of its general utility.

**The clearest overreach:** they state that the trapezius works "mostly isometrically" in the deadlift, transmitting force from legs through trunk to arms, and then conclude "you can expect solid growth from this exercise." Isometric force transmission under load is not established as a hypertrophy stimulus for the trapezius, no study is offered for the growth claim, and they have just told you a different exercise produces more activation. The deadlift's place at the top of this list is justified by tradition and general value, not by trap-specific evidence, and the article would be better for saying so.

The rest of the ranking follows the same pattern — EMG comparisons of rowing variants, EMG evaluation of neck and shoulder muscle activation for shrugs, and for face pulls a citation on **deltoid** anatomy and segmentation rather than anything about the exercise. The row section's verdict, that barbell rows suit hypertrophy and Pendlay rows suit strength, is stated as a preference call with "little difference between the two," which is the honest version.

**Fatigue reasoning, which is unusually good:** face pulls are recommended partly because light loads over a small range of motion across a small amount of muscle mass generate little systemic fatigue. That is a real programming consideration and it is applied consistently — deadlifts get RPE 6–8, isolation work gets RPE 8–9.

## The impingement section, and the shape of their pain-science position
This is why the source exists in this library, so it is worth setting out properly.

The historical model of subacromial impingement was mechanical: the rotator cuff and/or subacromial bursa irritated in the space between the acromion and the humeral head, with the upright row's internally rotated, elevated arm position singled out as a risk. The article's response is the current physiotherapy-research position — that "impingement" now denotes **a clinical presentation of shoulder pain and dysfunction rather than damage to an identified structure**. On that view, an exercise cannot be condemned by geometry alone.

Their practical conclusion is load management, not avoidance: start conservatively with load and volume, then progress. That is the standard Barbell Medicine prescription and it is defensible.

**Flagged, in one sentence, per the library's rule:** *"upright rows do not appear to be uniquely injurious to the shoulders in practice"* is an empirical claim about injury rates, and no injury-incidence data of any kind is cited for it — the references in this area cover impingement's clinical definition and the theoretical case against the exercise, not outcomes. The reconceptualisation of impingement is well supported; "not uniquely injurious" is absence of evidence presented as evidence of absence. It is very likely true. It is not shown here.

The clinical-uncertainty note is also worth keeping: they direct readers with existing shoulder pain to seek help rather than self-manage — though the help they direct them to is their own paid coaching service, mid-paragraph.

## The "benefits" half of the article
Roughly half the word count is a generic benefits-of-strength-training section with only a nominal connection to traps: bone density, osteoarthritis, disease prevention, blood pressure and cholesterol, mental health, sleep, and weight management. Read it as search-optimised padding, and note two things about it.

**The good part.** The weight-management section refuses the easy claims. Resting metabolic rate does rise with muscle mass, but the arithmetic (13 kcal/kg of muscle per day) means the effect is negligible at any realistic rate of gain; EPOC scales with training volume, intensity and muscle mass involved and is **larger in untrained than trained** people; and the contribution of EPOC to total daily energy expenditure is probably minimal and understudied. Most sources selling training would not write this paragraph.

**The weak part.** The mental-health passage cites a **consumer lifestyle website** in the running prose as a source alongside its journal references — an obvious lapse for a physician-authored publication. And the reference list supporting these general claims includes an NSCA position statement and a **two-subject case report** sitting alongside systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Because the citation mapping for this article did not verify (below), I cannot say which specific claims those sit under — but a reader should know the list is not uniformly strong before treating any number in this section as settled.

## What they get right about uncertainty
The FAQ is better than the article. Are traps easy to grow? "Probably about as easy to grow as any other muscle… it's not clear if they're easier or harder." How fast? "There's little in the way of research regarding how quickly traps grow in response to a particular training program," and two people will differ anyway. Can you build traps without deadlifts? "Yes, absolutely" — followed by the admission that they would include a loaded hinge regardless of its trap effects because it is a good exercise overall.

That is the correct epistemic position, and it sits oddly next to a headline promising "the best" trap exercises. The honest summary of this article is: *these five are reasonable choices, we have activation data and no growth data, and the ordering is judgement.*

## Commercial note
Stated plainly, without insinuation. The shoulder-pain paragraph routes readers to Barbell Medicine's paid coaching ("our coaching team includes physicians and rehab specialists"). The article closes with links to their training programmes, their fitness coaching, and an email-list capture offering free introductory programmes. One of the references supporting the disease-prevention claims is an UpToDate chapter authored by Sullivan, Feigenbaum and Baraki — Barbell Medicine's own principals. Self-citation to a peer-reviewed clinical reference is legitimate; a reader should still know whose work it is.
