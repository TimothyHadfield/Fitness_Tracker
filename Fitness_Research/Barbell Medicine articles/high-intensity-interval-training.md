# HIIT Works and Is Not Special — 80/20, Intervals Over Two Minutes, and Nothing for Fat Loss

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "What is High Intensity Interval Training and Should You Do It?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/hiit-high-intensity-interval-training/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-11-06 · 1,437 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **They open by discounting their own topic's literature:** most HIIT research is in untrained people over 6–8 week studies, which they say makes it hard to know whether it lives up to the marketing. That caveat is stated once and then not applied to the claims that follow.
- **Definitions.** HIIT is repeated hard bouts alternating with rest, generally above **80% of maximum heart rate** (zones 3–5). **SIT** is the subset performed above VO2max, typically **>90% of max heart rate**, zone 5 and beyond.
- **HIIT and moderate continuous training raise VO2max similarly**, depending on how much is done. Within interval training, **intervals longer than 2 minutes appear more effective than shorter ones** for VO2max. Neither claim carries a citation.
- **On fat loss they are blunt: exercise alone, HIIT included, has very little direct effect on weight**, and there is no significant difference between HIIT and moderate continuous training for fat loss. The benefits they do claim are indirect — appetite signalling, muscle retention during weight loss, and offsetting the fall in energy expenditure.
- **The programming recommendation is polarised: roughly 80% of aerobic training in zones 1–2, about 20% at high intensity.** They immediately soften it — people short on time may skew harder, elite endurance athletes typically skew *easier*.
- **"All out" is treated as a mistake, not a goal.** Repeated maximal efforts are not physically sustainable; performance collapses by the second interval, and training at very high intensity under fatigue is said to raise injury risk and reduce adaptation.
- **Their floor for a useful dose:** 15-minute HIIT sessions including warm-up, three times weekly for six weeks, can meaningfully improve cardiorespiratory fitness.

## The workouts they prescribe

Six templates, given by pace or heart rate and portable across running, cycling and rowing.

*Zone 3–4:* 4–6 × 30 s at 600–800 m pace with 4 min easy; 8–10 × 1 min at 1 mile–5 km pace with 1 min off; 3–5 × 5 min at zone 4 (85–95% max HR) with 3 min rest.

*Zone 5 SIT:* 4–6 × 30 s at 400–600 m pace with 4 min easy; 4–8 × 15–30 s hill sprints with 3+ min rest.

For beginners they give a five-step on-ramp — moderate jogging, repeated moderate hill runs, hill sprints, moderate flat intervals, then flat sprint intervals — on the reasoning that hills cap speed and impact while the tissues adapt. That progression is coaching judgement, offered as such.

## Where the argument is thin

The article's empirical claims are the interesting ones and **none of them is sourced.** The 2-minute interval threshold, the HIIT-versus-MICT null for fat loss, the dose-response between cardiorespiratory fitness and all-cause mortality, and the 15-minute-three-times-weekly figure are all stated flat. Their own opening caveat about short trials in untrained subjects is never brought back to bear on the 80/20 recommendation, which is derived from endurance-athlete practice rather than from a trial in general trainees.

The one place they do attribute is a figure: the heart-rate and RPE boundaries of the five-zone model, credited in the caption to Sylta 2014 and Seiler 2009.

**They sell things here.** A free max heart rate and zone calculator, and — explicitly — a paid Barbell Medicine Plus membership offering the "more in-depth summary, complete with academic citations." The citations are behind the paywall; the free article has none.
