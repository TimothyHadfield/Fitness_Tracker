# Choose Exercises by Fatigue Cost and Repeatability, Not by Ranking — Also Uncited

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How to Choose Exercises for Hypertrophy" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-to-choose-exercises-for-hypertrophy/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2023-03-19 · 1,423 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **No citations of any kind.** No reference list, no numbered markers, no links to research. Everything below is the authors' framework, offered as reasoning.
- The core move is to replace "which exercise is best?" with **four screening questions**: does it load the target muscle through a meaningful range, can you do it consistently, what does it cost in fatigue, and does it fit the rest of the programme.
- **Fatigue cost is the criterion they add that most exercise content omits.** Heavy compound movements produce more systemic fatigue and therefore compete with everything else in the week; stable or isolated movements buy volume more cheaply. That trade, not the movement's intrinsic quality, is what they say should decide.
- **Stability and skill are treated as neutral, not hierarchical.** Machines are not inferior; they may make it easier to train close to your limit, while barbell lifts take longer to learn and are harder to push near failure.
- Their named mistakes: chasing the "best" exercise, **switching exercises too often** (which destroys your ability to track progress), stacking only high-fatigue movements, and treating novelty as a benefit.
- They recommend thinking in **movement patterns** — press, row, squat, hinge — and filling each with whatever variant your equipment, joints and schedule actually permit.

## The framework

*Does it train the target muscle?* Set at a deliberately low bar: the question is not whether a movement is uniquely effective but whether it is **effective enough to contribute**. They say many pressing and fly variations overlap substantially.

*Can you repeat it?* Equipment access, time, preference, comfort and joint tolerance. Their worked example is a pressing variation that reliably irritates a shoulder — a poor long-term choice however well it is regarded. This is the clinician's instinct showing: modify the load rather than defend the exercise.

*What does it cost?* The article's best paragraph. Because hypertrophy depends on total work over time, an exercise that eats the rest of your week may reduce net progress even if it is a good stimulus in isolation.

*Does it fit?* Exercises are chosen as a set, not individually, with variety used to distribute stress across tissues and patterns.

## Where this sits against the rest of the library

This article is, in effect, an argument that exercise rankings are the wrong object — and this library's adjudicated position is that **almost every exercise ranking it contains is built on EMG, moment-arm modelling or coaching feel rather than measured growth**, with only about a dozen claims surviving on hypertrophy outcome data. Barbell Medicine's conclusion is compatible with that finding and arrives ahead of most of the field.

**But it is compatible by coincidence, not by argument.** They never engage the activation-versus-outcome problem, never cite a within-subject growth trial, and never acknowledge the handful of comparisons that *do* show real between-exercise differences — overhead triceps extensions over pushdowns, seated over lying leg curls, squats leaving the rectus femoris short. Their "many movements are interchangeable" is broadly right and slightly too flat: some are not.

Their own sibling article, [the bench press for hypertrophy](bench-press-hypertrophy-technique-programming.md), applies this framework to one movement; a much longer and better-referenced Barbell Medicine article on the same lift is noted at [bench press technique, benefits and muscles worked](bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md).

**They sell the solution.** The article closes by pointing at Barbell Medicine's Hypertrophy I template as the way to assemble these decisions.
