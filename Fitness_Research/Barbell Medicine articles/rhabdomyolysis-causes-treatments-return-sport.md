# Rhabdomyolysis: What the CK Number Does and Does Not Tell You

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Rhabdomyolysis: Causes, Treatments, and Return to Sport" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/rhabdomyolysis-causes-treatment/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2025-08-12 · 4,096 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **The classic triad is rare.** Muscle pain, weakness and cola-coloured urine all appear together in **fewer than 10% of cases**, and **over half of patients report neither muscle pain nor weakness** despite having rhabdomyolysis ([Cervellin 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20298139/)). Absence of soreness is not reassurance.
- **CK >5× the upper limit of normal is sensitive but not specific.** Below it you can be reasonably confident rhabdomyolysis is absent; above it you have learned very little, because **athletes routinely exceed that threshold in normal training** ([Kenney 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22334169/)). Rhabdomyolysis is a clinical diagnosis, not a lab value.
- **CK is a marker, not the injury.** Normal is 26–140 U/L; in rhabdomyolysis it runs from roughly 1,500 to over 100,000 U/L ([de Meijer 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12768237/)). CK itself is non-toxic. **Myoglobin** is what obstructs the renal tubules and causes kidney injury.
- **Statins almost never cause it.** A review of over one million statin users found **102 cases (0.009%)** of statin-induced rhabdomyolysis ([Safitri 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8593596/)), with drug interactions responsible for close to half ([Omar & Wilson 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11847951/)). In a blinded RCT of patients labelled *statin-intolerant*, **25% reported muscle pain on statin and on placebo alike** ([Moriarty 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26687696/)).
- **Discharge is not decided by CK.** Two retrospective reviews (30 and 41 cases) had discharge CK values ranging **1,410–94,665 U/L** and **10–61,617 U/L**, with no association between higher CK and readmission ([Xiao 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9154772/)).
- **Return to activity has four gates** — syndrome resolved, episode adequately explained, risk factors addressed, graded return planned — followed by a four-phase progression that Feigenbaum explicitly labels as expert recommendation rather than evidence.

## What it is, and how it differs from being very sore

Rhabdomyolysis is rapid skeletal muscle breakdown spilling intracellular contents into the bloodstream ([Torres 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4365849/)). The mechanism explains nearly all the complications: myoglobin filters into the kidney and can obstruct the tubules, producing anything from mild recoverable injury to failure requiring dialysis; potassium and calcium disturbances create arrhythmia and cardiac arrest risk; and swelling within a fascial compartment — worsened by the IV fluids used to treat it — can produce compartment syndrome, a surgical emergency ([Odeh 1991](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJM199105163242007)).

The DOMS comparison is the section a lifter most needs. Both involve muscle damage; rhabdomyolysis involves far more of it. DOMS raises CK, but not reliably in proportion to how sore you feel, and not to the magnitudes seen in rhabdomyolysis.

Sitting between them is **HyperCKemia**: CK above 5× ULN with myoglobin in the urine but no meaningful symptoms and no organ injury ([Reijneveld 2002](https://doi.org/10.1002/mus.10232); [Venance 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27922494/)). Both military personnel and Division I American football players have shown this pattern ([Kenney 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22334169/)). Feigenbaum's honest conclusion is that this is genuinely hard for a clinician: some of these elevations are benign, and some signal an undiagnosed muscle disorder that warrants work-up ([Finsterer 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31743252/)).

## Causes, and the multiplier effect

Direct causes are largely traumatic or mechanical: crush injury, prolonged surgical immobilisation or vascular occlusion, restraints, tourniquets, burns and electrical injury — and prolonged strenuous exertion, particularly at *unaccustomed* activities. Indirect causes include inherited muscle disorders (Duchenne's), metabolic disorders impairing energy production (McArdle disease), seizures, medications, supplements and toxins — **stimulants especially** ([Hohenegger 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3387368/)) — infections including influenza, HIV and COVID ([Bawor 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36298810/)), sickle cell, and thermal stress ([Huerta-Alardín 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1175909/)).

The clinically important point is that these combine multiplicatively. Dehydration, heat and humidity, or a subclinical myopathy each lower the exertion required to trigger an episode, which is why an event that a trainee has previously survived can produce rhabdomyolysis on a different day. Sickle cell trait gets a specific mention via ECAST (Exercise Collapse Associated with Sickle Cell Trait).

## The statin digression

This is a substantial section and it is arguing a position, not just reporting one. The structure is: absolute rhabdomyolysis risk is tiny; muscle *pain* attributed to statins is mostly not caused by statins; and the misattribution has real costs because muscle pain drives discontinuation and non-adherence ([Wei 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24079289/)).

The evidential core is unblinding. When patients know they are on a statin, muscle side effects are reported more often; under blinding they are reported at the same frequency as placebo ([*Lancet* 2017](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(17)31075-9/fulltext); [Wood 2020, *NEJM* n-of-1 trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMc2031173)). Feigenbaum attributes this to nocebo and uses it to explain the gap between community "statin intolerance" rates of **8–12%** and the ~**5%** seen in blinded RCTs ([Tobert & Newman 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27578103/)).

The risk-benefit framing is the sharpest number in the article: in 10,000 patients with existing cardiovascular disease treated for five years, expect roughly **5 cases of muscle damage** against roughly **1,000 major vascular events prevented** (JAMA 2016).

**Worth noting:** he does not claim statin myalgia is never real — the closing line concedes "a small increase in risk of experiencing muscle pain, muscle breakdown, and in some rare cases, rhabdomyolysis." That is the correct level of hedging, and it distinguishes this from the more common online version of the argument, which treats nocebo as the whole explanation. The Wood n-of-1 design is worth remembering: it can separate drug from expectation *within* an individual patient, which group-level trials cannot.

## Diagnosis and treatment

CK kinetics, which matter for interpreting a series of results: it rises **2–12 hours** after injury, peaks at **24–72 hours**, and starts falling within **3–5 days**, with a serum half-life around **1.5 days** and a fall of roughly **40–50% of the previous day's value**. A CK that does not fall as expected suggests ongoing injury, an underlying myopathy, or developing compartment syndrome. That is a genuinely useful clinical heuristic and it is stated without citation.

Treatment is supportive — fluids, and monitoring of electrolytes, kidney function, urine output and examination — with the admission decision individualised. Feigenbaum makes a point most sources skip: some exertional cases can be managed at home safely, which is cheaper and generally what patients prefer. And there are no established lab-based discharge criteria, hence the enormous ranges in the Xiao review. What matters instead is normal or improving kidney function and reliable follow-up.

## Return to activity

Four requirements before returning: (1) the syndrome has resolved — pain, swelling and urination normal; (2) the episode has an adequate explanation, and a case triggered by trivial exertion such as light housework does *not*, and warrants work-up for medication, metabolic, hormonal, autoimmune or sickle cell causes; (3) risk factors are identified with a plan; (4) a graded return is planned. An athlete who has not clinically recovered within **one week of rest** is at higher recurrence risk. Return criteria: no symptoms, CK below 5× ULN, normal urinalysis.

The four-phase protocol, which Feigenbaum introduces with "we currently lack strong evidence-based guidelines... but some clinician scientists have recommended":

- **Phase 1** (minimum 72 hours): rest or light indoor activity, 7–8 hours sleep, generous fluids, and *increased dietary sodium*. No moderate or high-intensity activity, no resistance training.
- **Phase 2** (typically days 3–7, gated on CK <5× ULN, normal urinalysis, symptom-free): walking, bodyweight or resistance training below **20–25% 1RM**. One symptom-free week advances to phase 3; returning symptoms mean staying put with weekly follow-up. **Acetaminophen and NSAIDs are to be avoided in this phase** so that muscle pain remains a usable progression signal.
- **Phase 3**: intensity up to **50–75% 1RM**, agility at **70–80%** of maximal effort, running at **50–75%** of normal time and distance, with volume kept low.
- **Phase 4**: full training, volume progressed over several weeks to avoid another sudden workload jump.

The "treat it as a rehabilitation phase" framing carries through: a previously well-trained athlete may reasonably start on a beginner-style programme, which links to Barbell Medicine's own free beginner-programme article.

**Evidence vs recommendation:** this article is unusually clean about the distinction. The pathophysiology, diagnostic thresholds and statin sections carry citations; the return-to-activity protocol is flagged as expert recommendation in the absence of evidence. The one recommendation that deserves a reader's own judgement is withholding analgesia in phase 2 — the reasoning (do not mask the progression signal) is sound, but it is a clinician's judgement call with no supporting trial, and it trades comfort for information.
