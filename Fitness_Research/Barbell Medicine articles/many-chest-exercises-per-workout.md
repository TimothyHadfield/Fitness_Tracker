# How Many Chest Exercises: Volume Sets the Dose, Frequency Mostly Doesn't

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How Many Chest Exercises Per Workout?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-many-chest-exercises-per-workout/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2023-10-31 · 8,560 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The headline answer — **3–4 chest exercises per workout for hypertrophy, 2–3 for strength** — has **no citation**, and the article's own closing summary contradicts it, recommending 4–6 chest exercises *per week* for intermediates and 2–3 per session for beginners on a body-part split. The number you should take away is not a number.
- The load-bearing argument underneath it is well sourced and worth more than the headline: **volume has a graded dose-response with strength and hypertrophy; frequency does not, once weekly volume is equated** ([Grgic 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x), 22 studies; [Thomas & Burns 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27182422/), chest press and hack squat 1×/wk vs 3×/wk at matched volume). So "how many per workout" is downstream of "how many sets per week", and the split is a scheduling preference.
- They **call the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines' rep prescription made up** — the 8–12 reps and the "within 1 rep of failure" instruction — and cite work showing similar strength and hypertrophy at ~4–5 reps shy of failure. That is a rare, correct and unusually blunt criticism of a national guideline.
- They are **sceptical about upper-chest targeting**: incline bench is not shown to preferentially train the upper pecs ([Saeterbakken 2017](https://doi.org/10.1515/hukin-2017-0047); [Christian 2023](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004250); [Chaves 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32922646/); [Kotarsky 2018](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002345)), and cable-fly height is "unclear" for regional growth. This contradicts most of the exercise-selection content elsewhere in this library.
- **The health-benefits half is much weaker than the programming half**, contains at least two clear mis-citations, and includes a figure — skeletal muscle as 80% of resting metabolic rate — that the article's own arithmetic four sentences later disproves.
- Three identical "Find your Hypertrophy Program" call-to-action blocks are embedded in the body, with campaign tracking parameters, plus a coaching pitch at the end.

## Decision 1: how many exercises, really

The article gives three different answers in three places:

| Where | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Intro table | 3–4 per workout (hypertrophy), 2–3 per workout (strength) |
| Beginner section | 1 per day on a full-body split; all three (primary/secondary/tertiary) on a dedicated press day |
| Closing summary | Beginners 2–3 per session on a body-part split; strength: 1 heavy on one upper day, 2 on the second; hypertrophy: 4–6 per week for intermediates, more for advanced |

None of these carry a citation. What *is* cited is the framework that makes the exact number unimportant: volume determines the size of the adaptation ([Figueiredo 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0793-0); [Schoenfeld 2019](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001764)), and intensity determines what kind of adaptation you get. Their own conclusion is that **it doesn't seem to matter how the weekly work is broken up**, which makes the exercise count a personal-preference variable within a sane range.

Their house guidelines, stated plainly: **1–3 exercises per major muscle group, 2–3 sets of 3–20 reps, ~4–5 reps from failure, 2–3 sessions per muscle group per week.** That is a recommendation, not a finding, and unlike much of the article it is honestly presented as one.

## Decision 2: does training the chest more often help?

This is the best-evidenced section and the answer is clean.

[Grgic 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x) meta-analysed 22 studies of training frequency and found an apparent dose-response for strength that **disappeared when volume was equated**; within the volume-matched subset, frequency alone had no significant effect. [Thomas & Burns 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27182422/) trained the chest press and hack squat either once or three times a week at the same weekly volume and got the same hypertrophy and strength.

The obvious objection is that these are low-volume sessions. The article raises it themselves: this evidence covers sessions under roughly 10–12 sets per muscle group, and "less than a handful" of studies have tested whether higher-volume programmes behave differently ([Brigatto 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002563); [Lasevicius 2019](https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2019-0062)) — with no clear frequency benefit found there either. Flagging your own evidence gap rather than waiting for a reader to find it is the thing this source does better than most.

Their practical argument for spreading volume out is fatigue, not adaptation: concentrating sets in one session produces more fatigue than distributing them ([Morán-Navarro 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3725-7); [Santos 2021](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002915)), and fatigue interferes with the motor learning that strength performance depends on. Plus the mundane point that shorter sessions are easier to keep doing.

## Decision 3: beginner, strength, or hypertrophy programme

**Beginners.** The recommendation is deliberately broad — multiple rep ranges, multiple exercise types, no early specialisation — justified by [Ford 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2010.536849) on long-term athlete development and [Post 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546517690848) on sport specialisation and injury history in youth athletes. **Flagged:** both are youth-sport-specialisation papers. Extending them to justify rep-range variety for an adult gym-goer's bench press is a considerable stretch, and the article does not mark the leap.

The blunt passage: the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines recommend 1–3 sets of 8–12 reps twice weekly within 1 rep of failure ([Piercy 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.14854)). Barbell Medicine accept the 2–3 sets, then say the **rep ranges "are made up and not based on experimental evidence"** and the "1 repetition shy of failure" instruction is **also made up**, since strength and hypertrophy gains are similar at around 4–5 reps from failure ([Pareja-Blanco 2017](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2016-0170); [Pareja-Blanco 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12678); [Carroll 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports7070169); [Helms 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247)). Two of those four are velocity-loss studies, which measure percentage velocity loss rather than reps-in-reserve, so the translation to "4–5 reps shy" is inference rather than a directly measured finding. The direction of the argument is nonetheless well supported, and it is materially useful: it lowers the effort demanded of a beginner without lowering the expected result.

Sample beginner press day: bench 3×4 @ RPE 7–8, dumbbell bench 3×10 @ RPE 7–8, push-ups 3 sets to RPE 7–8.

**Strength.** Framed by the SAID principle: to bench more you must bench, and use movements similar in range of motion, joint angles, velocity and contraction type. Heavier loads work better than lighter for maximal strength — roughly above 60–70% 1RM ([Schoenfeld 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200)) — with some work above 90% 1RM ([Androulakis-Korakakis 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports6030086)). That last is **a pilot study in a small group of powerlifters**, which is thin support for a >90% prescription; the recommendation is standard practice rather than a demonstrated requirement. Sample: bench 1×1 @ RPE 7–8 plus 4×4 @ RPE 6–7, close-grip bench 4×6 @ RPE 6–7, floor press 3×10 @ RPE 6–7.

**Hypertrophy.** More exercise variation, more isolation work, higher volume, sets closer to failure. Sample: incline bench 4×6 @ RPE 7–8, dumbbell bench 4×10 @ RPE 7–8, dips 4×8 @ RPE 7–8, cable fly 4×12 @ RPE 8–9, dumbbell fly 4×15 @ RPE 8–9. Hyperplasia is dismissed as, at most, a small contributor ([Jorgenson 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/cells9071658); [Antonio & Gonyea 1993](https://doi.org/10.1249/00005768-199312000-00004)).

## Decision 4: should you chase the upper chest?

Their answer is essentially no. The claim that incline bench emphasises the upper pecs over flat bench is "not really supported by evidence," citing four studies. In fairness to the reader: three of the four ([Saeterbakken 2017](https://doi.org/10.1515/hukin-2017-0047), [Christian 2023](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004250), [Kotarsky 2018](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002345)) are activation or strength studies rather than regional-hypertrophy studies, and only [Chaves 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32922646/) is a training trial measuring adaptations — 8 weeks, untrained young men. That is a real but limited evidence base for a fairly confident negative.

They repeat the scepticism for cable flies: they describe the standard advice (pulleys at waist height for "upper", overhead for "lower") and then say twice that it is unclear whether any of it produces differential growth. Their bench-angle recommendation of ~30–45° is offered as preference.

**This is where the article contradicts most of the exercise-selection material elsewhere in this library**, which routinely treats incline and decline variants as regionally selective. It is also where they are at their most useful, because the reason given is that the evidence is absent, not that the mechanism is impossible.

## Decision 5: how much is too much

Volume has a dose-response "but only to a point" — the point being the individual's tolerance. Increase stress faster than adaptation and you get fatigue, lower performance and higher injury risk; increase it too slowly and adaptations stall. Their stated position is that the sweet spot is wide, that "optimising" the rate of adaptation over a training career is not critical, and that it is not really achievable given the number of variables. That is a defensible and unusually anti-perfectionist message for this genre.

**Two mis-citations sit inside this section.** The claim that muscle growth mostly occurs when muscle protein breakdown is *low* carries marker [27], which is Schoenfeld's 2019 volume study — not a protein-turnover paper. The intended source is almost certainly [Damas 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3792-9), which is reference 29 and is never cited anywhere in the text. The follow-on sentence carries [28] — [Biressi 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ydbio.2007.06.006), a developmental-biology paper on embryonic muscle heterogeneity, which has nothing to do with programme-matched reductions in muscle damage.

## The health-benefits section: read with care

This half of the article is loosely sourced and contains the clearest errors.

- **Injury prevention.** "Strengthening the pectoral muscles by lifting weights may reduce the risk of shoulder and upper body injuries in lifters" is cited to [Byram 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546509360404) — preseason shoulder strength measurements in **professional baseball pitchers**. Different population, different exposure, and a prospective risk-screening study rather than a training intervention. The claim outruns the citation.
- **Strength training is safe**, at 2–4 injuries per 1,000 participation hours against 9.6 for American football, 5.7 for wrestling and 8.5 for soccer ([Aasa 2017](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096037); [Watson & Mjaanes](https://doi.org/10.1542/9781610026109-sports_injuries_ch08)). This one is well supported and is a genuinely useful number.
- **Metabolic rate — the internal contradiction.** They state skeletal muscle is ~40% of body weight and accounts for **80% of resting metabolic rate** ([Frontera & Ochala 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00223-014-9915-y); [Yu 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/2050-6511-14-53)). Four sentences later they cite [Wang 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajhb.21137): 1 kg of muscle burns ~13 kcal/day, 1 kg of fat ~4.5 kcal/day, so **gaining 10 kg of muscle — which takes years — adds only ~85 kcal/day**. Those two claims cannot both be true; 30 kg of muscle at 13 kcal/kg is roughly a quarter of a typical resting expenditure, not 80%. The debunking is correct and valuable; the 80% figure is wrong and should not be carried forward.
- **Back pain.** Acute back pain resolves in 70–90% of cases within seven weeks, guidelines advise staying active — both **uncited**. The one cited study is [Jackson 2011](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181b2c83d): 45 recreationally active men with chronic non-specific low back pain, 16 weeks of periodised whole-body resistance training, showing strength, pain and quality-of-life improvements not seen with aerobic training alone. That is a single small trial supporting a modest claim, which is roughly how they present it.
- **Respiratory and cardiac.** Upper-body resistance training improved pulmonary function in sedentary male smokers ([Singh 2011](https://doi.org/10.4103/0970-2113.83971), small); resistance training in COPD improves strength, endurance, functional capacity and quality of life and lowers inflammatory markers ([Vonbank 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rmed.2011.11.005), RCT). In stable coronary artery disease, combined resistance plus aerobic training beats aerobic alone for strength, work capacity and VO2 max without more adverse events ([Marzolini 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/1741826710393197); [Yamamoto 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jjcc.2015.09.005); [Daub 1996](https://doi.org/10.1097/00008483-199603000-00004)). Blood pressure and lipid effects rest on [Kadoglou 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12020-012-9650-y) (type 2 diabetes, ApoB/ApoA-I) and [Naci 2019](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099921), a network meta-analysis of 391 RCTs comparing exercise with antihypertensive medication.

**Commercial disclosure:** the article carries three identical in-body CTA blocks routing readers into a hypertrophy-programme quiz, each tagged with campaign parameters identifying this article as the source, and closes with a pitch for their programme catalogue and bespoke coaching. The recommendation to buy programming appears in the same breath as the argument that the exact number of exercises does not much matter.
