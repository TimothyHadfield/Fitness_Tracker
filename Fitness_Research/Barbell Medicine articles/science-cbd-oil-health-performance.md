## Bottom line
- **One indication has real evidence: drug-resistant seizures in Lennox-Gastaut and Dravet syndromes**, where pharmaceutical-grade CBD (Epidiolex) roughly halved seizure frequency. That is the only FDA-approved CBD indication, and the populations are rare paediatric genetic epilepsies.
- **For pain, the entire human RCT literature at the time was two trials.** Wade 2003 (n=24 crossover, only 12 completed the full pain assessment, mixed neurological conditions) found a small pain difference and nothing else; van de Donk 2019 (n=20 fibromyalgia, single doses) found no CBD-versus-placebo difference on any pain outcome. The article's conclusion — the data do not support CBD for pain — is well earned.
- **The anxiety result is a single 60-person study with 12 people per arm**, and its finding is odd: 300 mg of CBD reduced anxiety during a public-speaking test, but **100 mg and 900 mg did not**. The author flags it as underpowered, notes there is no established minimal clinically important difference for the scale used, and notes that only the 300 mg arm showed a rise in systolic and diastolic blood pressure during the speech.
- **There is no data at all on exercise performance, recovery, or sleep in healthy people.** The article's title promises "health and performance"; the honest answer it delivers is that the performance half is empty.
- **Product quality is the strongest practical finding.** In Bonn-Miller's testing of 84 products from 31 companies, **only 31% were accurately labelled for CBD content**, label accuracy fell to 12.5% for vaporised products, and **THC was detected in 21% of samples** at an average 0.45 mg/mL.
- **Adverse effects are not trivial**: roughly a third of subjects report a reaction, with reported rates of diarrhoea 9–20%, anaemia 30%, and liver injury 8–17%, plus drug interactions.

## What the evidence supports, condition by condition

**Seizures.** Four trials of prescription CBD in drug-resistant epilepsy, with roughly 50% reduction in seizure frequency against placebo. **Flagged:** the article describes these as "four, multicenter, double-blind, placebo controlled trials," but its own reference list describes Devinsky 2016 as an **open-label interventional trial**, and Hess 2016 is a study in tuberous sclerosis complex rather than the two syndromes named. The two NEJM trials (Devinsky 2017 in Dravet, Devinsky 2018 on drop seizures in Lennox-Gastaut) are the double-blind placebo-controlled ones. The conclusion holds; the description of the evidence base is one or two studies more rigorous than the studies actually are.

**Pain.** Handled carefully, and this is the best section. The article walks through Palmieri 2017 — 12 young women with a post-HPV-vaccine "dysautonomic syndrome", three months of sublingual CBD, two dropouts for adverse events and two for lack of effect, leaving 8 analysed against their own baselines with **no placebo or no-treatment arm** — and says outright that this design cannot distinguish drug effect from natural course. It also pauses to note that the HPV-vaccine-to-dysautonomia link is itself weak, citing the Scandinavian cohort study of roughly 300,000 vaccinated versus 700,000 unvaccinated girls that found no increase in autoimmune or neurological disease. That is a careful clinician correcting a premise inside someone else's study.

Wade 2003: 24 patients (18 multiple sclerosis, 4 spinal cord injury, 1 brachial plexus damage, 1 amputation), each cycling through CBD, THC, CBD+THC and placebo for two weeks apiece. CBD scored 54.8 versus placebo 44.5 on a 0–100 scale where higher is better; **no other outcome differed**, and only 12 patients completed the full pain assessment. van de Donk 2019: 20 fibromyalgia patients, single doses, no difference from placebo on any pain outcome.

**Parkinson's disease.** The article describes a trial finding no benefit for motor symptoms or on the PDQ-39, with an incidental finding in 4 patients with REM sleep behaviour disorder. **Flagged:** the paper in the reference list — Chagas 2014 in *J Clin Pharm Ther* — is the **case series on REM sleep behaviour disorder**, not the randomised trial the paragraph describes. The trial being summarised is not the paper listed.

**Psychosis.** Three RCTs in schizophrenia, all under 100 patients, with very small differences from placebo or from standard antipsychotics — reported via White's 2019 review rather than from the trials themselves. Appropriately downgraded.

**Anxiety — the study the article is built around.** 60 healthy adults aged 18–35 with no psychiatric history, five arms of 12, matched on sex, age, BMI and trait-anxiety score, given CBD 100/300/900 mg, clonazepam 1 mg, or corn-oil placebo before a simulated public-speaking test. Anxiety was measured on a visual analogue mood scale at four time points.

Result: 300 mg CBD and clonazepam both reduced anxiety during and one hour after the speech; clonazepam beat 300 mg CBD during the speech but not afterwards; **only clonazepam produced sedation**; and neither 100 mg nor 900 mg differed from placebo at any point. The inverted-U dose-response the researchers predicted from animal work is what they got.

The article's own criticism of it is unusually complete: 12 per group is underpowered; the authors never published the raw scores; there is no established MCID for the scale, so a statistically significant shift cannot be called clinically meaningful; the participants were healthy people without anxiety disorders, who would be expected to benefit less from an anxiolytic than patients would; and the fixed dosing in a narrow BMI band (22–23) means a weight-based dose might behave differently, as it does for the seizure indication at 2.5 mg/kg twice daily.

**A gap worth recording:** this anxiety trial — the centrepiece of the whole article — is linked in prose but **does not appear in the article's numbered reference list at all**. The list contains three other anxiety papers (Martin-Santos, Arndt, Hundal) that are cited only as a group at the end of the section.

**Nothing on training.** Explicitly: no data on exercise performance, recovery, or sleep quality or duration in otherwise healthy people. For this library's audience that is the finding.

## Pharmacology and delivery, in one place

The endocannabinoid system is distributed across brain, nerve, skin, bone, muscle, gut and most organs, with CB1 receptors concentrated in brain and CB2 in white blood cells and peripheral organs. **CBD appears not to bind either receptor directly** while still stimulating both by a mechanism that is not established — and the anti-seizure effect of Epidiolex is thought **not** to be mediated by cannabinoid receptors at all. The article is honest that the mechanism is unknown rather than papering over it.

Route matters a great deal. Oral bioavailability is roughly **6%**; inhaled roughly **31%** (Zhornitsky 2012). Eating a meal an hour after an oral dose increases absorption and detection time (Millar 2018, a systematic review of human pharmacokinetics). **Topical CBD has no human absorption data** — the evidence is one mouse and one rat study, and the article's defence of rat skin as a model (stratum corneum 18 µm in both species, whole skin 2.09 mm in rat versus 2.58 mm in human) is anatomical plausibility, not evidence of human absorption. It says so.

## The regulatory and product-quality picture

The legal history is laid out precisely: the 1970 Controlled Substances Act made all cannabis Schedule I; the 2014 Agricultural Act separated hemp (≤0.3% THC by dry weight) from marijuana and permitted hemp cultivation for research; the Rohrabacher-Farr amendment let states set their own rules provided nothing crossed state lines; the 2018 Farm Bill made hemp-derived products legal in interstate commerce. The consequence they draw is worth keeping: **marijuana-derived CBD remains illegal even if refined to under 0.3% THC**, because legality tracks the source plant, not the final THC content.

Three cannabis-derived drugs had FDA approval: Epidiolex (CBD; Lennox-Gastaut and Dravet; roughly $1,300–1,500 per 100 mL bottle), dronabinol and nabilone (both synthetic THC, for chemotherapy nausea and AIDS-related weight loss). Everything else marketed for sleep, pain, stress or recovery is making a drug claim on a product regulated as a supplement, which is why the FDA sent warning letters to more than 20 companies in 2019 alone.

The Bonn-Miller product-testing numbers are the article's most actionable content, and they are worse than the general supplement market.

## Against the ISSN position stands in this library

**There is no ISSN position stand on CBD.** The library's stands cover caffeine, creatine, beta-alanine, HMB, omega-3, probiotics, sodium bicarbonate, protein, nutrient timing and the rest — cannabidiol appears in none of them. So there is **no direct clash to report**; there is a silence.

The nearest comparators sharpen rather than contradict this article:

- The ISSN ultra-marathon stand cites **Geyer 2008**: up to **15% of legal supplements are contaminated with banned substances**. Bonn-Miller's CBD figures are worse on every axis — 69% inaccurately labelled for the active ingredient, and **THC present in 21% of samples**.
- The ISSN probiotics stand advises third-party-tested products specifically because of **WADA strict liability**. A fifth of tested CBD products containing an average 0.45 mg/mL of THC is exactly the scenario that advice exists for, and this article's numbers give it a concrete magnitude.

So: consistent with the ISSN's general supplement-quality caution, and an escalation of it. Nothing here conflicts with a position stand in this library.

## Who is selling what

Worth stating plainly, because it runs the opposite way to most Barbell Medicine articles. **They do not sell CBD.** The article criticises a competitor's marketing by name (Beam CBD's "Better everything") and reaches an entirely negative conclusion about a supplement category they have no stake in. It carries no product links and no coaching pitch. It is drawn from their monthly Research Review, which was a paid subscription product at the time, so the article functions as a sample of that — but the analysis itself is not shaped around anything for sale.

## Evidence versus recommendation

The closing line is a recommendation, and it is correctly labelled as one: "I don't think that CBD should be viewed as a panacea, but rather should be approached with caution until better data emerge." That is a clinician's judgement built on an honest reading of thin evidence, not an evidence claim dressed up. The article does not overreach in the direction this source usually does; if anything it is more conservative than its own material requires, since the seizure evidence is genuinely good.
