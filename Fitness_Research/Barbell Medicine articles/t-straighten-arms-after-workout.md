# Arms You Cannot Straighten: DOMS, Its Missing Mechanism, and the Line to Rhabdomyolysis

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Can't Straighten Arms After A Workout?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/cant-straighten-arms-after-a-workout/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-07-15 · 2,309 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **Nobody knows what causes DOMS.** The article walks through five candidate mechanisms — muscle damage, lactate, inflammation, temperature, neural — and concludes none explains it alone. That is the honest state of a 120-year-old literature ([Hough 1902](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajplegacy.1902.7.1.76)).
- **Damage markers do not track soreness.** Blood and urine markers of muscle and connective tissue breakdown do not reliably correlate with how sore you are or with subsequent strength ([Nosaka 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12453160/)), and they peak at the wrong times.
- **Lactate is dead as an explanation.** It returns to baseline within minutes, and concentric-only work generates *more* lactate while producing *less* soreness ([Cheung 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12617692/); [Armstrong 1984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6392811/)).
- **Age and sex do not predict DOMS; mood does.** Anxiety and depression correlate with more severe soreness ([Fabero-Garrido 2022](https://www.jpain.org/article/S1526-5900(22)00002-5/fulltext)) — a pain-science finding consistent with the rest of their output.
- **The best treatment is training.** Exercise reduces current soreness (exercise-induced analgesia) and prevents future soreness (the repeated bout effect). Massage, stretching, compression, heat and cold move the pain rating a little and do not reliably change fatigue or performance.
- **The dose-marker line is the practical one:** constant DOMS means the training load is too high; never having DOMS suggests it is too low. That is a coaching heuristic, offered as one.
- **Protein does not fix DOMS** provided intake is adequate (~1.4–1.6 g/kg/day, their house figure — the **lowest in this library**, below the adjudicated 1.6–1.8 g/kg).

## What DOMS is, and whether it counts as an injury
Their working definition: discomfort in the trained muscles that builds over the first 24 hours, peaks at **24–72 hours**, then subsides, often with stiffness and tenderness to touch. The reliable trigger is **novel and/or high-volume work with an eccentric component** ([Hedayatpour & Falla 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4620252/)).

The classification question is more interesting than it sounds. Most international muscle-injury classification systems *do* count DOMS as an injury — the **Munich consensus statement** grades it **1B**, where "1" means a *functional* rather than structural disorder and "B" distinguishes it from ordinary fatigue ([Mueller-Wohlfahrt 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3607100/)). Fatigue happens during and immediately after a session and costs force production; DOMS is absent then and arrives days later, and is **not** reliably associated with reduced force ([Armstrong 1984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6392811/)).

## The five mechanisms, and why each falls short
**Muscle damage.** There is good evidence that eccentric work produces small ruptures near the Z-disc and disrupts the connective tissue surrounding fibres. The article notes the irony that stretching, routinely recommended for soreness, also stresses Z-band integrity. But the damage story fails on correlation and timing, so it is at best partial. (It also raises the **fascial** hypothesis — that the connective tissue, not the muscle, is the source: [Wilke & Behringer 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8431437/).)

**Lactate, then inflammation.** Lactate is disproven on both timing and dose. The successor theory — immune signalling generating pain and swelling ([Malm 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11412135/); [Yanagisawa 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26155447/)) — is more plausible, and the article's methodological objection is the sharpest thing in it: many of these studies use **muscle biopsies with no control group for the biopsy itself**, which also causes inflammation. Tissue-fluid/swelling variants have not replicated ([Cheung 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12617692/)).

**Neural.** Two versions: an evolutionary protective-signal account, and physical compression of nerve endings in the muscle spindle ([Sonkodi 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7139782/)). They dismantle the evolutionary version efficiently — a warning arriving 48 hours late protects nobody, and peak soreness often coincides with restored maximal force. They add a genuinely useful confound: after single-limb work, the **untrained** limb also loses performance, so fatigue and soreness cannot be cleanly separated ([Newham 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6822050/)).

**Temperature and enzyme efflux** get a line each ([Priego-Quesada 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7558480/); [Castellani 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4873630/)).

## Where DOMS ends and rhabdomyolysis begins
This article introduces exertional rhabdomyolysis as the dangerous look-alike and covers the pathophysiology at an introductory level: ion channel failure, sodium and calcium flooding the fibre, self-sustaining necrosis, myoglobin release causing red-to-brown urine and acute kidney injury through tubular obstruction and third-spacing hypovolaemia, plus potassium/phosphate/calcium disturbance and arrhythmia risk ([Torres 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4365849/)).

**This library already has the full treatment** — see [Rhabdomyolysis: What the CK Number Does and Does Not Tell You](rhabdomyolysis-causes-treatments-return-sport.md) for CK thresholds, discharge criteria, the statin question and the four-gate return-to-training protocol. What is worth carrying over from *this* article is only the **boundary**:

- In rhabdomyolysis the muscle cell is **breaking down**; in DOMS it stays intact.
- **CK is close to pathognomonic for rhabdomyolysis** (usually ≥5× the upper limit at presentation) and is **not** reliably correlated with DOMS. Soreness severity is not a rhabdomyolysis test in either direction.
- The classic triad — myalgia, weakness, red-brown urine — appears in **under 10% of cases**, and over half of patients report neither pain nor weakness.
- **HyperCKemia** sits between: CK >5× ULN with myoglobinuria but no severe symptoms and no organ injury, documented in military personnel and Division I football players ([Gemelli 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34687219/)).
- COVID-19 is listed among the infectious triggers ([Bawor 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36298810/)).

## Risk factors and what actually helps
Risk is dominated by **novelty and volume** — new exercises, higher-than-normal eccentric loads. Beyond that: **age**, no effect ([Sorensen 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29466693/); [Hayes 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10317890/)); **sex**, no effect ([Dannecker 2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3513404/)); **mood**, anxiety and depression associated with worse soreness.

Management, in their order of confidence:
- **Keep training, even if lighter.** Exercise-induced analgesia reduces current soreness ([Sakamoto 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20634739/); [Zainuddin 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16604130/)) and the repeated bout effect blunts the next episode ([Zourdos 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25647658/); [Chen 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30663816/)). Note the honest limit: Zainuddin found the analgesia **temporary**, with no effect on actual recovery.
- **Soft tissue work** — compression, stretching, massage, heat and cold — variable and possibly negligible effects on pain, and no reliable effect on perceived fatigue or measured performance ([Lund 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9764443/)).
- **Programming:** cut arm volume, leave more reps in reserve, progress loads gradually.
- **Protein:** no meaningful effect on soreness once intake is adequate ([Pasiakos 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24435468/)).

**Flagged overreach:** the summary asserts that never experiencing DOMS means "their training load isn't high enough." Nothing cited supports soreness as a training-adequacy marker, and the article's own argument — that soreness tracks *novelty* rather than stimulus — cuts directly against it.
