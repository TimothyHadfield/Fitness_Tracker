# Arms Stuck Bent After Arm Day: DOMS, and the One Thing That Isn't

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Can't Straighten Arms After A Workout?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/cant-straighten-arms-after-a-workout/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-07-15 · 2309 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- Arms you cannot fully extend for a few days after biceps work is almost always **delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS)** — onset in the hours after training, peak at **24–72 hours**, then fading. It needs loaded **eccentric** work and is worst after novel exercises or a jump in volume.
- **Nobody knows what causes it.** The article walks through five candidate mechanisms and rejects or heavily qualifies each. This is unusually honest for a "why am I sore" article.
- The clinically important job of the piece is the **rhabdomyolysis** differential: red-to-brown urine, CK typically **≥5× the upper limit of normal**, and a real risk of acute kidney injury. The catch is that **fewer than 10% of rhabdo cases show the classic triad** of myalgia, weakness and dark urine, and **over half report no muscle pain or weakness at all** — so soreness severity is a poor screen in either direction.
- **Age and sex do not predict DOMS severity**; anxiety and depression do. That is a psychosocial finding, consistent with the rest of Barbell Medicine's pain-science position.
- The management advice inverts the intuition: **keep training**. Soft-tissue work (massage, stretching, compression, hot/cold) changes soreness ratings unreliably and does not track performance; exercise itself reduces soreness acutely and reduces the next bout's soreness durably.

## What DOMS is, and whether it counts as an injury
Most international muscle-injury classification systems do call DOMS an injury. The **Munich consensus statement** (Mueller-Wohlfahrt 2013) grades it **1B** — "functional" rather than structural, with the B distinguishing it from ordinary fatigue. The article uses this to separate two things people conflate: fatigue happens during and immediately after training and shows up as reduced force output that recovers within days; DOMS appears later and, notably, **is not reliably tied to reduced force production** (Armstrong 1984; Hough's 1902 paper, still the origin point).

## Five mechanisms, none of them sufficient
- **Muscle damage.** Eccentric work is associated with micro-ruptures near the Z-disk and changes in the surrounding connective tissue. But blood and urine markers of muscle breakdown **do not correlate with soreness severity or with subsequent strength**, and they peak on a different timeline than the pain. Damage is at best partial.
- **Lactate.** Dead. Lactate returns to baseline within minutes, and concentric-only exercise produces *more* lactate than eccentric-only while producing *no* DOMS (Cheung 2003; Armstrong 1984).
- **Inflammation.** More plausible, and the theory the lactate idea evolved into (Malm 2001; Yanagisawa 2015). The article's own objection is methodological and sharp: many of these studies use muscle biopsies to measure inflammation **without a control group for the biopsy itself**, which is also an injury.
- **Tissue fluid / swelling.** Peak swelling has not reliably tracked peak soreness across studies (Cheung 2003).
- **Neural.** Either an evolutionary protective signal or mechanical compression of muscle-spindle nerve endings (Sonkodi 2020). The article dismisses the evolutionary version on its own terms — a warning that arrives 48 hours late protects nothing, and peak soreness often coincides with force production having already returned.

Their conclusion is that no single theory explains it and that the mechanism question is academic, because DOMS is benign and self-limiting.

## Rhabdomyolysis: the part that matters clinically
**This library covers rhabdomyolysis in full elsewhere** — see [Rhabdomyolysis: What the CK Number Does and Does Not Tell You](rhabdomyolysis-causes-treatments-return-sport.md) for CK thresholds and their limits, discharge criteria, the statin question and the four-gate return-to-training protocol. What this article adds is only the **DOMS boundary**, summarised below.

Rhabdomyolysis is rapid breakdown of skeletal muscle with release of intracellular contents. The mechanism given is a failure of the sodium/calcium/potassium ion channels, causing calcium and sodium influx, further damage, and a self-sustaining necrosis cycle. Myoglobin released into the blood is filtered by the kidney and can cause **acute kidney injury** through tubular obstruction and iron-mediated tubular damage; hypovolaemia from fluid "third spacing" into damaged muscle adds to that risk. Potassium, phosphate and calcium release can drive **arrhythmias**.

Triggers include crush injury, burns, prolonged immobilisation, heat illness, seizures, certain drugs, and infections including COVID-19 (Bawor 2022) — as well as prolonged strenuous exertion, especially at novel exercises. Treatment is inpatient fluids, and full recovery without sequelae is expected.

Two useful distinctions. First, in DOMS the muscle cell stays intact and **CK does not track soreness**, whereas a high CK is near-pathognomonic for rhabdo. Second, **hyperCKemia** exists: military personnel and Division I American football players have been documented with CK above 5× the upper limit and myoglobin in the urine but no symptoms and no organ damage (Gemelli 2022) — so a scary CK number in an athlete is not automatically rhabdomyolysis.

## Risk factors and what actually helps
Novel eccentric work at higher-than-usual volume is the main risk factor. Beyond that: **age** shows no effect (Sorenson 2018; Haynes 2023), **sex** shows no effect (Dannecker 2013), and **anxiety or depression** correlate with more severe DOMS (Fabero-Garrido 2022).

On interventions:
- **Soft tissue work** — compression, stretching, massage, heat, cold. Variable and possibly negligible effects on pain ratings, and no reliable relationship to perceived fatigue or measured performance (Mizuno 2016; Dupuy 2018; Lund 1998).
- **Training** — reduces soreness acutely via exercise-induced analgesia and reduces future soreness via the repeated bout effect (Sakamoto 2010; Zainuddin 2006; Zourdos 2015; Chen 2019).
- **Protein** — little to no effect on DOMS once intake is adequate at roughly **1.4–1.6 g/kg/day** (Pearson 2022; Pasiakos 2014; Kerksick 2018). Note that **1.4–1.6 g/kg is Barbell Medicine's house figure and the lowest in this library** — below the library's adjudicated 1.6–1.8 g/kg. It is used here only as an adequacy floor for a soreness question, which is a defensible use of a low number.

**The recommendation, as distinct from the evidence:** if you are constantly sore, cut arm volume and back away from failure (leave more reps in reserve); if you are *never* sore, your training load is probably too low. That second claim — soreness as a floor-check on training adequacy — is a coaching heuristic, not something the cited literature supports, and it sits awkwardly next to their own point that soreness does not track performance.
