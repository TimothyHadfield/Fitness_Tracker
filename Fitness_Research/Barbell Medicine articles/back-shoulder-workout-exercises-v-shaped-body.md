# A Back-and-Shoulder Day Chosen Entirely on Anatomy, With the References Spent Elsewhere

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "7 Back and Shoulder Workout Exercises For A V-Shaped Body" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-back-and-shoulder-workout/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-07-12 · 5,857 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **None of the seven exercise choices is backed by hypertrophy outcome data, and only one is backed even by activation data.** Of the article's fourteen references, exactly three sit anywhere near the exercise selection: an open-access anatomy textbook chapter on agonists and synergists [1], Fenwick 2009 on trunk activation and lumbar loading across rowing variations [2], and Sakoma 2010 on the anatomical segments of the deltoid [3]. The other eleven support general benefits of resistance training and say nothing about which exercise to pick.
- The selection logic is stated openly and it is anatomical: pick movements where the back muscles are prime movers and the shoulder muscles are synergists (or vice versa), then train each region from multiple angles through large ranges of motion. That is a reasonable heuristic and they present it as one — **they do not claim a growth advantage for any specific exercise over any other.**
- Their own caveat is the most valuable sentence in the piece: "It's not clear that the anatomy related here translates perfectly, as there is a lot of interindividual variation in training response." That is the honest version of the ranking claim that most of this genre asserts flatly.
- The programming is more specific than the evidence: seven movements, RPE 7–8 for compounds and 8–10 for isolation, newer lifters at the low end of set ranges. Compounds first, isolation later, on fatigue-management grounds, uncited here.
- The "benefits of training back and shoulders together" section is where the citations live, and it is the weakest-attributed part. A **case study of two powerlifters** [9] props up a bone-density claim, a VO2max study [12] is cited for **blood pressure and cholesterol**, and the last three bullets carry footnote markers pointing at a numbering series that does not exist in the reference list.

## The workout, in full

One session, seven movements, ordered largest-to-smallest and compound-to-isolation:

| # | Exercise | Category | Sets × reps | Proximity to failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pendlay or barbell row | Primary / Secondary | 3–4 × 5–8 (primary) or 2–4 × 6–10 (secondary) | 2–3 reps in reserve, RPE 7–8 |
| 2 | Overhead press | Primary | 3–4 × 5–8 | 2–3 RIR, RPE 7–8 |
| 3 | Pull-ups or chin-ups | Secondary | 2–4 × 6–10 | 1–2 RIR, RPE 8–9 |
| 4 | 1-arm dumbbell row | Tertiary | 2–4 × 8–12 | 1–2 RIR, RPE 8–9 |
| 5 | 1-arm cable lateral raise | Tertiary | 2–5 × 10–15 | 0–2 RIR, RPE 8–10 |
| 6 | Bent-over dumbbell flye | Tertiary | 2–4 × 10–15 | 0–2 RIR, RPE 8–10 |
| 7 | Face pull | Tertiary | 2–4 × 10–15 | 0–2 RIR, RPE 8–10 |

The general rule they give elsewhere in the article — compound exercises at RPE ~5–8 most of the time, isolation work at RPE 7 or higher — is looser than the per-exercise prescriptions above, and the two are never reconciled.

## Why each exercise is on the list, and on what evidence

The article is unusually explicit that its method is anatomical rather than empirical. Its stated procedure is: identify the prime mover, identify the synergists, and pick movements that stack a "back day" prime mover onto shoulder synergists (or the reverse).

- **Pendlay/barbell row** — trains lats, rhomboids, teres major and minor and traps as primary, with rotator cuff, posterior deltoid, biceps brachii and brachialis assisting. The single citation, Fenwick 2009 [2], is an **activation and spine-load study** comparing rowing variations on trunk muscle activation, lumbar motion, load and stiffness. It supports the anatomical description; it does not rank the row for growth. Their Pendlay-versus-barbell distinction — Pendlay better for strength because of heavier loads and deadlift-like positioning, barbell row better for size because of the controlled eccentric — is explicitly labelled "in our experience". **Opinion, not evidence, and correctly marked as such.**
- **Overhead press** — anterior deltoid primary, with lateral deltoid, triceps, pec major, traps and trunk assisting; the seated version loads the abs and low back much less. The claim that implements produce similar shoulder activation ("research shows similar levels of electrical activity") is **uncited**, and is an activation claim regardless.
- **Pull-ups / chin-ups** — lats primary, with teres major, rhomboids, traps, elbow flexors and rectus abdominis assisting; chin-ups shift the biceps toward prime-mover status via supination. No reference. Their line on grip is the notable one: variations in grip angle and width "will load the muscles slightly differently but likely impart a similar overall training effect" — a deliberately deflationary claim against the online chin-up-versus-pull-up argument, and one this library's other sources broadly support.
- **1-arm dumbbell row** — same back musculature plus posterior deltoid, biceps and brachialis. No reference.
- **1-arm cable lateral raise** — lateral deltoid primary. The preference for cable over dumbbell rests on a **moment-arm argument stated in prose**: the machine keeps tension on the shoulder through the early range where a dumbbell has almost none. No reference, no measured outcome.
- **Bent-over dumbbell flye** — posterior deltoid primary. Their "Pro Tip" claims it isolates the rear delt better than anything else on the list, again with no citation of any kind, activation or otherwise.
- **Face pull** — posterior deltoid primary with lateral and anterior heads assisting, cited to Sakoma 2010 [3]. That paper is an **anatomical segmentation study of the deltoid**; it supports the claim that the deltoid has functionally distinct segments, not the claim that the face pull preferentially trains one of them.

**Summary of the evidential status:** seven rankings, zero supported by measured hypertrophy, one (the row) supported by an EMG/spine-load study that was not designed to rank exercises for growth, one (the face pull) supported by an anatomy paper that establishes the target muscle exists in segments. Everything else is inference from origin and insertion. To their credit, the article never dresses this up — the "Key Information" box says outright that how you perform the exercise changes the loading, that the anatomy may not translate, and that individual variation is large.

## The benefits section, where the references actually are

Eleven of the fourteen references support a list of general resistance-training benefits, none of them specific to training back and shoulders together. Read as a claim about strength training in general, this section is fine; read as a reason to adopt this particular split, it is not evidence at all.

- **Neck and shoulder pain** [4, 5, 6]. The headline claim — an RCT of over 500 people with neck pain, tightness and upper trapezius tenderness in which high-intensity strength training significantly reduced neck and shoulder pain — is Zebis 2011, a randomised trial in industrial workers (n≈537). Andersen 2012 (BJSM) is a second RCT, on the frequency and duration needed. **Andersen 2008, however, is an EMG study of muscle activation in women with chronic neck pain, cited in support of a pain-reduction outcome.** That is an activation study doing outcome work, in the one section of the article where genuine outcome trials were available.
- **Bone health and density** [7, 8, 9]. Kristensen 2011 is a systematic review of resistance training in musculoskeletal rehabilitation; Almstedt 2011 is a 24-week BMD intervention in college-age men and women. **Walters 2012 is a case study of two elite senior female powerlifters — n = 2.** A case report of two people cannot support a population claim about bone density, and the article cites it alongside the review without distinction.
- **Osteoarthritis** [10, 11]. Turner 2020 is a systematic review of resistance-training dosing for pain and function in knee osteoarthritis, and fits. Hong & Kim 2018 is a review of resistance exercise and **bone health**, not osteoarthritis. The article's sentence that strength training "can slow down and prevent the loss of bone and muscle mass due to osteoarthritis" conflates osteoarthritis with osteoporosis; the linked internal article is about osteoporosis. **Flag: this is a clinical error, not just a citation slip, and it appears in a piece whose brand rests on clinical authority.**
- **Blood pressure and cholesterol** [12]. The reference is Ozaki 2013, on resistance-training-induced increases in **VO2max** in young and older subjects. It does not measure blood pressure or cholesterol.
- **Mental health** [13, 14]. Both are apt and the numbers are reported correctly: Barahona-Fuentes 2021, a meta-analysis of seven studies on strength training and psychosocial disorders in adolescents; and Gordon 2018 in *JAMA Psychiatry*, a meta-analysis of 33 randomised clinical trials finding resistance training significantly reduces depressive symptoms **regardless of how much strength participants gained** — which the article correctly reads as "the important thing was that they were regularly exercising."
- **Sleep and weight management.** These last two bullets carry superscript markers `^[1]`, `^[2]`, `^[3]` for a literature review on resistance exercise and sleep and a network meta-analysis of exercise for people living with obesity. **Those references do not exist in the article's list**, whose entries 1–3 are the anatomy textbook, Fenwick and Sakoma. The claims are unsupported as printed, and any reader following the numbers lands on the wrong papers.

## Programming, and what they sell

The closing sections restate Barbell Medicine's standard variables — volume as sets × reps, intensity as load and proximity to failure, frequency as a lever for adjusting either volume or session length, and structure as the split. Newer lifters take the low end of set ranges; veterans the high end; volume is tuned to feedback and results. Their rep-scheme advice is deliberately diverse rather than optimised: some work under 6 reps, some 6–10, some 10-plus, unless a competition goal demands specificity. Movement categories (primary / secondary / tertiary) are defined and used consistently. All of this is coaching convention, presented as such, with no supporting references in this article.

They sell strength, endurance and hypertrophy templates, plus fitness and nutrition coaching, in a "How Can We Help?" block at the end. The offer is described as "evidence-backed"; the exercise selection in this article is not, and the article does not claim it is.
