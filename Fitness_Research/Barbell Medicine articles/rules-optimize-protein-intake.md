# Seven Protein "Rules" From 2014 — Half of Which the Evidence Has Since Overturned

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "7 Rules to Optimize Protein Intake" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/7-rules-to-optimize-protein-intake/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2014-02-26 · 2280 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **This article cites nothing.** No numbered markers, no reference list, no inline links to papers. Every claim below is Feigenbaum's assertion in 2014, and it should be read as a period document rather than as a current Barbell Medicine position — their own later material (the protein recommendations page, the DOMS article) uses a simple **1.4–1.6 g/kg/day** total-intake target and drops most of the machinery here.
- The core scheme is bottom-up: set a **per-meal** dose that maximises muscle protein synthesis (**3–4 g of leucine**), then multiply by meals per day to get the daily target.
- It argues for a **3–5 hour "refractory period"** on muscle protein synthesis, and therefore for **3–5 meals a day at most**. This is the leucine-threshold / muscle-full model, and it is the part of the article that has aged worst.
- It is correct and useful on two myths: you **absorb essentially all** the protein you eat regardless of dose, and **protein does not damage healthy kidneys**.
- It recommends whey over every other protein and then names **Whey Rx, Barbell Medicine's own product**, in the same rule. Worth knowing before weighing rule 6.

## The seven rules, compressed
1. **Enough protein per meal** — enough to deliver 3–4 g leucine. Worked example: about 20 g of whey, versus about 40 g of brown rice protein, for the same leucine. Equal for MPS purposes, not equal in calories, which matters when dieting.
2. **Optimise meal frequency** — 3–5 meals, spaced 3–5 hours. The stated mechanism is a refractory period after each MPS-triggering dose, such that a second protein feeding two hours later "would contribute nothing to MPS" and be stored as glycogen or fat.
3. **Daily target = per-meal dose × meals**, then adjust for sex, age and diet quality. Men are described as slightly more amino-acid-sensitive than weight- and age-matched women; older people less sensitive, so intake rises with age; plant-heavy diets need more total protein for the same effect because of lower digestibility and amino acid profile.
4. **Ignore "you only need X grams"** — because needs (avoiding deficiency) and optima (performance, body composition) are different questions.
5. **Ignore "you can only absorb X grams per meal."** Absorption is essentially complete; tracer studies put apparent absorption above 100% because enterocytes contribute endogenous protein. A 100 g meal takes longer to absorb than a 20 g meal but the first 20 g absorbs at the same rate.
6. **Don't overpay** — the piece puts a ceiling of roughly **$20–25/lb** on protein powder and argues whey beats casein, egg, beef, soy, pea, rice and hemp on leucine and essential amino acid density. Casein is described as releasing amino acids too slowly to raise blood levels enough to drive MPS at ordinary doses.
7. **Don't megadose.** Not for kidney reasons — the article is emphatic that in healthy people there is no upper limit for renal safety, and that urea and ammonia clearance upregulate adaptively. The stated ceiling is a *usefulness* one, argued at roughly **300 g/day for anyone under 350 lb**, on the grounds that habitual very high intake upregulates the enzymes that oxidise protein and displaces carbohydrate and fat.

## Where this holds up, and where it does not
**Holds up.** Rule 5 is straightforwardly right and remains a useful correction. Rule 7's renal argument is also right and is the strongest clinical passage in the piece: high protein intake does not injure healthy kidneys, and the article makes the further point that in end-stage renal disease, *very* low protein diets performed worse than moderate or low protein diets — an observation that cuts against the reflex to restrict protein whenever renal function is reduced. That is a clinician making a clinical point, and it is the reason this article is worth keeping.

**Does not hold up.** The leucine-threshold and refractory-period model in rules 1–3 has been substantially revised since 2014. Subsequent work found no meaningful upper limit on the anabolic response to a single large dose over 24 hours, and daily total intake now looks far more important than per-meal distribution. The claim that a second meal two hours later "contributes nothing to MPS and is therefore stored as fat" is a hard version of a soft finding, and it is stated without a citation. Anyone reading this note against the training-variable material elsewhere in this library should treat total daily protein as the operative variable and meal timing as a minor one.

**Rule 6 needs the disclosure.** The recommendation to prefer whey — and specifically whey isolate if concentrate causes gastrointestinal symptoms — ends with a plug for **Whey Rx**, which Barbell Medicine sells. The underlying amino-acid-profile argument for whey is defensible; the article itself concedes that at high enough total intake the source "probably doesn't matter", which undercuts the ranking. The identification of beta-lactalbumin as the fraction responsible for whey concentrate intolerance is also idiosyncratic — lactose is the usual explanation, and isolate helps mainly because it has less of it.

**Sex differences.** "The more male someone becomes, the more sensitive to amino acids they are" is asserted with no source, and the article immediately concedes that the lean-mass-driven variation in leucine requirement across a 100–300 lb range is 0.5–1 g and can be ignored. It is a claim without evidence attached that then disclaims its own practical relevance.
