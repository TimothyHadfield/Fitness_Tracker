# Seven Protein Rules From 2014: Half Right Early, Half Superseded

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "7 Rules to Optimize Protein Intake" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/7-rules-to-optimize-protein-intake/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2014-02-26 · 2,280 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **This is a 2014 article with zero citations**, written in a bro-baiting register, and it should be read as a period document. Some of it was ahead of the field; some of it is the position this library has since adjudicated *against*.
- **Right, and early:** you absorb essentially all ingested protein — in fact ~110–120% of it, because enterocytes contribute endogenous protein. The "you can only use 20 g per meal" claim is attacked on absorption grounds and correctly killed. This library's verdict, reached from four sources, agrees: **the per-meal ceiling is dead**.
- **Right on kidneys:** in healthy people there is no demonstrated upper limit for renal or hepatic harm; urea and ammonia excretion upregulate adaptively. He also notes that in end-stage renal disease, *very* low protein produced worse outcomes than low or moderate protein.
- **Superseded:** the article's central architecture — a **3–4 g leucine threshold** per meal that "maximises" muscle protein synthesis, a **3–5 hour refractory period** after which MPS can be triggered again, and therefore **"eat 3–5× per day tops"** — is the muscle-full model. This library's position is that **daily total dominates and distribution buys a few percent**, with confidence *low* that distribution matters much beyond that.
- **Not supported at all:** the claim that a meal eaten inside the refractory window "would contribute nothing to MPS and then, by definition, be stored away" as glycogen or fat. No source; the inference does not follow.
- **He sells the product he recommends.** Rule 6 argues whey is the superior protein and closes by naming **Whey Rx, Barbell Medicine's own whey**, available in their shop.

## The architecture: leucine, then a refractory period, then a daily total
The whole system is built bottom-up from the meal. Rule 1 sets the per-meal dose as **whatever yields 3–4 g of leucine**, on the reasoning that this maximises MPS and that if it is maximised, more protein cannot raise it further. The worked comparison — whey at roughly 3 g leucine per 20 g serving versus brown rice protein at 3 g per 40 g — is the article's genuinely useful bit of practical arithmetic, and the caloric implication for someone in a deficit is a fair point.

Rule 2 adds the timing constraint: after a large enough dose, a **3–5 hour refractory period** must pass before another dose produces another MPS response. Hence the instruction to eat **three to five times a day, spaced three to five hours apart**, and hence the explicit rejection of "eat every two hours to stoke the metabolism."

Rule 3 then derives the daily target as **per-meal dose × number of meals**, adjusted by three modifiers: sex (framed crudely — "the more *male* someone becomes, the more sensitive to amino acids"), age (older adults become less sensitive, so intake should rise), and protein quality (lower-quality plant sources require more total protein, which matters most under caloric restriction).

**How this reads now.** The per-meal-times-meals derivation gives roughly 100–200 g/day for most people, and **the article never states a g/kg figure at all** — which makes it hard to place against the 1.4–1.6 g/kg that Barbell Medicine now uses, itself the lowest number in this library and below the adjudicated **1.6–1.8 g/kg**. The refractory-period model has not held up as a *constraint on daily totals*: the library's position is that the 20–30 g ceiling is dead, that 3–5 feedings is a reasonable stopping point, and that the mechanism argument for spacing rests on thin acute data. So the *practical* advice (3–5 meals) survives; the *reason* given for it does not.

The bigger residual error is the assertion that protein eaten inside the refractory window is necessarily oxidised or stored. That treats an acute MPS measurement as a complete accounting of protein fate, and it is stated with a confidence nothing here earns.

## Where he was ahead of the field
**Rule 5 is the strongest passage.** Written in 2014, it dismantles the absorption version of the per-meal-limit myth: tracer studies show essentially complete absorption of whatever enters the gut; failure to absorb would present as post-prandial osmotic diarrhoea; and the >100% figure follows from enterocytes secreting their own protein into the lumen. He is careful to separate *rate* from *extent* — 100 g takes longer than 20 g, and the first 20 g of a 100 g meal absorbs at a similar rate given comparable fat and fibre — and concludes that absorption timing matters to almost nobody outside multi-session athletes. That is precisely the distinction the popular argument still gets wrong a decade later.

**Rule 7 on kidneys** is the other durable section, and the clinical framing is the valuable part: the objection physicians raise runs through the urea cycle, but excretion pathways upregulate adaptively in healthy people, with no evidence of kidney or liver damage. The ESRD observation — very low protein doing worse than low or moderate — is the more interesting claim and is exactly the sort of thing that needs a citation and does not get one.

He does concede an upper limit of *useful* intake, arguing loosely against exceeding **~300 g/day for anyone under 350 lb**, on the reasoning that excess protein is oxidised or converted, that the enzymes doing so upregulate, and that a subsequent drop in intake could leave turnover elevated. He labels this "food for thought" and tongue-in-cheek, which is the right confidence level, because it is speculation.

## Where to be careful
- **Rule 6 is a product argument.** Whey is asserted superior to casein on satiety, MPS rate and duration of elevated plasma amino acids, and superior to egg, beef, hemp, rice, pea and soy — with the important concession buried mid-paragraph that **at a high enough dose this probably does not matter**. The practical guidance (whey concentrate unless it upsets your gut, then isolate; ignore anything over ~$20–25/lb) is reasonable. The conflict is that the article recommends whey and then sells you theirs.
- **The sex modifier** — males needing slightly less protein per pound than weight- and age-matched females — is asserted with no source and is not a claim this library supports from anywhere else.
- **The tone** includes several passages about "bros", Lululemon and combovers that were presumably funny in 2014. They carry no content; strip them.

## References
**This article cites nothing.** It has no numbered markers, no reference list, and no inline citation links — so there was no mapping to verify, and no claim in it can be attributed to a specific study. It refers in passing to "tracer studies" and to outcome data in end-stage renal disease without naming either.

Every claim above is therefore **the author's assertion**, and the accuracy varies accordingly. Where it agrees with better-sourced material elsewhere in this library — absorption is not the limit; the per-meal ceiling is a myth; healthy kidneys tolerate high protein — treat those notes as the evidence and this article as an early, uncited statement of the same position. Where it disagrees — the refractory period as a hard constraint, and the meal-derived daily total — the library's position is the later one.
