# Rehab That Never Gets Fast Never Gets the Athlete Back

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How-To Train For Power After an Injury" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-to-train-for-power-after-an-injury/)) · Derek Miles · 2023-04-03 · 3,069 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- The argument: standard rehab prescribes movements without specifying **effort or intent**, so athletes go through the motions and arrive at return-to-sport underprepared for the velocities and forces their sport imposes.
- Two citations carry the empirical case. A 2022 scoping review of **117 exercises across 41 studies** of ACL reconstruction rehabilitation found the exercise descriptors that determine strength gains were largely **missing from the reported programmes** ([Vlok 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34784243/)). And only **28% of young athletes cleared for return to sport after ACLR actually met recommended return-to-sport criterion cutoffs** ([Toole 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28990491/)).
- **Effort is independent of load.** Even an isometric quad set can be high-effort — "flex your quad" versus "flex your quad as hard as you can and hold it for 20 seconds" — which means intensity can be programmed from the first week after an acute injury.
- The contrarian position: **cueing athletes to "land soft" trains a quality sport does not reward.** In a landing-cue study, soft landings produced **1.3× bodyweight** ground reaction force, self-selected normal landings **1.5×**, and stiff landings **1.8×** ([Guy-Cherry 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30539115/)). Miles's reading is that rehab should raise the floor of force the athlete can accept, not lower the ceiling.
- **Volume outside the clinic is the neglected variable**: an athlete used to 10 hours a week of training who is now doing 2 hours a week of one-to-one rehab is being deconditioned by the rehab schedule itself.

## The two goals, and the programming that follows
Miles states them plainly: **keep the athlete as athletic as possible throughout**, and **return them in the best possible condition**. Everything else is downstream.

Goal one is programming around, not through, the injury. A non-weight-bearing lower limb still leaves a rowing erg (seated, uninvolved leg working) for conditioning, bench pressing with the leg off the ground, and open-chain knee extension on the uninvolved side. The general point — an athlete with a knee injury still has another leg, a trunk and two arms — is the practical core.

Goal two is where the article makes its actual argument: **power has to be explicitly programmed, not deferred until clearance**. His working definition of power is force produced in the shortest time possible. He notes that a squat, a clean and a jump are the same movement at different speeds and loads, with the maximal-strength end and the power end differing chiefly in intent — and that the *intent to move fast* is what should be prescribed, not any particular exercise. He is explicit that there is no best exercise for power development.

## Scaling power before an athlete can jump
This is the most useful section and the one with no citations behind it. The unifying question is "what is a jump?" — deceleration against the ground. A forward lunge technically lands, and can be scaled by how high the foot is picked up, the external load, and how the landing is cued. A rear-foot-elevated split squat performed with rhythmic oscillation can be cued for speed with neither foot leaving the ground.

The progression he lays out: **isometric mid-thigh pull** (high intensity, very early); **spider stomps** (sprint mechanics without large ground reaction force); then **A-skips, B-skips, jumping rope** (which can be biased toward ankle-dominant by cueing limited knee flexion), **oscillating split squats**; then **drop vertical jumps, reactive ball drills, maximum accelerations** once the athlete needs to land, cut and sprint. He concedes that the point at which both feet may safely leave the ground after lower-extremity surgery **is still up for debate**, which is the right admission.

The premise underneath — "athletes typically do not get hurt while jumping, they get hurt while landing" — is asserted without a source. It is widely believed and plausible, but it is doing a lot of work here.

## The critique of protocols
He reproduces a typical 8-week post-ACLR protocol: elliptical, stair climber, flutter-kick swimming, pool jogging; leg press, hamstring curl, hip abduction/adduction and extension machines, Roman chair, seated calf raise, squat to chair, lateral lunge, RDL, step down, step up, seated knee extension; single-limb balance with perturbation. His objection is not the exercise list — he grants these *could* be performed at an RPE sufficient to build strength and power — but that **without prescribed intensity they invite going through the motions**. He then cites a real programme given to an athlete **8 months** after ACL surgery with no sets, reps or intensity recorded and nothing addressing power: compliant with the protocol, useless for return to sport.

This connects cleanly to Vlok 2022: if the published literature does not report the descriptors that determine strength adaptation, clinicians have no template for reporting them either.

## Where the reasoning outruns the evidence
**The landing argument is the weak link.** Guy-Cherry 2018 measured knee flexion and peak ground reaction force under three landing cues in a laboratory session; its own title claims landing style influences reactive strength index *without increasing risk for injury*, which is an acute observation, not a longitudinal injury outcome. The study tells you that "soft" cueing reduces landing force by roughly 30% relative to "stiff". It does **not** show that training stiff landings improves return-to-sport outcomes, reduces re-injury, or produces better athletes — and Miles's conclusion ("'soft' is not athletic", raise the floor rather than lower the ceiling) is a training philosophy resting on a force-measurement study. It is a reasonable philosophy, and it is a stronger claim than the citation supports.

**The 28% figure is used with an unsupported bridge.** Toole 2017 establishes that most young athletes cleared to return did not meet criterion cutoffs. The sentence adds "leaving them at higher risk for re-injury" — true in the broader literature, but that literature is not cited here, so the causal link is imported rather than shown.

**Almost everything else is recommendation.** Three references for a 3,069-word article is thin for this source. The exercise progressions, the effort-before-load principle, the argument that GPP belongs in a powerlifter's programme, the claim that maximum effort should not dominate a programme — all clinical judgement. Miles flags the status of his own programming honestly: "this is *a* way, not *the* way", and "there is no universal optimal load", and "there is no one optimal dose for power training". That is the correct confidence level.

## The section for lifters
He extends the argument to powerlifters, who face no sprinting, jumping or change of direction. His position: they need less volume of athletic power work, not none. Well-designed powerlifting programmes already instruct moving the load as fast as possible, but that is power expressed in a single narrow pattern. His framing is that "powerlifter" often functions as an excuse to skip power and cardiovascular training, and he puts the trade-off bluntly — 2 kg on a competition total against being winded on a flight of stairs or unable to sprint.

The programming guidance is periodised by proximity to competition: near an important meet, specificity dominates; in the offseason, general power work should be part of the programme. He explicitly rejects chasing metrics — no need to target vertical jump height or 40 m time — in favour of exposure to coordination, timing, rhythm, reaction and occasional maximal effort. Loads from bodyweight to a 1RM all qualify provided the intent is to move fast.

**No product is sold in this article** — no template links, no coaching upsell, no supplement. That is unusual for the source and worth recording.
