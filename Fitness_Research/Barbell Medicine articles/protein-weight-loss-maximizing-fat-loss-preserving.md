# Protein While Cutting: The Fat-Loss Result Holds, the Satiety Story Does Not

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Protein and Weight Loss: The Scientific Guide to Maximizing Fat Loss and Preserving Muscle" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/protein-and-weight-loss/)) · Nick Occhipinti · 2025-10-13 · 8,494 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Their final recommendation is **1.4–1.6 g/kg/day**, with **25–40 g per meal** — the lowest deficit target in this library, and below their own house guideline of 1.4–2.2 g/kg quoted earlier in the same article.
- **The satiety mechanism is largely rejected.** Of three proposed routes — gut hormones, circulating amino acids, gluconeogenesis — only the hormonal one survives, and even there the acute hormone rise does not reliably translate into eating less at the next meal or across the day. An unusually honest negative verdict from a source that sells whey.
- **The outcome holds without the mechanism.** In a deficit, [Wycherley 2012](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.112.044321) (24 RCTs, ~1,000 participants, ~12 weeks) found higher-protein arms lost **0.8 kg more weight, mostly fat**, retained **0.5 kg more lean mass**, and spontaneously ate about **300 kcal/day less**.
- Without a deficit, protein does much less: [de Carvalho 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00394-020-02321-1) pooled 10 ad libitum studies in over 1,000 adults and found **no meaningful weight difference**, the satiety effect strongest in short trials and fading with time.
- Their preferred explanation for why higher-protein dieters eat less is **food displacement**, not satiety. They label it a hypothesis, correctly — none of the cited work tests it.
- **They sell the product in question:** their WheyRx whey isolate, a paid dietitian consult, and two of their own podcast episodes.

## Decision 1 — how much protein in a deficit

The article walks from the RDA (0.8 g/kg/day, framed correctly as a deficiency floor, not a target) to the ISSN's 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day for exercising adults ([Jäger 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8)), notes their own guideline of 1.4–2.2 g/kg, and makes a point worth keeping: a protein increase only means something relative to where someone started and finished. The US adult already averages **88 g/day, or 1.07 g/kg** ([McDowell 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25585443/); [Austin 2011](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.110.000141)), about half from animal sources ([Pasiakos 2015](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu7085322)). Most people are not protein-deficient; they are being sold an increase from an already-adequate base.

Then the conclusion lands somewhere the rest of the article does not obviously support: **1.4 to 1.6 g/kg/day**, with the claim that no good evidence supports going higher "beyond satisfying someone's personal preference."

**Flag — clash with existing library material.** This is the clearest disagreement I found. The [2017 ISSN protein stand](../ISSN%20position%20stands/protein-2017.md) frames 1.4–2.0 g/kg as a *minimum* and says greater amounts may be needed when restricting energy, citing [Pasiakos 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23739654/) where both 1.6 and 2.4 g/kg beat 0.8 g/kg for lean retention in a 30% deficit. The [ISSN diets and body composition stand](../ISSN%20position%20stands/diets-and-body-composition.md) goes further on Longland 2016 — four-compartment model, all food provided — where **2.4 g/kg gave +1.2 kg lean and −4.8 kg fat against +0.1 kg and −3.5 kg at 1.2 g/kg**, and endorses 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass for lean trained athletes cutting. [Henselmans](../Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/really-need-1-5g-lb-protein-maximize.md), the high-protein sceptic in this library, still lands on **1.6–2.0 g/kg**. Barbell Medicine's ceiling is at or below everyone else's floor.

The gap is visible in the evidence they chose: their deficit numbers come from meta-analyses of overweight and obese non-lifters at 1.1–1.6 g/kg against comparators eating 0.6–0.8, where 1.6 genuinely is the top of the studied range. The two papers supporting higher intakes — [Helms 2014](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2013-0054), the review titled *A Case for Higher Intakes*, and [Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608) — **appear in their reference list but are never attached to a sentence.** A hard-training, already-lean reader should treat 1.4–1.6 as the general-population figure it is.

The per-meal figure, **25–40 g**, is offered to clear the muscle-protein-synthesis threshold and give a satiety signal. It carries no citation and quietly raises the ISSN's lower bound from 20 g to 25 g. To their credit they immediately concede that **the minimum per-meal protein needed to trigger satiety is unknown**.

## Decision 2 — is protein actually more filling?

The analytical core of the article, and the part worth reading. Three mechanisms are tested against outcomes rather than against plausibility.

**Gut satiety hormones — partially supported.** Higher-protein meals raise CCK and PYY, and to the extent gut-derived GLP-1 is satiating, protein raises that too ([Roberts 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11010056); [Belza 2013](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.112.047563); [Leidy 2015](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.114.084038)). They add a caveat most popular writing omits: mice lacking gut-derived GLP-1 do not overeat or gain weight, so brain-derived GLP-1 looks more relevant to intake ([Smeets 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/138.4.698)). Ghrelin falls after protein ([Cummings 2004](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.00582.2003)), reducing appetite rather than increasing satiety — a distinction they keep clean.

Their handling of **leptin** is the best passage in the article. Leptin resistance has no established mechanism ([Izquierdo 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11112704)); exogenous leptin does essentially nothing to appetite, energy intake or body weight ([Flier & Maratos-Flier 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2017.05.013)); and leptin's strong effects appear at the *low* end, where falling levels drive food-seeking, reduced activity and hunger, as in starvation and anorexia nervosa ([Herpertz 2000](https://doi.org/10.1530/eje.0.1420373)). The conclusion — leptin is a defence against weight loss, not a lever for it — is well supported by what they cite.

**The aminostatic theory — rejected.** The idea that rising blood amino acids suppress appetite ([Veldhorst 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2008.11.014)) fails on its own terms: fasting amino acid levels do not track appetite, and increases in circulating amino acids are not consistently associated with satiety ([Moon & Koh 2020](https://doi.org/10.7570/jomes20028)).

**Gluconeogenesis — rejected.** The claim that protein-driven glucose production stabilises blood sugar and so promotes fullness rests on [Azzout-Marniche 2007](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00566.2006), **a rat study**, which the article does not tell the reader. Human data show higher gluconeogenesis on higher-protein diets but no consistent relationship with appetite ratings ([Veldhorst 2011](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007114511003254)).

Their verdict: hundreds of studies show a modest acute satiety effect on visual-analogue scales, but when the same studies measure energy intake at the *next* meal the effect is unreliable, and nobody has shown it produces voluntary long-term reductions in intake. **"We cannot yet confidently say that more protein = more satiety."**

**Flag — clash on satiety.** This is more sceptical than the [ISSN diets stand](../ISSN%20position%20stands/diets-and-body-composition.md), which calls protein the most satiating macronutrient and cites Weigle 2005, where raising protein from 15% to 30% of energy ad libitum cut spontaneous intake by **441 kcal/day** and produced −4.9 kg in 12 weeks. Barbell Medicine never addresses Weigle. The positions are reconcilable — a real short-run reduction that decays, which is what [de Carvalho 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00394-020-02321-1) reports — but they are not the same claim.

## Decision 3 — what higher protein buys you, with and without a deficit

**Without calorie restriction, the effect is small and inconsistent.** [Skov 1999](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ijo.0800867) randomised 65 adults with overweight or obesity to 25% protein, 12% protein, or usual diet for 6 months, eating to fullness: the high-protein arm lost **8.9 kg total and 7.6 kg fat** against **5.1 kg and 4.3 kg**, with 35% versus 9% losing over 10 kg. But pooling 10 such studies in over 1,000 adults, [de Carvalho 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00394-020-02321-1) found both arms lost weight with no meaningful separation, the higher-protein arm holding somewhat more lean mass, and the satiety advantage concentrated in shorter trials.

**With a deficit, the effect is real, modest and reproducible.** [Wycherley 2012](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.112.044321) — 24 RCTs, ~1,000 participants, mean ~12 weeks, high-protein arms at 1.1–1.6 g/kg (~30% of energy) versus standard arms at 0.6–0.8 g/kg (~20%) — found the higher-protein groups ate roughly **300 kcal/day less**, lost **0.8 kg more body weight, predominantly fat**, retained **0.5 kg more lean mass**, and had triglycerides about **20 mg/dL lower**. The larger [Santesso 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/ejcn.2012.37), 74 RCTs, found the same direction at smaller magnitude: **just under a pound** of extra loss, with waist and blood pressure changes the authors themselves treat as possibly not clinically meaningful.

**For weight regain, one trial.** [Lejeune 2005](https://doi.org/10.1079/bjn20041305) followed 113 adults with overweight who had already lost 5–10% of body weight; the arm given roughly one extra scoop of whey per day regained **0.8 kg over 6 months** against **3.0 kg** in controls. Largest practical effect in the article, and a single study.

**Flag on the resting-energy-expenditure number.** The article reports resting energy expenditure "about 300 Calories/day higher in the high protein group," attributing it to retained muscle. That cannot be right: the [ISSN diets stand](../ISSN%20position%20stands/diets-and-body-composition.md) puts skeletal muscle at **13 kcal/kg/day**, so Wycherley's extra 0.5 kg of lean mass is worth roughly **6 kcal/day**, not 300.

A structural gap: the ISSN diets stand names three things that change body composition in a deficit — protein, resistance training, and rate of loss. This article covers one. Training appears nowhere in its deficit evidence and rate of loss is never mentioned.

## Decision 4 — source, distribution, supplements

The protein-sources section, the FAQ and the supplement guidance carry **no citations at all** — no reference marker appears anywhere between the calorie-restriction section and the conclusion. The advice is conventional and mostly sound: complete versus incomplete proteins, lean cuts to hold down saturated fat and calories, tofu/quinoa/chia as complete plant sources, and the claim that **protein source does not influence strength or hypertrophy provided total intake is adequate**. That last claim is defensible — the [ISSN protein stand](../ISSN%20position%20stands/protein-2017.md) supports it at sufficient doses via Joy 2013 (48 g/day rice matched whey) and Sharp 2017 (beef, chicken and whey equivalent at 46 g/day) — but the article asserts it without a reference, immediately before recommending a supplement.

**Product plug, stated plainly.** The supplement criteria are reasonable and checkable — cGMP manufacture, third-party testing, under ~120 kcal, ~20 g protein per serving, no amino-spiking with glutamine, creatine, taurine or glycine — and the article then names **their own WheyRx whey isolate** as meeting them at 20–21 g protein and 90 kcal. The digestive-issues FAQ directs readers to a **paid consultation with a Barbell Medicine dietitian**. Two of their podcasts and their protein-guidelines post are also linked. The criteria are useful independent of who sells the powder.

## Decision 5 — is a high-protein diet safe

Yes, for people with normal kidney and liver function, and the clinical reasoning is laid out rather than asserted. Hyperfiltration — a rise in GFR under a higher nitrogen load — is presented as a normal adaptive response to increased solute clearance rather than as damage, the point most kidney-protein scares miss. Meta-analytic data at around 1.8 g/kg/day show trivial or absent GFR effects in normal kidney function ([Cheng 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1408424)). Protein above the RDA with adequate calcium may reduce bone loss and hip fracture risk in older people, where insufficient protein is the bigger clinical problem ([Calvez 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/ejcn.2011.196)). Stone risk tracks overall dietary pattern rather than total protein ([Rodriguez 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqaa066); [Taylor 2009](https://doi.org/10.1681/asn.2009030276)). This agrees with the ISSN stand, which has the stronger evidence here — a one-year crossover at 3.3 g/kg with no adverse effects.

**Two citation problems.** Cheng 2024 is an observational meta-analysis of association, converted here to "may even be protective against kidney disease" — causal language the design cannot carry. And the sentence about blood ammonia not reaching harmful levels in a healthy liver is supported by [Tsompanaki 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cgh.2021.11.026), a meta-analysis of **diet and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease**, not of ammonia handling. The mapping is verified, so this is the article's own attribution, not numbering drift.

## Where the evidence stops and judgement starts

The article closes with three summary points, and the modal verbs do the work: protein **may** cause spontaneous calorie reduction; a little more protein **likely** increases fat loss in a deficit; a higher-protein diet **definitely** supports muscle growth and maintenance better than a lower-protein one.

**Flagged overreach:** "definitely" is the only unqualified word in the summary, and it sits on the one claim the article never cites — no resistance-training trial is attached to any sentence in the piece, and the two papers in its own reference list that would support it (Helms 2014, Morton 2018) are never used. The muscle claim is almost certainly true; it is not established anywhere in this article. Nineteen of the 49 references are never attached to a sentence at all.

The displacement hypothesis — that higher-protein eaters lose weight because lean protein crowds out palatable ultra-processed food, and that higher-protein meals are simply less rewarding — is cited to four satiety reviews ([Paddon-Jones 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/87.5.1558s); [Leidy 2015](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.114.084038); [Blatt 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jada.2010.10.047); [Soenen 2008](https://doi.org/10.1097/mco.0b013e328311a8c4)), none of which tests displacement against satiety directly. They label it a possibility, which is right.

The posture is worth respecting: a company that sells whey published 8,500 words concluding that the flagship mechanism for its product does not hold up, that most readers already eat enough protein, and that going above 1.6 g/kg is preference rather than physiology. Read the number as clinical judgement for a general population, not as a target for a lean, hard-training lifter in a deficit.
