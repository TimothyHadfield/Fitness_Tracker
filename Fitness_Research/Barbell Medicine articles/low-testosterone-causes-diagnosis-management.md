## Bottom line
- **Testosterone deficiency is a clinical syndrome, not a lab value.** Diagnosis requires *both* signs or symptoms *and* low blood levels. A low number without symptoms is not the diagnosis, and Baraki is emphatic that "just checking" in an asymptomatic man is unlikely to produce useful information.
- The symptom taxonomy — **specific, suggestive, non-specific** — is the most useful thing in the article, because it inverts the marketing. The symptoms direct-to-consumer advertising targets (fatigue, low motivation, fat gain, poor sleep) are precisely the *non-specific* ones that are usually caused by something else.
- **It is a disease of metabolic health, not of age.** Among lean, healthy ageing men, deficiency is stated as well below 0.5%; among men with obesity and diabetes, 30-50%. Obesity is given a ~13-fold risk increase. None of these figures is sourced.
- **The under-recognised harm of TRT is infertility.** Sperm production can be fully suppressed in as little as 10 weeks, and 4-10% of men never fully recover after stopping, requiring advanced fertility treatment. That is the single most actionable line in the piece.
- **Product content, and it is heavy.** The article defers substantive content to Barbell Medicine's paid Testosterone Course roughly ten times — the physiology, the differential diagnosis, the forms of circulating testosterone, specific supplements, the non-testosterone medical agents, and most of the side-effect detail are all held back behind it. There is also a free "10-step action plan" acting as a lead magnet. The article is best read as a competent primer that is also a course prospectus.

## Why the diagnostic bar exists

The article opens on the prescribing epidemic: US testosterone prescriptions rose nearly ten-fold between 2000 and 2011, and of those treated, one in four had no testosterone level measured beforehand, one in three did not meet diagnostic criteria, and one in two never had levels rechecked. That is the clinical reasoning behind everything that follows — the two-part diagnostic requirement is a defence against a market, not a bureaucratic formality.

**Specific symptoms** (raise suspicion most): incomplete or delayed sexual development, loss of axillary or pubic hair, small testes (normal volume given as 15-35 mL).
**Suggestive symptoms** (common in deficiency but also in other conditions, so investigate both at once): loss of libido from a previously normal baseline, gynaecomastia, infertility, erectile dysfunction, hot flushes, reduced spontaneous erections, reduced genital sensation or orgasm.
**Non-specific symptoms** (so common they are usually something else): depressed mood, poor concentration, low bone density, unexplained anaemia, disturbed sleep, muscle loss, fat gain, low energy or motivation.

The worked example is the point of the whole taxonomy: a man with poor sleep and rising body fat but normal libido and erectile function is *highly unlikely* to have testosterone deficiency and needs a broader workup, not a testosterone panel.

## Causes: primary versus secondary

Deficiency arises from disease anywhere along the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis. **Primary** — the testes cannot produce testosterone — essentially always requires therapy. **Secondary** — the signal from the brain is impaired — often does not, because many causes are reversible: excess body fat, insulin resistance, diabetes, fatty liver, opioids, corticosteroids, and the period after stopping anabolic steroids. Excessive exercise and extreme leanness sit at the other end of the same axis. Secondary deficiency is much more common in practice, which is what makes reversibility matter clinically. (Physiology in passing: ~95% of testosterone is made in the Leydig cells and ~5% in the adrenals; most circulates bound to SHBG and albumin, with only 1-4% free.)

## Testing, and the measurement problem

This is where the clinical training shows. Draw fasted, between 8:00 and 10:00 AM on a normal sleep-wake schedule (otherwise within three hours of waking), not during illness or a course of a testosterone-lowering drug such as a glucocorticoid, and ideally on a CDC-certified assay. Conversion: ng/dL ÷ 28.8 = nmol/L.

Two honest admissions follow. First, **there is no consensus lower limit of normal**. Guidelines mostly use 300-350 ng/dL total testosterone regardless of age, and no study has identified a cutoff that reliably separates men who will benefit from treatment from those who will not — so a rigid threshold is not clinically appropriate, and some clinicians will trial treatment slightly above it when symptoms are strongly suggestive. Second, **repeat measurements are noisy**, and the BMJ tool is offered so a reader can tell a real change from assay variation. Telling a patient to check whether their own before-and-after numbers exceed measurement error is unusually rigorous for consumer health writing.

Free testosterone is said to correlate better with symptoms than total, via SHBG effects: 65-100 pg/mL for both lab-calculated and equilibrium-dialysis methods, 1.0-1.5 ng/dL (10-15 pg/mL) for the less-preferred analog/RIA. Bioavailable testosterone is judged less useful.

## Management: body composition first

The diet section is a series of small effects around one large one. **Energy balance at a healthy body composition dominates**, and testosterone rises in proportion to excess fat lost — or to normalising body fat upward from extremely low levels. The initial target is a waist circumference under ethnic-specific cutoffs.

The nutrient-level claims are all hedged: very low fat diets (<25% of calories) lower testosterone modestly relative to 35-40% fat; the saturated-versus-unsaturated evidence is inconsistent, so they default to general cardiovascular advice (saturated fat around 10% of calories); eating more dietary cholesterol has no strong evidence of a large effect. Severe carbohydrate restriction (<5% of energy) lowers testosterone, moderate restriction does not consistently. Protein has no consistent effect in normal ranges; three small short-term studies found intakes above 3.4 g/kg/day lowered it, and they recommend 1.2-2.2 g/kg/day. On soy, they quote a meta-analysis of 41 studies and over 1,000 men finding no effect of soy protein or isoflavones — the paper is not named in the article, so it cannot be identified here.

**Sleep:** testosterone production is described as sleep-dependent rather than truly circadian, requiring at least three hours of initial uninterrupted sleep, peaking at waking and declining through the day, with morning levels independently predicted by total sleep duration. Target 7.5-8 hours.

**Exercise:** flatly stated to have little direct effect on resting testosterone regardless of modality — weights, sprints or aerobic work. Its value is indirect, through appetite regulation, weight-loss maintenance, sleep, metabolic function and fatty liver. Two further claims are made without support: that exercise improves the effectiveness of testosterone therapy over therapy alone, and that continuing to train preserves the benefits after therapy stops.

**Supplements:** dismissed as a category. Most have little or no clinical effect; where an effect exists it is inconsistent and usually smaller than general health improvement; and a large proportion of testosterone-boosting products are mislabelled or contaminated, sometimes with actual anabolic steroids. Worth noting plainly: Barbell Medicine sells supplements, and this advice runs against its commercial interest in this particular category.

## Therapy and its risks

Monitoring guidance is admirably deflationary — **there is no evidence supporting a specific blood-level target**. Aim mid-range (300-800 ng/dL) and titrate to symptoms and side effects, which usually track excessive peak levels.

The side effects covered: fertility suppression as above; cardiovascular risk, where they say low testosterone correlates with higher cardiovascular risk and that appropriately treating true deficiency does not increase heart disease risk "and may actually lower it"; and prostate cancer, where they say current evidence shows no increased risk with appropriate treatment.

**Flag:** "may actually lower it" is the one place a conclusion outruns what an unsourced article can carry. The best available trial evidence supports cardiovascular *non-inferiority* of treatment, not benefit, and a correlation between low testosterone and cardiovascular disease is confounded by the metabolic illness the article itself identifies as the main cause of low testosterone. Stated with no citation, this reads more confidently than the field warrants.
