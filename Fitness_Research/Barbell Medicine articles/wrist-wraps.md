# Wrist Wraps Do Not Make You Stronger, and That Is Not Why People Wear Them

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "What Are Wrist Wraps For?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/what-are-wrist-wraps-for/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-08-08 · 2,628 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **Wrist wraps do not reliably change grip strength** — flexible, stiff or athletic tape, no difference from no wrap across four studies.
- **They do not change 1RM either.** The only head-to-head is tiny: 9 men and 9 women benching a 1RM with no wrap, a flexible wrap and a stiff wrap. No significant differences. A survey of 70 competitive powerlifters found **71% wrap for squats and 89% for bench**, with no correlation between wrap use and strength.
- What *does* show up consistently is **subjective stability** and **wrist joint position sense** — an RCT in healthy subjects found external supports improved position sense. So the honest summary is: they feel more stable, and that feeling is measurable, but it does not convert into load.
- On injury: **no study has tested wrist wraps against injury rates in any barbell sport.** Their recommendation — wraps for people who *currently have* wrist pain, not as prophylaxis — is a clinical judgement built on an adjacent RCT in dorsal wrist pain, and they say so.
- They kill the "wraps weaken your wrists" myth on reasoning rather than evidence: the tissues are still loaded, and no data show wrap users have weaker grips or less range of motion. Absence of evidence, stated as such.

## Wraps versus straps, which the article separates properly
Wrist **wraps** encircle and compress the joint (woven elastic, velcro, on and off between sets; athletic tape counts by function). Wrist **straps** loop around the wrist *and* the bar to hold the grip together — a different job entirely.

That distinction matters for reading the evidence, because the two ergogenic findings in the reference list are about **straps**: they let lifters move more weight and complete more reps when grip is the limiter. The article does not smuggle those results across to wraps, which is the right call and worth noticing.

## What the evidence actually supports
**Grip strength.** Dynamometry studies find no reliable change with a wrap, whatever the material — including a taping study from 1997 and CrossFit-population testing. The article's *compression* grip (forearm below the hand: bench, press, squat) versus *tension* grip (forearm above: rows, pulldowns, deadlifts, carries) is useful vocabulary; no claim rests on it.

**Lifting performance.** Two studies, both weak. The powerlifter survey is cross-sectional and self-reported — it tells you what competitors do (stiff 60–99 cm wraps for bench; flexible 60–99 cm or 100 cm+ for squat) and nothing about whether it works. The 1RM comparison is n=18 and null. The correct conclusion is not "wraps don't work" but "**no adequately powered study has found a performance effect**", and the article states the null cleanly without overclaiming a proof of absence.

**Stability.** This is where the article is at its most careful. Subjective stability ratings favour wraps consistently, and a randomised trial found two different external supports improved wrist joint position sense equally. Then they add the caveat themselves: both "stability" and "position sense" are murky constructs with questionable measurement reliability, so the defensible statement is that **some people feel more stable in a wrap**. That is honest about a soft outcome.

## The injury reasoning, which is the clinical part
The chain here is explicitly inferential and the article does not hide the joins:

1. Resistance training injury rates are **2–4 per 1,000 hours** across bodybuilding, powerlifting, weightlifting and CrossFit.
2. Most of those injuries are not catastrophic — they resolve without specialist care, with **average symptom duration under two weeks**. (This comes from a study of *elite* weight and power lifters, so generalising to gym-goers is a stretch the article makes silently.)
3. Roughly **half of powerlifting injuries involve the bench press**, and wrist injuries are common in CrossFit — so a wrist support is plausible.
4. People with existing dorsal wrist pain had better range of motion and less pain during activity with a carpal-stabilising tape (a randomised controlled trial).
5. Therefore: **wraps probably do not reduce injury risk; they are a training aid for people who already have wrist pain.**

Step 5 is the clinically valuable claim and it is the one they commit to. It is consistent with their broader position that pain is managed by modifying load and inputs rather than by resting — a wrap here is a way to keep training, not armour.

## Practical guidance, and where it is opinion
Everything from here is preference, and the article says so repeatedly. Put wraps on near the end of warm-ups so the working sets feel the same. The author used them for years on heavy squats, benches and presses, stopped after an unrelated shoulder injury, and then set squat and bench PRs — an anecdote deployed *against* their necessity, which is at least the honest direction to use one. Competitors should check their federation's approved-equipment list.

Their picks: a medium (60 cm) flexible wrap as the do-it-all; medium or large stiff wraps for competitive powerlifters wanting maximum support; an 18" Rogue wrap for CrossFit/weightlifting; a small flexible wrap for managing pain. The closing verdict is the article's own — **accessories are a relatively unimportant part of training**; use them if they help you train consistently, ignore them otherwise.
