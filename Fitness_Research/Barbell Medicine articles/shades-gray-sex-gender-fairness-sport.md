# Sex, Gender and Eligibility in Sport: What the Evidence Supports and Where the Argument Runs Past It

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Shades of Gray: Sex, Gender, and Fairness in Sport" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/shades-of-gray-sex-gender-and-fairness-in-sport/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2019-08-06 · 15,072 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

This is a note about what a contested article argues and how well its citations support it. It is not an argument of my own. Where the evidence and the conclusion diverge, I say so in both directions.

## Bottom line
- The article's strongest, best-supported section is the **history of sex testing**: every method tried — physical inspection, buccal smear, SRY testing — failed on its own terms, and the article's sources say so ([Ferguson-Smith 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1817477/), [Heggie 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20980057/)). This part is uncontroversial among historians of the topic.
- Its central **performance number is well sourced**: a stable ~10–12% men-vs-women gap in world records since ~1983 ([Thibault 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24149688/)), widening to >15% in upper-body power events and narrowing below 5% in ultra-endurance swimming ([Sandbakk 2018](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2017-0196)). The barbell-sport gap it reports (~37% weightlifting; 40–70% powerlifting) is real but the powerlifting figure comes from **an unpublished federation analysis relayed by personal communication**, not a paper.
- The **testosterone argument is the weakest link.** The papers cited ([Karkazis 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22694023/), [Morton 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00154.2016), [Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30356739/)) show that variation *within* a sex's normal range predicts performance and hypertrophy poorly. The article uses that to cast doubt on testosterone as an explanation for the *between-sex* gap — a different claim, and one those papers were not designed to address.
- The **direct evidence on transitioned athletes is thin and the article says so plainly**: essentially four studies as of 2019, the largest performance dataset being eight self-selected runners' race times ([Harper 2015](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Race-Times-for-Transgender-Athletes-Harper/1e6abd2c1e03ba88e9ac8da94ea1d69ff3f4878a)). Its critique of the Wiik study's control group and missing individual data is genuinely rigorous.
- Its most-quoted line — **"the phrase 'biological sex' is meaningless and is ill-advised"** — is a definitional claim, not a finding. The cited prevalence data ([Dreger 1998](https://doi.org/10.2307/3528648)) put "not XX and not XY" at roughly 1 in 1,666 births. That falsifies a strict genetic binary; it does not make the category meaningless, and no cited paper says it does.
- Nothing in this article sells a Barbell Medicine product. It is one of the few long pieces on the site with no commercial hook.

## Decision 1: Can any test define who belongs in the women's division?

The article's answer is no, and this is where its evidence is strongest.

The definitional groundwork: sex is assigned at birth from external genitalia plus chromosomes ([Raveenthiran 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28920018/), [Frankowski 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15173519/)); gender refers to socially constructed roles and norms ([Short 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23927517/)). Differences of sex development — 46,XX phenotypic males carrying SRY, 46,XY phenotypic females with Y-chromosome mutations, monosomies and polysomies — occur at roughly **1 per 1,000–2,000 live births**, and the narrower "neither XX nor XY" figure at about **1 in 1,666** ([Dreger 1998](https://doi.org/10.2307/3528648); Blackless 2000, unresolved). The article then reports a figure that matters much more for sport: **46,XY prevalence in elite female athletes is about 7 per 1,000, roughly 140× the general-population rate** (Bermon 2014, cited without a working link), implying ~32 of the 4,676 women at London 2012.

The historical record supports the "no workable test" conclusion directly. Pre-1966 federations accepted medical certificates; 1966–67 brought compulsory visual genital inspection ([Ferguson-Smith 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1817477/), [Heggie 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20980057/)); 1967–96 the buccal smear, abandoned as unreliable ([Genel 2005](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(05)67843-9/fulltext), unresolved); 1996 SRY testing, which failed 8 of 3,387 women at Atlanta, **all of whom were then permitted to compete** — the clearest demonstration that the test did not decide anything. Universal testing was dropped from Sydney 2000 onward in favour of suspicion-based referral, which produced the Chand and Semenya cases.

The article also documents that governing bodies do not define the terms they regulate. It notes the IWF's 2019 technical rules use "women" 42 times, "gender" 23 times and "sex" zero times, while specifying the permitted sock colour for female technical officials. That is a rhetorical jab, but the underlying point — that no federation surveyed publishes a definition of who is a woman — is checkable and appears correct for the organisations listed (IOC, NCAA, CrossFit, USAW, USAPL/IPF).

**Where this outruns its evidence.** Concluding that eligibility cannot be regulated "using science" is defensible from the record above. Concluding that "the phrase 'biological sex' is meaningless" is a stronger, categorical claim that none of the cited sources make; the same data are usually read as showing a bimodal distribution with rare intermediate cases. The article is arguing philosophy of classification here while the citations behind it are epidemiology.

## Decision 2: How large is the gap, and what causes it?

**The number.** [Thibault 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24149688/) compared world records and top-10 performances across events: gap stabilised after 1983 at 10.0% ± 2.94 (top-ten analysis: 11.7% from 1982), ranging 5.5% (800 m freestyle) to 18.8% (long jump). [Sandbakk 2018](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2017-0196) corroborates, with >15% where upper-body power dominates and <5% in ultra-endurance swimming — where women hold the Catalina Channel and Manhattan Island records outright. Olympic weightlifting sits at 36.8% (Thibault), and the article reports 40–70% for IPF powerlifting depending on the statistical approach. **That last figure is a personal communication from USAPL's TUE chair, not published data**, and the article labels it as such.

**The causal question.** The article argues the barbell-sport outlier is partly artefactual: women were barred from Olympic weightlifting until Sydney 2000, from Paralympic bench press until 2000 (men from 1992), and the first women's World Games powerlifting data are from 1997 versus 1981 for men. Participation asymmetry persists — USAPL's ~22,000 members ran 2:1 men-to-women in 2018, improved from 4:1 in 2014 — and every national delegation first fielded a woman only at London 2012 ([Capranica 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23302143/)). About 50 women's weightlifting world records were held by Chinese athletes, which the article reads as evidence of pipeline effects rather than biology (Guillaume 2009, unresolved). This is a plausible and well-argued point, but it is **inference from participation history, not a decomposition of variance**; no cited study partitions the gap into biological and sociocultural components.

**The biological differences, and a recurring methodological move.** The article grants real differences: men average 10–15 cm taller ([Courant 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19933393/)); 30–40% more lean mass ([Janssen 2000](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.2000.89.1.81)); ~48% and ~34% greater upper- and lower-body strength ([Miller 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00235103)); hemoglobin ~12% higher ([Shahani 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19494706/)); ~5-fold higher testosterone ([Clark 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30136295/)).

It then repeatedly reports that these differences shrink or vanish **after normalisation**: lean-mass difference falls to ~10% when corrected for height and weight ([Kirchengast 2010](https://doi.org/10.1080/09751270.2010.11885146)); strength differences disappear when normalised to muscle cross-sectional area ([Miller 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00235103), [Bishop 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3608972/), [Hannah 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22308163/)); peak acceleration power in 100 world-class sprinters is equivalent once normalised for body mass ([Slawinski 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26644061/)); Wingate power in 155 untrained students is equivalent per kg fat-free mass ([Perez-Gomez 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18084774/)). Fibre-type composition does not differ by sex and tracks training status instead — elite women weightlifters showed 71% type IIa versus 63% in national-level men ([Serrano 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30917128/)). Training responsiveness is similar between sexes ([Abe 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10638374/), [Gentil 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26893958/)), and twin data attribute 50–60% of muscle and strength variance to non-sex-specific genetics ([Silventoinen 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14624724/), [Beunen 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15604933/), [Silventoinen 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18271028/)).

**Flag:** every one of those normalised findings is real, and every one of them removes the quantity that actually decides sporting contests. Nobody wins a 100 m or a squat by force per square centimetre. That muscle is equally strong per unit area is a statement about muscle tissue; it is not a statement about who crosses the line first. The article does not surface this distinction, and the reader should carry it through the whole section.

The Handelsman-derived argument about the Y chromosome is used the same way: because SHOX sits on both X and Y and 47,XYY and 47,XXY individuals reach similar heights, the article concludes the Y chromosome's height effect is negligible. That is Handelsman's own reasoning, but Handelsman's conclusion is that **testosterone is the dominant driver** — the article cites the negative half of his argument and treats the positive half as unsettled.

## Decision 3: Is testosterone a defensible eligibility criterion?

This is the crux, and the article presses hard against it.

The regulatory history: the IAAF's 2011 hyperandrogenism rules required <10 nmol/L plus, if uncertainty remained, physical examination for "feminine" characteristics via the Tanner-Whitehouse scale; the 2018 revision cut the threshold to 5 nmol/L and **removed all physical-exam criteria**. The IOC's 2015 transgender consensus set <10 nmol/L for 12 months and a four-year binding gender declaration; USAW requires two years of suppression; USAPL and IPF bar transgender women from the women's division outright and bar anyone on testosterone entirely, with no TUE available.

The evidence marshalled against testosterone thresholds:
- **Within-range variation predicts little.** Men at the top of the normal range do not outperform men at the bottom ([Karkazis 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22694023/)); training-study data find neither systemic hormones nor load determine hypertrophy or strength gains, with androgen-receptor content a better correlate ([Morton 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00154.2016), [Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30356739/)).
- **The ranges overlap in athletes.** [Bermon 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28673896/) found 101 of 795 elite men (13%) had free testosterone within the female reference range (<0.23 nmol/L).
- **The IAAF's own advantage data are inconsistent.** Bermon 2017's top-versus-bottom-tertile advantages were 2.73% (400 m), 2.78% (400 m H), 1.78% (800 m), 4.53% (hammer) and 2.94% (pole vault) — but the same dataset showed *negative* associations in the 100 m (−2.63%), 100 m hurdles (−1.69%), 200 m (−1.59%) and javelin (−2.53%). Averaged across all events the advantage is 0.31% (running) and 1.07% (non-running) (Bermon 2018 correction, unresolved link). The article reports that retraction was formally requested (Pielke 2018, on an advocacy site).
- **Asymmetry.** No men's division anywhere sets a testosterone ceiling; CAS found requiring the test of women only to be *prima facie* discriminatory (CAS 2015).

The CAS Chand award is the article's anchor: IAAF's own evidence put the men-women gap at 10–12% and the hyperandrogenic-women advantage at 1–3%, which CAS held insufficient to justify separating the female category. The article extrapolates this into a working precedent — an advantage under ~3% should be tolerated, above 10–12% should not.

**Two things to flag.** First, the within-range studies do not bear on the between-sex question; conflating them is the article's main analytical stretch, and its own aside ("the effect of different testosterone levels on the performance gap is a different story") is dropped by the time the summary bullets are written. Second, the article's precedent rests entirely on Chand (2015). It does not engage with the CAS award in *Semenya v. IAAF*, decided in April 2019 — months before publication — which upheld the DSD regulations as a necessary and proportionate means of preserving the female category. A reader should not take the "1–3% precedent" framing as the settled state of sports arbitration.

## Decision 4: What does transition actually do to a body?

Four studies, all small.

- **[Gooren & Bunck 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15476439/)** (Eur J Endocrinol): trans men on 250 mg testosterone fortnightly gained 3.4 kg body weight and ~47 cm² thigh muscle CSA at one year. Trans women on cyproterone acetate then estrogen gained 3.8 kg body weight but lost ~40 cm² CSA at one year, falling to 271 cm² by three years, with hemoglobin and IGF-1 down. The authors' own conclusion is the informative one: post-suppression trans women's mean muscle area remained significantly greater than untreated trans men's, "though with an overlap that was almost complete."
- **[Harper 2015](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Race-Times-for-Transgender-Athletes-Harper/1e6abd2c1e03ba88e9ac8da94ea1d69ff3f4878a)**: eight transgender women's race times before and after transition, age-graded. Post-transition age gradings were near-identical to pre-transition; two runners were better as men, one better as a woman; changes tracked training habits. **n = 8, self-selected, retrospective, no controls.** The article notes the sample size but still cites it in its conclusions as having "found no performance advantage."
- **[T'Sjoen 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19121966/)**: 50 trans women, 12 months of HRT, "strikingly lower muscle mass" and higher fat mass — but the article notes **no absolute values were reported**.
- **Wiik et al.**: 11 trans women, 12 trans men, one year. Trans women lost 5% thigh volume with no change in isometric knee extension or flexion strength; trans men gained 15% volume, +10% extension and +26% flexion. Trans women retained greater muscle mass than trans men at 12 months.

The article's critique of Wiik is its best piece of methodological work: the cisgender comparison groups may not have matched activity, diet or testing protocol, and since both transgender groups improved on repeated testing, a learning effect may have inflated the apparent gap between post-transition trans women and untested cis men. It also notes no individual-level data were reported, so inter-individual variability — which is large for every training adaptation — is invisible.

**A structural note.** Two later reviews pointing the other way — [Hilton & Lundberg 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33289906/), which argues testosterone suppression leaves substantial strength and muscle advantages intact, and [Harper 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33648944/), a systematic review finding retained muscle mass and grip strength — appear as a **two-sentence insertion** in a 2019 article. They are acknowledged as "food for thought" and then neither the Recommendations nor the Executive Summary is revised to account for them. A reader deciding what to believe should weight those two reviews independently rather than through this article's framing of them.

## Decision 5: What follows for policy

The article's three options, stated as its own recommendations rather than as findings:

1. **Treat gender like nationality** — registration and adult affidavit, changeable no more than once every four years, with sport-by-sport tailoring for transgender athletes and a universal policy for DSD athletes.
2. **Add a third division** — the LGBT International Powerlifting Championships' "MX" division as precedent, supported in principle by [Harper 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29420345/). The article lists its own four objections honestly, including that it forces self-disclosure ([Ellis 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/14681994.2014.902925)) and could function as a de facto ban where participation is too low to field a field.
3. **Status quo**, with the observation that it may conflict with Olympic Charter Principles 4 and 6.

The harm-side evidence is real and cited: 25 million transgender people worldwide, 0.3–0.6% of population (Winter 2016, unresolved link); 50.8% lifetime suicide attempt rate in trans-masculine adolescents and 29.9% in trans-feminine, versus 17.6% and 9.9% in cisgender peers, in a sample over 120,000 ([Toomey 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30206149/)); UK data on avoiding gyms specifically for fear of harassment ([Ellis 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/14681994.2014.902925)); physical inactivity as the fourth-ranked global mortality risk factor (WHO 2009).

**The load-bearing sentence is normative, and the article knows it:** "We should be hesitant to further exclude transgender athletes based on undemonstrated harms to competition when real harms to the excluded individuals are already recognized." That is an argument about where the burden of proof should sit under uncertainty. It is a defensible position, it is not derived from any cited study, and the article's own executive summary concedes "a scientific consensus does not yet exist."

Also worth noting for balance: the article makes a further claim — that biological-underpinning evidence exists for gender identity itself, from DSD reassignment cohorts where 22% and 50% of 46,XY individuals raised female later changed identity to male ([Saraswat 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25667367/)), 40% concordance in identical versus 0% in fraternal twins ([Heylens 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22146048/)), and cortical thickness differences ([Luders 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23724358/)). These are small case-report-literature and imaging studies, and Saraswat's own framing ("although the cohort sizes in these studies were small") is quoted accurately.

**Currency warning:** this is a 2019 article. Everything after — the 2021 IOC Framework replacing the 2015 consensus, World Athletics' 2023 restrictions, and successive federation reversals — is outside it. Its history, its physiology and its critique of the Bermon data age well; its policy landscape does not.
