# A Pull Day Where None of the Seven Exercise Choices Has Growth Data Behind It

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Best Pull Day Workout Routine & Exercises That Are Effective" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-pull-day-workout-routine-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-01-30 · 6,688 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- The routine: **Pendlay/barbell row** (3–4 × 5–8), **chin-ups** (3–4 × 5–8), **seated cable row** (2–4 × 6–10), **lat pulldown** (2–4 × 8–12) at RPE 7–8; **bent-over dumbbell flye** (2–4 × 10–15), **preacher curl** (3–5 × 8–12), **concentration curl** (3–5 × 10–15) at RPE 8–9. Compounds first, isolation last.
- **Not one of the seven selections is supported by a hypertrophy outcome study.** Five carry no citation at all. The two that do — lat pulldown grip width and the preacher curl — both cite **EMG**, and in the lat pulldown case the article converts an activation/strength paper into a claim about "muscle development".
- The volume/frequency guidance is the part that *is* backed by outcome data: 2×/week per muscle group ([Schoenfeld 2016 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8)), ~6–10 sets/week for back and biceps for newer lifters, roughly double for advanced.
- The nutrition half is better sourced than the training half: 1.6 g/kg/day protein from [Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608) (49 RCTs, 1,863 participants — the article miscounts it as "89 articles"), 3–5 g/kg/day carbohydrate, and a genuinely rigorous, deflationary supplement section.
- **They debunk vitamin D, fish oil and multivitamins using USPSTF and AHA-grade evidence, then immediately recommend their own caffeinated pre-workout and two flavours of their own whey isolate.** Worth knowing before weighing the section.
- Timeline claim, and the best-sourced number in the article: across 40 resistance training studies, significant strength gains appeared at **4.3 weeks on average** (range 1–12 weeks), with squat 1RM improvements from **3% to 40%** ([Lambrianides 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000004300)).

## The seven exercises: what backs each one

Taking the instruction to be explicit, exercise by exercise:

**Pendlay/barbell row (primary).** Described as "the bench press of the back muscles", said to work lats, biceps, rear delts, rhomboids, glutes and hamstrings, and to "benefit individuals who are experiencing pain in their backs". **No citation.** The back-pain claim is a clinical assertion with nothing attached to it in this article.

**Chin-ups (primary).** **No citation.** Muscle list only.

**Seated cable row (secondary).** **No citation.**

**Lat pulldown (secondary).** The only sourced back exercise: "Research suggests that although not very dramatic, the grip width in a lat pull-down could result in some small differences in **muscle development**", citing [Andersen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1097/jsc.0000000000000232). That study measured **1RM strength and surface EMG** across narrow, medium and wide grips in an acute design. It contains no measure of muscle size. **Activation, not outcome — and the sentence overstates the source.** Their practical recommendation (a grip just wider than shoulder width) is preference dressed as inference; the honest version is that grip width is unlikely to matter much.

**Bent-over dumbbell flye (tertiary).** **No citation.** Rationale is that rear delts are under-trained relative to front delts, which is an anatomy-of-typical-programmes argument, not evidence about the flye.

**Preacher curl (tertiary).** The bench flexes the shoulder, which lengthens the biceps long head and trains it at longer length, citing [Oliveira 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24150552/) — a small **EMG** study of shoulder position across dumbbell curl variants. The follow-on sentence — that training at long muscle lengths through a large range of motion likely produces more growth — is the one that would need a hypertrophy trial, and it **carries no citation at all**. There is a real literature behind that claim (this library holds it elsewhere); this article does not cite it. **Activation plus an uncited mechanism.**

**Concentration curl (tertiary).** Called "one of the best biceps variations for muscular hypertrophy". **No citation.** The supporting reasoning is long ROM and minimal shoulder involvement, plus advice to "focus on squeezing the biceps" — a mind-muscle-connection claim with nothing behind it here.

**Summary: seven exercises, two citations, both EMG, zero growth outcomes.** The ordering rule they give (largest muscle mass first, smallest last) is a fatigue-management convention, not a finding.

## Where the article does rest on outcome data

**Frequency.** "Hitting each muscle group twice per week may improve hypertrophy results, as this increases total training volume" cites [Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8), a systematic review and meta-analysis that did find a frequency advantage. Note the mechanism they attach: they say frequency works *because* it raises volume — which is precisely the confound in that meta-analysis, since the higher-frequency conditions often were not volume-equated. They state the confound as the explanation without noticing it is a confound.

**Time course.** [Lambrianides 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000004300) is used carefully and is the article's best moment: an analysis of 40 studies that measured progress *during* the programme rather than only before and after, showing significant strength gains at 4.3 weeks on average, anywhere from 1 to 12 weeks, and 3–40% improvement on squat 1RM. They use it to argue that inter-individual variation is large and non-responders exist. That is a fair reading.

**Benefits framing (weak).** The opening claim that back and biceps training brings "reduced sensitivity to pain, increased energy levels, and better sleep quality" cites [Tjøsvoll 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-020-00181-0) — a **mixed-methods feasibility study** of periodized resistance training in persistent non-specific low back pain. A feasibility study is designed to test whether a trial can be run, not to establish effects. **This is the clearest case in the article of a conclusion outrunning its evidence**, and it is a clinical claim, which makes it matter more.

## Nutrition, which is the better-sourced half

**Protein.** Muscle protein synthesis rises for a few hours after a high-protein meal and returns to baseline ([Atherton & Smith 2012](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2011.225003)); protein around exercise improves net balance ([Kerksick & Leutholtz 2005](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-2-1-50)). The dose recommendation is **~1.6 g/kg/day**, rising to **2.2–3.1 g/kg/day** for physique athletes or people training multiple hours a day on 4+ days, and slightly higher during weight loss or with anabolic-resistance risk factors ([Stokes 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu10020180)). Meal pattern: 3–5 meals a day, 3–5 hours apart, protein at each — a **recommendation**, and the reference attached to it ([Chourpiliadis, *Biochemistry, Gluconeogenesis*, StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK544346/)) does not address meal frequency.

Two source-quality notes. [Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608) is described as "a meta-analysis of 89 articles"; it pooled **49 RCTs and 1,863 participants**, and found dose-dependent gains in strength and lean mass from supplemental protein *where baseline intake was otherwise low* — which the article does state, correctly. [Antonio 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-015-0100-0) is presented as showing better body composition on 3.4 g/kg/day; it is a free-living, self-reported-intake, non-randomised follow-up investigation, and its striking result (very high intake without fat gain) is design-limited.

**Carbohydrate.** ~**3–5 g/kg/day** ([Kerksick 2018 ISSN update](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-018-0242-y)); 20–30 g/hour during workouts for those who need it. They are candid that a systematic review of 49 studies found **no clear link** between carbohydrate intake and strength performance, then argue the trials were short, poorly controlled for actual intake, used untrained subjects and low volumes. That is a reasonable critique, but it is a defence of the recommendation against the evidence, and should be read as such. The physiology that follows — glycogen depletion, altered SR calcium release, a ~70 mmol/kg threshold for force decline — is mechanism, not a demonstration that eating more carbohydrate improves lifting.

**A citation-numbering caveat specific to this section.** The article's own reference numbers drift here: the "meta-analysis of 49 studies" is marked [13], which is Sahlin 2014 on muscle energetics, while the review that actually examined 49 studies ([Henselmans 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14040856)) is marked [18] against the glycogen-threshold sentence; and the carbohydrate-supplementation recommendation is marked [20], which is Kantor 2016 on US supplement-use trends. The extraction of markers passed its check, so these are the article's own misnumberings. The papers below are the right ones for the section; the sentence-level numbers are not reliable in this passage.

**Supplements.** Genuinely rigorous and worth the space: vitamin D ([Barbarawi 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2019.1870) — 21 RCTs, >83,000 participants), fish oil ([Siscovick 2017](https://doi.org/10.1161/cir.0000000000000482), AHA science advisory) and multivitamins ([O'Connor 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.15650), USPSTF evidence report) do not reduce cardiovascular disease or cancer risk for most people, and supplements are "absolutely not necessary". **The very next block sells Barbell Medicine's caffeinated and non-caffeinated pre-workout and two flavours of their own whey isolate**, supported by a link to a Harvard nutrition page for the proposition that whey digests fast and "may help build muscle quicker". Sound scepticism applied to other people's products, not their own.

## Two smaller flags

- "Overall, controlling the eccentric phase is important for strength and hypertrophy" cites [Coratella 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000004039), an RCT on *including* the eccentric phase to counteract detraining in women, and [Hughes 2018](https://doi.org/10.1101/cshperspect.a029769), a review of adaptation mechanisms. Neither tests **tempo control** on a normal eccentric, which is what the advice is about.
- "Most gym accidents are due to faulty equipment, not trainees doing the exercise wrong" is **uncited** and is a strong empirical claim. It fits their broader position that technique is less load-bearing than the industry says, but that position does not license this particular statistic.

## What they sell
The article routes readers to the Barbell Medicine **template quiz**, their **training programmes**, their **fitness coaching**, and — in the supplement section — their own **pre-workout and whey protein**. The training recommendations are generic enough not to depend on any of it; the supplement recommendation does.
