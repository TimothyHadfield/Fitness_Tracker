# A Meniscus Tear on MRI Is Common in People With No Knee Pain

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "A Second Opinion on Your Meniscus Injury" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/a-second-opinion-on-your-meniscus-injury/)) · Derek Miles · 2023-06-21 · 3,113 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **Meniscal findings are common in people without symptoms.** In 230 knees of asymptomatic adults (mean age 44) scanned at 3.0 T, **30% had a meniscus tear** ([Horga 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32060622/)). In asymptomatic collegiate basketball players, **50% had meniscal changes preseason, rising to 62% postseason** ([Pappas 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27347867/)). A systematic review of almost 300 asymptomatic athletes found **about one third** with meniscal abnormality ([Beals 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27075327/)).
- The clinical inference Miles draws is narrow and correct: a tear found *after* symptoms begin was very possibly there before them, so imaging early in a routine knee-pain episode biases management toward intervention without changing what the person should do first.
- **Conservative management is first-line** for knee pain associated with degenerative meniscal change — supported by a systematic review of systematic reviews ([Rotini 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36057031/)) and a systematic review with meta-analysis of arthroscopic partial meniscectomy ([Abram 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30796103/)).
- **Red flags that do warrant evaluation:** a clear mechanism (slip, fall, sudden twist) followed by swelling or inability to bear weight; swelling obscuring the kneecap border or "like an orange" along the medial joint line; persistent loss of range of motion or true locking.
- The rehab prescription is four levers — **modify load, modify range of motion, modify exercise selection, manage expectations** — with an explicit symptom rule: **do not let symptoms rise more than 2 points out of 10** during a movement.

## The imaging argument, and what it does and does not establish
All three prevalence studies are **cross-sectional imaging studies of asymptomatic people**. That design supports exactly one claim: these findings are common in the absence of pain, so their presence is weak evidence that they are the cause of any given person's pain. It does not support the stronger claim that meniscal tears never cause symptoms, and Miles does not quite make that claim — his wording is that changes "should not be automatically assumed to be the singular cause".

The Pappas basketball data are the most interesting because they are longitudinal within a season: the same asymptomatic athletes accumulated more meniscal findings across a competitive season without developing symptoms. That is a genuine dissociation between imaging change and pain, not just a prevalence figure.

He supplements this with a well-known oddity: [Dye 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9850777/), in which an orthopaedic surgeon underwent arthroscopy of both his own knees without intra-articular anaesthetic and mapped the conscious sensation from each structure; direct probing of the meniscus produced only mild sensation. **Flagged:** this is **n = 1**, and it is used to support a general statement that "there does not appear to be a specific place on the knee where athletes can feel their meniscus". Miles labels the sample size himself, which is to his credit, but a single-subject neurosensory mapping cannot carry a population claim about meniscal innervation. The imaging studies would have been sufficient without it.

## When to send someone for evaluation
This is the section that justifies the source's place in this library — it is clinical triage, not gym advice. Evaluate if: sudden mechanism plus swelling or inability to weight-bear; visible effusion (kneecap border indistinct, or a swelling "like an orange" along the medial joint line); prolonged loss of range of motion or a knee that feels stuck. If those symptoms are transient and self-resolving, or absent, conservative management is reasonable.

For pain severe enough to limit weight-bearing **without** a clear mechanism, the advice is **weight-bearing as tolerated with crutches** — crutches to support the leg, not to unload it, with weight progressively increased over days. For swelling that is actually limiting: elevate with the heel propped so the back of the knee is off the ground and the leg is above the heart, which also helps recover extension; an over-the-counter compression sleeve if the person is on their feet at work. If swelling is not bothersome, no treatment is needed. **Absolute rest is rejected throughout.**

## The rehab framework
**Expected timeline** for the deconditioned person who overdid it: **two to four weeks** to symptom resolution with modification. This number is offered without a citation.

The diagnostic move for a new-to-training person is to look for a recent jump in dose — restarting squats three times a week after five years, or a weekend laying flooring after a decade of not kneeling. His formulation is that it is not the squats, it is *how much* and *how hard*.

**Load selection** uses RPE autoregulation, with the same "could versus should" reframe Barbell Medicine uses across their rehab writing: the normal anchor for RPE 8 is "could you do 2 more reps", but when training to reduce symptoms the anchor becomes "*should* you do 2 more reps". That deliberately builds in a wider margin.

**Range of motion as a second progression variable** is the most practically distinctive idea here. Pin squats at a tolerable depth give two things to progress — weight on the bar, and pin height — and he recommends progressing **one variable per session**: one session adds load through the tolerable range, the next lowers the pins.

**Exercise substitution:** leg press, hack squat, goblet squat, lunge, leg extension. For lunges, temporarily keeping the shin vertical, then letting the knee travel over the toes as symptoms allow.

**For the true beginner**, the starting programme is deliberately unimpressive: 10 minutes of biking, straight-leg raises, sit-to-stands from a chair, one single-leg exercise — aerobic work, basic resistance work, and a balance exposure. Progression targets are the public health ones (**150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity, resistance training 2×/week**), with intensity at **5–6 out of 10** or above and the 2-point symptom rule as the brake. Progress can be tracked by load, reps, range of motion, or the same reps at a lower RPE.

## Meniscectomy, joint "wear", and one stretched citation
On surgery, Miles is careful: long-term outcomes after arthroscopic partial meniscectomy are typically not different from conservative treatment, but removing tissue does change load distribution — [Willinger 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30800688/) measured tibiofemoral contact pressure in medial-meniscus-deficient knees. He explicitly frames the cushioning point as something that "could be argued", not as demonstrated. That is a **mechanistic argument, not an outcome argument**, and he marks it as such.

Post-operatively, the two stated priorities are **regaining full knee extension and controlling swelling**, with the same anti-unloading stance: weight-bearing restrictions may apply short-term, but prolonged unloading is not the goal, and there is always something trainable.

**Flagged overreach.** The section "Will lifting weights wear out the knees?" leans on a [2020 JAMA Internal Medicine study](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2763720) reporting roughly **10 additional years free of chronic disease** for people meeting physical activity guidelines, not smoking, drinking only moderately, and with BMI under 25. That is a lifestyle-and-chronic-disease cohort finding. It says nothing about joint tissue, cartilage, or whether resistance training degrades knees, and using it to answer a question about joint wear is a stretch — the honest answer to that question is the asymptomatic-prevalence data he already presented, plus the absence of evidence that loading damages joints. The adjacent sentence, "menisci adapt to loading", is asserted with no source.

**Recommendation versus evidence.** The imaging prevalence data, the conservative-first line, and the meniscectomy outcome data are evidenced. The 2–4 week timeline, the 2-point symptom rule, the one-variable-per-session progression, the crutch protocol and the beginner programme are **clinical recommendations without citations** — sensible, conventional in physiotherapy, but a different class of claim.
