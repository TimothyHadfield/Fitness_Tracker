# Youth Training Part I: The Problem Is Early Specialisation, Not Lifting

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Resistance Training for Youth: Part I" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/resistance-training-for-the-youth-population/)) · Derek Miles · 2019-09-09 · 2,964 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **Note the title against the content.** This is Part I of a series and contains almost no resistance-training programming. It is an argument that youth are under-trained in the gym and over-specialised on the field. The prescriptions are promised for Part II (Long-Term Athletic Development).
- **The old objections are dead in the literature.** "Lifting stunts growth" and "lifting damages growth plates" have been replaced — the 2014 Lloyd international consensus statement puts it the other way round, that childhood may be the opportune window for building bone mass through weight-bearing activity. The 2008 AAP position statement says the same.
- **The population problem is undertraining.** WHO recommends 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily for ages 5–17. PE lessons deliver that threshold only ~50% of the time in elementary school (Hollis), 34.2% in one earlier analysis (Fairclough & Stratton), and 40.5% in secondary school. Accelerometer data suggests **today's 16-year-olds are about as active as today's 60-year-olds** (Varma 2017).
- **Specialisation carries a roughly doubled injury risk.** Bell 2018 meta-analysis: highly specialised athletes **1.81× more likely to be injured (95% CI 1.26–2.60)**. Rugg 2018 in NBA first-round picks: 42.8% major injury in early specialisers versus 25% in non-specialisers. O'Kane 2017 in female soccer players: playing for more than one team **raised** injury risk 2.5×, while participating in multiple different activities **lowered** it by 61%.
- **And specialisation does not buy the outcome parents want.** Among Division I athletes at one major university, only 16.9% had specialised by their freshman year of high school — **over half were multi-sport athletes.** NCAA high-school-to-college conversion rates run 12.4% (male lacrosse) down to 2.9% (male wrestling); the average scholarship is **$10,400/year**, against parental spending that can reach **$1,000/month** starting as early as age six.
- **Load is not intrinsically good or bad.** The article's sharpest clinical point: humeral retroversion in youth throwers (usually treated as adaptive) and cam-type deformity in youth hockey players (treated as pathological) arise by essentially the same loading mechanism. Whether repetitive load is a stimulus or a problem depends on the adaptation it produces, not on the load itself.

## What the evidence is, and what it is not

**Nearly all of it is observational**, and the designs vary widely in what they can support. The article is generally careful with its verbs ("correlated with", "associated with", "more likely to"), but the accumulated effect is a causal story the individual studies do not establish. Three things are worth separating:

**The strongest evidence:** Bell 2018 is a **systematic review with meta-analysis** in *Pediatrics*, and McGuine 2017 is a **prospective** study of over 1,500 high school athletes finding higher lower-extremity injury risk in moderately and highly specialised athletes. These are the two citations that carry the specialisation-injury claim.

**The weakest:** Bush 2019 on American weightlifters is a **retrospective survey of 141 athletes** reporting an odds ratio of **23.9 (95% CI 4.1–138)** for injury with youth specialisation. The article handles this well — it names the sample size, calls the design retrospective, and argues only from the lower bound ("even on the low end... over 4 times as likely"). That is the right way to use a wildly imprecise estimate, and it is worth noting that a confidence interval spanning 4 to 138 tells you almost nothing about magnitude.

**A miss the article sets up and then does not apply.** Miles explicitly raises **survivorship bias** — that we only observe the athletes who did not burn out or get injured — and then cites Rugg 2018 (NBA first-round picks) and Wilhelm 2017 (102 professional pitchers) as evidence about specialisation and injury. Those cohorts are *definitionally* the survivors. The findings still stand within their populations, but they cannot tell us about the children who never made it.

**Flagged overreach:** "Quite possibly, taking baseball players, swimmers, volleyball players off the field... and getting them in the gym can make them better *athletes*." That is a recommendation with no trial behind it in this article. The evidence presented shows that specialisation associates with injury and that non-specialisers reach elite levels at least as often; **it does not show that resistance training is the substitute that works.** The article's honest framing — "we must assume from the general heuristic" — appears in the weightlifting paragraph and should apply here too.

## Beyond injury: burnout and the parent problem

The psychological section is the part least covered elsewhere in this library. Sport burnout is defined by three components — sport-related exhaustion, sport-related cynicism, and feelings of inadequacy — and tracks not just hours played but the **expectation** attached to them.

**Padaki 2017** is the number that should stop a reader: in a cohort of youth athletes averaging just under 14 years old, **20.4% of parents hoped their child would play professionally** and 36.4% at collegiate level, while **72% of the children had already sustained an overuse injury and 16.6% had already had surgery.**

**Madigan 2018** found injury likelihood **more than doubled for each one-standard-deviation increase in perfectionistic concerns** — a psychological disposition converting into physical injury risk. The recommended alternative, from Sorkkila 2018, is **mastery goals** (motivated by improvement) rather than performance goals (motivated by demonstrating superiority).

**Under-recovery compounds it.** Luke 2011 links overscheduling and practices within 48 hours of competition to injury; Milewski 2014 found adolescents averaging **under 8 hours of sleep were 1.7× more likely to be injured**.

## Advice versus evidence, and the commercial angle

The prescriptive content amounts to: youth should resistance train, should not specialise early, and should sleep. All three are defensible; only the second has this article's evidence directly behind it. **The resistance-training recommendation rests on the consensus statements (Lloyd 2014, AAP 2008) rather than on anything demonstrated in the body of the piece** — which is fair, because the programming is deferred to Part II, but it means Part I argues against specialisation far more thoroughly than it argues for lifting.

**Commercial note:** unusually, this article sells nothing. No template links, no coaching pitch, no supplement placement. It contains a jab at the supplement industry — "we have people purchasing supplements with *no* evidential support or possible single-digit effects, when there is evidence that the addition of resistance training can generate double-digit benefits" — which is worth noting because **Barbell Medicine runs a supplement line of its own**. The point is aimed at low-evidence products rather than the category, but a reader can weigh it.

**Cross-reference:** the Dankel 2016 finding used here (18.3% of adults meet the 2–3×/week resistance training recommendation; a 23% reduction in all-cause mortality among those who do) is the same observational cohort cited in [exercise-home.md](exercise-home.md). It is an association, not a trial result, in both places.
