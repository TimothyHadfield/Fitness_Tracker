# Concussion: Red Flags, Relative Rest, and a Staged Return to Play

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "What is a Concussion? A Guide to Symptoms, Treatment, and Recovery" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/concussion-symptoms-treatment-recovery/)) · Charles Abrams · 2025-10-23 · 2816 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- A concussion is a **functional** brain injury, not primarily a structural one. Loss of consciousness is not required for diagnosis — it is made on symptoms after a significant head impact.
- Clinical priority order: rule out red flags, then set expectations, then progress activity. The red-flag list is the part worth memorising.
- **Relative rest for 24–48 hours, not absolute rest.** Prolonged rest is associated with worse symptoms, not faster recovery; light walking inside the first 48 hours is safe and probably helps.
- Return to play runs through seven stages, each at least 24 hours, with one stopping rule: symptoms worsened for over an hour, or needing medication, means don't advance.
- Hyperbaric oxygen, neurofeedback, TMS, blue-light lenses, creatine and electrolytes are all listed as unproven here.

## The injury, and why symptoms outrun the imaging
The mechanism is acceleration-deceleration, the teaching case being coup-contrecoup: the brain strikes one side of the skull and rebounds into the other. Contusions occur, but the article's central point is that **most early symptoms come from disturbed brain function**: stretched axons trigger inflammation, swelling and chemical signalling, and the brain burns more energy trying to restore normal operation. A normal scan does not mean a normal brain — the same loose coupling of imaging and symptoms Barbell Medicine argues for in musculoskeletal pain. Diagnosis is therefore clinical, with the SCAT6 (2023) named as the standard sideline tool.

## Red flags
These raise the probability of something worse than an uncomplicated concussion, and are more serious in people **over 60 or on anticoagulants**: limb weakness or paraesthesia; true vision loss or diplopia (not blurring); abnormal or asymmetric pupils; seizures (under 5% of concussions); loss of consciousness beyond one minute; persistent altered mental status; skull fracture; more than two vomiting episodes; retrograde amnesia extending more than 30 minutes before injury; severe or worsening headache; escalating agitation; neck pain or tenderness. Any of these means urgent care, now.

## Timeline and the case against rest
Symptoms peak over 7–10 days and mostly resolve by 1–3 months; beyond three months the label is persistent post-concussion syndrome. Over 30% still have lingering symptoms past one month — which warrants physician review, often MRI and neuropsychological testing — and roughly 15% of those who develop PCS remain symptomatic beyond a year.

The management shift is from absolute to **relative** rest: keep as much of ordinary daily life as symptoms permit, cut screen time rather than substitute it for the activity you stopped, start light walking inside the 24–48 hour window, and minimise daytime naps to protect night sleep. Avoid alcohol and cannabis; paracetamol or NSAIDs are fine within normal dosing.

## Return to play
Seven stages, each a minimum of 24 hours after the initial rest period, with self-assessment between each. (1) Relative rest and daily activity, with actual rest reserved for symptoms above 7/10. (2) Steady-state aerobic work under **55% of max heart rate**. (3) Moderate aerobic work under **70% max HR** plus light resistance training limited by symptoms. (4) Non-contact sport-specific drills, ideally done alone to avoid accidental impacts. (5) Non-contact team practice, strength and conditioning back at previous intensities. (6) Full-contact practice. (7) Unrestricted competition. Clearance is advised before stage 4 if there is head-impact risk, and again before stage 6.

Re-injury is the main hazard, concentrated in the first 7–10 days: the article cites 1 in 15 college football players sustaining a second concussion in the same season, and three prior concussions tripling future risk. On CTE it is careful — no clear consensus on the entity itself, but repeated concussions are associated with cognitive impairment, psychiatric symptoms, dementia risk and movement, gait and speech disorders, mostly in military personnel and contact-sport athletes.

## Evidence versus recommendation
Almost everything above is **recommendation** rather than demonstrated effect. The article reports no study designs, sample sizes, durations or effect sizes anywhere — unusual for this source, and a real weakness in a clinical piece. The seven stages, the 24-hour minimum and the 55%/70% heart-rate ceilings are consensus scaffolding (CDC HEADS UP and the Amsterdam 2022 statement are both in the reference list), not measured doses.

**Overreach flags.** "Longer rest periods lead to worse symptom intensity and duration" is stated causally, when the underlying literature — including the screen-time RCT and early-activity work in the list — is largely paediatric and supports *early activity helps* far more securely than *rest harms*, with generalisation to adults assumed rather than argued. The re-injury figures are bare point estimates with no population, interval or source.

**Products.** Barbell Medicine sells supplements and recommends none here. The two in-house links are their free max-heart-rate calculator, used for the stage 2 and 3 ceilings, and their own creatine explainer — attached to a paragraph saying creatine has *not* been shown to help concussion. Electrolytes get the same treatment, with a carve-out for genuine deficiency. Hyperbaric oxygen is called inconsistent and bias-prone at a cost of thousands; neurofeedback and TMS run $300–500 per session with no consistent benefit; blue-light lenses have not been studied in concussion at all.
