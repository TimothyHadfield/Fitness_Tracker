# Strength Training vs Bodybuilding: The Same Tools Aimed at Different Tests

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Strength Training vs. Bodybuilding - Similarities & Differences" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/strength-training-vs-bodybuilding/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-08-08 · 3,847 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The difference is the **test**, not the tissue. Strength training is judged by a 1RM or similar specific force test; bodybuilding by size, symmetry and lean mass. Every programming difference downstream follows from that.
- **Bigger does not automatically mean stronger.** They cite [Reggiani & Schiaffino's 2020 "provocative review"](https://doi.org/10.4081/ejtm.2020.9311) to say the size-strength relationship is correlational in cross-section and causally unsettled, and that short-term strength gains are mostly neuromuscular. This is a more cautious line than most training content takes and it is correctly sourced.
- **Load range is the sharpest practical split.** Strength wants a narrow band; hypertrophy tolerates a wide one. Both claims lean on the *same* paper — [Schoenfeld 2017's low- vs high-load meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200), cited at reference 2 and again at reference 8 — which is legitimate, since that meta reports load-dependent strength gains alongside load-independent hypertrophy.
- **Their "stay away from failure for strength" position rests on two velocity-loss trials from one research group** (Pareja-Blanco 2017, twice), not a meta-analysis. It is a defensible reading, but thinner evidence than the confident phrasing suggests.
- The article's own heading "**the higher the training volume, the greater the strength improvement**" carries **no citation at all**. The only volume dose-response reference is [Schoenfeld 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197), which is a *hypertrophy* meta-analysis and is cited in the hypertrophy section, correctly.
- **Product content:** two free beginner programmes, then a catalogue of paid templates — Strength I, Powerlifting II, Hypertrophy I and II, Bodybuilding I, Powerbuilding I, II and III — plus one-to-one coaching. The nutrition section's advice to buy only GMP-certified, third-party-tested supplements also describes the standard Barbell Medicine claims for its own supplement line.

## Size and strength are not the same variable

The article opens on the point most gym content skips. Muscle size correlates strongly with strength when you photograph a population at one moment, and the lifting world has silently upgraded that correlation into a causal law. Their citation, [Reggiani & Schiaffino (2020)](https://doi.org/10.4081/ejtm.2020.9311), is a review arguing that hypertrophy and strength may be partly independent variables. Note what kind of claim each half of their position is: "the correlation is cross-sectional and causation is unresolved" is evidence; "hypertrophy *likely* raises long-run strength potential, but short-term strength change is mostly neuromuscular" is their inference, hedged, and they say so.

## What separates the two programmes

**Load.** For maximal strength they want most work at 65-85% of 1RM at RPE 6-8, plus single-rep practice at 85-93%. The evidence for load mattering is [Schoenfeld 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200): above versus below 60% 1RM, higher loads produce more strength. The evidence for singles is [Androulakis-Korakakis 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports6030086) — a **pilot study** in a handful of competing powerlifters, which is why the article says "some evidence" rather than asserting it. The 65-85% and 85-93% windows are house prescription, not numbers any cited paper produced. For hypertrophy they liberalise: 3-20 reps at RPE 6-8 for compounds, 6-20+ reps at RPE 8-10 for isolation, on the strength of the same Schoenfeld meta showing hypertrophy is comparatively load-indifferent. Again their own ranges.

**Exercise selection and ROM.** Strength athletes train the movements they are tested in, because adaptation is specific and transfer scales with similarity ([Suchomel 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0486-0), a narrative review). Bodybuilders are judged on appearance, so they can draw on a much wider pool including far more single-joint work — said to suit hypertrophy because it targets a muscle directly, allows a large range of motion, and generates less systemic fatigue so it can be run at higher volume. Larger ROM is said to stimulate more growth (below-parallel over half squats). Both claims are uncited here, though consistent with the ROM literature elsewhere in this library.

**Proximity to failure.** Two claims, differently supported. That sets closer to failure cause more fatigue is cited to [Morán-Navarro 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3725-7) and [Santos 2021](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002915), both of which measured recovery time-course and discomfort rather than long-run adaptation. That *strength gains are greater when you stay further from failure* is cited to two 2017 Pareja-Blanco papers on velocity loss during resistance training — from the same group, in trained athletes, squat-based, with small samples. Velocity loss is a proxy for proximity to failure, not the same construct. Their RPE 6-8 recommendation for compound lifts follows from this, and it is advice built on two studies.

**Volume.** The claimed dose-response for strength has no reference under it, and the text immediately concedes that the optimal dose is unknown and varies widely. Their starting prescription — 8-12 sets per week per major movement pattern, ~80% of it at 3-8 reps in the 65-85% range at RPE 6-8, the remaining 20% split between singles at RPE 6-8 and 8-15 rep sets at RPE 7-10 — is entirely house guidance. For hypertrophy, the dose-response claim *is* cited, to [Schoenfeld 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197).

**Nutrition and conditioning.** All uncited in this article: 1.6 g/kg bodyweight/day protein from minimally processed sources, ~3 g/kg/day carbohydrate weighted to fibre-containing foods, saturated fat under roughly 10% of calories, added sugar limited, and a minimum of 150 minutes per week of moderate-to-vigorous conditioning at RPE 4-6. They argue cardiorespiratory fitness belongs in a strength programme for work capacity and training tolerance as well as health — a reasonable position, presented as one.

## Where they agree with themselves

The similarities section is the honest core: both load the body across varied ranges of motion and rep ranges at similar proximities to failure; both are volume-dose-dependent; and given adequate food and sleep a good strength programme should add lean mass while a good hypertrophy programme should add strength. Bodybuilding differs by degree — more reps, more isolation, more movement variety, slightly closer to failure. A difference of emphasis, not of kind, and the article says so rather than manufacturing a dichotomy.

## Flags

- The velocity-loss evidence for training away from failure is treated as settled ("based on available evidence, strength improvements are greater") on the basis of two papers from one lab.
- The strength-volume dose-response heading is stated as a principle and supported by nothing.
- Most of the specific numbers a reader would act on — the RPE bands, the set counts, the rep splits, the macronutrient targets — are clinical and coaching judgement, not findings. The article does not consistently mark that line, though it does admit individual variation repeatedly.
