# Hip Tendinopathy: Isolate the Tissue, Load It Slowly, Expect Symptoms

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Training With Hip Pain Part II" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/training-with-hip-pain-part-ii/)) · Derek Miles · 2021-03-06 · 2,127 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- Part II of the hip series. Part I covered anatomy and "normal" versus "abnormal" hip findings — see `training-hip-pain-part-i.md`; this note does not repeat it. Part II covers one diagnostic category: **tendinopathy**.
- What puts you in this bucket: **gradual onset, no clear mechanism of injury, pain at a muscle's origin or insertion**. Greater trochanteric bursitis, greater trochanteric pain syndrome and "snapping hip" are folded in — Miles says the treatment approach is the same whichever label you are given, a deliberate lumping move.
- **They admit up front that there is no research on heavy slow resistance training for hip tendinopathy at all.** The protocol is extrapolated from Achilles and patellar tendon trials (Beyer 2015; Kongsgaard 2009, 2010). This is honest, and it means almost everything below is "we recommend", not "the evidence shows".
- The operating theory is **capacity**, not damage: symptoms mean the tissue cannot currently tolerate the load being asked of it, so the job is to raise capacity. Rest lowers it (detraining) and is treated as counterproductive.
- The practical core is **isolating the affected tissue**, because a dozen muscles cross the hip and a lifter doing bilateral compound work will unconsciously find a pattern that gets the bar up without loading the sore tendon — then overload it anyway.

## The generic dosing ladder
One progression, applied to whichever hip-specific exercise fits the diagnosis, all at RPE 8:

| Weeks | Sets × reps |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | 4 × 12–15 |
| 3–4 | 4 × 10–12 |
| 5–6 | 4 × 8–10 |
| 7+ | 3 × 8 |

Placed **at the start of the session**, doubling as rehab and warm-up. No source is cited for these numbers — it is clinical practice, and the article says explicitly that there is no single normal starting dose. Unilateral variants (single-leg RDL) and slow eccentric tempos are the two main levers for cutting load without cutting the movement.

**Expectation-setting is treated as part of the treatment.** Symptoms are expected to continue throughout rehab; the progress marker is not their absence but how much you can do before they appear. Their illustration: 100 kg before onset at week 1, 200 kg at week 6 — same symptom, far more capacity. Note this is an invented example, not data.

## Region by region
**Proximal hamstring** — pain right under the buttock, hits deadlift and squat, and in bad cases sitting and sleeping. Start on a prone or seated hamstring curl, single leg, full range, **3-0-3 tempo**. Reduce range if very sensitive; slow the tempo further (6-0-6, 5-1-5) or raise the reps for people who habitually load too heavy. In a powerlifting gym: RDL or stiff-legged deadlift, single-leg suitcase deadlift or single-leg RDL with a rack for balance. The reason to avoid the competition deadlift early is behavioural — prior performance sets the expectation and the lifter does too much too soon.

**Gluteal** — lateral hip pain at the greater trochanteric insertion, not the buttock mass most people mean by "glutes". Hip abduction machine seated or standing; otherwise curtsy lunge, lateral step-down, or partial-depth pistol squat.

**Quadriceps** — pain just above the kneecap; included despite not being a hip problem. Early on, limit how far the knee travels forward: leg press with feet high on the platform, or split squats with the shin against a bench, moving the foot back as sensitivity drops. **The constraint is temporary and the point is tension, not load** — "no one cares how much weight you split squat during rehab".

**Hip flexor** — anterior hip pain when the region is put under tension. First choice is the **reverse Nordic curl**, 4 × 6 through a tolerable range, bodyweight only (hence the different rep scheme), progressing by leaning further back. Alternative: rear-foot-elevated split squat with the symptomatic leg behind.

**Adductor** — groin pain, affects the bottom of the squat and both deadlift styles. This is the only region with cited evidence, and it is for adductor-related groin pain generally rather than tendinopathy. Yousefzadeh 2018 ran athletes with longstanding groin pain through the Hölmich protocol: isometric adductor and trunk work first, progressing to **5 sets of 10** strengthening exercises at clinician-chosen load. **78% returned to sport at just over 14 weeks**, with >20% gains in isometric adductor and abductor strength and >7% in eccentric strength. The underlying protocol is Hölmich 1999, a randomised trial in the *Lancet* — Miles' point being that progressive loading is not a new idea, just a badly dosed one. Practical entry point: lateral lunge, or an adductor machine.

**Flagged:** two things. Yousefzadeh is presented as evidence that "simple loading protocols can elicit positive results", but as described it is an uncontrolled cohort — 78% returning at 14 weeks in a condition with a long natural history cannot be separated from time. Hölmich 1999 is the randomised evidence carrying the claim; the 2018 study mostly adds strength measurements. Second, "avoiding the movements with absolute rest only serves to further decrease capacity, and does not facilitate recovery" is stated absolutely and cited to nothing. Detraining is real; "does not facilitate recovery" is stronger than anything referenced here.

**They sell things:** the article closes by directing readers who do not improve to the paid Barbell Medicine Pain and Rehabilitation consultation service. Nothing hidden about it, but worth knowing.
