# Exercise Lowers Blood Pressure About as Much as the Drugs Do — With Caveats

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Effect of Resistance Training on High Blood Pressure" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-effect-of-resistance-training-on-high-blood-pressure/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2020-11-20 · 2,010 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- Since the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline, **hypertension means SBP ≥130 or DBP ≥80** (previously ≥140/90), and **≥120/<80 is "elevated"**. That redefinition alone moved millions of Americans into the diagnosis. About **1 in 3 US adults (108 million)** now meet criteria, and fewer than half are adequately controlled ([Dorans 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29858369/)).
- The article is built around one paper: **[Naci 2019](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/14/859)**, a network meta-analysis of **391 RCTs** — 197 exercise, 194 antihypertensive drug — totalling **39,742 subjects**. It is the first attempt to compare exercise and medication on the same scale.
- **In hypertensive subgroups (SBP >140), exercise lowered SBP by 8.96 mmHg on average versus 8.8 mmHg for the drug trials.** By exercise type: endurance −8.69, **resistance training −7.83**, combined endurance + resistance −13.5.
- Across all 197 exercise trials — which include many normotensive people — the average was only **−4.84 mmHg** (endurance −4.88, **resistance −3.5**, isometric −5.65, combined −6.49). The gap between the two sets of numbers is the point: **the higher your starting blood pressure, the more exercise does.**
- **Intensity did not matter.** No difference between medium and high intensity in either analysis.
- Only **26%** of diagnosed hypertensives are counselled to exercise at all ([Mellen 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15333055/)) — the article's actual call to action, and it is aimed at clinicians rather than lifters.

## What resistance training specifically does
The pre-Naci evidence base for lifting and blood pressure was small. [Cornelissen 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21896934/) pooled **33 RCTs / 1,012 subjects** and found resistance training lowered both SBP and DBP by about **3.9 mmHg**. [de Sousa 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28769100/) looked specifically at prehypertensive and hypertensive people — only **5 RCTs / 201 subjects** — and found **−8.2 SBP / −4.1 DBP**. Feigenbaum's framing is fair: those studies were mainly useful for **killing the folk belief that lifting raises resting blood pressure**, but they were too small and never compared against drugs.

Naci fixes the sample-size problem and adds the comparison. The resistance-training result in hypertensives (−7.83) lands close to de Sousa's −8.2, which is genuine convergence from independent evidence and worth noting.

## Where the claim outruns the evidence
The article's summary line — "the effect of exercise on systolic blood pressure in those with hypertension is **slightly greater** than the effect of antihypertensive medications" — is the sentence to be careful with.

**8.96 versus 8.8 mmHg is not a difference.** More importantly, this is an **indirect comparison across separate trial populations**, not a head-to-head. No RCT has randomised hypertensive patients to exercise or a drug. The exercise trials and the drug trials differ systematically in almost every way that matters: the drug trials were larger (29,281 subjects), older (mean 55 vs 50), and started at higher pressures (mean 150 vs 132 mmHg overall). Naci's own authors were more cautious about this than the article is.

**The outcome is also different.** The drug trials sit inside a literature with hard endpoints — stroke, myocardial infarction, mortality. The exercise trials measured **a surrogate**: millimetres of mercury, usually over weeks to months. Showing equivalent SBP reduction is not the same as showing equivalent event reduction, and adherence over years is a much larger problem for exercise than for a daily tablet.

Feigenbaum does note that exercise gives "additional ammunition" for patients who do not want to be on multiple medications indefinitely, which is a reasonable clinical use of the finding. But this is **advice built on a surrogate comparison**, and the note should not be read as "exercise replaces antihypertensives".

One thing the article does well: it flags its own weak data. The isometric-training result (−5.65 mmHg) is footnoted as coming from very small samples that may inflate the effect, and there were **no isometric studies at all** in the hypertensive subgroup.

## Citation problems worth recording
The article cites by **named author-year hyperlinks in prose** with a lumped numbered list at the bottom, so there are no in-text numbered markers and no claim-level mapping to verify — but because the links are named, most claims can still be attributed confidently, and they have been below.

Two specific issues:
- **[Zanchetti 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20016302/), "Looking forward with a grateful heart", is a one-page *J Hypertens* editorial** cited next to Cornelissen as though it supported the resistance-training meta-analysis figures. It does not support that claim; it appears to be inherited from an UpToDate reference list.
- Four of the prose links (Cornelissen, Zanchetti, de Sousa, MacDonald) point at **UpToDate abstract-proxy pages**, not the papers — which is consistent with the whole cluster having been lifted from a single UpToDate topic's bibliography.

No Barbell Medicine product is recommended in this article.
