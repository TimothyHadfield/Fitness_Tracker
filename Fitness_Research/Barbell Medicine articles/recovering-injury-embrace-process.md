# Recovering From Injury: Manage the Load, and Manage the Expectations

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Recovering From an Injury: Embrace the Process" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/recovering-from-an-injury-embrace-the-process/)) · Charlie Dickson · 2019-04-01 · 2,305 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- The practical claim is that after an acute injury the task is to find a **dose** of loading, not to pick between rest and pushing through. Both extremes are named as failure modes: avoidance causes deconditioning and fear of movement, while ignoring symptoms and loading hard exacerbates them and delays return.
- The best-supported material is about load, not psychology. **Eckard 2018**, a systematic review of **57 studies**, concluded the training-load/injury-risk relationship is established, with **session RPE** (internal load) and the **acute:chronic workload ratio** the best-evidenced measures.
- The pain-science section — pain correlates poorly with tissue damage; pain-free people show rotator cuff tears, disc degeneration, labral tears on imaging — is cited to **two commentaries** (Moseley & Butler 2015; Lewis & O'Sullivan 2018), not to the primary prevalence literature. No prevalence figures are given. The position is mainstream in physiotherapy research; the citation offered here is thin.
- **Expectations are treated as a modifiable variable**, using Thompson & Sunol's four-type taxonomy, with *predicted* expectations (what you believe will happen) the type associated with musculoskeletal outcomes.
- Two concrete recommendations, both clinical judgement rather than tested protocol: rate each session 1–10 for difficulty, and train **to get back to baseline** rather than toward a performance goal.

## What they mean by "injury"
They take the definition from the **Timpka 2014 athletics consensus statement** — physical complaint or observable tissue damage from energy transfer, regardless of whether it got medical attention — then narrow to *acute* injury: identifiable mechanism, sudden symptom onset, measurable performance drop. The worked example is a heavy deadlift single, a sudden pull in the low back, and failed back-off sets. Narrowing to acute matters, because it is the case where their pain-science argument is weakest and they say so.

## The pain argument, and what actually supports it
The reasoning runs: catastrophizing (defined as focusing on pain and negatively appraising your ability to cope) raises pain intensity, lowers activity, and predicts persistent pain; therefore the first move after an injury is to not panic. That claim is attached to a **concept analysis of catastrophizing** — a conceptual paper, not an outcome study — which supports the definition better than the causal chain.

The stronger version of their position is stated absolutely: pain *always* results from multiple factors, *never* one problem. They immediately soften it, conceding that in acute injury pain correlates more strongly with tissue damage, and pointing to the IOC pain-management consensus for the other contributors (fear, attentional focus, prior beliefs, self-efficacy, responses of others). **Flagged:** "poorly correlated with tissue damage" is not "unrelated to tissue damage", and "abnormal imaging findings are common in pain-free people" is not "imaging findings never matter". The article does not blur these badly, but the *always/never* phrasing is more absolute than two editorials can carry.

A useful clinical observation sits in the middle of this: **training fatigue can be misread as pain and tissue injury**, and sleep, stress and accumulated fatigue can amplify symptoms — offered as reassurance rather than dismissal.

## Load management, the quantitative core
Training load is defined per the **IOC "How much is too much?" consensus** as cumulative physiological, psychological and mechanical stress; **Impellizzeri 2019** supplies the external (kg, sets, reps) versus internal (perceived response) split. Then Eckard's 57-study review. Their practical translation is session RPE: after each session, rate 1 (rest) to 10 (hardest of your life). They explicitly defend the *subjectivity* of the rating as the point, since it absorbs the intangibles — a defensible argument, not an evidenced one. The "Baraki Exertion Scale" figure is labelled a joke in the article itself.

Gabbett's "sweet spot" of relative loading is cited for the return-to-training phase: after an injury there is deconditioning, so resuming previous loads creates a workload spike. **Flagged:** the acute:chronic workload ratio was already contested when this was written and has been substantially criticised on methodological grounds since 2019 — a reader should treat the "strongest evidence" line as a 2019 snapshot, not current standing.

## Expectations
Predicted expectations are associated with outcomes across work injury, joint replacement, chronic pain, neck, shoulder, whiplash and low back pain (Bialosky 2010, a narrative review of association data). The recommendation — deliberately reset the goal to "return to baseline" so that symptomatic, reduced-capacity sessions read as progress rather than failure — is **advice, not a tested intervention**. The self-efficacy claim ("a major contributing factor for the resolution of symptoms") is causal language resting on a review of correlational work.

**Selling:** unusually for this source, there is no product pitch in the article. It embeds their own free video on training around pain and thanks three staff clinicians. The reasoning nonetheless underwrites the rehab-oriented templates and coaching Barbell Medicine sells.
