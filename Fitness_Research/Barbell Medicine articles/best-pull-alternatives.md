# Ten Pull-Up Substitutes, Chosen on Anatomy Rather Than Data

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The 10 Best Pull-Up Alternatives" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-pull-up-alternatives/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-05-15 · 3,817 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **The list is not a ranking and is not backed by hypertrophy outcome data.** It is not backed by activation data either. Ten exercises are listed, and **only two carry any citation at all** — and neither citation is a comparison of growth between exercises. Read the numbering as an order of presentation, nothing more.
- Of the two references, one ([Ronai 2019](https://doi.org/10.1249/FIT.0000000000000469)) is a practitioner technique column in *ACSM's Health & Fitness Journal*, not a study; the other ([Fenwick, Brown & McGill 2009](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e3181942019)) is an **electromyography and spine-loading study of trunk muscles** during rowing variations. It is used here to support a list of prime movers for the barbell row — muscles it was not designed to adjudicate.
- **Every muscle attribution in the article is uncited anatomy.** "Ring rows primarily train the lats, traps, rhomboids, biceps and posterior deltoid" and its nine equivalents are textbook assertions. In a library where nearly every exercise ranking turns out to rest on EMG, this one does not even reach EMG.
- **The genuinely useful content is scaling, not selection.** Ring and inverted rows are load-adjustable by body angle and anchor height; the lat pulldown is adjustable by moving a pin; the lever row lets you go heavier than the available dumbbells. That is a real practical advantage over pull-ups and it is well explained.
- **Their actual recommendation is additive, not substitutive**: do a horizontal row *in addition to* a vertical pull, on the grounds of loading the muscles through varied planes and ranges of motion. That is a reasonable coaching position and is presented as one, not as a finding.
- **Product content:** one closing paragraph pointing to Barbell Medicine's paid template lines (strength, endurance, hypertrophy, bodybuilding, rehab) and custom coaching. Light by their standards.

## What the article actually claims

Pull-ups and chin-ups train the lats plus teres major, rhomboids, trapezius, biceps brachii, brachioradialis, all three deltoid heads and rectus abdominis. The ten alternatives offered: ring row, inverted row, lat pulldown, one-arm dumbbell row, seated cable row, dumbbell pullover, Pendlay/barbell row, chest-supported row, lever row, T-bar row.

The article is upfront that seven of the ten are **horizontal** pulls while pull-ups are vertical, and says these "load the muscles slightly differently" while training the same muscles "through a similar range of motion". That second half is the weakest structural claim in the piece: a row and a pulldown load the lat at meaningfully different shoulder positions, and the article offers nothing to support the equivalence. It is asserted, not shown.

## Where the two citations actually sit

The verified marker mapping puts them precisely:

- **Section 3, Lat-Pulldown.** Reference 1 supports "the lat pulldown is a great alternative to the pull-up or chin-up, as it's another vertical pulling movement that trains the muscle groups in the back, shoulders, and arms." The source is Ronai's *Lat Pulldown* column — a coaching-technique review for exercise professionals. It is a reasonable source for how to perform the movement. It is not evidence that the pulldown substitutes for a pull-up in adaptation.
- **Section 7, Pendlay/Barbell Row.** Reference 2 supports the prime-mover list "latissimus dorsi, infraspinatus, teres major, teres minor, infraspinatus, and trapezius" (infraspinatus is listed twice — the article's own duplication). Fenwick et al. compared inverted row, bent-over row and one-arm cable row for **trunk muscle activation, lumbar spine motion, load and stiffness** in a small sample of men. Its findings concern spinal loading and the abdominal/erector musculature, not which back muscles pull the bar. This is a mismatch between claim and source, and it is the only place in the article where a citation could have been checked against a claim.

Nowhere in the article is a hypertrophy outcome measured, compared, or cited.

## The scaling logic, which is the real content

**Ring row.** Rings roughly a forearm's width apart; start with the rings at hip height, chest height if a beginner. Difficulty is set by body angle — more horizontal is harder. Regressions: raise the rings, bend the knees and put the whole foot on the floor. Progressions: lower the rings to heels-only, elevate the feet, or go single-arm (which will need a more vertical position).

**Inverted row.** Same principle with a fixed bar in a rack or Smith machine, again starting around hip height. They note the trade-off honestly: some people find the fixed bar easier because it doesn't move, others harder because it forces a fixed hand position.

**Lat pulldown.** The scaling argument here is the strongest in the article. Pull-ups need bands, an assisted machine, chains, a dip belt or a vest to change load; the pulldown needs a pin moved. Grip width and attachment can be varied, with the close-grip V-bar described as biasing lats and teres major — again an uncited attribution.

**Lever row and T-bar row.** Both landmine-style: load one end of a barbell, anchor the other with a landmine attachment or a heavy plate/dumbbell. The lever row is a one-arm row for people who have run out of dumbbell, which is a genuine equipment problem solved simply. The T-bar is the two-handed version, straddling the bar at roughly 45 degrees.

**Dumbbell pullover.** Worth flagging: they list pectoralis major, serratus anterior and latissimus dorsi as the primary movers. That makes it a chest-and-lat exercise with no elbow flexion, which is a considerable distance from what a pull-up trains. Its presence on a pull-up-alternatives list is the loosest inclusion here.

**Barbell/Pendlay row.** The distinction given is range of motion: the Pendlay resets the bar on the floor each rep. They recommend it to strength athletes on grounds of similarity to the deadlift and power clean and its capacity for heavy loading — a specificity argument, offered as preference ("choose whichever one you enjoy better").

## The programming claim at the end

The closing paragraph contains the only claim a reader would actually change a programme over: **these rows are less fatiguing than pull-ups and chin-ups relative to the loads and muscle mass involved, so they can add training volume without eating recovery.** That is plausible and it is uncited. It is also the reason to keep rows alongside vertical pulls rather than as replacements, which is what they recommend.

Their overall position — train the pattern through multiple planes and ranges of motion for broad physical development — is standard Barbell Medicine "exercise variety over exercise optimisation". It is a coaching philosophy, and this article does nothing to test it.
