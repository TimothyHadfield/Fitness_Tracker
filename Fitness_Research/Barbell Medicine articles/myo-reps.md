# Myo-Reps Buy Volume Per Minute, Not Extra Stimulus — and the Mechanism Story Is the Dated Part

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Myo-Reps" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/myo-reps/)) · Leah Lutz · 2020-08-07 · 1,776 words
**Topic:** Load & reps

## Bottom line
- **The protocol.** One "activation" set of roughly **12–30 reps taken to ~RPE 8** (about 2 reps in reserve), then repeated **3–5 rep back-off sets at the same load with 20–30 s rest**, continued until you cannot complete 3. Worked example given: 100 kg × 14 @ RPE 8, then 5, 5, 4, 4, 3.
- **Load anchors, if you know your 1RM:** 10–12 reps ≈ **62–68% 1RM**; 12–15 ≈ **58–62%**; 12–20 ≈ **50–62%**, with the article conceding "considerable variation" at the wide end.
- **The stated purpose is time, not superiority.** Barbell Medicine programmes them for **lower-priority supplemental lifts under time pressure** — the 10-to-15-minutes-left case — and explicitly not for the competition lifts, because "the task demands are vastly different" from 1RM strength.
- **The article cites no study of myo-reps.** Not one direct comparison against straight sets appears. Every mechanistic justification is borrowed from the general volume literature and then applied by analogy.
- **The mechanism section leans on muscle damage and metabolic stress**, both of which the material already in this library treats as largely discarded drivers of hypertrophy. That is the clash worth recording.
- **Consistent with the library on effort, though.** RPE 8 activation sets sit inside the 3–1 RIR band that the cross-source consensus says matches failure; the back-offs then run to RPE 10. Myo-reps are a set-extension technique built around failure, so they are not a way of training easier.

## How to run them, precisely

The prescription notation is *activation reps @ RPE, then back-off reps* — e.g. `12-15 reps @ RPE 8, 3-5 reps`. Lutz is emphatic that **the RPE target beats the rep range**: stop the activation set when it becomes an RPE 8, "whether that's at 10 reps or 25 reps." If the exercise is unfamiliar, pick a load you expect to produce RPE 8 at the *top* of the range, then correct next week.

Back-offs use the same load, 20–30 s rest (or "5–6 slow, deep breaths"), aiming for 5 reps and continuing until a set yields only 3. Their RPE drifts across **6 to 10**. If you complete five back-off sets of 5, the load was too light — add weight next session. The stated design target is **failure within about five back-off sets**, which the article correctly labels "a rather arbitrary number."

Two phase variants, and they trade in opposite directions from what the labels suggest:
- **Higher-volume block:** a *higher*-rep activation set with *lower*-rep back-offs (14@8 → 4@10). The larger gap leaves back-offs further from failure, so more sets and more total reps accumulate.
- **Lower-volume, higher-intensity block:** a *lower*-rep activation set with *higher*-rep back-offs (10@8 → 5@10), which caps accumulated volume.

## The mechanism argument, and what is wrong with it

Lutz's chain is: mechanical loading through a range of motion → motor unit recruitment → volume → **muscle damage** to the myofibrils, which "stimulates hypertrophy… by recruiting satellite cells to the damaged area(s)" → plus **metabolic stress** from anaerobic glycolysis and the short rests. Myo-reps are then argued to deliver a lot of volume fast, with possibly more damage and metabolic stress than conventional sets.

**This clashes with the mechanism material already in this library.** The House of Hypertrophy corpus dismantles both legs: the damage case fails on Damas 2016 (the myofibrillar protein-synthesis spike tracked hypertrophy only *after* damage attenuated), on the repeated-bout evidence where soreness, creatine kinase and CRP fall to near-nothing by weeks 8–10 while growth continues, and on Foley 1999, where high-damage eccentric work left muscle volume ~10% *below* baseline for weeks. The metabolic-stress case fails on lactate infusion raising muscle lactate without raising anabolic signalling, and on Dankel 2016, where cuffing *after* sets **reduced** growth in women. Mechanical tension is the one stimulus left standing. A 2020 article resting its rationale on damage and metabolites is arguing from the framework that material was written to retire.

**In the article's defence, it hedges properly.** These are introduced as "theoretical mechanisms that may explain some of the unique effects," and the paragraph closes: "the field of muscle hypertrophy is still rapidly evolving and none of this is well-established science yet." That is the right confidence level, and it is stated in the text rather than buried.

**One attribution does not hold.** The claim that "during dynamic movements, most motor units are recruited similarly during both light (<50% 1RM) and heavy (>85%) efforts" is linked to **Potvin & Fuglevand 2017**, which is a *computational simulation* of motor-unit fatigue in *PLoS Computational Biology* — not an experiment on dynamic lifting, and not a light-versus-heavy comparison. The empirical support for that proposition in this library sits with Morton 2019 and Muddle 2018 (see `../House of Hypertrophy videos/best-rep-range-build-muscle.md`), neither of which is cited here. Read the recruitment claim as sound and the citation under it as the wrong paper.

**A small internal tension.** Having argued myo-reps work partly through recruitment, Lutz then says they probably *don't* recruit more motor units, and that "it is unlikely that some of our highest-threshold motor units will be available to contribute much after the activation set." Both statements point the same way — myo-reps are a light-load, high-volume tool — but they undercut the recruitment framing offered a paragraph earlier. Her closing position is the defensible one: myo-reps are unlikely to be optimal for 1RM strength, and the high-threshold units get their stimulus from the heavy work elsewhere in the programme.

## Evidence versus recommendation

**Evidence cited:** training volume has a dose-dependent relationship with hypertrophy (Schoenfeld 2019) and with strength (a second meta-analysis linked but unresolved). That is all.

**Recommendation:** that myo-reps are a good way to spend a constrained fifteen minutes on a supplemental lift. This is coaching judgement built on a plausible chain — same load, short rests, more reps per minute — with no trial behind it. Nothing in the article claims otherwise, which is to its credit, but a reader should not come away thinking myo-reps have been tested against straight sets. They have not, here.

**Sells:** the article opens by pointing at Barbell Medicine's paid [training programmes](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/training-programs/) and [coaching](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/coaching/), and its framing is explicitly "you may have seen myo-reps programmed in your training" — it exists partly to explain a customer-facing prescription. The technique itself is not theirs; the term was coined by **Børge Fagerli** in the mid-2000s, which they credit.
