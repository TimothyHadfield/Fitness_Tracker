# Myo-Reps: One Near-Failure Set Then Clusters of 3–5 on 20-Second Rests

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Myo-Reps" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/myo-reps/)) · Leah Lutz · 2020-08-07 · 1,776 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **The protocol, precisely.** One "activation" set of roughly **12–30 reps taken to RPE 8** (about two reps in reserve), then repeated **3–5 rep "back-off" sets with the same load on 20–30 second rests**, stopping when you can no longer complete 3 reps. Written in their programs as e.g. `12-15 reps @ RPE 8, 3-5 reps`.
- **Load calibration.** They give rep-to-1RM estimates: 10–12 reps ≈ **62–68% 1RM**, 12–15 ≈ **58–62%**, 12–20 ≈ **50–62%**, with the explicit caveat of large between-person variation. Target a load that reaches failure within about **five back-off sets**; if you complete five sets of five, go heavier next session.
- **Why they use it: time, not superiority.** The stated primary use case is a lower-priority supplemental lift when a lifter has 10–15 minutes left. They say plainly they would **not** use myo-reps for competition lifts, because the task demands are wrong for maximal strength.
- **The mechanism section is explicitly speculative** — Lutz says the metabolic-stress and muscle-damage explanations are "theoretical" and that "none of this is well-established science yet." That is unusually honest for a technique article.
- **They correct a common myo-rep sales pitch:** the technique does *not* recruit more motor units than ordinary training. If anything, the highest-threshold units are unlikely to contribute much after the activation set has drained them.

## What the format is actually buying you
The design logic is volume density. A conventional straight-set prescription spends most of its time resting; myo-reps compress the rest so that a large number of near-failure-ish reps accumulate in a few minutes at a submaximal load. Lutz's stated targets are hypertrophy, work capacity and strength-endurance — not 1RM.

The two-parameter structure also gives a tuning knob most cluster methods do not. She spells it out: a **large gap** between activation reps and back-off reps (14@8 → 4@10) leaves the back-off sets further from failure, so more of them fit and total reps go up — a high-volume, lower-intensity phase. A **small gap** (10@8 → 5@10) pushes each back-off set closer to failure and caps total volume — a lower-volume, higher-intensity phase. Same technique, opposite programming intent.

Practical instruction worth keeping: stop the activation set **when it hits RPE 8, whatever the rep count** — 10 reps or 25 — because the effort target, not the rep range, is the prescription. And if you have no timer, 5–6 slow deep breaths approximates the 20–30 second rest.

## The evidence, and one bad citation
The article makes only three evidence claims, each carried by an inline hyperlink rather than a numbered marker.

**Volume dose-response — sound.** The claim that training volume has a dose-dependent relationship with hypertrophy links to [Schoenfeld 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30153194/), the 1/3/5-sets-per-exercise trial in trained men, which found hypertrophy increased with volume but strength did not. The parallel strength claim links to [Ralston 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28755103/), a meta-analysis of weekly set volume and strength gain. Both are the right papers for the claims.

**Motor unit recruitment — the wrong kind of paper.** The sentence "recent evidence suggests that during dynamic movements, most motor units are recruited similarly during both light (<50% 1RM) and heavy (>85%) efforts" links to **[Potvin & Fuglevand 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28574981/), "A motor unit-based model of muscle fatigue" in *PLoS Computational Biology*.** That is a computational simulation, not an empirical study of dynamic human movement. The underlying claim is defensible — there is real EMG and interpolated-twitch work supporting comparable activation at task failure across loads (Morton 2019 and Muddle 2018, both elsewhere in this library) — but the paper cited here does not contain that evidence. **Flag it: the conclusion is probably right, the citation does not support it.**

**Muscle damage as a growth mechanism.** The explanation that training damages contractile proteins and recruits satellite cells is stated as settled and cited to nothing. By 2020 this was already contested — exercise-induced muscle damage is now generally regarded as a by-product rather than a required driver of hypertrophy. Treat it as period explanation, not evidence.

## Where this sits against the library's material on effort and mechanism

**Two clashes, one real and one only apparent.**

*Real, on mechanism.* The article's stimulus chain runs through **muscle damage and metabolic stress**, and the House of Hypertrophy corpus in this library was largely written to retire both. On damage: Damas 2016 found the myofibrillar protein-synthesis spike tracked hypertrophy only *after* damage attenuated; the repeated-bout evidence has soreness, creatine kinase and CRP falling to near-nothing by weeks 8–10 while growth continues; Foley 1999 left muscle volume roughly 10% *below* baseline for weeks after high-damage eccentric work. On metabolites: infusing lactate raised muscle lactate without raising anabolic signalling, and Dankel 2016 found cuffing *after* sets **reduced** growth in women. Mechanical tension is the one stimulus left standing there. Lutz's hedge — "none of this is well-established science yet" — is what keeps this a dated framing rather than an error, but a reader should not take the damage/metabolite story as the reason myo-reps work.

*Apparent, on effort.* An RPE 8 activation set is 2 RIR, which sits inside the **3–1 RIR band** the cross-source consensus treats as matching failure; the back-off sets then run to RPE 10. So myo-reps are not an easier way to train — they are a set-extension technique built around failure, and the honest description is that they buy **volume per minute**, not extra stimulus per set. That is exactly the use case the article gives, so there is no contradiction with the library's position; the point is that the library's finding (3–1 RIR matches failure once volume is equated) predicts myo-reps should be *equivalent* to conventional sets at matched volume, not superior. Nothing in the article claims otherwise, and nothing in it tests the question.

## Where their advice outruns the evidence
There is **no cited comparison of myo-reps against volume-matched straight sets** anywhere in the article — no trial, no meta-analysis. Every claim about myo-reps specifically (more metabolic stress, more damage, better strength-endurance) is mechanistic reasoning, and Lutz labels it as such. The defensible summary is: this is a time-efficient way to accumulate volume at a submaximal load, and volume is what has the dose-response data. Whether the clustering adds anything beyond the volume it lets you fit is untested here.

The technique is not Barbell Medicine's — it is credited to Børge Fagerli, mid-2000s. The article's opening frames it as something you will encounter **in their programs and coaching**, both of which they sell; there is no separate product pitch.
