## Bottom line
- The thesis: **strength standards are descriptive, not prescriptive.** They tell you where a population landed under a given amount of training; they do not tell you your ceiling, your programme, or whether your training is working.
- **The health threshold is far lower than the performance threshold.** In the Aerobics Center Longitudinal Study (8,762 men, aged 20–82, mean 18.9 years follow-up), the middle third of muscular strength was ~28% less likely to die of any cause than the weakest third, and the strongest third ~23% less likely, after adjusting for cardiorespiratory fitness and the usual confounders. **The gain is in leaving the bottom third, not in reaching the top.**
- **The performance dataset is enormous and honestly framed**: 809,986 drug-tested, unequipped powerlifting competition entries, 1968–2022, 571,650 male and 238,336 female, across 15 federations (van den Hoek 2024). Barbell Medicine repeatedly warns that this is a self-selected, specifically-trained population and that most recreational lifters will sit well below its 50th percentile.
- **Age decline in trained people is a different curve from the population average.** General-population strength falls roughly 1–3% per year after 40; in Latella's masters powerlifting data, women over 59 still *gain* ~2.5–5% per year and men over 69 lose ~0.35% per year — about a third the sedentary rate.
- **Fiatarone's nursing-home trials are the anchor and deserve to be**: mean age 90, eight weeks at 80% of 1RM, **+174% leg strength**; then a 100-person RCT with mean gains of 113%. Two subjects stopped needing canes; tandem gait speed improved 48%.
- **The sex claim is the clean one**: men and women differ in absolute and relative performance, but not in how they respond to training. Roberts' 2020 meta-analysis found no significant difference in hypertrophic response.
- Sells templates, a free "Training Plateau Action Plan" lead magnet, and coaching. The percentile calculator it points to (thestrengthinitiative.com) is a third party's.

## The numbers the article reports

The charts themselves — the Vital 5 leg-press zones and the male/female decile tables — did not survive extraction, so what follows is what the prose states.

**Competitive powerlifters, 90th percentile male, squat across the lifespan:** 2.83× bodyweight in young adulthood, ~2.50× in middle age, 2.05× at 60–79, 1.72× at 80+. Deadlift on the same bands: 3.25× → 2.51× → 2.19× → 2.30×. Female 90th percentile young adult: squat 2.26×, bench 1.35× (against male 1.95×); at 80+, squat 1.01× and deadlift 1.68×.

**Recreational lifters after one year** of consistent progressive barbell training: squat bodyweight to 1.5×, bench 0.7–1.1×, deadlift 1.25–1.75× — which the article says corresponds roughly to the **10th percentile of competitive powerlifters** in the same age and weight class. That is a useful calibration and it is uncited.

**Training-career trajectory** (Latella, >9,000 powerlifters, 15-year retrospective with longitudinal growth modelling): ~7.5–12.5% gain in the first year of competitive training, ~20% cumulative by ten years, logarithmic rather than linear, with women progressing slightly faster early. Note carefully that this is measured **from a lifter's first competition**, not from untrained — a reader should not take "20% over ten years" as the total return on a lifting career.

**Grip strength** (PURE, 139,691 adults aged 35–70 across 17 countries): each 5 kg lower handgrip was associated with 17% higher cardiovascular mortality and 7% higher non-cardiovascular mortality, and grip predicted mortality better than systolic blood pressure. No relationship with incident diabetes, fractures or fall injuries.

**Hypertrophy's share of strength gain**: Erskine's data puts muscle growth at roughly **25% of the individual variance** in 1RM strength gains in trained men, the rest being neural and skill factors. **Heritability**: twin work puts 50–60% of the variance in baseline strength down to inherited factors — this is the claim whose marker points past the reference list.

## The best argument in the article

The **denominator problem** is the section worth keeping. A 5 lb increase on a 135 lb squat is 3.7%; the same 5 lb on a 405 lb squat is 1.2%. Identical new contractile capacity, very different percentage. Feigenbaum's conclusion — that "decelerating gains" after the first few years are substantially an artefact of measuring against a moving denominator rather than a change in physiology — is a genuinely useful reframe, and it does the work the beginner/intermediate/advanced tiers are usually asked to do.

He then goes further than that, arguing that **the underlying adaptive processes do not change rate at any point in a training career**. That is a strong claim, it is not supported by anything cited in this article, and it is deferred to a separate piece (*Progressive Loading Part 3*). **Flagged as overreach relative to what is presented here** — the denominator argument explains part of the deceleration, not obviously all of it.

The tier labels get the same treatment: beginner / intermediate / advanced describe **how quickly an improvement becomes detectable** (session-to-session vs monthly), and nothing else about the person's physiology or programme. Barbell Medicine's own preferred split is binary — untrained (under roughly a year of consistent progressive loading) and trained.

## Where the evidence is stretched

- **The Vital 5 leg-press cutoffs are "calibrated against" the ACLS mortality data.** ACLS was **men only**, aged 20–82, and its strength measure was not a leg-press relative-strength ratio of the kind the Vital 5 uses. Deriving sex-neutral leg-press thresholds from it is an extrapolation the article does not flag.
- **"The biggest survival benefit came from moving out of the weakest third, not from moving from strong to elite."** The reported figures are 28% (middle third) and 23% (top third) reductions versus the weakest third. Those two estimates almost certainly have overlapping confidence intervals; treating the ordering as real is a mild overread, though the broader point — that the return is front-loaded — is sound and matches the wider strength-mortality literature.
- **LIFTMOR-M is described alongside LIFTMOR** (bone density gains of 2.2–4.1% in the lumbar spine over eight months of twice-weekly heavy training), but only the original LIFTMOR trial in postmenopausal women appears in the reference list.
- **The power section is entirely uncited** — muscle mass declining ~1%/year, strength 1.5–3%/year, power fastest of all via selective loss of high-threshold motor units serving Type II fibres. Plausible and standard, but unsourced here.

## Where the article is unusually rigorous

The **grip strength** discussion is the strongest clinical writing in the piece. It reports PURE's headline result, then immediately limits it: much of a grip score reflects build, lean mass and genetics rather than current health; grip does not scale tightly with training in lifters who do not train it specifically; and **the sarcopenia cutoffs disagree with each other** — a 30 kg male grip is sarcopenic under the US SDOC definition and normal under the European EWGSOP2. It then offers relative grip (force ÷ bodyweight) as the more useful version for non-clinical adults, with target zones above 0.66 for men and 0.50 for women, which are Barbell Medicine's own thresholds rather than published cutoffs.

The **sex-differences** section is likewise clean: real average differences in absolute and relative performance, **no evidence of a different response to training**, and therefore programming should be individualised to training history and capacity rather than to sex. "Programs do not need to be 'made for women' or 'made for men'."

## How to use the numbers, per the article

**Do** use standards as a sanity check (two years of training and still in the 5th percentile is a signal to investigate stimulus, technique, life stress, or programme structure), as shorthand, and as a longitudinal benchmark on yourself. **Do not** use them as a target, a substitute for individualised programming, or evidence of biological capacity. What matters is whether your trend is up against your own 4–8 week anchor.
