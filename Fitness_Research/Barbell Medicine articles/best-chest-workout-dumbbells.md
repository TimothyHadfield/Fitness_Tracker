# Seven Dumbbell Chest Exercises, and an Admission That the Standard Set Prescriptions Are Made Up

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The 7 Best Chest Workout With Dumbbells" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-chest-workout-with-dumbbells/)) · Barbell Medicine (unattributed staff) · 2024-02-12 · 4,459 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **The seven-exercise ranking is backed by neither hypertrophy outcome data nor activation data.** Only one of the seven carries any citation at all, and that one ([Allen 2013](https://doi.org/10.70252/mhgt5804)) is an sEMG study of push-up variations. The order, the movement-category labels and the rep/RPE prescriptions are uncited coaching judgement.
- **The best thing in the article is a caveat, not a recommendation.** On bench angle they state that "sEMG differences using different bench angles don't reliably predict actual outcomes, e.g., differences in muscle size and strength" — then keep the angle recommendation as preference rather than prescription. On decline pressing they say outright that whether it produces regional hypertrophy differences "is unknown."
- **They also disown two of the most repeated prescriptions in the industry**: that 2–3 sets per exercise for beginners "is actually not backed by any experimental evidence", and that stopping 1 rep short of failure is "completely made up." They then cite evidence that gains are similar at **4–5 reps short of failure**.
- **The reference list has a real defect that the automated mapping check did not catch.** References [15], [16] and [17] are **verbatim duplicates of [3], [4] and [5]** (Ford, Post, Piercy) and are attached to the resistance-training injury-rate claim, which none of them supports — that claim belongs to Aasa 2017, which is filed at [8] and attached instead to a proximity-to-failure claim it has nothing to do with. Two claims in this article point at the wrong papers.
- Useful hard number that *is* correctly sourced elsewhere in the literature: resistance training runs about **1–4 injuries per 1,000 training hours**, against 0.5–2 per 1,000 hours for cycling and walking.
- Sells Barbell Medicine templates and one-on-one coaching at the close.

## What is recommended, and on what basis

Dumbbell bench press (3–4×6–10, RPE 7–8), dumbbell push-up (2–3 sets, RPE 8–9), incline dumbbell press at 15–30° (2–4×6–10, RPE 7–8), dumbbell fly (2–4×10–15, RPE 8–9), crush-grip press (2–4×10–15, RPE 8–9), 15-degree decline press (2–4×8–12, RPE 7–8), dumbbell pullover (2–4×10–20, RPE 8–9). Three sample workouts follow — beginner, strength, hypertrophy — differing in rep range, volume and proximity to failure in the conventional way.

**Evidence type, stated explicitly: none of this is outcome-based.** The framing claim — "save for improving strength performance in the barbell bench press, dumbbells are just as good as barbells for training your pectorals" — is uncited, though it is consistent with the wider equipment-equivalence literature and they repeat it later as "resistance training done with any type of equipment is similarly effective."

## Where the article is better than its genre

Three passages are worth extracting because they run against the incentive to sound certain:

**On bench angle.** They summarise the sEMG picture honestly — more vertical bench, more anterior deltoid and upper pec; lower bench, less deltoid and more pec — and then say the surface EMG differences do not reliably predict size and strength outcomes. **This is the correct treatment of activation data** and it is the single most transferable line in all three of Barbell Medicine's chest listicles.

**On decline pressing.** "Whether this produces regional differences in hypertrophy compared to using a flat or incline bench is unknown, but we think training a muscle group at multiple angles is a good idea." That sentence separates the evidence claim from the recommendation cleanly, which is exactly what a reader needs.

**On set and proximity-to-failure prescriptions.** They quote the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans (2×/week, 1–3 sets of 8–12 reps, within 1 rep of failure), then dismantle it: the 2–3 set figure has no experimental basis, the guidelines say nothing about how many exercises or movements per muscle group per session, and the 1-rep-from-failure instruction is invented. They cite work indicating similar strength and hypertrophy at **4–5 reps short of failure**. This is a **clinician arguing against a government guideline they themselves quoted**, and it is well done.

## Where the citations break

This article passed the automated marker-to-reference offset check, but its reference *list* is defective in a way that check cannot see.

- **Entries 15, 16 and 17 are word-for-word duplicates of entries 3, 4 and 5** — Ford's long-term athlete development review, Post's youth sport-specialisation study, and Piercy's Physical Activity Guidelines. They are cited to support "the risk of injury with resistance training is so low that the average rate is only 1–4 injuries per 1,000 resistance training hours." **None of those three papers is about resistance-training injury rates.** The paper that is — Aasa's systematic review of injuries among weightlifters and powerlifters — sits at [8].
- **[8] Aasa is instead cited, alongside three velocity-loss and relative-intensity studies, for the claim about training 4–5 reps short of failure.** An injury epidemiology review has no bearing on that claim. Strip it out and the remaining three ([Pareja-Blanco 2017a](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2016-0170), [Pareja-Blanco 2017b](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12678), [Carroll 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports7070169)) do support it reasonably.

Two smaller mismatches:

- The "**Better Range of Motion**" claim is cited to [Solstad 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33239937/), which compared **muscle activation** between barbell bench press and dumbbell flyes. It is not a range-of-motion study.
- The "**Improved Stability**" claim is cited to [Saeterbakken 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2010.543916), which compared three chest presses with different stability requirements and found that **less stable conditions reduce 1RM and, in places, activation**. Using it to argue dumbbells "help improve stability, even with lighter loads" reads more into it than it shows.

**Flagged overreach:** the argument against early specialisation for a beginner choosing chest exercises is supported by a review of the long-term athlete development model and a **cross-sectional association study in youth athletes**. Extending youth-sport specialisation findings to an adult beginner's exercise variety is a substantial leap, and it is stated as fact ("early specialization increases the risk of injury and decreases long term gains in health and performance").

## Recovery, and a small contradiction

The recovery section cites a 2003 study for the claim that complete muscle recovery takes 72 hours, then immediately argues that complete muscle recovery is not required to keep training productively — fitness accumulates across sessions while the fatigue cost of a given load falls over weeks and months. **The reasoning is sound and the citation is thin** (a protocol-development paper on testing muscular endurance recovery, unresolved in the citation record). The section then recommends the same 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines they had just criticised as a reasonable starting point for beginners — reasonable in practice, but the article does not acknowledge the switch.

## Commercial disclosure and duplication

The closing section sells Barbell Medicine training templates and personalised coaching. Separately, this is one of a near-identical set of chest listicles: the anatomy framing, RPE ranges, movement-category labels and the "change exercises every 6–10 weeks" line recur in *Best Bodyweight Chest Workout Without Equipment* and *5 Exercises For Chest and Back Workout Day*. **Read this one for the sEMG caveat and the set-prescription demolition; those two passages do not appear in the other two.**
