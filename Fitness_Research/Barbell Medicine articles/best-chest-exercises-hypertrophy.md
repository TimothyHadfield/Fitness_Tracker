# The Chest Exercise Ranking That Says Rankings Don't Exist — Then Ranks Anyway

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Best Chest Exercises For Hypertrophy" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-chest-exercises-for-hypertrophy/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2023-12-07 · 5,254 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- The article's own thesis is that **there is no best chest exercise** — growth is set by how much trainable, recoverable, progressible work you accumulate, and exercises are just delivery vehicles. It opens and closes on this. Most of the middle contradicts it.
- **The one place they are unusually rigorous is incline vs. flat.** They cite two EMG studies, then cite [Vigotsky's methodological rebuttal](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001249) arguing that greater EMG does not imply greater recruitment or hypertrophic potential, then cite [Chaves 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32922646/), an actual 8-week training study. Conclusion: bench angle is not established as better or worse for growth. **This is the correct standard, and no other "best exercises" article in this library applies it.**
- **They then abandon that standard two sections later.** The 15-degree decline dumbbell press is justified entirely by [Glass & Armstrong 1997](https://doi.org/10.1519/00124278-199708000-00006), a pure EMG study of pec activation across bench angles — exactly the class of evidence they had just told the reader cannot support this inference. The ranking is activation-based, not outcome-based, and the article knows it.
- **Every exercise recommendation here is activation data or opinion, not hypertrophy outcome data.** No cited study compares any of these eight exercises for muscle growth. The one training study cited (Chaves) is used to say a difference *wasn't* found.
- The verified citation mapping exposes several references attached to sentences they do not support — the pullover claim and the isolation-exercise claim are each pinned to papers about something else entirely.
- They sell the programmes at the end: Hypertrophy I and II, Bodybuilding I and II templates, plus individualised coaching.

## The eight exercises, and what backs them

The recommendations are grouped into four paired "workouts" using Barbell Medicine's own template vocabulary — a whole-chest day, a mechanics variation, a loading variation, and a fatigue variation.

| Exercise | Prescription | Evidence cited for choosing it |
|---|---|---|
| Barbell bench press | 3–4 × 4–6, RPE 7–8 | None. Popularity and familiarity. |
| Dumbbell fly | 2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9 | None. "Larger ROM, less triceps." |
| Incline dumbbell press | 3–4 × 8–12, RPE 7–8 | EMG studies **plus the rebuttal to EMG** → verdict: no angle superiority |
| High-to-low cable crossover | 2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9 | None. "Isolation of upper and inner chest" — asserted. |
| Cable fly | 3–4 × 8–12, RPE 8–9 | [5] Schoenfeld 2010 mechanisms review — does not address standing cable flies |
| Dumbbell pullover | 2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9 | [6] Ahtiainen 2016 — an unrelated paper on inter-individual variation |
| 15° decline dumbbell press | 3–4 × 6–10, RPE 7–8 | [7] Glass & Armstrong 1997 — **EMG only** |
| Parallel bar dips | 2–3 sets to failure | None. Torso-angle claims explicitly labelled "mostly speculative" |

Read as evidence, that table is the article. Two exercises have a citation attached to the selection rationale; one of those two citations is EMG and the other is off-target. The rest is clinical preference, which is a legitimate thing for coaches to offer but is a different kind of claim from "research shows."

To their credit, they say so in two places. On dips: torso angle claims are "mostly speculative, so we recommend trying both and seeing what you prefer and respond best to." On incline: "many claim that incline presses target the upper pecs more effectively than flat, but there isn't enough evidence to support this claim."

## The internal contradiction on EMG

This is the most useful thing in the article and worth stating plainly, because it is a controlled demonstration of the failure mode this library has documented across every activation-based ranking.

In the **incline dumbbell press** section they write that studies have found differences in muscle excitation across bench angles, "but this isn't predictive of actual outcomes, so we can't say that one inclination is particularly better for building strength or hypertrophy." The four references behind that sentence include Vigotsky's explicit argument that surface EMG amplitude cannot be read as recruitment or growth potential, and Chaves 2020, which trained untrained young men on horizontal versus incline bench for eight weeks and is a genuine outcome study.

Roughly 6,000 characters later, in the **15-degree decline** section: "whereas an incline dumbbell bench seems to generate more muscle excitation in the upper portion of the pectoralis major, the decline dumbbell bench does the opposite — showing increased excitation of the lower sternocostal and/or abdominal portions." The single reference is a 1997 EMG study. The excitation finding is being used exactly as they said it could not be used, to justify placing a specific bench angle in the programme.

**Flagged:** the two sections are irreconcilable as written. Either excitation differences justify exercise selection or they do not. The incline section states the defensible position; the decline section quietly reverts to the industry one.

## Citations attached to the wrong sentences

Because the marker-to-reference mapping verified clean for this article, we can check individual attributions rather than guess. Three do not hold up:

- **The pullover.** "Dumbbell pullovers also work the latissimus dorsi, but likely not as much as they work the pectoralis major. [6]" Reference 6 is Ahtiainen 2016, a study of heterogeneity in strength and mass responses across 287 adults. It says nothing about pullovers, lats or pecs.
- **The cable fly.** "A standing cable fly can be more demanding for the muscle groups that are engaged, meaning that you could efficiently train for hypertrophy with much lower loads. [5]" Reference 5 is Schoenfeld's 2010 mechanisms-of-hypertrophy review. It is a general mechanisms paper, not a comparison of standing versus supported flies, and it does not support a load-efficiency claim about a specific exercise.
- **The isolation-versus-compound point.** "This allows the trainee to target a specific muscle group directly compared to doing compound lifts. [12,13]" Reference 12 is the Schoenfeld/Ogborn/Krieger frequency meta-analysis, which is about weekly session frequency and has nothing to do with joint count. Reference 13 (Gentil 2015, single- vs multi-joint) is on point.

There is also a garbled sentence in the frequency section: the meta-analysis of 22 studies is described as concluding that "when the exercise volume is equated between programs, the **intensity** of the programs doesn't significantly change the results." The cited paper ([Grgic 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x)) is about **frequency**, not intensity. The surrounding paragraph makes clear frequency is what they meant.

## The programming section, which is better

Set aside the exercise list and the last third of the article is a competent summary of training variables, and its citations mostly land.

- **Volume.** Hypertrophy has a dose-dependent relationship with volume [10,11]. They immediately qualify: work at a volume you can tolerate. But they then claim that if training stress is too high an individual "might incur too much muscle protein breakdown, which may lead to muscular atrophy rather than hypertrophy" — no citation, and it does not follow from the dose-response evidence they just cited. **This is overreach**; overreaching training reduces the rate of gain, but training-induced net atrophy in a fed lifter is not an established outcome.
- **Intensity.** Above roughly 60–70% 1RM beats lower loads for strength ([Schoenfeld 2017 low- vs high-load meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000002200)); loads above 90% 1RM should feature for maximal strength ([Androulakis-Korakakis 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports6030086) — note this is a **pilot study** in a small group of competitive powerlifters, thin support for a general prescription); and **for hypertrophy the usable range is roughly 30–90% 1RM provided sets go close to failure**, with comparable gains across it.
- **Frequency.** Frequency matters mainly through its effect on volume. Equate volume and frequency stops mattering much; spreading sets out reduces per-session fatigue and is often more practical.
- **Individual response.** The best-evidenced claim in the article. Ahtiainen 2016 followed 287 adults on the same programme for six months and found muscle size changes ranging from **−11% to +30%**, with sex and age explaining little of it.

That intensity finding lines up cleanly with the [rep-range material already in this library](../House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/best-rep-range-build-muscle.md) — both rest on the same 2017 Schoenfeld meta-analysis, and 30–90% 1RM to near failure is the same claim as "roughly 5 to 35 reps." Two independent sources reaching the same conclusion from the same evidence base.

## Where the thesis and the content part company

The opening states there are no best chest exercises. The body then asserts that "for most, hypertrophy is best achieved when all the muscles are targeted individually" and that "you can only train a muscle group for maximum growth by isolating it and loading it with resistance" — an absolute claim, uncited, and in tension both with the opening and with Gentil 2015, which found single- and multi-joint work produced similar outcomes.

**Recommendation vs. evidence:** the rep and RPE prescriptions, the four-workout structure, the compound-then-isolation ordering and the advice to train larger muscle groups first are all coaching preference presented without citation. They are reasonable. They are not findings.

**Commercial note:** the article closes with a pitch for four Barbell Medicine hypertrophy templates and for individualised coaching. The "there is no best exercise, what matters is a structured programme" framing is both their honest position and the exact frame that makes a programme the product. Worth naming; not a reason to discount the argument.
