# Protein Does Not Slow Your Digestion

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Protein Slows Digestion? Nope." ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/protein-slows-digestion-nope/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2014-03-26 · 1,012 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- A line-by-line rebuttal of a content-farm article claiming high protein intake causes "sluggish digestion". The central correction: **protein-rich and carbohydrate-rich meals empty from the stomach at roughly the same rate**, and normal gastric emptying after a meal is **2–6 hours**.
- Protein is not "hard to digest". It is broken down by stomach acid and pancreatic enzymes as routine physiology, and absorbed rapidly — **whey raises plasma amino acids within about 20 minutes**.
- What actually changes gastric emptying rate in a mixed meal: **fat slows it; soluble fibre slows it and insoluble fibre speeds it; larger total calorie load is slower; isotonic meals empty faster than hypo- or hypertonic ones**. Prior meals, circulating ghrelin, food already in the tract and medications all confound it further.
- The rebuttal also corrects the source article's clinical claims: **diarrhoea is transit that is too fast, not too slow**; **IBS is a mixed picture**, not uniformly sluggish; and **Crohn's causes malabsorption because the mucosa cannot absorb, not because food moves slowly**.
- **No references.** This is an old forum post reposted to the blog, written entirely from clinical knowledge with nothing cited.

## The digestion primer

The most durable part of the article is the absorption route, which he gives because the source article got it wrong by saying nutrients go straight into the blood and out to organs:

- **Protein and carbohydrate** are absorbed as amino acids and monosaccharides across the small-intestinal brush border into the enterocyte, into the **portal vein**, and to the **liver first** — distribution happens after that.
- **Fat** is absorbed as fatty acids into the enterocyte, packaged with cholesterol and phospholipids into **chylomicrons**, into the **lymphatic system**, then into venous circulation — bypassing the first-pass liver route. It goes preferentially to tissues with high mitochondrial density for beta-oxidation or peroxisomes for long-chain fatty acid oxidation, chiefly liver and skeletal muscle.

The clinically useful point buried in this is the distinction he insists on: a **pathologically** slow stomach — gastroparesis, ileus, obstruction — is a different thing from a meal that happens to empty slowly because of its composition. The source article conflates the two, which is what lets it turn a normal physiological variation into a health problem.

## The gastroparesis correction

This is where being written by a physician shows. Against the source article's claim that gastroparesis is a vagus-nerve malfunction, he gives the actual differential, ranked:

1. **Diabetes mellitus**
2. **Idiopathic**
3. **Post-surgical**, especially with vagus nerve damage

with further causes including medications; alcohol, tobacco and cannabis; infection (mononucleosis, Chagas, rotavirus); CNS injury such as tumour or stroke; peripheral nervous system pathology (Parkinson's, Guillain-Barré); and a miscellany of cancers, hypothyroidism, lupus, obstruction, portal hypertension and HIV. Vagal injury is one route into the condition, not the definition of it.

Similarly on Crohn's: he describes it as dysregulated inflammation in response to bacteria in the gut wall, producing pro-inflammatory mediators and direct mucosal injury, presenting with diarrhoea, fatigue, weight loss, cramping abdominal pain, oral ulceration, perianal fissures, perirectal abscesses and malabsorption. His emphasis — in capitals in the original — is that the malabsorption is a **mucosal** failure at normal transit speed, not slowed transit.

## What to take from it

The practical conclusion is narrow and correct: **there is no reason to reduce protein intake out of concern for digestive transit**. If a high-protein meal sits heavily, the likelier culprits are its fat content, its total size, or its fibre profile.

The tone is a 2014 internet argument — profanity, "lol", the sign-off "-thefitcoach" — and it is a takedown of a low-quality article rather than a review of a literature. Everything in it is uncontroversial physiology, but a reader should note that **not a single claim is sourced**, including the specific ones (20-minute whey response, 2–6 hour gastric emptying, protein and carbohydrate emptying at similar rates). Those numbers are broadly consistent with the physiology literature but are asserted here on the author's authority alone.

Nothing is sold in this article.
