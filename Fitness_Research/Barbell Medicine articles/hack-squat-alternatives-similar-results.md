# Twelve Hack Squat Alternatives, None of Them Compared to a Hack Squat

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "12 Hack Squat Alternatives With Similar Results" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/hack-squat-alternatives/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-10-09 · 6,421 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- Twelve squat-pattern substitutes, sorted into **primary / secondary / tertiary** movement categories with rep prescriptions and a uniform loading rule: **RPE 7–8, i.e. 2–3 reps short of failure**, on every single exercise.
- **No ranking in this article is backed by hypertrophy outcome data, and none of the twelve is ever compared to a hack squat.** The title promises "similar results" and the body never tests the similarity. Eight references support six sentences across a 6,400-word article; five of the eight are **EMG or biomechanics** papers.
- The one training study in the reference list ([Walker & Häkkinen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000000381)) measured **strength** gains from leg press training in young and older men — not a comparison between exercises, and not a size outcome.
- **The clearest misattribution:** the claim that the goblet squat "tends to use much lighter absolute loads" cites two biomechanics papers on the back squat/powerlifting squat/box squat and on patellofemoral stress during squatting. Neither studied the goblet squat, and the same Swinton paper is listed twice, as [3] and [5].
- Their candour is the article's real strength and is unusual for the genre: repeatedly "it's not clear", "if forced to speculate", "there's little experimental data", and of the pendulum squat, "it's not some top-secret exercise that's going to change your life."
- **The belt squat is recommended clinically** — same lower-body training with less spinal loading, for people currently sensitive to loading or returning from injury. This is their most useful contribution here and it carries **no citation**; it is clinical reasoning, presented as a recommendation.

## The twelve, with their prescriptions

Primary (the article's own tiering, roughly: heavy, barbell, high skill):
- **Back squat** — 3–4 × 4–6
- **Front squat** — 3–4 × 4–6

Secondary (loadable, lower skill or lower systemic cost):
- **Cannonball / Platz squat** (narrow stance, heels elevated) — 2–4 × 6–10
- **Belt squat** — 2–4 × 6–10
- **Leg press** — 3–4 × 6–10
- **Pendulum squat** and **Hatfield squat** — 2–4 × 6–10 as secondary

Tertiary (accessory, lighter, unilateral or machine):
- **Goblet squat** — 2–4 × 10–15
- **Split squat** and **Bulgarian split squat** — 2–4 × 8–12
- **Lunge** — 2–4 × 10–15
- **Landmine squat** — 2–4 × 8–12
- **Pendulum / Hatfield** — 2–4 × 8–12 as tertiary

Their closing pick, one per tier: **back squat, leg press, and split squat or Bulgarian split squat** — offered explicitly as a preference, not as a result.

## Evidence audit, exercise by exercise

Taking the instruction to be explicit about what each claim rests on:

**Back squat.** One citation, on one sentence: "in sports, training the back squat can be useful for developing high-velocity strength, e.g. power" → [Myer 2014](https://doi.org/10.1519/ssc.0000000000000103), a *Strength & Conditioning Journal* article proposing an assessment of functional deficits in the squat. **Expert review, not a transfer trial.** The hypertrophy rationale given — large muscle mass through a long range of motion, good training economy — is **uncited reasoning**.

**Front squat.** No citation, and unusually honest: "it's unclear whether front squats produce greater amounts of quadriceps hypertrophy or strength gain than back squats… there's no long-term data available to show that one variation is superior." They note similar muscular excitation between variations and conclude carryover claims are "speculative". **Correctly labelled as speculation.**

**Cannonball/Platz squat.** No citation. "It's not clear whether the Platz squat produces greater amounts of quadriceps hypertrophy… as there's little experimental data available to say one way or another." The heels-elevated/narrow-stance rationale is a **moment-arm and range-of-motion argument**, offered as such.

**Goblet squat.** Two citation clusters, both problematic. The upper-back-stabiliser claim cites [Slater & Hart 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001323), an **EMG** comparison of squat techniques. The lighter-absolute-load claim cites [Swinton 2012](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e3182577067) (biomechanics of traditional, powerlifting and box squats) twice over, plus [Kernozek 2018](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2016-0197) (patellofemoral joint stress with anterior knee displacement). **None of those papers involve a goblet squat**, and the claim they are attached to — that you cannot hold a heavy dumbbell against your chest — is an ergonomic observation that needs no citation at all. **Activation and unrelated biomechanics.**

**Split squat and Bulgarian split squat.** No citations. Rationale is unilateral loading, balance, coordination, and increased range of motion from the elevated rear foot. **Mechanical reasoning only.**

**Lunge.** One citation on the sentence "the movement also requires some balance to execute" → [Marchetti 2018](https://doi.org/10.1515/hukin-2017-0174), an **EMG and balance** comparison of in-line versus traditional lunges. The citation supports the least consequential sentence in the section.

**Belt squat.** No citations. The claims: trains the same lower-body musculature with less spinal loading; better tolerated by people recently injured or sensitive to loading; relatively specific to the back squat so carryover is "more probable"; reduced lower-back and upper-body demand allows more tolerable volume; reduced balance demand allows harder effort before technical failure. **All uncited clinical and mechanical reasoning.** It is plausible reasoning and it is the section a reader is most likely to act on, which is why the absence of support is worth naming.

**Landmine squat.** No citation. Framed as "a heavier version of the goblet squat or an easier version of the front squat".

**Leg press.** Two citations. The muscles-worked description cites [Martín-Fuentes 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17134626), an **EMG systematic review** of the leg press and its variants. The claim that it is "an excellent exercise to promote strength and hypertrophy gains for novice and experienced trainees alike" cites [Walker & Häkkinen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000000381) — a short-term training study in young and older men that measured **strength** gains and the neuromuscular adaptations behind them. **This is the closest the article comes to outcome data, and it supports "leg press training works", not "leg press beats X".** The foot-placement guidance (higher = more adductor/hamstring, lower = more quad) is uncited and is a moment-arm claim.

**Pendulum squat.** No citation, and the most deflationary passage in the article: they like the machine, there is no convenient substitute for it, and it does not matter much either way.

**Hatfield squat.** No citation. Positioned as a free-weight substitute for the belt squat or leg press.

**Net: twelve exercises, six cited sentences, and the only ranking-relevant conclusion — the three they would pick — is stated as preference.** That is a more honest structure than most articles of this type, but the title's promise of "similar results" is not evidence-backed in either direction.

## Technique, and where they decline to prescribe

The how-to sections are consistent and worth recording as their standard: shoulder-width stance with toes out 15–30°, "the correct squat stance varies significantly between individuals and over time", descend to the hip crease below the top of the knee, brace with a held breath, and — for the back squat — high bar and low bar "train the same muscles" with similar training effects, so it is "mostly up to personal preference". That last point is consistent with what they say elsewhere and is one of the few claims here supported by the EMG literature they cite.

For split squats and Bulgarian split squats they recommend a **slower tempo (~2 s each phase)** to make the balance demand manageable, accepting that this reduces load — and suggest holding a rack with one hand while loading the other. For the Bulgarian, they recommend starting with a **10–12" rear-foot surface** rather than a standard 14–18" bench. These are coaching judgements, offered as such.

## How to actually use this list

Stripped of the evidence claims, the useful content is a **substitution map organised by constraint**, and that is how a reader should treat it. No rack or no spotter: leg press, pendulum squat, belt squat, landmine squat. No machines but plenty of free weights: Hatfield squat, goblet squat, split squat variants. Lower back currently unhappy: belt squat, leg press, goblet squat — all of which shift load off the spine. Bored, and everything is available: Platz squat or front squat as a secondary to a back squat you already do. Small space and dumbbells only: goblet squat, split squat, Bulgarian split squat, lunge. The tiering then tells you where in a session to put it and how heavy to go, which is the part their coaching experience is genuinely good for.

## Overreach, in one place
The title. "12 Hack Squat Alternatives **With Similar Results**" asserts equivalence of outcome; the body never measures an outcome and never mentions the hack squat again after the introduction. The article's own repeated admissions — no long-term data, little experimental data, speculation — contradict its headline. Everything under the headline is more careful than the headline.

## What they sell
The article closes by directing readers to Barbell Medicine's **coaching** (licensed personal trainers, physicians, dietitians and physical therapists) for programme design, and links in passing to their **Titan (CrossFit)** and **Strongman** templates from the back-squat section. No supplements here. The exercise recommendations do not depend on the products.
