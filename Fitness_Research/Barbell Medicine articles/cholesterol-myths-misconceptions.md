# Cholesterol Myths: What the Counter-Arguments Get Wrong, and Where This Rebuttal Overreaches

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Cholesterol Myths & Misconceptions" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/a-basic-guide-to-cholesterol-part-2-myths-misconceptions/)) · Austin Baraki · 2022-01-21 · 11,811 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

Part 2 of their cholesterol series: eleven common objections to the lipid hypothesis, each answered with the specific study being misread. Marker mapping is verified, so claims below link to the paper cited on that sentence. This article sells nothing.

## Bottom line
- **The single practical recommendation is to measure apolipoprotein B**, not particle size and not LDL-C alone. The argument is mechanistic and well cited: risk tracks particle *number*, and both "large fluffy" (>25 nm, ~27 nm mean) and "small dense" (<25 nm, down to 18 nm) LDL particles sit far below the ~70 nm threshold at which a lipoprotein can penetrate the vessel wall ([Tabas 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17938300/), [Williams 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1619368/)).
- **The strongest empirical claim** is that LDL-C relates linearly to plaque even inside the "normal" range: among 1,779 people with *no* conventional risk factors, half had plaque, rising from 11% at LDL-C 60–70 mg/dL to 64% at 150–160 (Fernández-Friera 2017). Lowering below 70 mg/dL halts progression ([Ahmadi 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2019.08.012)).
- **The safety case for very low LDL-C is genuinely strong**: LDL-C driven as low as 6.9 mg/dL in a prespecified FOURIER analysis, without excess death, cancer, cognitive decline, muscle problems or hypogonadism ([Giugliano 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28859947/)), backed by Mendelian randomisation showing genetically low lifelong LDL-C confers better survival with no cancer excess ([Postmus 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25855712/)).
- **The statin side-effect debunking is careful, not triumphalist.** They report the nocebo evidence and still retain that true myopathy is real, just rare — a distinction many popular rebuttals drop.
- **The saturated-fat section is the weakest.** Their methodological critique of the null studies (contrast, replacement nutrient, over-adjustment) is legitimate and specific, but the same scrutiny is never applied to the studies supporting their position, and the "18–20% versus <10% of calories" threshold is presented as an established breakpoint when the cited sources ([Hooper 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD011737.pub2), [Li 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26429077/)) show a dose-response rather than a documented threshold.
- **On seed oils they say "the theory must be rejected."** The human data are consistently null on inflammatory biomarkers and favourable on outcomes, which supports "not supported" more comfortably than "refuted."

## Decision 1: which number should I actually test?

**Not particle size.** The article's worked example is the clearest part of the piece. Two patients both at LDL-C 140 mg/dL, one pattern A and one pattern B: patient 2 genuinely is at higher risk, but not because the particles are small. LDL-C is a *mass* of cholesterol per volume of blood; smaller particles carry less cholesterol each, so it takes more of them to reach 140 mg/dL. More particles means more chances to enter the vessel wall. Once total particle number is accounted for, the size effect disappears ([Ip 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19349632/), [Mora 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16765964/)). Their conclusion — "a particle is a particle" ([Marston 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34773460/)) — means size testing is not clinically useful.

**Yes to apoB, because of discordance.** The corollary is the useful bit: high LDL-C with low apoB (few particles carrying plenty of cholesterol) is low risk; low LDL-C with high apoB is high risk ([Pencina 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25633587/), [Wilkins 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26791067/), [Lawler 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28174174/)). Discordance is common in insulin resistance and diabetes, which is exactly the population most likely to be falsely reassured by a normal LDL-C. Note the register shift: the evidence supports apoB as a *better predictor*; "we recommend measuring apoB" is a clinical recommendation, and in 2022 it ran ahead of most guidelines.

**Not a CAC score, at least not routinely.** Their handling here is nuanced. A zero score does mean much lower short-to-medium-term risk ([Hussain 2020](https://doi.org/10.1161/circulationaha.119.045026)), but calcification is a *late* marker: soft non-calcified plaque is invisible to it, and about a quarter to a third of events in young people occur at CAC 0 ([Gabriel 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29723329/)). Worse, calcium can rise for benign reasons — high-intensity exercise ([DeFina 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30698608/)) and statins ([Puri 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24448227/), [Puri 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2015.01.036)) both increase it while *reducing* risk, apparently by shifting plaque composition toward more stable calcified forms. So a zero score is most reassuring in an older person (where calcium is expected) and least in a young person or a woman (where it is not). They recommend against universal CAC screening, citing [Mandrola 2019](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2019/1215/p734.html) — a position more sceptical than the 2018 ACC/AHA framing of CAC as a decision aid in intermediate risk, and worth knowing is contested. Their practical advice: if you get one and it's zero, repeat in 5–10 years ([Lehmann 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29142010/)); if it's above zero, don't re-scan — treat the risk factors.

**A single test near an acute event is worthless.** LDL-C and total cholesterol are *negative* acute-phase reactants, along with albumin, vitamin D and testosterone ([Balci 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22758629/)). Cholesterol drawn on admission for a heart attack reads falsely low, which manufactures the association between low cholesterol and severe illness. This is the article's sharpest single methodological point and it explains a large share of the confusing observational literature.

## Decision 2: will lowering my cholesterol hurt me?

**Cells make their own.** The framing correction is that a blood test measures cholesterol *in transit on lipoproteins*, not cholesterol inside cells — and every cell makes essentially all it needs ([Brown & Goldstein 1986](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3513311)). The comparative anchors: newborn LDL-C sits around 30 mg/dL, when cholesterol demand is highest; isolated hunter-gatherer populations run 35–70 mg/dL lifelong with very low heart disease rates, against a modern US average near 130 (O'Keefe 2004).

**Testosterone.** The claim is narrower than a casual reader will assume, and correctly so. The testes rely mainly on locally synthesised cholesterol for steroidogenesis ([Hu 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20515451/)), and the cited work shows **no increase in clinical hypogonadism and no significant change in FSH or LH** ([Stamerra 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33227286/), [Travia 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7883839/), [Dobs 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11016911/), [Mondul 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20512526/)). That is not the same as "statins never move a testosterone number" — some of these analyses do find small reductions in total testosterone — and the article does not claim otherwise. Precision worth noting in a piece written for lifters.

**Muscle.** Significant muscle damage occurs in well under 1% of users. For the far more common complaint of aches without objective damage, the evidence is blinding: in a randomised trial of over 10,000 patients, muscle symptoms occurred at the same frequency on statin and placebo while patients were blinded, and diverged only once they knew ([Gupta 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(17)31075-9)). SAMSON then ran the design that settles it for the individual: 60 patients who had already stopped statins for perceived side effects were randomised through bottles of statin, placebo and no pill, rating symptom intensity 0–100 daily. Side-effect ratings on statin and placebo were statistically indistinguishable, and both roughly **double** the no-pill months; 90% of symptoms reported on statin were also reported on placebo ([Wood 2020](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmc2031173)). On objective function, statins do not consistently reduce strength or aerobic capacity ([Noyes 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28807461/)).

**Dementia and cancer.** HPS randomised 20,536 patients and found no difference in cognitive impairment or dementia over five years, including in the 75–85 subgroup ([HPS 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12114036/)); PROSPER (n=5,804) found no cognitive difference ([Trompet 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19653027/)); simvastatin did not slow established Alzheimer's ([Sano 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21795660/)). On cancer, randomised data show no effect in either direction ([Collins 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27616593/)).

**Effect size to weigh against all that:** lowering LDL-C by 1 mmol/L (~38 mg/dL) yields a consistent 22–24% reduction in cardiovascular events, and it does not matter how the reduction is achieved — statin, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitor, or diet ([Ference 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28444290/)). Longest-run data: 20 years of follow-up in children with familial hypercholesterolaemia treated with statins ([Luirink 2019](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa1816454)).

**One useful clinical aside:** for people struggling to tolerate a statin, they list switching agent, lowering the dose while adding ezetimibe, bedtime dosing, and every-other-day dosing of longer-acting statins. Practical, and offered as clinical judgement rather than trial evidence.

## Decision 3: should I believe the "paradox" arguments?

Three separate objections get separate answers.

**"People with high cholesterol live longer."** Findings of this shape come from cross-sectional or short-term studies started late in life. Two things generate them. First, **survivorship bias** — those with the highest lifetime exposure have already died of cardiovascular disease before the cohort is assembled. Second, **reverse causation** — cancer, chronic liver disease, cachexia and advanced organ failure all lower cholesterol, so low levels mark undiagnosed illness rather than cause it. The rebuttal is that genetic studies remove both problems, because the exposure is fixed from birth: genetically low LDL-C tracks with lower heart disease, better survival, and no cancer excess, across the lifespan including the oldest groups ([Postmus 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25855712/)), corroborated by 40–50 year prospective follow-up from early middle age (Hyttinen 2011). Randomised trials at scale add the same null on cancer and death ([CTT 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31942-1), Sabatine 2018).

**"It's the inflammation, not the cholesterol."** They concede more here than most rebuttals do: chronic inflammation genuinely raises risk, and anti-inflammatory drugs genuinely reduce events — colchicine ([Nidorf 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32865380/)) and canakinumab ([Ridker 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28845751/)). Their argument is that the two are independent, not competing: at low inflammation (CRP <2), elevated cholesterol still carries higher heart-attack risk than low cholesterol with low inflammation (Storey 2018). Oxidised LDL loses its association with events once apoB is accounted for, and fully oxidised particles do not circulate in meaningful concentrations because the liver clears them ([Holvoet 2008](https://doi.org/10.2217/17460875.3.6.637), [Wu 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16949489/)). In MESA, CRP, IL-6, fibrinogen and LDL particle size all associated with plaque progression until adjustment for traditional risk factors, after which significance disappeared (Zeb 2021).

**"Plenty of heart attacks happen at normal cholesterol."** Their answer is that this is true and does not mean what people think it means. About a third of heart disease occurred below the "normal" cutoff as far back as Framingham ([Kannel 1961](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-55-1-33), [Castelli 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3179802/)). The 200 mg/dL threshold, set by ATP-I in 1988, was explicitly acknowledged as arbitrary in the original document — which the article quotes at length, and which is a fair use of a primary source. Their case is that a continuous risk gradient was dichotomised for clinical convenience and the cut-point was set too high, exactly as happened with blood pressure before it was progressively lowered ([Saklayen 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26942184/)). The Fernández-Friera plaque data and the Framingham authors' own caveat about their reference population are the supporting evidence.

**Timing.** Because risk tracks *cumulative lifetime exposure*, intervening early buys far more than intervening late ([Robinson 2018](https://doi.org/10.1161/jaha.118.009778)); by advanced age most exposure has already accrued, so a much larger LDL reduction is needed for the same risk reduction (PSC 2007). This reframes "statins don't work in the elderly" as a prediction of the model rather than a refutation of it — a genuinely elegant argument, though it is also unfalsifiable in the direction that matters: any weak result in the old can be absorbed by it. Their concession that stopping statins in the very elderly is a reasonable trade against pill burden, and that the supporting data ([Giral 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehz458)) are observational with the usual limits, is honest.

## Decision 4: what should I do about saturated fat and seed oils?

**Saturated fat: the mechanism claim.** Saturated fat acts *indirectly*, by raising LDL/apoB, which then drives plaque — not by "clogging" arteries directly ([Clarke 1997](https://www.bmj.com/content/314/7074/112.full), [Ference 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28444290/), Borén 2020). Everything in the section follows from that mediation structure.

**Their three methodological critiques of the null studies are specific and checkable:**

1. **Insufficient contrast.** PURE divided saturated fat into quintiles where, in Asian regions, four of five quintiles ate 2–7% of calories and the top ate 12%; in non-Asian regions the top two quintiles were 11% and 14% ([Dehghan 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28864332/)). None of that tests >18–20% against <10%. The stroke signal at very low intake plausibly reflects general dietary inadequacy at 2% of calories from fat, not a protective effect of saturated fat. Malmö compared 13% against 22%, and its own authors wrote that "the SFA-CVD hypothesis is thus not fully testable in this population." Quoting a study's own limitations section back at people citing its headline is the right move.
2. **Replacement nutrient ignored.** Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat lowers risk substantially; with plant monounsaturated fat or fibrous carbohydrate, moderately; with refined carbohydrate, not at all; with trans fat, risk rises. The Sydney Diet-Heart Study's harmful "polyunsaturated" arm was 1960s margarine, i.e. trans fat. And [de Souza 2015](https://www.bmj.com/content/351/bmj.h3978) concluded flatly that "saturated fats are not associated with all-cause mortality, CVD, CHD…" while its own methods section states that most included studies did not model substitution and that the displaced carbohydrate was typically high-glycaemic-load processed food. That gap between a paper's methods and its abstract is a real and well-documented problem.
3. **Over-adjustment.** Studies that compare high versus low saturated fat and then adjust for LDL-C have controlled away the mediator — the entire causal path — and are guaranteed to find nothing ([Scarborough 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20534750/), applied to [Siri-Tarino 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20071648/) and de Souza). This is correct as stated and is the most transferable idea in the article.

They also make the useful non-obvious point that **dairy saturated fat other than butter behaves differently**, attributed to milk fat globule membrane, which churning removes ([Rosqvist 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26016870/)) — so lumping yoghurt and cheese in with butter and fatty meat is a modelling error.

**Flag on this section.** The critique runs in one direction. Studies finding no association get three pages of methodological dissection; the supporting sources — the Cochrane review, Li 2015, Jakobsen 2009 — are cited approvingly without comparable scrutiny. That matters because [Hooper 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD011737.pub2), their own citation, concludes that reducing saturated fat produces a modest reduction in *combined cardiovascular events* with little or no effect on all-cause or cardiovascular *mortality*. Separately, the "18–20% versus <10%" threshold is stated as a known property of the dose-response; neither cited source establishes a breakpoint at those figures. It functions in the argument as a criterion for dismissing null studies, so it carries more weight than its sourcing supports.

**Omega-6 and linoleic acid.** The proposed mechanism is linoleic acid → arachidonic acid → eicosanoids → inflammation, and the article tests each link in humans rather than in vitro:
- Increasing dietary linoleic acid by 551% or cutting it by 90% produced **no change in tissue arachidonic acid** ([Rett 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21663641/)); radiotracer work puts conversion at 0.2–0.3% ([Demmelmair 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10411666/), [Hussein 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15576848/)).
- 15 RCTs of linoleic acid feeding found virtually no evidence of raised inflammatory markers in healthy non-infant humans (Johnson 2012); a meta-analysis of 83 RCTs and >41,000 participants found little or no effect on inflammatory bowel disease or long-term inflammatory status ([Ajabnoor 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33084958/)); high omega-6 intake reduced liver fat versus a high-saturated-fat diet in abdominal obesity ([Bjermo 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22492369/)).
- On outcomes, 30 cohorts and 68,659 people using linoleic acid **biomarkers** (a good proxy, since humans cannot synthesise it) found higher levels associated with lower cardiovascular disease and death, with not one cohort showing a significant increase; arachidonic acid biomarkers showed no risk increase either ([Marklund 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30971107/)).
- Canola oil produced a small *decrease* in body weight versus saturated fat across 23 RCTs ([Raeisi-Dehkordi 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30809634/)).

Their concluding line — the theory "must be rejected" — is stronger than the evidence class. These are null biomarker results plus biomarker-based observational outcome data, which is a good case that the harm has not materialised; it is not a demonstration that the hypothesis is false. Their own framing elsewhere ("what we care about are demonstrable results in real-world outcomes") is the more defensible version. The related observation that people who feel better cutting seed oils are usually cutting ultra-processed food is plausible and uncited.

## Decision 5: is heart disease actually getting worse?

Briefly, and correctly: absolute heart-disease deaths rise with population growth, while the *rate* has fallen roughly 72% between 1950 and 2018, and premature death at ages 25–64 by about 70% since 1968 (CDC 2018, Ritchey 2020). Better survival also raises the *prevalence* of living people with heart disease. Overlaying a raw death count on vegetable-oil consumption is the rhetorical move being answered.

## What to weigh

- **Evidence versus recommendation.** "LDL-C relates linearly to plaque from ~70 mg/dL up" is evidence. "Measure apoB," "don't screen everyone with CAC," "reconsider the 200 mg/dL cutoff" are recommendations — reasonable ones, ahead of the guidelines in places, and labelled as their view.
- **Asymmetric scrutiny** in the saturated-fat section, and a threshold figure doing more work than its citations support.
- **Categorical language** on omega-6 where the evidence supports "unsupported" rather than "refuted."
- **The elegant-but-unfalsifiable move** on statins in the elderly: cumulative-exposure logic explains any weak result in older cohorts.
- **Unusual rigour** worth noting: quoting studies' own limitations sections rather than paraphrasing them; keeping "clinical hypogonadism" distinct from "any testosterone change"; retaining that real statin myopathy exists while showing most reported symptoms are nocebo; conceding that anti-inflammatory drugs really do reduce events.
- **No product placement.** Unlike most long Barbell Medicine articles, this one links only to part 1 of the same series.
