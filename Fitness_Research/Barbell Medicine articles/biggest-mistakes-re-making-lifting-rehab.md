# Three Rehab Mistakes: Not Cutting Load Enough, Adding It Too Fast, Training Too Few Movements

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The 3 Biggest Mistakes You're Making with Lifting Rehab" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/lifting-rehab-mistakes/)) · Austin Baraki · 2024-04-08 · 3,639 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **This article cites nothing.** Zero references, zero inline study links across 3,639 words. It is the worked-example companion to their 2019 "Pain in Training" piece, and everything in it is clinical opinion — well-organised, internally consistent, and consistent with modern physiotherapy practice, but not evidenced here.
- The three named mistakes are **(1) not reducing load enough, (2) increasing it too quickly, (3) not varying movement enough**. The first is framed as the characteristic error of *motivated lifters*, in contrast with the general population's error of stopping the activity entirely.
- The diagnostic for "not enough" is a three-part symptom-trajectory test: pain rises set to set as weight rises; pain worsens in the 24–48 hours after; **pain never returns to baseline before the next session**. Any of these means pull back further.
- The worked reduction is deliberately shocking: a lifter squatting a hard set of 5 at **300 lb** is moved to a set of **12 at 95 lb with a 3-second tempo both directions**, with a hard load cap for the day. Baraki's point is explicit — taking 15 or 25 lb off the bar is not a load reduction.
- Progression tools: **RPE autoregulation with pain as the brake** ("could I do another rep?" versus "*should* I?"), explicit load caps in absolute weight or percentage (≤10% per session), and **double progression** (hold weight through an 8–12 rep range, add load only at the top).

## Mistake 1: the load reduction is never big enough
The case is a lifter with a 300 lb squat, gradually worsening anterior left knee pain that throbs for hours afterwards and is noticeable the next day, who is chasing a 315 lb milestone. The things they have tried — high-dose ibuprofen, knee sleeves or wraps, warm-up routines, technique tweaks to keep the knee from travelling forward, and now considering an MRI or an injection — are listed and dismissed together. **That dismissal is asserted, not argued**: no evidence is offered that these fail, and the claim that imaging and injections "typically" do not restore normal training is exactly the sort of statement this source is qualified to support and here does not.

What replaces them is a combinable set of levers: reduce the weight (more than expected), set a pre-emptive weight cap for the day, reduce target exertion (6 RPE instead of 8), raise the rep range (5 → 12), and add controlled tempo. The instruction that carries the psychological work is the one about ego: "don't be afraid to do something you would have previously dismissed as too light or meaningless."

## Mistake 2: progressing faster than the tissue or the symptoms allow
Two failure modes are named. Jumping from 12 tempo reps at 95 lb straight to 8 reps at 135 lb at normal speed — three variables changed at once. And, later in the process, the lifter who feels good doing sets of 5 and decides to "test things out" with a heavy single, then relapses.

The countermeasures are ordinary programming tools repurposed. Autoregulation is reframed so that **symptoms, not force capacity, set the ceiling**, which explicitly licenses *decreasing* load from the previous session — a real departure from how RPE is normally used, and the most useful single idea in the article. Load caps are absolute ("115 lb today") or relative ("no more than 10% up from last session"). Double progression gives the on-ramp: work up to a set at a fixed weight, extend from 8 reps to 12 across sessions without a flare, then add load.

The de-escalation order for tempo is spelled out and is easy to get wrong: **return the concentric to normal speed first, keep the eccentric slow, normalise the eccentric last.** Rep ranges come down gradually (10–12 → 8–10 → 6–8 → 4–6). Explosive work is introduced only in the final phase.

## Mistake 3: refusing to change the exercises
The claim is that lifters resist exercise substitution for the same reason they resist load reduction — it reads as regression. The article's list of variation options is the most concretely useful part: simple barbell variants (close-grip bench, front squat, sumo, specialty bars); unilateral work (sissy squats, split squats, lateral lunges, step-ups, single-leg press or extension; single-leg RDLs, suitcase deadlifts, machine rows; single-arm dumbbell and landmine pressing); rotational work (rotating lunges, windmill presses) specifically flagged for back and hip pain; bodybuilding isolation work; ballistic movements *later only*; and conditioning.

Two framings do the persuasion. First, novelty is treated as therapeutic in itself — "the weirder the movement, the better" — because it is a new challenge and a new movement pattern. Second, isolation work and conditioning are offered as a way to feel like you have trained hard (the "pump") while the main lifts are deprioritised. Neither is supported here; both are reasonable coaching psychology.

## The sample programmes
**Knee pain**, two sessions per week: bike 10–15 min @ RPE 5–6; bodyweight front-foot-elevated split squat 2 × 8–10 per leg controlled tempo; dumbbell box step-up 2 × 8–10 per leg; bodybuilding accessories. Day two swaps in bodyweight lateral lunges and a 3-0-3 tempo squat worked up to 10–12 reps @ RPE 6 or a load cap. Everything unaffected by symptoms continues as normal.

**Low back pain**, two sessions: 15-minute walk; single-leg RDL with dumbbell or kettlebell 2 × 8–10 per leg; kettlebell windmill 2 × 6–8; accessories. Day two: walk; **Jefferson curl starting as light as 2.5–5 lb**; posterior medial tap 2 × 8; accessories. Loaded spinal flexion as a rehab entry point is the position statement here, and it follows directly from their 2019 argument that flexion is a normal movement rather than a hazard.

**Shoulder/elbow**, two sessions: dumbbell bench, lat pulldown, lying triceps extension, accessories; then high-incline dumbbell press or DB overhead press, machine or dumbbell row, ab wheel rollout, accessories.

If the test session makes symptoms worse, the escalation is: more reps, slower tempo (3-count to 5-count both directions), further exercise substitution or temporary removal of the main lift, then reduced volume or frequency.
