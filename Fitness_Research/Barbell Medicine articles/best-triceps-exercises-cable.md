# Six Cable Triceps Exercises, One of Which Has Growth Data Behind It

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The 6 Best Triceps Exercises with Cable" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-triceps-exercises-with-cable/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-03-08 · 5,583 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **The ranking is not backed by hypertrophy outcome data.** Five of the six exercise recommendations rest on EMG activation and joint-kinematics work. Exactly one claim in the article is supported by a measured growth outcome: that overhead (shoulder-flexed) elbow extension grows the triceps more than neutral-arm elbow extension.
- That one exception is [Maeo 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279), which measured triceps brachii hypertrophy directly and found it "substantially greater" in the overhead position. It is cited twice, and it is the load-bearing paper in the whole piece. **The practical takeaway — include at least one overhead triceps exercise — is the only ranking claim here that survives on growth evidence.**
- The claim that cables load the triceps better than free weights rests on [Signorile 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001493) and a 2016 ACSM conference abstract (listed twice, as separate references) comparing muscle activity and joint kinematics between cable and plate-loaded machines. That is activation and range-of-motion data, not growth.
- Attachment choice — rope, straight bar, E-Z bar, V-bar — is explicitly left to preference. Credit where due: the article says the evidence does not identify a superior handle. The supporting references for that section are **ergonomics studies of screwdriver torque and cylindrical handle diameter**, not lifting research.
- Programming: 2–4 sets of 10–15 (8–12 for the lying extension) at RPE 8–9; 6–12 direct triceps sets/week for beginners, 15–20+ for the experienced. **The set-count numbers carry no citation and are presented as coaching experience.**
- Barbell Medicine sells training templates, and the article closes by recommending them plus an email-list opt-in.

## The six exercises, and what each rests on

All six are classed as **tertiary** movements — accessory work, not the centre of a session:

| Exercise | Sets × reps | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overhead cable extension | 2–4 × 10–15 | Long head at long muscle length |
| Cable press down | 2–4 × 10–15 | Shortened-position elbow extension |
| Cable lying extension | 2–4 × 8–12 | Shoulder *and* elbow move; longest stretch on the long head |
| 1-arm overhead extension | 2–4 × 10–15 | As above, unilateral, shoulder static |
| 1-arm press down | 2–4 × 10–15 | Unilateral press down |
| 1-arm cable kickback | 2–4 × 10–15 | Shoulder extension past the torso |

The organising idea is anatomical rather than empirical. The medial and lateral heads arise from the humerus and do not cross the shoulder; the long head originates on the scapula and does. So shoulder position changes the long head's length and mechanical advantage while leaving the other two heads alone. From that, the article infers that varying shoulder position across exercises covers the muscle better. **That inference is anatomically sound and has never been tested as a training prescription.** It is a mechanism argument being used as a programming rule.

Three of the six are functionally the same exercise at different shoulder angles (overhead two-arm, overhead one-arm, lying), and two more are the same press down done with two hands or one. Nothing in the article claims that six exercises beat two; the list is a menu, and the recommendation is to pick one or two per pressing session.

## Where the article is honest, and where it overreaches

**Honest:** on attachments, it says outright that although handle type changes the weight used and the muscle activity to some degree, no attachment is demonstrably superior, so use what you prefer. Given that most of the fitness industry sells the opposite claim, this is the right level of confidence.

**Honest:** it flags the lying cable extension as a *compound* movement — shoulder and elbow both move — rather than pretending it is an isolation exercise, and explains why that is a feature (more stretch on the long head).

**Exercise order.** [Brandão 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003550) randomised **43 men over 10 weeks** to bench press before lying triceps extension or the reverse, and found similar 1RM bench improvement either way — in untrained individuals. Barbell Medicine reports this accurately and then recommends doing triceps work *after* the heavy compound anyway. That is fine, but it is a preference stated after presenting evidence that the variable did not matter. **"The evidence shows X" and "we recommend Y" are visibly different claims here, and the article does not mark the seam.**

**Overreach flag — muscle protein breakdown.** The article states that too high a training dose leaves "too much muscle protein breakdown from the workout to build muscle size," and that growth is only feasible when a workout is planned to reduce breakdown and promote synthesis. This is a stronger mechanistic claim than the cited literature supports, and the reference list at that point contains a developmental-biology paper on vertebrate muscle fibre heterogeneity — a paper about embryonic myogenesis, not about training dose.

**Overreach flag — injury risk.** The safety section says a year-long study showed "people who don't exercise have the same injury rate as individuals who train for multiple hours on a weekly basis." The study in the list matching that description ([Little 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2013-002831)) is a 12-month incidence study of exercise-related injuries in previously sedentary older adults *following an exercise intervention*. It cannot support a head-to-head comparison of injury rates between exercisers and non-exercisers. The general point — that resistance training injury rates are low, at roughly **2–4 injuries per 1,000 participation hours, or 1–2 per lifter per year** ([Keogh & Winwood 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0575-0)) — stands on better ground.

**Overreach flag — osteoarthritis.** The healthy-aging section says starting to exercise earlier in life produces "a complete stop of the disease's progression" in osteoarthritis. Nothing in the reference list supports arrest of structural disease progression. [Turner 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1941738119887183) is a systematic review of resistance training *dosing on pain and physical function* in knee OA — symptom and function outcomes, not disease modification.

## Progressive loading, not progressive overload

One position here is worth recording because it is a house doctrine that recurs across Barbell Medicine's writing and is stated more clearly in this article than in most. They reject the term "progressive overload" as a misnomer and prefer **progressive loading**.

The distinction: people read "progressive overload" as meaning the training must get *harder* over a career. Barbell Medicine's claim is that it should stay the same level of hard indefinitely — the weight on the bar and the reps completed rise as fitness improves, but **relative difficulty holds roughly constant**. An RPE 8 at 40 is an RPE 8 at 20; the load underneath it is not.

This is offered as a definitional correction rather than an empirical finding, and no citation is attached to it. It is also the conceptual justification for the RPE-based prescriptions running through the article — if the target is a constant relative effort rather than an escalating absolute one, then autoregulated loading is the only way to prescribe it. Whether or not the terminology sticks, the practical implication is sound and it is the opposite of the "add 5 lb every week forever" model most novice programmes carry.

The volume guidance in the same section is presented as experience: **6–12 sets of direct triceps work per week for those newer to training, 15–20 or more for the experienced**, with recovery of triceps strength between workouts "usually taking a couple of days" ([Ferreira 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/mus.25541) measured pectoralis and triceps recovery after bench press). The article adds the useful qualifier that **complete strength recovery is not required to train the muscle again**, provided weekly dose matches the individual. That is a defensible coaching position and it is not what the cited recovery study measures.

## The last third of the article is filler

Roughly 1,500 words go to generic benefits of hypertrophy training: healthy aging, endurance, physique, disease prevention, weight management, and an extended detour through EPOC. Two things are worth extracting.

The endurance claim — resistance plus endurance training improved VO2max **12.9%** versus **6.8%** for endurance alone — comes from a small early-phase concurrent-training study (Balabinis 2003) that appears in several Barbell Medicine articles. It is a single small trial and the effect size is being quoted as if settled.

The EPOC discussion actually lands well: it explains the mechanism, then says plainly that whether EPOC meaningfully raises total daily energy expenditure "isn't something that's been tested," and that current work suggests the contribution is small. **That is the correct conclusion, and it undercuts the fat-loss framing the section opens with.**

Rest periods get a real recommendation with real support: at least **two to three minutes** between sets for strength and hypertrophy, from two 2017 Grgic systematic reviews.

## What to actually take from this

One recommendation is evidence-backed: **train the triceps in a shoulder-flexed (overhead) position for at least one exercise**, because that is where the only growth-outcome study in the article points. Everything downstream of that — cable versus dumbbell, rope versus bar, one arm versus two, six exercises versus two — is activation data, mechanism, and coaching preference, and the article's own framing does not distinguish between those tiers. Read the six-item list as a menu of things that all load the triceps through a long range, not as a ranking.
