# Kettlebells Are Not Better Than Dumbbells for Chest Growth, and the Article Admits It

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "5 Best Kettlebell Chest Workout Exercises" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-kettlebell-chest-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine (unattributed staff) · 2023-12-21 · 3,760 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- The article's own headline finding undercuts its premise: **"when it comes to hypertrophy, kettlebells don't really provide any advantages that other free weights don't."** Swap in dumbbells and you get the same result. That is an unusually deflationary thing for a "5 best kettlebell exercises" piece to say, and it is the most useful sentence in it.
- **The five-exercise list is not a ranking backed by anything.** No hypertrophy outcome data, no activation data, no comparison between the five. They are presented in the order the author chose to write them. The only outcome trial cited anywhere in the article compares kettlebell training to weightlifting on vertical jump and squat strength — it never measured the chest.
- The marker-to-reference mapping **is verified**, so claims can be linked to papers exactly. Doing that exposes several places where the cited paper does not support the sentence it sits under — including a range-of-motion review cited for a claim about core strength and dynamic balance.
- **The regional claims are unsupported.** The incline kettlebell press is said to target the clavicular head "for defining the upper chest" and the deficit push-up to build "the lower pecs." Neither claim carries a citation and no study cited here measured growth in a region of the pec.
- Where the article is genuinely good is on the mechanics of holding a kettlebell — grip placement, the "broken wrist grip", and the double-progression fix for fixed-weight implements. All of it is uncited coaching experience, which is fine as long as the reader knows that is what it is.

## What the five exercises actually rest on

Kettlebell floor press, kettlebell bench press, incline kettlebell bench press, kettlebell flye, deficit push-up with kettlebells as handles. Each gets a movement category (primary/secondary/tertiary), a set-and-rep prescription and an RPE target: 3–4 × 6–10 at RPE 7–8 for the presses, 2–4 × 10–15 at RPE 8–9 for flyes, push-ups to RPE 7–8. **None of the prescriptions are cited.** They are the house programming convention, applied to kettlebells.

The one comparative claim in the whole list — that the kettlebell swing is worse than direct chest work for chest strength and size — is cited to Otto 2012, a six-week trial comparing weightlifting to kettlebell training on vertical jump, strength and body composition. The paper's outcomes are jump height, squat strength and body composition. **It contains no measure of the chest.** The claim is probably true on first principles; the citation does not establish it.

On the kettlebell bench press the article makes a claim I did not expect and should be credited for: the longer range of motion "probably doesn't build more strength in the chest unless strength is tested using kettlebells specifically," and hypertrophy differences "probably aren't significant if present at all" against a barbell or dumbbell. This is a specificity argument stated correctly. It is cited to Schoenfeld & Grgic's 2020 systematic review on range of motion, plus a 2010 kettlebell rehabilitation paper — neither of which measured kettlebell versus dumbbell range of motion, so the size of the "not by a lot" is asserted, not measured.

## Where the citations do not carry the sentence

The mapping is verified, which means these are real mismatches and not a numbering drift.

- **Kettlebell flyes "improve strength along with stability in the shoulder joints"** is cited to Hedt 2020, an electromyographic profile of the shoulder during kettlebell stability exercises. That is **activation data**. An EMG profile can tell you which muscles are excited during a movement; it cannot show that strength or joint stability improved, because nobody was trained and nothing was retested.
- **"Kettlebell training has also been proven to be very effective in building core strength and dynamic balance"** is cited to reference 5, which is the Schoenfeld & Grgic range-of-motion review again. That review is about how far a joint travels during resistance training and its effect on muscle development. It has nothing to say about core strength or balance. "Proven" is also doing more work than any of the cited evidence supports.
- **The grip-strength claim** — "two studies, one with college-aged subjects and the other with older adults" — points to an unpublished 2012 master's thesis and a 2013 paper in the *Journal of Fitness Research*. Neither is an older-adult study. The article's own list includes the trial that would have supported it (Meigh 2022, the BELL pragmatic controlled trial in insufficiently active older adults, in *BMC Geriatrics*) — **but no marker in the article ever cites it.** It sits in the list, unused, while the sentence it would have supported points somewhere else.

The reference list is also padded: twelve numbered entries containing six unique papers, several repeated two or three times.

## The useful part: handling the implement

The kettlebell's centre of mass hangs below the hand rather than sitting in it, which is the one genuine mechanical difference from a dumbbell. The article draws two practical consequences. First, load the web space between thumb and index finger rather than the middle of the palm — palm loading forces the wrist into extension when the bell rotates over, the fault they call the "broken wrist grip." Second, grip lightly while the bell is moving and let it travel its own arc rather than forcing it. Both are ordinary kettlebell coaching, presented without evidence and without pretending otherwise.

The programming note is better than most: kettlebells, like dumbbells, come in discrete jumps that are often too large to progress load on directly, so use double progression — add reps within a range at a fixed weight, then move up a bell and reset the reps. This is a real solution to a real problem with fixed-weight equipment.

## What they sell

The article closes with a pitch for Barbell Medicine's personalised coaching and pre-made training templates. The programming advice in the body is consistent with those products, and the reader should weigh it accordingly. There is no product placement inside the exercise descriptions themselves.

**Flagged:** the article states a defensible negative conclusion (kettlebells confer no hypertrophy advantage) and then publishes a "best exercises" list anyway, complete with regional-targeting language it elsewhere disclaims. "Kettlebell training has also been proven" is the clearest overreach — the cited paper does not address the claim at all, and no cited paper anywhere here is a training trial with a hypertrophy outcome in the chest.
