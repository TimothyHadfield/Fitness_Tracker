# The Bar Should Be Reactive, Not Proactive: Load Follows Adaptation

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Progressive Loading" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/progressive-loading/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2022-08-29 · 3,059 words
**Topic:** Programming

*The 2022 original. The 2025 follow-up is [beyond-progressive-overload-work-smarter-not-harder.md](beyond-progressive-overload-work-smarter-not-harder.md), which restates the same thesis with tighter definitions and adds double progression and plateau troubleshooting. This one carries the supporting evidence and the worked example; that one carries the conceptual clean-up.*

## Bottom line
- **The question the article poses:** does lifting more weight make you stronger, or do you get stronger and then lift more weight? Their answer is the second, and the whole argument follows from it. **"The weight loaded on the bar should be reactive, not proactive."**
- The vocabulary matters. **Training stimulus** is what the programme prescribes (external). **Training stress** is what the body does with it (internal, measured by RPE, RIR, bar velocity, heart rate). Stress produces both **adaptations** and **fatigue**; the balance of the two sets **performance potential** on any given day.
- **Intensity floors, from the evidence:** roughly **65–75% 1RM** for multi-rep strength work, **>85–90% 1RM** for singles. Note these are *wider* than the ~70% / 85% figures the 2025 article gives from the same sources — the drift is unexplained.
- **Higher stress does not reliably mean more adaptation.** Two claims support this: velocity loss beyond 10–20% within a set does not produce greater strength gains (Pareja-Blanco 2016), and training to failure does not produce more growth than 2–4 reps in reserve outside untrained people doing isolation work.
- **The worked example is the article's best teaching device.** Same lifter, same feeling, three choices next week. The Repeater — same 315 lb, same RPE 8 — is the right answer. The Full Send (320 lb at RPE 9) proved they were not stronger, only more fatigued.
- **Prefer undershooting to overshooting over a training career.** Both stay inside the productive band; only one accumulates avoidable fatigue and injury risk.

## The evidence chain

The argument is built as a sequence, each link cited:

**1. There is a load floor for strength.** Schoenfeld 2017 (the low- vs high-load meta-analysis), Mangine 2015 (volume and intensity in resistance-trained men) and Androulakis-Korakakis 2018 give the 65–75% / 85–90% brackets. Worth noting what Schoenfeld's meta actually found: **hypertrophy** was similar across a wide load range, **strength** favoured heavier loads. The floor is a strength-specific claim, correctly applied here. Androulakis-Korakakis is a **pilot study in a small group of powerlifters** — thin support for a general 85–90% threshold, and the article does not say so.

**2. Volume drives strength, within tolerance.** Radaelli 2015 (dose-response of 1, 3 and 5 sets), Rhea 2002 (three sets beat one at equal intensity) and Ralston 2017 (a meta-analysis of weekly set volume on strength). This is the best-supported step in the chain — a dose-response study, a controlled comparison and a meta-analysis pointing the same way.

**3. Proximity to failure is the fatigue lever.** Morán-Navarro 2017 tracked the recovery time course after sets taken to failure versus not; Dos Santos 2021 found failure and non-failure protocols reaching similar total volume with **different fatigue and discomfort**. Both fit the claim exactly.

**4. Velocity loss indexes within-set fatigue.** Here the citations are mechanistic rather than applied: Fuglevand 1999 on force-frequency and fatigue in **motor units controlling the digits of the human hand**, and Nocella 2011 on force decline arising from fewer and weaker cross-bridges. **Flagged:** these are single-motor-unit and cross-bridge-level studies. They explain why force falls under fatigue; they are a long way from validating barbell velocity as a field measure. The inference is reasonable but the evidence is being asked to travel.

**5. More stress is not more adaptation.** Two strands. On velocity, two Pareja-Blanco 2016 papers found that stopping at a 10–20% velocity loss produced strength gains equal to going further. On failure, the article cites **Carroll 2019** and **Helms 2018**.

**Check this one.** Carroll 2019 is *Skeletal Muscle Fiber Adaptations Following Resistance Training Using Repetition Maximums or Relative Intensity* — a small fibre-level study comparing RM (failure) to relative-intensity (non-failure) training. It is on point but underpowered for the general claim. **Helms 2018 is *RPE vs. Percentage 1RM Loading in Periodized Programs Matched for Sets and Repetitions*.** That study compared two ways of assigning load; it did not test failure against 2–4 reps in reserve, and it did not measure hypertrophy as the article's sentence requires. **It cannot support the sentence it sits on.** Treat the failure claim as resting on Carroll alone.

That matters, because the "failure is not required" claim is the most contested thing in the article. It is stated flatly here in 2022 — "outside of untrained individuals doing isolation exercises, training to failure does not produce more muscle growth than training in the range of 2–4 repetitions in reserve" — and subsequent work on proximity to failure has made the picture less clean, generally finding small advantages for training closer to failure for hypertrophy. **The direction of the article's advice survives; the absoluteness of the sentence does not.**

## The worked example, and why it teaches well

A lifter squats 5 reps at 315 lb, rates it RPE 8 (2 RIR), estimates a 389 lb 1RM, then does two back-off sets of 5 at 292 lb (75%). Next week, everything feels the same. Three options:

- **Full Send** — 320 lb, comes back RPE 9. The load went up; the effort went up with it. *Nothing was demonstrated except extra fatigue.* The article's diagnostic rule is clean: **someone who is genuinely stronger can add weight without the RPE rising.** A load increase accompanied by an RPE increase is evidence that adaptation had not yet happened.
- **Repeater** — 315 lb, RPE 8 again. Correct. No visible progress in a week, but productive stress continues to accumulate inside the useful intensity and effort band.
- **Undershoot** — 305 lb, RPE 7. Wrong in the same sense as Full Send, but harmless: still inside the productive band, no excess fatigue.

The asymmetry is the point. Both errors miss the target; only one costs anything. The article does add the counterweight — chronic severe undershooting means never progressing at all, which is a coaching problem worth addressing if performance is the goal.

## Advice versus evidence

The seven criteria for a general strength programme (exercise specificity, the intensity floor, matched rep schemes, tolerable volume, small velocity losses, autoregulation tools, a supporting progression model) are **a recommendation, not a finding.** Items 2 and 4 have evidence behind them; the rest are coaching structure.

Two places where the article is notably restrained, and both deserve credit:

- **"This is not an exact science, and there is no way to definitively prove that we have perfectly matched these variables."** The goal is stated as a "good enough" stimulus that is not "too much" — not an optimum.
- **"Over a sufficiently long training career it is not critically important to optimize rates of adaptation, nor is there any way to ensure such optimization given the number of variables involved."** This is an unusually honest disclaimer for a coaching business.

**Flagged overreach:** the sprinter analogy — that telling someone to run faster to get faster is "silly on its face" — is rhetorically effective but overstated. Sprint training does prescribe target paces and does use overspeed methods; the disanalogy with adding weight is weaker than the article implies.
