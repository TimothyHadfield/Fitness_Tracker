# What Lifting Belts Are Shown to Do, and What They Are Only Assumed to Do

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "What Does a Weightlifting Belt Do and How Do They Work?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-science-of-weight-lifting-belts/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2023-06-05 · 5,890 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- Feigenbaum says he pulled **every** study comparing a belted to an unbelted condition — 36 in total, 20 occupational and 16 in the weight room. That accounting, rather than any single finding, is what makes this the most defensible equipment article in the corpus.
- **Shown:** belts raise intra-abdominal pressure (5 of 7 lifting studies; the two nulls were isometric tasks), and the effect is additive with a Valsalva. Belts do **not** meaningfully alter trunk muscle sEMG, which stays strikingly submaximal either way. Belts do **not** weaken the trunk — construction workers who wore lumbar belts for two to three months got *stronger* on direct trunk strength and endurance tests [21].
- **Shown to fail:** belts do not reduce low-back injury rates, lost work days or compensation claims in large occupational trials [25, 26, 27, 28]. NIOSH's position, quoted in full, is the weaker one — the studies can neither support nor refute the effect [29]. About 90% of belt-wearing gym-goers say they use one to prevent injury [1]; that is the belief the data do not back.
- **Assumed:** the mechanism. Feigenbaum concedes outright that **"there are no studies looking directly at whether or not people can lift more weight with a belt."** The performance case is built from proxies — 15.5% faster concentric velocity at 90% 1RM squat [17], faster final reps of an 8RM [12], RPE two points lower on an 80% 1RM deadlift [22] — plus one null [9]. Trunk stiffness, the proposed mediator, is never measured in any cited study.
- **Recommendation, not evidence:** belt the heaviest weekly exposure of your priority lifts; go beltless otherwise to manage absolute-load fatigue. He labels this "just a theory" and it is. He discloses that Pioneer, one of three brands he recommends, sponsors the Barbell Medicine podcast.

## What a belt does to pressure inside you

Intra-abdominal pressure is the pressure generated by the abdominal contents, varying with breathing phase and abdominal wall tension [4, 5]. Baseline numbers from surgical literature: roughly **20 mmHg standing, rising to about 65 mmHg during a Valsalva** in healthy adults [6]. Lifting blows past both. Harman 1989 had nine young people deadlift 90% of a beltless-tested 1RM and measured **175 mmHg with a belt versus 156 mmHg without**, with IAP also rising faster before the pull began [9]. Measurement is invasive — a catheter into the stomach or rectum — which is one reason samples are tiny throughout.

Of the seven lifting studies measuring IAP, five found a significant belt-related increase. McGill 1990 (six adults, machine deadlifts at 73–91 kg) found IAP highest with belt *and* conscious Valsalva together [10]. Kingma 2006 (nine young men, rack pulls and deadlifts at 37.5% and 75% bodyweight, two breathing techniques) reproduced the same additive pattern [11]. Lander 1992 (five young men, 8RM squat averaging 125 kg) found the belt raised IAP **25–40%** over beltless [12]. The two nulls used isometric tasks, which Feigenbaum discounts as unrepresentative of lifting — a defensible call, though it does mean the negative evidence is being set aside on judgement rather than on a pre-specified rule.

Then comes the honest part. Why should higher IAP help? The obvious answer — it increases muscular force — is contradicted: under maximal isometric conditions, total force production is unchanged with the belt [8, 14]. So the fallback hypothesis is **trunk stiffness enabling more efficient force transfer**. Feigenbaum states plainly that supporting this requires performance data, and goes looking for it. He never claims stiffness was measured, because it was not.

## What a belt does to your muscles: essentially nothing

Fourteen studies looked at trunk muscle excitation with a belt. After discarding occupational tasks unlike gym lifting, the picture is that **sEMG of the trunk is low during squats and deadlifts regardless of belt use**. McGill's participants "never exceeded 15% of that produced by an isometric maximal voluntary contraction, with or without a belt" [10]. Escamilla 2002, comparing 12RM sumo and conventional deadlifts, found obliques at **62% MVC beltless versus 53% belted**, and rectus abdominis at **63% belted versus 56% beltless** — a swap, not a suppression [16]. Two squat studies (8RM; and 90% 1RM) found no sEMG difference; a third found more erector spinae excitation *with* a belt during three reps at 60% 1RM [12, 17, 18]. Kingma found no rectus abdominis difference but lower iliocostalis values at 1.5× bodyweight [11]. Warren 2001 found a sex-split result with a soft non-leather belt — higher right-oblique values in men, lower in women — at a trivial fraction of maximum [19].

One study measured **intramuscular pressure** in erector spinae via catheter, which tracks muscle force production more directly than surface EMG: IMP rose significantly with a belt during isometric lifting, while the parallel sEMG rise was not statistically significant [8, 20].

Feigenbaum flags the interpretive limit himself, citing Vigotsky's paper on reading sEMG amplitudes [15]: EMG cannot be used to infer motor unit recruitment, and the assumption that more excitation means more strength or hypertrophy "remains yet to be proven." **This is the standard the exercise-ranking articles elsewhere in this library never apply** — those pieces build whole rankings on activation amplitudes that this article treats as too weak to carry a conclusion.

The practical payoff is a negative one, and it is the most useful finding in the piece: **belts do not appear to offload or decondition the trunk.** Holmström & Moritz followed construction workers using lumbar belts for two and three months and directly tested trunk strength and endurance — both improved on average [21]. That is a small, uncontrolled occupational follow-up, not an RCT, but it points the opposite way from the "belts make you weak" folk claim, and no cited study points the other way.

## Performance: proxies, not a lift

The section opens with the admission. No study has tested whether people lift more weight with a belt. What exists:

- **Zink 2001** — squats at 90% 1RM. No sEMG difference in erectors, hamstrings, glutes or quads, but the concentric was completed **15.5% faster** with the belt, with a trend toward slightly greater depth [17]. Faster bar speed at a fixed load is a reasonable surrogate for a heavier attainable load, but it is a surrogate.
- **Lander 1992** — 8RM squats with a rectal balloon catheter in place; the final few reps tended to move faster belted [12]. "Tended to" is the article's own wording.
- **Fong 2022** — one rep at 80% 1RM deadlift, belted and not. Time to complete the rep was similar, but **RPE averaged two points lower** with the belt [22]. This is an observational study, and RPE is unblindable — you know whether you are wearing a belt, so expectancy is fully in play.
- **Harman 1989** — the null. At 90% 1RM deadlift, no change in velocity or ground reaction forces [9].

Feigenbaum's conclusion is appropriately hedged: belts "tend to improve lifting performance to some degree, likely through increasing the efficiency of force transfer through the trunk plus **some placebo-type effect**", with large individual variation, and more controlled 1RM data would help. Naming placebo as part of the mechanism is unusually candid for an equipment article.

**Flag:** the recap at the foot of the article drops the hedging. Point 4 states flatly that "lifting belts work by increasing the stiffness of the trunk by increasing intra-abdominal and intra-muscular pressures", which is the hypothesis the body of the article says has not been tested. A reader who only reads the summary gets a firmer mechanism than the evidence section supports.

## Injury: the clearest negative result, and its limits

Low back pain is the leading global cause of years lived with disability, affecting over 600 million adults in 2020 [23], and in the US accounts for roughly 265 million lost workdays a year [24] — which is why industry funded so much of this work.

Multiple large, long-duration occupational studies across industries converge: **belts do not reliably reduce low-back injury risk, lost or restricted work days, or workers' compensation claims** [25, 26, 27]. Wassell 2000 is a prospective JAMA study; Martimo 2008 is a BMJ systematic review of training and lifting equipment; Reddell 1992 studied airline baggage handlers. Mitchell 1994 followed over 1,000 employees at Tinker Air Force Base and found no difference in back injury incidence, lost or limited duty days, or treatment between belt wearers and non-wearers [28].

Feigenbaum then quotes NIOSH accurately and against his own rhetorical interest: because of the limitations of the available studies, the results "cannot be used to either support or refute the effectiveness of back belts in injury reduction" [29]. That is a claim of *insufficient evidence*, which is weaker than his own preceding sentence that belts "do not" reduce risk. The two coexist in the article without being reconciled.

Two contextual points close the section. Lifetime risk of low back pain is around **85% in the general population and 61% in athletes** [30, 31] — i.e. common enough that preventing it is a high bar. And resistance training is already among the safest forms of exercise, at roughly **1–4 injuries per 1,000 participation hours** across styles [32, 33, 34], leaving little injury rate for a belt to reduce.

**Flag on the generalisation:** all the injury data is occupational — repetitive material handling by workers, not maximal barbell attempts by trained lifters. Feigenbaum applies it to the gym-goer's "prevent injury" belief without discussing whether the tasks transfer. The conclusion is probably right, and the inferential gap is real.

## Buying and using one (advice, not evidence)

Everything from here is clinical/coaching opinion, and the article does not pretend otherwise. Construction: leather between two suede layers; plastic or artificial-leather cores are cheaper and never break in. Dimensions: **4 inches wide** (IPF maximum, 10 cm [2]; IWF allows up to 12 cm [3]), **10 mm thick for general strength training, 13 mm for powerlifting, 6.5 mm full leather for weightlifting**. Size so your measured waist circumference falls mid-range for that manufacturer, allowing for body fat; hole spacing should be 1 inch centre-to-centre, wider spacing signalling weaker material. Closure — single prong, double prong or lever — is preference; double prong offers no extra stiffness or durability.

**Disclosed conflict:** he recommends Pioneer, Inzer and SBD, and states plainly that Pioneer sponsors the Barbell Medicine podcast. He also links Best Belts, Titan and Cardillo as fine. This is the right way to handle it and worth crediting.

Usage: belt the heaviest weekly exposure of your priority lifts, from the last warm-up onwards; consider beltless work otherwise. The rationale is his own untested theory that absolute load imparts fatigue independent of RPE — 3×4 at RPE 8 with 315 lb belted is more stressful than 300 lb beltless at the same RPE. He calls it a theory. Finally, and consistent with Barbell Medicine's pain-science position: someone with back pain may reasonably use a belt **more** liberally, not because it treats or prevents pain (it does not), but because staying active matters more than the belt does.
