# Tendinopathy Is a Loading Problem, and the Treatment Is Loading

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Barbell Medicine Guide to Tendinopathy" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/tendinopathy-guide/)) · Derek Miles · 2024-06-07 · 4,701 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **"Tendonitis" is the wrong word.** The `-itis` implies inflammation, and [a systematic review of inflammatory cells and markers in human tendinopathy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7006114/) found the evidence for it unclear. Miles uses *tendinopathy* — a maladaptive tissue response, not an inflammatory one.
- **The causes he names are all dose variables**: over-specificity (same stress, same tissue, too often), chronic overuse, and acute overload from a sudden programming change. He explicitly rules out poor flexibility and imperfect technique — "your quadriceps tendinopathy was not the result of insufficiently stretchy quadriceps."
- **Rest is the wrong prescription.** Removing load detrains the tendon, so symptoms recur on return. The alternative is to find an *entry point* — the exercise, tempo, position or load the athlete tolerates — and build from there.
- **Heavy slow resistance training is the cornerstone**, starting at a 3-0-3 tempo (three seconds eccentric, no pause, three seconds concentric), 2–3× weekly at the *start* of the session, held in the programme for **12–16 weeks** because tendons adapt slowly.
- **Passive modalities do not speed healing.** Cupping, dry needling, taping, e-stim, massage: [a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs in midportion Achilles tendinopathy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37284136/) comparing loading protocols with passive modalities is the single citation offered against them.
- **Caveat on the evidence base:** this is a 4,700-word clinical guide carrying **four citations**. Almost all of the programming content is clinical reasoning and practice pattern, not evidence, and it should be read that way.

## Why loading, and what kind

Tendons transmit muscle force across joints. They are mostly collagen, poorly vascularised and sparsely populated with cells, which historically led to the belief that they stop adapting after adolescence. Miles's argument against that is partly mechanistic and partly a reductio: if tendons did not adapt, strength training would routinely produce tears as muscles outgrew their attachments, and it does not.

The direction of adaptation is where the two tendon-loading meta-analyses do real work. [Bohm 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27747846/) (healthy adults, exercise interventions) and [Lazarczuk 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/) (lower-limb tendons, mechanical, material and morphological outcomes) are both cited for one sentence: beneficial adaptation comes from **higher loads at slower tempos**. Note the population — these are *healthy* tendons adapting, not painful ones recovering. The extrapolation to rehab is the authors', and it is a reasonable one, but it is an extrapolation.

Miles is honest about a puzzle this creates. If slow heavy loading is what tendons want, powerlifters should be protected, and they are not: tendinopathy is common in strength athletes. His resolution is that dose and variety, not tempo, are the operative variables — the same tissue loaded the same way repeatedly is the problem, however good the tempo. He also lists non-training contributors that a gym-based reader will not have considered: high cholesterol, diabetes, inflammatory disease (rheumatoid arthritis, gout, ankylosing spondylitis) and fluoroquinolone antibiotics.

## The management principles

**Should, not could.** Barbell Medicine normally sets load by RPE, which asks whether you *could* do more reps. In rehab, Miles inverts this: ask whether you *should*. Pain, not force capacity, becomes the brake. This is a genuinely useful reframing and it is presented as their practice, not as a tested protocol.

**A pain threshold, not pain-free.** He recommends keeping symptoms under a subjective **3–4 out of 10** during heavy slow resistance work, and accepts that this may make the prescribed RPE unreachable early on. This aligns with the broader "painful exercises are acceptable in chronic musculoskeletal pain" literature, though he does not cite it here.

**Addition by subtraction.** The reflex in rehab is to add an exercise per symptom. He argues the opposite: audit the whole programme and cut total exercises, sets or mileage in the early phase. He extends this off the gym floor — sleep and work stress get equal billing with tempo work, on clinical reasoning rather than cited evidence.

**Working around vs working through.** Most athletes work *around* symptoms with substantial exercise and load modification, rather than *through* them. The corollary is that both extremes fail: total rest does not help, and doing the same thing unchanged does not either.

**Start lower than feels reasonable.** He names the two commonest errors as choosing too aggressive a starting load and returning to prior performance too fast, and points out that fractional plates make sub-5 lb progression possible. Setbacks are framed as expected, not as evidence the plan is wrong.

The sample structure: weeks 1–4, 4×10 at RPE 8, 3-0-3 tempo; weeks 4–8, 3×10 at RPE 8, 3-0-0; weeks 8–16, 3×10 at RPE 8 at consistent tempo. Early on it goes first in the session so the athlete is fresh and it warms up the area; later it moves to the end with other accessory work.

## Three cases, one diagnosis of programming

The back half of the article is three worked scenarios, all resolved by the same moves.

1. **Over-specificity.** A powerlifter squatting 3×/week, benching 4×/week, deadlifting 2×/week for six months, habitually exceeding target RPE, now with shoulder and knee pain. Fix: drop heavy singles, replace low bar squats with safety bar and dumbbell split squats, swap the overload slingshot bench for a tempo variant, add machines and single-leg work, leave the non-painful deadlift alone.
2. **Dramatic increase in exertion.** A one-year lifter who moved from a varied self-guided programme to a rigid linear-progression template and developed elbow and gluteal pain with strength losses. Fix: reintroduce autoregulation, cut intensity, add variation — a top set of 3 at RPE 7 then back-off sets 10–15% lighter. He deliberately *declines* to add wrist curls first, on the addition-by-subtraction principle, and holds them in reserve.
3. **Too much, too fast, new modality.** A lifter who tripled running volume from 8 to 24 miles a week in four weeks and got bilateral Achilles pain at the 3-mile mark. Fix: calf tempo work 2×/week, runs kept under the 3-mile pain onset, the other two aerobic days moved to rowing or biking, mileage rebuilt from there.

The common thread is that the tendon is treated as a symptom of the programme. That is the clinical reasoning worth taking from this article, and it is consistent across all three.

**Overreach to flag:** "There is simply no physiological justification for why any of these modalities would expedite healing" is stronger than the cited Achilles meta-analysis supports. That review addresses comparative *effectiveness* in one tendon; absence of a demonstrated effect on healing is not the same as absence of any mechanism, and short-term analgesia — which he concedes in the next paragraph — is itself a mechanism of sorts. Likewise, "flexibility and technique should be relegated to minor, if not irrelevant, status" is asserted without citation; the evidential claim available to him is that loading dose predicts tendinopathy far better than technique does, which is not the same as irrelevance.
