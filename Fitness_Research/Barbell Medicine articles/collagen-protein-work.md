# Collagen Protein: A PDCAAS of Zero, and No Outcome It Reliably Improves

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Collagen Protein: What It Is and Does It Work?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/collagen-protein/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2025-05-27 · 2,936 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

*Companion to [collagen-protein-tendinopathy.md](collagen-protein-tendinopathy.md), which dissects one Achilles trial in depth. This 2025 article is the broader verdict across muscle, connective tissue, joint pain and skin. Where the two overlap — PDCAAS, the "smart amino acid" objection — the tendinopathy note has the fuller numbers.*

## Bottom line
- **Collagen's PDCAAS is 0**, because it contains no tryptophan and the score is set by the limiting amino acid (ref 11). Whey scores 1.00. Collagen is ~80% protein by weight against >90% for whey isolate; its BCAA content is roughly **half** whey's (ref 12).
- **The proposed mechanism is the weak point, not the trial data.** Glycine and proline are non-essential — the body makes them — dietary deficiency is rare, and there is no route by which ingested amino acids are preferentially routed to tendon. Feigenbaum calls the idea that collagen supplies "smart" amino acids implausible on its face.
- **Muscle:** 30 g collagen did not stimulate muscle protein synthesis; 30 g whey did (ref 13). A meta-analysis of 19 RCTs found collagen added to habitual diet may raise strength and size versus *no extra protein*, but underperforms whey even with leucine added (ref 14) — which is the "any protein beats no protein" result, not a collagen result.
- **Tendon and joint:** no increase in connective tissue protein synthesis (ref 12), no difference in tendon mechanical properties versus placebo (ref 14), and four joint-pain trials with no clinically meaningful benefit (refs 15–18).
- **The best single number in the article:** Benito-Ruiz 2009 (n=250) reported a **4.7 mm** advantage for collagen on a 100 mm visual analogue pain scale, against a minimal clinically important difference of **13.7 mm**. Statistically significant, clinically irrelevant. That distinction is the article's sharpest methodological move.
- **Skin:** a 26-RCT, >1,700-patient meta-analysis reported positive effects (ref 19), but the article discounts it for methodological flaws and heterogeneity. This is the one place its scepticism goes beyond what it demonstrates.

## What the evidence shows, section by section

The marker mapping is **verified**, so the numbers below sit on the sentences quoted.

**Protein quality (refs 10–12).** The argument is arithmetic, not contested. PDCAAS compares a test protein's limiting essential amino acid to a reference; collagen's is tryptophan at zero, so the product is zero by construction. Gorissen 2018 supplies the comparator figures (pea 77–81%, soy 61–91% protein by weight); Holwerda and van Loon's narrative review supplies the BCAA comparison. Nothing here is an inference — it is composition data.

**Muscle (refs 13–14).** Aussieker 2023 is the acute study: matched 30 g doses, collagen failed to raise muscle protein synthesis where whey succeeded. Bischof 2024 is the chronic evidence, and the article reads it honestly — collagen beat *nothing*, lost to whey. Note the comparator problem Feigenbaum names explicitly: almost no trial compares collagen to an energy-matched, protein-matched control, so "collagen works" and "more protein works" are not separated anywhere in this literature.

**Connective tissue and joint pain (refs 12, 14–18).** Four trials, and the article's summary of each is accurate: Balshaw 2023 (15 g/day, 15 weeks, resistance-trained young men) found no patellar tendon or functional difference; Trč 2011 compared collagen to glucosamine **with no placebo arm**, which the article flags itself; Chen 2023 found nothing at 24 weeks in knee osteoarthritis; Benito-Ruiz 2009 produced the sub-threshold VAS difference above. **Design matters here and the article respects it** — it does not let a no-placebo comparison count as evidence of benefit.

**Safety (refs 7–9).** Immune reactions to bovine collagen are uncommon (a 1989 implant study in 705 patients). The Creutzfeldt-Jakob item is a **single case report published as a conference abstract**, and the article immediately contextualises it with an EFSA risk assessment concluding the risk is exceptionally rare. That is the correct handling of a scary-sounding, near-zero-probability finding.

## Advice versus evidence, and where they push it

The recommendation — don't take it — follows from the evidence for muscle, tendon and joint pain. For **skin** it does not quite. The meta-analysis they cite reports a positive effect and its own authors call for larger trials; the article dismisses it as "difficult to justify" optimism without specifying which methodological flaws, then concludes there is "no need" to take collagen for skin. **Flagged:** absence of confident evidence is being reported as evidence of absence in that one section.

A smaller flag: the Balshaw trial is described as showing no difference "with respect to musculoskeletal pain," but its primary outcomes were tendon remodelling and mechanical properties. The direction of the finding is right; the framing overstates what it measured.

## The commercial angle, both ways

**Barbell Medicine sells supplements, and this article argues against an entire supplement category.** That is worth recording plainly: the conclusion runs against the commercial incentive, which is rare enough in this space to be a mark in their favour.

The qualification is that **whey is the comparator they favour, and they sell whey** — the phrase "whey protein isolate" in the protein-content section links directly to their own Gainzzz Rx product page. So the argument is not "supplements don't work"; it is "this supplement is worse than the one we stock." Nothing in the reasoning depends on that, and the underlying protein-quality data are independent of who sells what — but a reader should see the shape of it.
