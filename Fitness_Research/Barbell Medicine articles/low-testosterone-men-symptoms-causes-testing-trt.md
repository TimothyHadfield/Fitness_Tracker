# A Single Low Testosterone Reading Is Not a Diagnosis

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Low Testosterone in Men: Symptoms, Causes, Testing, and TRT" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/low-testosterone-in-men/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2026-05-06 · 9,272 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **More than half of men who meet criteria for testosterone deficiency at one visit have a normal value on a repeat draw with no treatment at all.** That comes from the Massachusetts Male Aging Study, >1,700 Boston-area men with blood drawn at multiple visits over years ([Travison 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1532-5415.2008.01679.x)). It is the single number the whole article is built around, and it is the reason a one-draw diagnosis is not a diagnosis.
- **The prevalence gap is the space overprescribing lives in.** The HIM study found 38.7% of middle-aged US primary-care men below 300 ng/dL ([Mulligan 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1742-1241.2006.00992.x)); requiring symptoms *plus* labs drops prevalence to roughly 2–6% ([Wu 2010](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa0911101)). About a six-fold spread.
- **In most adult men presenting to primary care the low value is secondary** — a brain-level signalling problem downstream of visceral fat, fragmented sleep, opioids, prolactin or systemic illness. Location in the HPG axis (LH/FSH pattern) determines the treatment.
- **Undertreatment is real too**, and the article treats it as a symmetric failure: men with genuine primary or persistent deficiency turned away, or scared off by a cardiovascular concern that [TRAVERSE](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2215025) has since largely retired.
- **Higher is not better.** The saturation model (prostate saturating around 250 ng/dL) plus the sex-differences meta-analysis — men and women differ 15–20-fold in circulating testosterone and gain similar *relative* muscle and strength on matched programmes ([Roberts 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003521)) — is the argument against "optimising" inside the normal range.
- **Barbell Medicine sells the book, the coaching and the templates this article routes to**, and says so in an explicit incentives paragraph near the end. That disclosure is unusually direct and worth noting.

## Decision 1: Do your symptoms even point at testosterone?

The European Male Aging Study is the paper doing the work here. Of the long list of complaints men bring to clinics, only **three sexual symptoms — reduced morning erections, low libido, erectile dysfunction — clustered reliably with biochemical hypogonadism** ([Wu 2010](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa0911101), *NEJM*). Everything else tested either failed to correlate with testosterone or showed weaker, less specific relationships.

The clinical reasoning matters more than the list. Fatigue, low mood and poor concentration are the symptoms that actually drive men into wellness clinics, and they are exactly the symptoms that overlap with sleep deprivation, depression, untreated metabolic disease and anaemia. Feigenbaum's example is deliberately alarming: a man with severe fatigue from anaemia treated with testosterone instead of worked up, when the anaemia was the first sign of colon cancer. The point is not that testosterone never causes fatigue — it is that fatigue is worth nothing as a *discriminating* symptom, so a clinic that treats it as the indication has skipped the differential.

## Decision 2: How to test, and what one low number is worth

The article's testing protocol: **fasted, morning (ideally 8–10 a.m.), when not acutely ill and not sleep-deprived, LC-MS/MS assay if the lab offers it.** A low result triggers a *second* draw under identical conditions, with SHBG, LH and FSH added — free testosterone only when SHBG is abnormal ([Bhasin 2018](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2018-00229), Endocrine Society guideline).

Two measurement problems justify that caution.

**Diurnal and situational variation.** Testosterone can fall 20–50% from morning to afternoon, and the article's stronger claim is that production tracks *sleep duration* more than clock time — it begins rising at sleep onset and rises most steeply across the first three hours ([Leproult & Van Cauter 2011](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2011.710); [Wittert 2014](https://doi.org/10.4103/1008-682X.122586)).

**Assay error.** Immunoassays do not measure testosterone directly; they infer it from displacement of a labelled decoy, and estradiol, DHT and DHEA all cross-react. The consequence is systematic overestimation, worst at low concentrations ([Trost & Mulhall 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsxm.2016.04.068)). When stored samples were re-run by LC-MS/MS, the proportion below 300 ng/dL nearly doubled — which is why the Endocrine Society moved the lower limit of normal from 300 to **264 ng/dL** ([Bhasin 2018](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2018-00229)).

The audit data behind the "overtreatment" claim: in a US insurance claims database covering 2001–2011, roughly **25% of new testosterone prescriptions had no testosterone measurement in the prior year**; nearly half of men who were tested had no follow-up draw in the year after starting; and 19.5% of those tested did not meet Endocrine Society criteria but were prescribed anyway ([Baillargeon 2013](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.6895)). That window predates the direct-to-consumer telehealth boom — the article's inference that current figures are worse is reasonable but not measured.

## Decision 3: Where in the axis is the problem?

Total testosterone says whether there is a problem; **LH and FSH say where it lives.**

- **Primary** (testicular): low T, *high* LH and FSH. Klinefelter, prior chemotherapy, mumps orchitis, trauma, chronic alcohol exposure. Lifestyle change does not fix this, and replacement is usually the right answer.
- **Secondary** (hypothalamic/pituitary): low T with low or inappropriately normal LH and FSH. The commonest adult pattern, and the one that most often responds to fixing the upstream driver ([Corona 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/andr.12770), EAA/ESE recommendations).
- **Mixed**: variable LH/FSH, usually in the setting of haemochromatosis, advanced liver or kidney disease, advanced HIV. Treat the disease first.

**The drivers worth chasing, with the numbers:**

*Visceral adiposity.* Obesity more than doubles the odds of testosterone deficiency, dose-dependently: overweight men average about **66 ng/dL lower**, men with obesity about **147 ng/dL lower** ([Corona 2013](https://doi.org/10.1530/EJE-12-0955), systematic review and meta-analysis). The pattern has a name, male obesity-related secondary hypogonadism (MOSH). The mechanism is multi-point: aromatase in adipose tissue converts testosterone to estradiol (about 80% of a man's circulating estradiol is made this way), estradiol feeds back on the hypothalamus, leptin and inflammatory adipokines add independent GnRH and Leydig-cell suppression, and insulin resistance lowers SHBG. Low testosterone then promotes further visceral fat accumulation — a self-reinforcing loop.

*Sleep restriction.* One week at 5 hours per night dropped testosterone **10–15%** in healthy young men ([Leproult & Van Cauter 2011](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2011.710)).

*Obstructive sleep apnoea.* Affects roughly **15–30% of North American men** and is substantially underdiagnosed ([Wittert 2014](https://doi.org/10.4103/1008-682X.122586)). This is the article's best clinical argument: the fragmentation prevents the sleep-driven overnight rise, so the low testosterone is real but its proximate cause is the apnoea — and **exogenous testosterone can relax upper airway musculature and worsen apnoea events** ([Bhasin 2018](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2018-00229)). Treating the apparent problem makes the actual problem worse. STOP-BANG ≥3 warrants formal evaluation ([Chung 2016](https://doi.org/10.1378/chest.15-0903)).

*Chronic opioids.* GnRH and LH suppression at the hypothalamus and pituitary — consistent across opioid classes, dose-dependent, reversible on discontinuation ([Reddy 2010](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.c4462)).

*Hyperprolactinaemia* (serum prolactin; treat the cause), *haemochromatosis* (ferritin and transferrin saturation; phlebotomy, not hormones), plus depression, advanced renal or liver disease, uncontrolled type 2 diabetes, advanced HIV.

On endocrine-disrupting chemicals the article is appropriately restrained: mechanistic and animal plausibility, human data confounded because the ultra-processed food environment that delivers the exposure also delivers the calories and metabolic disease. **Recommendation, not evidence:** limit exposure where it is cheap to do so, without expecting it to move your testosterone.

## Decision 4: Can you fix it without a prescription?

Four levers, all with effect sizes attached.

1. **Lose visceral fat.** The largest lever where the suppression is metabolic. Roughly **+84 ng/dL for a 10% body-weight reduction** via diet and exercise; about **+250 ng/dL after bariatric surgery** at ~30% weight loss; the mechanism of loss appears to matter less than the amount ([Corona 2013](https://doi.org/10.1530/EJE-12-0955)). The EMAS prospective data support reversibility: a substantial proportion of men with secondary deficiency recovered normal testosterone, driven by improvements in body weight and illness burden ([Rastrelli 2015](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2015-1571)). Practical targets: waist-to-height below 0.5, and a weight-to-waist reduction ratio near 0.4–0.6 kg per cm.
2. **Sleep** — 7–8 uninterrupted hours, consistent timing, limit alcohol and sedatives. Screen for apnoea in anyone snoring, with witnessed apnoeas, morning headaches, resistant hypertension or BMI >30.
3. **Exercise — indirectly only.** The article is blunt that exercise does not raise resting testosterone directly, and that the post-workout spike is small, gone within about half an hour, and not predictive of hypertrophy or strength gain. The long-term effect runs entirely through body composition. Their starting prescription (2–4 resistance sessions/week, moderate-to-high relative intensity with progressive overload, 1.4–1.6 g/kg/day protein, 3–4 moderate aerobic sessions) is **advice, not a tested protocol for raising testosterone.**
4. **Treat the underlying conditions.**

Expected magnitude from a coordinated effort: **50–150 ng/dL**, reassessed at three to six months with a repeat morning draw under matched conditions.

**Supplements.** Of the 50 most popular "testosterone booster" products, **62% had no published data supporting the label claims**, and among those with data, 10% had evidence the active ingredients *lowered* testosterone ([Clemesha 2020](https://doi.org/10.5534/wjmh.190043)). Separately, **12% of muscle-building supplements subject to FDA enforcement action contained undisclosed synthetic steroids** ([Tucker 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.3337)) — a man taking a "natural booster" spiked with an androgen suppresses his own production and comes off lower than he started. The narrow exceptions are corrections of true deficiency: zinc ([Prasad 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0899-9007(96)80058-X)), vitamin D in confirmed deficiency, small and inconsistent ([Pilz 2011](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0030-1269854)), and ashwagandha in stressed or hormonally suboptimal men, modest and of unclear clinical significance ([Lopresti 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1557988319835985)).

## Decision 5: Is TRT the right answer, and is it safe?

**Clearly indicated:** primary testicular failure, confirmed central deficiency from structural pituitary disease, and confirmed persistent deficiency with a syndromic presentation that has not responded to addressing reversible drivers ([Bhasin 2018](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2018-00229)). Benefits in those men can be substantial — sexual function, body composition, mood, anaemia of chronic disease, bone mineral density ([Snyder 2016](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1506119)).

**Usually the wrong first move:** a single low or borderline value; non-specific symptoms with no differential worked up; likely apnoea, adiposity, medication or illness drivers; or "optimisation" inside the normal range.

**Cardiovascular safety.** TRAVERSE is an RCT — 5,246 men aged 45–80 with testosterone deficiency plus established cardiovascular disease or high risk, topical testosterone, mean follow-up about two years. **MACE occurred in 7.0% on testosterone versus 7.3% on placebo** ([Lincoff 2023](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2215025)). No benefit, but not the harm signal that dominated the field after 2013. Feigenbaum handles the secondary findings well: higher atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury and pulmonary embolism in the testosterone arm were unadjusted secondary endpoints with multiple comparisons and no dose-response — flagged, not established. He is equally clear on generalisability: two years cannot address polycythaemia consequences, prostate cancer trajectories, or fertility recovery a decade later, and the trial studied a specific older high-risk population on a topical formulation, not younger men on injectables from a telehealth clinic.

**Costs of therapy:** HPG suppression in nearly all men, with spermatogenesis suppressed; fertility recovery typically 6–12 months or longer, and a meaningful minority never regain baseline sperm production. Erythrocytosis, acne, fluid retention, possible worsening of apnoea, gynaecomastia. Indefinite monitoring — total testosterone, haematocrit, PSA, lipids.

**SERMs and hCG.** Clomiphene/enclomiphene block estradiol negative feedback so the brain raises LH; hCG acts directly on the Leydig-cell LH receptor. Both preserve fertility, both are off-label in the US, both give a more variable response with weaker long-term safety data, and neither works in primary testicular failure.

## The "testosterone is collapsing" narrative

The original signal was real: MMAS found men born about 15 years apart had testosterone roughly **15–20% lower at the same age** after adjusting for health factors including BMI ([Travison 2007](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2006-1375)), with similar patterns in Finnish and Israeli cohorts. That is the source of the "1% per year" figure.

The correction has three parts. First, magnitude: a meta-analysis of **1,256 studies and more than a million subjects across 1971–2024** put the age- and BMI-adjusted decline at about **0.56% per year**, roughly half the quoted figure — with LH declining in parallel while FSH did not, pointing at the hypothalamus rather than the testes; and in the LC-MS/MS subset, **no decline at all** ([Santi 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.102871)). Second, BMI misses the relevant fat: about half of people classified non-obese by BMI are obese by DXA ([Romero-Corral 2008](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2008.11)), and waist circumference at a given BMI has risen across birth cohorts, so every secular-decline study adjusting only for BMI made the same measurement error. Third, assay drift: comparing immunoassay-era values to modern accurate ones manufactures a decline. When both errors were removed — archived NHANES samples from 1988–91 and 1999–2004 run in one lab on one day with waist circumference in the model — **the decline disappeared** ([Nyante 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2605.2011.01230.x)).

Age-related decline is a separate question: about **1–2% per year after age 30** in the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging ([Harman 2001](https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem.86.2.7219)), most of which tracks body composition, illness and medication burden rather than age itself. Active, healthy older men hold values in the middle-aged range ([Sartorius 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2265.2012.04432.x)) — and the article flags its own survivorship caveat, which is a good sign.

## Why higher is not better

The saturation model: androgen receptors are finite per tissue, and once occupied the tissue is at full response. The best-characterised case is the prostate, saturating around **250 ng/dL** ([Morgentaler & Traish 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eururo.2008.09.024)) — the finding that dismantled the fear that replacement causes prostate cancer in men with normal testosterone. The muscle argument is the cleaner one for lifters: men and women differ **15–20-fold** in circulating testosterone yet gain similar *relative* muscle and strength on matched resistance programmes ([Roberts, Nuckols & Krieger 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003521)). Testosterone is permissive across most of the physiologic range, not dose-dependent within it. The article concedes the framework is "more inferred than directly measured" for libido, mood, energy, bone and red cell production.

Why two men at the same value feel different: **CAG trinucleotide repeat length in the androgen receptor gene** — shorter repeats, more sensitive receptor ([Zitzmann 2009](https://doi.org/10.1159/000175843)).

## Where the argument outruns the evidence

- **"The population-level decline is almost entirely attributable to measurement error, not something in the water."** Stated more absolutely than the cited work supports. Santi 2025 still reports 0.56%/year after adjustment, and its own authors attribute the null LC-MS/MS result to a shorter surveillance window. Feigenbaum's rebuttal — that 100,000+ subjects over thirty years should detect 0.3%/year — is his own power argument, not the paper's conclusion. The weaker claim (the decline is small and mostly explained) is well supported; the absolute one is not.
- **The GLP-1 figure (20–30% improvement in testosterone) rests on an Endocrine Society conference press release**, not a peer-reviewed paper. The article's own numbering is unstable at reference 21, where the inline link and the list entry point to different sources, so I have not attached a specific paper to that number.
- **"Treat the upstream system and the readout improves on its own in most adult men."** The Corona 2013 effect sizes come from men with obesity and metabolic drivers; "most adult men" is a broader population than the evidence covers. It is a reasonable clinical generalisation, presented as a finding.
- **Commercial disclosure.** The article routes to Barbell Medicine coaching and to a preorder for their book *Signal*, and it says so plainly in a paragraph comparing its own incentives to those of the wellness clinics it criticises. The recommendations (fat loss, sleep, apnoea screening, exercise, treating comorbidities) are not products they sell, so the conflict is mild — but the article exists partly to sell the book, and the reader should know that.
