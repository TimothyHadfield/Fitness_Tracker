# Olympic Lifting vs Powerlifting: Different Lifts, Much the Same Strength

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Olympic Lifting vs. Powerlifting - What are the Differences?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/olympic-lifting-vs-powerlifting/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-08-08 · 3,868 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The sports differ in their contested lifts — snatch and clean & jerk versus squat, bench and deadlift — and in bar and plate specifications, but the article's own evidence says **weightlifting training does not build more strength than ordinary strength training** ([Morris 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01627-2)). Strength is specific: each group wins at its own lifts.
- It also concedes that **the Olympic lifts have no monopoly on power**. Plyometrics and other explosively-performed exercises produce roughly equal improvements in power, sprinting and change of direction. That is an unusually honest thing for an article partly written to sell an Olympic weightlifting template.
- **Neither sport is a hypertrophy programme.** The reasons given are structural: weight classes penalise mass that doesn't add performance, Olympic weightlifting volume is low, isolation work is rare, and the competition lifts are dominated by concentric work. The last of these is asserted without a citation.
- The fibre-type claim — elite weightlifters at ~67% fast-twitch in the legs (Serrano 2019, *PLoS One*) — is a **cross-sectional snapshot of elite athletes**. It cannot separate what training did from who self-selects into the sport, and the article does not say so.
- **Three of the eight numbered references are the same paper.** The effective reference list is six studies, one textbook chapter among them.
- **Product content:** roughly a third of the article is a catalogue of Barbell Medicine's paid templates — Olympic Weightlifting, SuperTotal, Strength I, Powerlifting II, Strength III, plus Beginner and General Strength & Conditioning as prerequisites — and their coaching service. Say that plainly: the article's structure is diagnosis-then-purchase.

## What actually differs

The competition lifts are the real dividing line. Weightlifting contests the snatch and the clean & jerk; powerlifting contests the squat, bench press and deadlift. Both give three attempts per lift, count only the best successful attempt into a total, and rank within weight classes. The article's own framing of the technical difference is good: at high bar velocity there is no room to salvage a bad position, whereas a slow deadlift that drifts forward can still be ground out. Technique matters in both; it is less *forgiving* in one.

Equipment differences are concrete and worth recording. Weightlifting uses a 25 mm (women's and junior) or 28 mm (men's) bar with droppable rubber bumper plates; powerlifting uses a 29 mm bar with calibrated steel plates that are not dropped. Some powerlifting federations add specialty bars — a stiff ~32 mm squat bar and a whippy 27 mm deadlift bar. The weightlifting bar is thinner, more flexible and has freer-spinning sleeves.

On footwear the article shifts register: essentially all Olympic lifters wear heeled weightlifting shoes, whereas "somewhere close to ⅔" of powerlifters wear them to squat. That fraction is offered explicitly as the author's impression from a long competitive history, not as a survey. Fine as colour, not a number to carry forward.

## What the evidence actually says about strength and power

The load-bearing citation is a systematic review with meta-analysis by [Morris and colleagues (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01627-2), comparing weightlifting, traditional resistance training and plyometrics on strength, power and speed. The article uses it three times, at references 3, 6 and 7. Its conclusion, as reported here: **weightlifting produces similar strength improvements to non-weightlifting strength training**, and explosively-performed non-Olympic exercises produce about the same gains in power, sprinting and change of direction. Hackett and colleagues (2016, *BJSM*) is a second meta-analysis supporting improved vertical jump in sportspeople from weightlifting training.

Two things follow that the article gets right. First, the "Olympic lifts are the best way to build power" claim is not supported; the mechanism is *lifting quickly*, and the barbell is one of several ways to do that. Second, strength is context-specific — weightlifters are stronger at weightlifting, powerlifters at the powerlifts — which is the correct reading of a null result, not a dodge.

One attribution problem. The claim that "high-velocity resistance training, such as Olympic weightlifting" significantly improves inter-muscular coordination and force-production efficiency is supported by [Balshaw 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00091.2016), which compared **explosive-contraction against sustained-contraction training** — not the Olympic lifts against anything. The article then immediately concedes the point itself ("it's not clear that the Olympic lifts themselves are unique"), so the overreach is self-corrected within the same paragraph, but the citation is doing less work than the sentence implies.

## The bone density claim outruns its study

The article says a 2021 study of 55 male participants "revealed that powerlifting promotes various physiological adaptations, including increased fat-free mass, appendicular lean mass, and bone density," citing [Hackett & Sabag (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11332-021-00740-z). That paper examines the **relationship** between powerlifting performance, muscle mass indices and bone mineral density — an association study in trained lifters, not a training intervention with a control group. "Promotes" is a causal verb attached to correlational data. To their credit the authors then walk it back within two sentences, saying it is unlikely powerlifting-specific training beats other similarly-loading resistance training for bone, and that this is the author's perspective rather than a finding. That distinction — evidence versus clinical opinion — is exactly the one the article marks correctly here and blurs above.

## Why neither sport builds much muscle

The reasoning offered is programme-structural rather than physiological mystery, and it is sound as far as it goes:

- **Weight classes** reward strength per kilogram of bodyweight, so mass that doesn't buy performance is a cost.
- **Volume is low** in weightlifting programmes, and direct isolation work is rare.
- **The competition lifts are mostly concentric**, whereas paired eccentric-concentric work is better for size. This is stated flatly with no citation, and it is the one hypertrophy mechanism claim in the article that a reader should treat as the authors' position rather than a sourced finding.

The practical conclusion — that dedicating training resources to snatch technique is a poor allocation if size is the goal — is advice, and reasonable advice, but it is inference from the above rather than a tested comparison.

## What they are selling

The back half is product placement and should be read as such. Weightlifting routes to their Olympic Weightlifting Template (12 weeks, four lifting days plus two GPP sessions, nine months' barbell experience) and SuperTotal (16 weeks, a year or more). Powerlifting routes to Strength I (13 weeks, three lifting days, three months), Powerlifting II (13 weeks, four lifting days, six to nine months) and Strength III (user-selected exercises, over a year). Beginners are routed to the Beginner Template first, and the article closes on a coaching pitch. No template recommendation carries evidence; the experience thresholds are house guidance.
