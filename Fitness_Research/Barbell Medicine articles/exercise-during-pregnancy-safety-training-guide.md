# Training Through Pregnancy: What the Evidence Actually Covers, and Where It Runs Out

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Exercise During Pregnancy: Safety and Training Guide" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/exercise-during-pregnancy/)) · Nick Occhipinti · 2026-08-03 · 8,459 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

> This is a summary of what one article claims and what its cited papers support. It is not medical advice, and nothing here is a substitute for the reader's own obstetric care.

## Bottom line
- The core safety claim — exercise does not increase risk of miscarriage, poor fetal growth, musculoskeletal injury or preterm delivery in **average-risk, uncomplicated** pregnancies — rests on the **ACOG 2020 committee opinion** (guideline consensus built on largely observational literature) plus a **2025 BJSM systematic review and meta-analysis** of resistance training pooling **47,619 participants**, which found no adverse maternal or fetal effects.
- The specific "heavy lifting cuts off the baby's blood supply" fear is addressed by **two small acute physiological studies**, not by outcome trials: a Valsalva-vs-no-Valsalva cardiac/haemodynamic study, and a **22-woman** 3D Doppler study showing placental blood flow did not fall during near-maximal chest press. That is reassuring about the proposed *mechanism*; it is not evidence about babies born months later.
- Guidelines say **moderate intensity, RPE 4–6**. The article's own practical summary prescribes **RPE 6–8** for lifting, borrowed from a **non-pregnant** low- vs high-load meta-analysis. They are explicit that it is non-pregnant data, but the jump from "guidelines say 4–6" to "do 6–8" is their clinical judgement, not a finding.
- They correctly report that guideline intensity caps reflect **absence of research at higher intensities, not demonstrated harm**, and that many listed contraindications are **expert opinion rather than evidence of harm** (Meah 2020, a systematic review of evidence of harm explicitly framed as a "call to action").
- **The article's stop list is empty.** The sentence introducing "a few conditions where exercise should be stopped" is followed by no list at all, and there is no warning-signs list (bleeding, leaking fluid, regular contractions, chest pain, calf swelling). For a safety guide, this is the most consequential gap in it.
- They sell coaching and dietitian consults, and link both. Say so up front.

## Decision 1: can I keep training at all, and what changes

The population the article is actually about is **women with uncomplicated, average-risk pregnancies**. Almost every safety claim carries that qualifier in the source, and the qualifier does most of the work.

For that population the position is: previously active women can usually continue their existing programme with little or no modification for much of the pregnancy, and previously inactive women can start. The support is a **guideline consensus stack** — the 2019 Canadian guideline, the ACOG 2020 committee opinion, a 2023 scoping review of pregnancy activity guidelines from **19 countries** (resistance training) and **17** (aerobic), and the US Physical Activity Guidelines. Agreement across many countries is evidence of expert convergence, not independent replication; these documents draw on an overlapping literature.

The maternal benefit list (less excessive gestational weight gain, less gestational diabetes and hypertension, less preeclampsia, fewer perinatal mood disorders, shorter labour, more vaginal births) is cited to a **2019 umbrella review**, ACOG, and the 2025 meta-analysis. An umbrella review is a review of reviews and the underlying material is heavily observational, so "associated with" is the right reading for most of it. The fetal benefit list (lower preterm birth, less macrosomia, better fetal blood flow and brain development) rests only on ACOG and the 2025 meta-analysis. The **epigenetics** paragraph is the weakest thing here — a 2024 review of maternal diet and activity effects on the offspring epigenome — though the article's hedging is appropriate.

**Modification, not cessation** is the operating principle: sumo instead of conventional deadlift, wider squat stance, dumbbells rather than barbells for cleans, standing core work, incline or seated pressing. All of it is presented as optional and comfort-driven, and **none of it is cited** — it is coaching judgement, which the article does not disguise.

## Decision 2: how hard, and how heavy

Two intensity numbers sit in this article and they do not match.

**What the guidelines say.** Moderate intensity, defined as **RPE 4–6** on a 1–10 scale, **3.0–5.9 METs**, heart-rate zone 2, and by talk test the point where you speak in short broken sentences. At least **150 minutes/week** of moderate conditioning, and resistance training on **at least two days per week** emphasising large muscle groups. Cited to the Canadian guideline, the scoping review, ACOG, the US guidelines and ACSM's textbook for the MET definitions.

**What the article recommends.** Resistance training at **RPE 6–8, roughly 65–85% 1RM, mostly 4–12 reps**, cited to **Schoenfeld et al. 2017, a low- vs high-load meta-analysis in non-pregnant trainees**. The article states that population plainly, to its credit. Two things still need flagging: that meta-analysis compares strength and hypertrophy outcomes across loads and does not itself establish an "RPE 6–8" prescription, and the closing practical summary reprints RPE 6–8 without repeating that guidelines say 4–6 — so a reader who skims to the summary gets a number no pregnancy guideline endorses.

Their argument for exceeding the guideline ceiling is honest and worth stating precisely: the moderate cap exists because **higher intensities are under-researched in pregnancy, not because harm has been shown**. The evidence offered for the higher end is ungraded and mixed:

- A **retrospective cohort of women who continued CrossFit through pregnancy** (Friedman et al., EJOG 2026) — retrospective, self-selected, and reported with **no sample size, no comparison group and no outcome numbers**, just "demonstrated safety". The thinnest citation in the piece relative to the weight placed on it.
- **"Evidence from elite athletes"** showing vigorous exercise is safe — **no citation at all**.
- Studies in which women lifted **at or near 1RM** without noted adverse effects, cited to ACOG, the two acute blood-flow studies, and a 2024 brief overview.

For conditioning, METs, heart-rate zones, RPE and the talk test are presented as interchangeable gauges. One misattribution: the claim that the **talk test is "valid and reliable"** is marked to **Scherr 2012, a study of Borg RPE against physiological measures**, not a talk-test validation — the reference list does contain a talk-test paper (Persinger 2004), but no marker points to it. Note also that the article **never engages the legacy 140 bpm heart-rate cap**; it presents heart-rate zones as valid and links its own zone calculator. The old ceiling is absent rather than rebutted.

## Decision 3: the specific worries

**Valsalva and breath-holding.** The fear is that raised intra-abdominal pressure interrupts fetal blood flow and causes decelerations. The rebuttal is two acute physiological studies. **Meah et al. 2020/2021** compared prenatal resistance exercise with and without a deliberate Valsalva and found no difference in maternal cardiac and haemodynamic response — the article does not report its sample size. **Gould et al. 2021** monitored placental blood flow by 3D Doppler power flow ultrasound in **22 pregnant women at various gestational stages** during near-maximal chest press and found no reduction versus rest; the article elsewhere calls this braced lifting **up to 90% of 10RM**, adding **Moolyk et al. 2024/25** on acute maternal and fetal responses to high-intensity resistance exercise. These are small acute studies with surrogate outcomes. They test the proposed mechanism directly and are the best available evidence on this precise question — but "no acute drop in placental flow in 22 women" is narrower than the FAQ's flat "No."

**Supine positioning.** Cited to ACOG: after roughly 20 weeks the gravid uterus can compress venous return, and lightheadedness may follow. The handling is good — the article notes the recommendation originated mainly around **sleep** position, that it does not affect everyone, and that the rule is symptom-guided (incline, seated, standing or side-lying if you feel it) rather than a ban. That framing is clinical reasoning, not a cited finding.

**Heat.** Extremes of core temperature from fever, hot tubs and saunas are associated with **first-trimester neural tube defects** (via ACOG); exercise is argued not to reach that range. The supporting study, **Carmichael et al. 2002**, is an observational analysis finding physical activity associated with a **30–50% lower NTD risk** — an association vulnerable to confounding by who exercises. The 90°F/32°C and 60% humidity thresholds come from a National Weather Service page listed in the references but attached to no marker.

**Diastasis recti.** Prevalence given as **66–100%** to some degree, with resolution ranging from spontaneous to persisting for years. The headline figure — **90% of non-exercising pregnant women with separation ≥2 cm versus 12.5% of the exercising group** — comes from a **single 2005 non-randomised study in a physical therapy journal** (Chiarello et al.), reported with no sample size, and that same study is the sole source for the mechanism and functional-deficit claims too. A lot of load on one small old paper. The conclusion drawn — that progressive abdominal training belongs in a pregnancy programme, with an accompanying exercise table — is reasonable clinical inference, but inference.

**Falls, contact and environment.** Avoid contact sports, downhill mountain biking, and exercises causing forcible abdominal contact; the article deserves credit for saying outright that this is **anatomical and anecdotal reasoning, not research evidence**. Scuba diving is out, and altitude above **2,500 m (8,200 ft)** should be avoided by women living below it, with acclimatised women above **1,800 m (6,000 ft)** possibly exempt — cited to the Canadian guideline and ACOG.

## Decision 4: what to actually stop for

This is where the article fails on its own terms. It sets up the section correctly: **Meah, Davies & Davenport 2020**, a BJSM **systematic review of evidence of harm** explicitly subtitled a call to action, found that many absolute and relative contraindications in pregnancy guidelines rest on **expert opinion rather than demonstrated harm from exercising or demonstrated benefit from stopping**. The article then writes "There are a few conditions that emerging evidence and existing guidelines suggest exercise should be stopped until the condition is managed:" — **and lists nothing.**

There is likewise **no warning-signs list** anywhere in the piece: no vaginal bleeding, no amniotic fluid leakage, no regular painful contractions, no chest pain, no dyspnoea before exertion, no calf pain or swelling. Those appear in the ACOG opinion the article cites throughout, and their absence here means a reader who takes this article as their guide learns everything about why restrictions are overstated and nothing about when to stop.

**Overreach, in one sentence:** the article states that "it is very rare to have a hidden or undiscovered cause of concern" and that women "should feel empowered to participate", which is a stronger and more universal reassurance than Meah 2020 supports — that review argued specific contraindications lack evidence, not that undetected risk is rare.

## Decision 5: coming back postpartum

The framing is that **how** you return matters more than **when**, sourced to **Deering et al. 2024**, an **international Delphi study and consensus statement** on postpartum return-to-running. A Delphi is structured expert opinion, not data, and the article does not label it as such.

The rest is guideline synthesis: the "fourth trimester" is the first 12 weeks, though a **2024 review of 22 postpartum guidelines** suggests the window may extend to a year, particularly for breastfeeding women, and activity levels typically fall in this period (ACOG). The article pushes back on the common **6-week wait** as not clearly evidence-based, citing ACOG's own position that activity may resume as soon as medically safe — for some women within days. Their staged table (pelvic floor and abdominal drawing immediately; light walking and strengthening as tolerated at 2–6 weeks; progressive return after the postpartum assessment) is a framework assembled from ACOG plus the guideline review, not a validated protocol. The FAQ claim that exercise **does not impair milk production** is uncited.

## Evidence ledger, and what they sell

**Strongest:** Prevett et al. 2025 — a large, recent BJSM systematic review and meta-analysis directly on resistance training in pregnancy. Then the Gould and Meah acute studies: small, but they test the exact mechanism the central fear rests on.

**Weakest:** the Chiarello 2005 diastasis figures carrying a whole section alone; the uncited elite-athlete claim; the CrossFit retrospective cohort cited with no numbers; the epigenetics paragraph; and the 45-woman second-trimester treadmill study described in detail with **no reference marker at all**.

**Sold here:** Barbell Medicine Coaching closes the practical summary, **registered dietitian consultations** are linked from the conditioning-nutrition paragraph, and their free heart-rate zone calculator and RPE explainer are linked inline. The recommendations do not depend on buying anything, but the article ends on a sales link.
