# Pain science

Four notes carrying the source's intellectual core, arguing one position: **pain is a protective
output shaped by biological, psychological and social inputs rather than a gauge of tissue damage**,
structural findings are common in people with no symptoms, and what a person is told about their
body measurably changes what they feel. Mainstream in physiotherapy research, heterodox in a gym,
well evidenced at the core. The failure mode is narrow and it repeats: a well-evidenced negative
finding about **measurement** gets restated as a positive claim about **reality**. Hold that
distinction and almost everything here is usable.

The four are [the SI joint note](si-joint-movement-cause-hip-low-back.md) (Derek Miles, 2023),
[the crepitus note](crepitus-expectations-vs-reality.md) and [the pain-in-training
note](pain-training.md) (both Austin Baraki, 2019), and [the placebo and nocebo
note](placebo-nocebo-effects-pain.md) (Nick Occhipinti, 2026). The first, second and fourth carry a
**verified marker-to-reference mapping**, so claims tie to papers exactly; the pain-in-training
article uses inline named links and its marker check failed, so attribution there comes from the
article's own prose.

## What pain actually tracks

The cleanest direct evidence is a spread, not a correlation: **321 adults** given an identical
standardised **48 °C** heat stimulus rated it from **4 to 100 out of 100** (Fillingim 2017, in [the
pain-in-training note](pain-training.md)). Identical input, nearly the whole scale of output.

Belief tracks outcomes instead — **Darlow 2013** on the enduring impact of what clinicians say to
people with low back pain, **Woby 2007** on self-efficacy mediating between pain-related fear and
outcome. The defensible one-line summary of the domain is that note's own: **imaging findings and
lifting posture predict symptoms poorly, and fearful beliefs predict outcomes well.**

Two systematic reviews carry the posture half. **Saraceni 2020** (systematic review with
meta-analysis) asks whether lumbar flexion during lifting is related to low back pain; **Nolan 2020**
(systematic review) asks whether lifting technique differs between people with and without back
pain. Both find the link far weaker than the industry assumes. Note what they are: **group-level
comparisons of people with pain against people without it**, not tests of whether a given position is
safe for a given lifter under a given load. The imaging half rests on **Brinjikji 2015**, a
systematic review of spinal degeneration in *asymptomatic* people, plus a meta-analysis (**Zhong
2017**) and systematic review (**Chiu 2015**) on spontaneous resorption of lumbar disc herniations.

Two papers get used for a job they cannot do. **Bunzli 2017** on making sense of pain-related fear
and **Setchell 2017**, a survey of what people believe causes their own persistent back pain,
establish that the mechanical "body-as-machine" model is *widely held* — the claim they are made to
support, and correct. They do not establish that it is wrong.

## The asymptomatic-findings argument, worked properly

The strongest card here, and [the SI joint note](si-joint-movement-cause-hip-low-back.md) plays it in
four separate empirical steps.

**How much does the joint move?** Goode's **2008 systematic review** of three-dimensional
measurement studies pooled roughly **8° about the Z-axis, 4° about Y, 2.2° about X**, its authors
concluding palpation-based diagnosis of SI pathology has limited clinical utility. **Nagamoto 2015**
sits alongside it — 3D CT in patients with degenerative lumbar disorders, a measurement paper, not a
pain paper. The two that matter most are **Kibsgård 2014 and 2017**: radiostereometric analysis with
**implanted tantalum markers**, the reference standard for skeletal micromotion, in **patients with
long-lasting pelvic girdle and severe SI joint pain** — the exact population manual therapy targets —
during the exact manoeuvres used to "test" the joint, single-leg stance and the active straight-leg
raise. **Under 1°**. Population and manoeuvre both match the clinical claim, which makes this far
stronger than cadaver or healthy-volunteer data, and the article underuses it.

**Can anyone detect it?** SI motion palpation gave **48% agreement and kappa −0.06** (**Robinson
2007**). Kappa corrects for chance: **zero means no better than guessing, negative means worse**.
Not "poor reliability" — the absence of a signal. The PSIS, the largest landmark available, managed
**kappa 0.27** (**Cooperstein 2016**, systematic review), conventionally "fair" and nowhere near the
**0.6–0.8** demanded of a test that directs treatment. **Stovall and Kumar 2010** found the same
across multiple lumbopelvic landmarks; **Nolet 2021** (systematic review) generalised it beyond the
SI joint — soft-tissue palpation "inconsistent", bony landmarks and joint mobility "poor". **Preece
2008** gives the anatomical reason from cadaveric pelvises: **side-to-side landmark differences up to
11° and 16 mm in hip bone position within the same specimen**. If a normal pelvis is asymmetric by
more than the joint's entire measured range, felt asymmetry cannot be read as displacement.

**Does treatment move it?** **Tullberg 1998**, roentgen stereophotogrammetry sensitive to **0.2 mm**,
found **under 1 mm of translation and under 1°** after a manual technique. Nothing is being reset.

**And the prevalence figure it turns on.** **Eno 2015** found SI joint degeneration in **65% of
pain-free adults, ~30% of it "substantial"** — the same shape as Brinjikji on the spine. A finding
present in two-thirds of people with no symptoms cannot confidently be named the cause when someone
hurts.

[The crepitus note](crepitus-expectations-vs-reality.md) runs the same argument in another joint and
pushes the prevalence further: **McCoy 1987** examined **247 symptomatic and 250 normal knees** and
found crepitus in **99% of the normal knees**. A finding present in essentially everyone cannot by
itself signal disease.

## Perception and performance come apart

The crepitus article is the best-behaved of the four because it keeps the inconvenient paper in.
**Lo 2018** found subjective crepitus in asymptomatic people **predicted later symptomatic
tibiofemoral osteoarthritis**; Baraki reports it, then argues the recommendation survives it anyway —
*even if* crepitus forecasts future OA in some people, that is not a reason to stop training.

**Pazzinatto 2019** is the sharpest paper because it controls for radiographic severity and so
isolates crepitus itself. Matching on Kellgren-Lawrence grade across two Osteoarthritis Initiative
samples of **584 and 361** people, it found slightly lower **self-reported** function and
knee-related quality of life in the crepitus groups but **no difference in objective knee function or
strength**. **de Oliveira Silva 2018** agrees from a different design: **165 women with
patellofemoral pain versus 158 pain-free women**, crepitus much more common in the pain group at
**odds ratio 4.19**, but **no relationship with function or other self-reported outcomes**. A
cross-sectional association that size tells you the two co-occur and nothing about direction; the
missing functional correlate is the more interesting half.

Baraki ties this to **Stanton 2017** on the spine, where **felt stiffness in chronic back pain is a
protective perceptual inference rather than a readout of measured stiffness** — the same
perception/measurement divergence in a different joint. Name the evidence base plainly, though: old,
observational and qualitative, with **no trials**. Joint auscultation by stethoscope goes back to
**Blodgett 1902**, and Baraki's judgement is that it lapsed because it was probably useless.

## Where the beliefs come from

**Robertson 2017**, a qualitative study, is the centre of the crepitus article. Participants were
preoccupied with the **meaning** of the noise — damage, "premature aging", feeling old. They were
embarrassed when others could hear it, especially in quiet rooms, and other people's reactions
(wincing, "*you should get that checked out*") acted as a social amplifier of danger. Several stopped
hobbies outright. Textbook fear-avoidance (**Vlaeyen 2016**).

Two seeding routes. **Family** — knowing a relative with knee problems led people to assume the same
process and the same fate, which is why Baraki suggests asking where the model came from.
**Clinicians** — Setchell's survey found **89% of people with persistent low back pain had learned
their negative beliefs from healthcare professionals**, and **Darlow 2018**'s qualitative study of
knee osteoarthritis found patients holding a literal "wear and tear", "bone-on-bone", "missing
cartilage" model, held with certainty *even while* their own symptoms improved in ways it could not
explain — a discordance they did not register. **Robertson's 2010 editorial** adds that loud
non-arthritic crepitus mostly produces anxious, hypervigilant patients who need reassurance.

The management recommendation is education and reassurance plus practical de-escalation of
hypervigilance — ambient noise, or a knee sleeve to muffle the sound, **explicitly without telling
the person the sleeve gives "support" or "stability"**, since that reintroduces the belief you are
removing. [The SI joint note](si-joint-movement-cause-hip-low-back.md) reaches the parallel
recommendation — drop "rotated" and "out of alignment" entirely, frame prognosis positively, be
facilitator not mechanic — on **Testa and Rossettini 2016** (contextual factors), **Bossen 2013**
(rewording MRI reports improved patient response), **Ferreira 2013** (therapeutic alliance predicts
outcome in chronic low back pain), and **Bishop 2017** / **Cook 2011** (clinician equipoise shapes
outcomes even for inert interventions).

**Record this: no study cited across these four notes shows that reassurance changes outcomes.** The
protocol is clinical judgement on descriptive evidence, not a tested intervention.

One device worth flagging as a device: if the joint barely moves and the tests are unreliable, what
are clinicians perceiving? The SI article answers **pareidolia**, illustrated with **Merckelbach
2001**, in which **~30% of undergraduates reported hearing "White Christmas"** in a recording
containing no song. An analogy, not evidence about palpation.

## Placebo is established; nocebo is borrowed

Not equally supported, and [the placebo note](placebo-nocebo-effects-pain.md) is where to keep them
apart.

**Two trials carry placebo.** FIDELITY (**Sihvonen 2018**) randomised non-traumatic degenerative
meniscus tears to a real arthroscopic procedure or a **sham operation** — anaesthetised, scoped,
given a scar, told they had been treated — and found **no significant between-group difference in
knee pain or function at 2 years**, both groups improving. **Ashar 2024** gave **101 patients with
chronic low back pain** a saline injection while **explicitly telling them it was a placebo**, and
still found significant pain improvement at **1 month** with functional neuroimaging. The open-label
result matters most: it removes deception as a precondition, which is what makes the clinical advice
possible at all.

**The boundary condition is the most useful sentence in the article**: placebo effects are confined
to **subjectively measured outcomes** — pain, mood, function, well-being. Not blood lipids, not
HbA1c.

Three mechanisms, honestly labelled "in the theory and discovery stage" and probably operating
together. **Classical conditioning** (**Bąbel 2019**) — a learned association between stimulus and
relief, the chiropractic "crack" as the bell — contaminated by **regression to the mean** (**Morton
2005**): people seek treatment at their worst, pain drifts back to its average regardless, and the
treatment takes the credit. **Response expectancy** (**Zhou 2019**) — relief tracks belief, and
**expectancy is weaker in pessimistic and higher-anxiety people**, so placebo effects are smallest in
exactly the population most likely to be in persistent pain; expectations are socially sourced, and
social learning of negative outcomes is a strong nocebo route (**Saunders 2024**). **Predictive
coding / Bayesian brain** (**Kaptchuk 2020**, *BMJ*) — in chronic pain with central sensitisation the
brain's prediction defaults to pain, and treatment may work by violating it. Note the register shift:
the first two have experimental support, the third is a theoretical model in an analysis piece
presented with equal confidence. The article also asserts that expectancy and conditioning dominate
in *acute* pain while chronic pain needs predictive coding — a claim its sources do not obviously
establish.

**Effect size, three families.** *Intervention*: being told a placebo costs more produces a larger
effect (**Andrade 2015**); premium branding raises expectation; more elaborate, skilled-seeming,
time-consuming treatments produce larger effects — machines with lights and sounds, specialised
postures and tools; surgery carries some of the largest, with a cited review of **17 studies and 535
patients** given sham surgery for chronic low back pain and **over half clinically improved**.
*Person*: two **competing** theories on severity, offered without picking a winner — more severe
symptoms leave more room to improve, versus shorter and milder pain responding better because central
sensitisation has had less time to develop. *Relationship*: empathy, trust, feeling heard, unhurried
history-taking enlarge placebo through expectancy — and symmetrically **a strong relationship
magnifies nocebo too**, extended explicitly to coaches.

**Nocebo has no equivalent flagship trial.** Its mechanism list — verbal suggestion, prior learning,
social observation, mass psychogenic modelling, negative clinical communication — is cited to
**Colloca 2024** and **Saunders 2024** (a genuine systematic review and meta-analysis of social
learning). The headline claim, **nocebo stronger and more persistent than placebo**, rests on
**Kunkel 2025**, whose title says plainly "**in healthy individuals**" and which used experimental
pain — a legitimate paradigm, not the same evidential object as chronic pain in patients, and the
article crosses the gap without a hedge. The biological-correlates table (placebo raising endogenous
opioids, endocannabinoids, dopamine, oxytocin; nocebo lowering opioids and dopamine, suppressing
endocannabinoids, raising **CCK**, a pain-amplifying peptide) is cited entirely to **Knezevic 2025**,
one narrative review, and presented as settled biology. The persuasive work is done by an analogy:
brakes that "look like the brakes of an 80-year-old car", mapped onto a doctor saying "you have the
spine of an 80-year-old".

**The portable output is a four-item ethics checklist.** If a treatment has real physiological
benefit, placebo is "the cherry on top". If it has none beyond placebo, pursue it only when (1) the
financial, time and effort cost is within reason; (2) there are no adverse effects; (3) it is not
displacing more effective interventions; and (4) the patient is properly informed of the true
effects. The mirror case is expensive, risky, displacing better care, or **packaged with a harmful
narrative** — their example is months of paid spinal manipulation instead of progressive exercise,
sold with the story that your spine is out of alignment. Their uncited argument: the short-term
placebo relief is outweighed by the long-lasting nocebo of being told you are broken.

## What to do when training hurts

[The pain-in-training note](pain-training.md) is the operational half, and read it as a different
kind of claim: **almost none of the practical section carries a citation** — not the entry-point
rule, the 24–48-hour window, the tempo strategy, the exposure frequency, nor the ordering of the
levers. Coherent clinical reasoning, broadly matching contemporary physiotherapy guidance, delivered
in the same voice as the referenced material. **Absolute rest is explicitly rejected** for routine
training aches (it detrains you, so you return wanting loads you are no longer adapted to) — also
uncited here.

The core is the **entry point**: a dose of the movement that leaves symptoms **improved or stable
over the following 24–48 hours**. A symptom *trajectory* rule, not a pain-threshold rule. Anything
markedly worse during or after is too much, and **"pain free" is explicitly ruled out as a short-term
goal**.

The levers are ordered. **Cut absolute load first, further than feels reasonable** — the named
failure is the lifter who drops 365 to 315, still hurts, and refuses 225 or 135 or the empty bar;
they prefer to **undershoot deliberately**. Two mechanisms force load down without changing the
exercise: reps to **12–15**, and tempo (**3-0-3**, or an isolated 6-count eccentric). The worked
example takes a **365 lb × 5 squatter to 85–95 lb for sets of 12–15 at 3-0-3** — about a **75% load
reduction**, which is the point. If nothing is tolerable, **alter range of motion** (block/pin
deadlifts, box or pin squats, board/floor/pin presses), with the caveat that **a shortened range is
not a licence to load heavier**. Only then **change the exercise**, including unilateral work — and
deliberately vary bilateral and unilateral movements, because people compensate away from a painful
side. Frequency **2–3 non-consecutive days per week**. Sample non-specific knee pain template: 3-0-3
low bar squat **12 @ RPE 6/7/8**, then 3-0-3 split squat **15 @ RPE 6/7/8**; day two the same with
high bar squat and weighted lunge.

Increments are **2.5–5 lb, or 5–10%**, called "arbitrary suggestions" in the article itself. If
symptoms are flat they hold load constant for several sessions to let desensitisation happen. As load
rises, reps drop (**12 → 10 → 8 → 6**) and tempo normalises. Expect **months, not days**, for
tendinopathy or radiculopathy. Flares are attributed to dose *and* to biopsychosocial inputs — sleep,
stress, illness — which is consistent with the model but **makes it hard to falsify**: any flare is
explicable as too much load or a bad week. Recurrence after a successful cycle is blamed on the
programme (rigid models, excessive intensity, excessive volume, no fatigue management, hyper-specific
exercise selection), fixed by RPE autoregulation and more variation.

## The sentences that outrun the evidence

Same shape every time: a real negative finding about **measurement** underneath, a stronger positive
claim about **reality** on top.

**"The joints themselves essentially do not move."** *Established:* SI motion is small, unreliably
detected, unchanged by manipulation. *Defensible version:* motion is **too small to palpate**, not
absent — the cited studies measured real, non-zero motion. *Cost:* a claim any anatomist can falsify
discredits the reliability evidence under it, which is the part that matters.

**The slide from "clinicians cannot reliably detect SI motion" to "the SI joint is not a source of
pain."** Different claims, blurred in the take-home points. The article's own **Laslett 2008**
material concedes that posterior pelvic pain is real, that provocation-test **clusters** have
diagnostic value against anaesthetic block, and that the missing piece is a gold standard for
localising it — not proof of innocence. Related: Robinson tested motion palpation *and* pain
provocation; the article quotes only the palpation figure. *Cost:* someone with genuine SI-region
pain reads a negative finding about **palpation** as a verdict on their symptoms.

**"It is difficult — if not impossible — to establish physical positions that are inherently 'bad'
across the population."** *Established:* Saraceni and Nolan show technique does not separate people
with pain from people without it **at group level**. *Defensible version:* that is not the same as no
position carrying risk for any individual under any load. Baraki half-concedes it two paragraphs
later ("there are absolute limits to tissue tolerance") and pins residual risk on high-velocity
unpredictable field-sport movements rather than the gym — plausible clinical intuition, offered
without evidence. His dismissal of cadaver work ("corpses can't adapt") is a fair external-validity
point stated as if it settled the matter.

**"All treatments for pain are mediated by placebo effects."** Asserted twice, once as a section
heading, with **no citation** — the article's thesis and its least defended sentence, close to
unfalsifiable as stated. *Defensible version:* any treatment for a subjective symptom carries a
placebo component of unknown size. *Cost:* it flattens the distinction the same article gets right
elsewhere — real physiological benefit *plus* placebo versus placebo *only* — which is what its own
ethics checklist runs on.

**Nocebo generalised from healthy volunteers to patients.** *Defensible version:* nocebo effects are
robust in experimental pain in healthy people, and plausibly — but not demonstrably — larger and more
durable than placebo in patients.

**"Inactivity appears to be a greater risk factor for worsening pain and disability due to
osteoarthritis"** (and the companion claim that lifelong activity maintains function, strength, ROM
and quality of life). Both **asserted with no reference** in the crepitus article. Plausible and
supported elsewhere, but the first is doing real work in the argument.

## Errors, and the commercial shape

**A clinical error worth correcting.** The placebo article repeatedly calls FIDELITY's active arm
"meniscus **repair** surgery". It was **arthroscopic partial meniscectomy** — removal of torn tissue,
not repair. Different operation, different rationale, and the distinction matters to anyone reading
it against their own knee; the counts given (**144 total, 70 vs 74**) should be checked against the
published trial before quoting. The same article summarises Ashar as "significant reductions in pain
and mood" — the finding is improvement in mood, not reduction of it.

**Citation hygiene.** The placebo article's reference list duplicates itself — **[6] and [20] are the
same Ashar 2024 paper, [18] and [21] the same Knezevic 2025 review** — and two references are
**StatPearls chapters**, tertiary teaching material. The Saunders social-learning result is garbled
past readability ("These results were if the learning took place in a face-to-face interaction"). The
weakest load-bearing citation in the domain is **Hartman 2009, "Why do ineffective treatments seem
helpful? A brief review"** (*Chiropractic & Osteopathy*), a short narrative review carrying **three**
claims alone — pricing, treatment intensity, and the 17-studies/535-patients sham-surgery statistic,
which is therefore secondhand. In the SI article the links for **Eno 2015** and **Rossettini 2018**
are broken and were resolved separately.

**Commercial routing splits.** [The SI joint note](si-joint-movement-cause-hip-low-back.md) and [the
crepitus note](crepitus-expectations-vs-reality.md) **pitch nothing at all** — unusual for this
source. [The pain-in-training note](pain-training.md) carries two "individualized guidance can help"
panels linking their paid rehab and coaching; the advice does not depend on buying anything, but the
escalation step points at their own product. [The placebo note](placebo-nocebo-effects-pain.md) ends
with three calls to action for Barbell Medicine coaching, and the shape is worth naming: it explains
why passive treatments people pay *other* providers for work largely through expectation, then routes
the reader to a paid product of its own. Their own ethics checklist would apply to it too.
