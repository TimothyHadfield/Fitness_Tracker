# Squat Moment Arms Explained — With the Torque Argument Inverted

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Barbell Forces: Levers, Moments, and Torque" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/barbell-forces-levers-moments-and-torque/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2012-06-14 · 1,774 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **The definitions are right.** A **moment arm** is the horizontal distance from the load's vertical vector to the joint acting as fulcrum; **torque** is that distance multiplied by the load. The forces in a loaded squat are compression through the skeleton, muscular tension holding the trunk, moment force, and — if the descent is fast — momentum.
- **The useful, correct claim:** bar position trades torque between joints. A **front squat** drives the knees further forward, lengthening the distal-femoral moment arm and loading the knees; a **low-bar back squat** forces a more horizontal torso, lengthening the spinal and proximal-femoral moment arms and loading the hips.
- **Moment arms grow as you descend.** At the top, upright with the bar over midfoot, horizontal distances to the hips and spine are near zero and torque is minimal. They increase through the descent, which is why the bottom is hardest.
- **The article's central mechanical argument is backwards.** It repeatedly claims that lengthening these levers *helps* the lifter — "a longer lever will increase the torque and thus allow for heavier weights to be moved," complete with an Archimedes quote. That inverts the physics.
- **This is a 2012 article, predating Barbell Medicine's evidence and pain-science turn**, and it reads that way, including a jab at physical therapists that the same author's later work argues against.

## The three levers, as he defines them

All three are measured from the barbell's plumb line, which must stay over midfoot:

1. **Spinal** — from the bar (he assumes low-bar, "below the spine of the scapula") to the lumbosacral junction. The erectors work isometrically to keep this segment rigid.
2. **Proximal femoral** — from where the bar's vector crosses the femoral shaft to the hip joint centre.
3. **Distal femoral** — from that same crossing point to the knee joint centre.

His observation that the low-bar squat has a longer proximal than distal femoral lever, transmitting proportionally more load to the hips than the knees, and that the front squat reverses this, is sound and is the article's real contribution.

## Where it goes wrong

**The moment arms he is measuring are *resistance* moment arms.** The distance from the barbell's line of action to a joint determines the torque the lifter's muscles must *overcome*. Lengthening it makes the lift harder, not easier. Mechanical advantage comes from the *muscle's* moment arm — insertion to joint centre — a different quantity that is never mentioned. The article conflates the two, so its repeated claim that the descent "maximises" the lever and thereby "magnifies" the lifter's force gets the sign wrong. The Archimedes analogy makes it worse: his advantage came from a long *effort* arm against a short resistance arm.

**"Force dissipated through an unlocked spine"** is energetic language, not a mechanical quantity. Keeping the trunk rigid is defensible for other reasons — controlling segment position and therefore moment-arm length — but nothing is "dissipated."

**Minor:** torque at the top is described as absent because the bar sits over midfoot, which is approximately true for the hips and spine but not for the ankle; the bar's vector cannot pass through every joint at once. The ascent explanation — better force-length relationship plus "increased actin-myosin cross-linking," aided by momentum — states a real principle too tidily, and momentum is not free work.

**Period marks.** The low-bar squat is assumed as the default ("hopefully in the low bar position"), a Starting Strength inheritance that Barbell Medicine's later material on bar placement softens considerably. The opening line — this "should keep your physical therapy and chiropractic friends happy if they tell you squats are bad for your ______" — frames biomechanics as a rebuttal to clinicians, the opposite of the position the same author later argues from.

**Sells:** nothing.
