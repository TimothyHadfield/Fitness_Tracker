# Squat Leverage Explained — With One Physics Error Worth Knowing About

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Barbell Forces: Levers, Moments, and Torque" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/barbell-forces-levers-moments-and-torque/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2012-06-14 · 1,774 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- A **moment arm** is the horizontal distance from the barbell's vertical force vector to a joint (the fulcrum). **Torque = barbell load × moment arm.** That is the whole model, applied three times.
- At the top of a squat, standing upright with the bar over midfoot, the horizontal distances are near zero, so joint torques are near zero. Every torque in the lift is created by descending.
- The three moment arms that matter: **bar-to-lumbosacral junction** (the back/spine lever), **bar-vector-to-hip** (proximal femur lever), and **bar-vector-to-knee** (distal femur lever). All three lengthen as you descend.
- Bar position sets the split. **Low-bar** gives a longer proximal femoral segment and a more horizontal torso, loading the hips; **front squat** shortens the proximal and lengthens the distal segment, loading the knees. This part is sound and is the article's genuinely useful contribution.
- **It contains a clear physics error.** It repeatedly claims a *longer* moment arm magnifies the lifter's force and lets heavier weights be moved. The opposite is true — see below.

## The mechanical model
Feigenbaum enumerates the forces acting in a loaded squat: **compression** transmitted down the skeleton from the bar, **tension** generated by muscle to hold the skeleton upright, **moment force (torque)** wherever a horizontal distance exists between the bar's downward vector and a joint, and **momentum** when the bar is moving fast — his example being that dropping hard into the bottom adds effective load on top of the bar's weight and can cost you the rep.

The bar-over-midfoot constraint does real work in his argument. Because the bar must stay balanced over midfoot, any forward or backward drift creates a new moment arm between midfoot and the bar, which either misses the rep or bleeds efficiency. Everything else in the lift arranges itself around that constraint.

He also makes an **isometric argument about the spine**: the vertebral column between the bar and the hips functions as a rigid lever, and the erectors' job is to hold neutral lumbar lordosis and thoracic extension so that force is not "dissipated through an unlocked spine". That is presented as mechanics, not as evidence.

## The error, stated plainly
The article says: because the lever is longest at the bottom of the squat, the force applied by the hip and knee extensors "is multiplied and more effective than the same force acting over a shorter lever", and quotes Archimedes on levers to support it.

This conflates the **effort arm** with the **resistance arm**. In a squat the barbell's moment arm about a joint is the *resistance* arm — lengthening it increases the torque the muscles must produce, which is exactly why the bottom of the squat is the hardest position. The *effort* arm is the muscle's own insertion distance from the joint, and that is what magnifies muscular force; it is fixed by anatomy and does not lengthen as you descend. A longer bar moment arm makes a squat **harder**, not easier, and no amount of leverage on the load side lets a lifter move a heavier weight.

The rest of the article is internally inconsistent with this too: he correctly says the front squat's longer distal femoral lever puts *more* torque on the knee, which is described as a cost, not a benefit. The magnification claim is the odd one out.

The closing paragraph is fine again — as you ascend, the levers shorten and torque drops, while the muscles move into more favourable regions of the force-length relationship.

## Context and caveats
This is a **2012 article** and the earliest-era material in this collection, written when Feigenbaum was coaching in the Starting Strength orbit; the low-bar back squat is treated throughout as the default correct squat rather than one option among several. It also opens with a jab at physical therapists and chiropractors "if they tell you squats are bad for your ___". That is worth noting because Barbell Medicine's later and much better-developed position is broadly pain-science-aligned and considerably less combative about clinical scepticism — this piece predates all of that.

Nothing here is an injury claim, a hypertrophy claim, or a training recommendation. It is a physics explainer, and it should be read as one. No products are recommended.
