# Youth Resistance Training: One Meta-Analysis, Read Critically, Then Translated Into RPE

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Recommendations for Youth Resistance Training" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/part-v-current-recommendations-for-youth-resistance-training/)) · Derek Miles · 2020-07-17 · 3,583 words
**Topic:** Programming

## How this article cites

**No numbered citations.** The article cites by **inline hyperlink in the prose**, five external sources in total, plus links to Barbell Medicine's own earlier articles and to YouTube explainers. There is no reference list and no numbering, so claim-level attribution by number was never available. Where Miles names a source in the sentence — Lesinski, Behm — that attachment is unambiguous and is used below. This is Part V of a five-part series; the earlier parts carry the background on safety and epiphyseal injury risk that this instalment assumes.

## Bottom line
- **The evidence base for dosing youth resistance training is essentially one meta-analysis**, Lesinski 2016 in *BJSM*, which recommends **≥23 weeks of training, 5 sets of 6–8 reps at 80–89% 1RM, 3–4 minutes rest**. Miles cites it and then immediately says it is not executable as written — nobody completes five sets of eight at 80–89% of a true maximum, let alone repeatedly in a week.
- **His reading of why is the most valuable thing in the article.** He infers that youth in these trials were so untrained that their measured 1RMs, and percentages of them, are not comparable to a trained adult's. That is a criticism of the *construct* the meta-analysis pooled, not of its arithmetic, and it is rare to see a coaching article take a meta-analysis apart that way rather than quoting its abstract.
- **The translation is to RPE/RIR**, not percentages: sets of 6–8 at RPE 7–9 (1–3 reps in reserve), anchored to Zourdos 2016. The argument is that a subjective, autoregulated anchor survives the day-to-day variability of an athlete who also has practices, school and sleep debt — a percentage of a shaky 1RM does not.
- **Duration beats intensity.** An 8-week block does not produce durable gains, because of reversibility. Resistance training should be present for the *majority* of the year: up to 4 days/week off-season, dropping to 1–2 days/week in season to *maintain* what the off-season built, rather than chasing small adaptations at 2 days/week while sport stress is high.
- **Strength before power.** Behm 2017, a meta-analysis in youth, concluded that "strength training should be incorporated prior to power training in order to establish an adequate foundation of strength for power training activities." Miles uses this against the speed-camp and plyometric-first industry.
- **They sell nothing here.** No template pitch, no coaching pitch, no product. For a Barbell Medicine article this is unusual and worth recording.

## The load-management position, and its restraint

Miles builds a simple envelope: every athlete has a baseline of fitness that must be exceeded to produce adaptation, and a maximum capacity above which the dose exceeds tolerance. Below baseline is a zone of homeostasis where nothing happens; between the two is the supraphysiological range where adaptation occurs. He notes that most youth athletes' resistance-training baseline is effectively zero, so almost any initial stimulus produces adaptation — which is also why the early literature is so easy to over-read.

He then invokes the acute-versus-chronic workload framework and, importantly, **declines to endorse it as a predictive tool**. His position: there is enormous inter-individual variability in tolerance to load change, and no metric has shown strong predictive power at the individual level for sports injury. Written in 2020, when acute:chronic workload ratio was at peak popularity in sports science, that is a restrained call and the right one.

He extends the point to terminology. Current paradigms label these injuries "overuse"; a movement exists to rename them "training load error." He notes that many authors still prefer "overuse" precisely because *load* is multifactorial and includes stress from outside the pitch, field or pool — academic load, sleep, home stress. This is the same reasoning Barbell Medicine apply to adults: fatigue is the sum of all stressors, not just training volume, and "stimulus" is the better word than "stress" because the response is the point.

## Programming, and what is evidence versus advocacy

Miles is explicit about the line, which makes the note easy to write.

**Stated as advocacy.** "While no one training modality is inherently superior to another, we will advocate for training built around the primary movements of the squat, bench, press, and deadlift with a barbell." The justification is not physiological — it is **scalability**. Barbells load in small increments; dumbbells, kettlebells and sandbags are less adjustable and are generally produced in larger jumps and smaller sizes. That is a practical argument for a training-age population who need many small progressions, and it is presented as such rather than dressed up as evidence.

**Also advocacy:** the session template (60–90 minutes: non-specific warm-up focused on physical literacy or general preparation, one lower-body compound, one upper-body compound, one to three variation lifts, optional skill work on jumping, change of direction or landing); the two-day and four-day split options; goblet squat as the entry point before a barbell; external cueing ("twist the ground apart" for knee position, "like you're going to pick up a bag of dog food" for the hinge) in preference to internal cues like "chest up"; and the observation that knees travelling past the toes is fine.

**Stated as evidence:** the Lesinski dose, the Behm strength-before-power ordering, the Zourdos RPE-to-RIR mapping. That is all of it. Three papers do the empirical work in a 3,600-word article.

On rep schemes he makes a specific, checkable claim: 3 × 10 at RPE 8 and 5 × 6 at RPE 8 contain the same total reps but the second uses heavier loads, so if absolute strength is the goal the second scheme has the advantage — while higher-rep schemes have a place early, for skill acquisition and work capacity under load. This is standard specificity reasoning and it is not cited.

## Technique, and the pain-science position applied to children

This is where the article carries Barbell Medicine's house position into a population where it is most contested. Miles argues that a high degree of initial variation in technique is expected and integral to learning; that "small perturbations from that ideal help to build a more broad foundation"; and that the common deterrent to starting youth resistance training — the belief that imperfect form means injury — should not gate participation. His analogy: we don't wait for a perfect swing before letting a child play baseball, or a perfect cut before letting them play soccer.

He is careful with the hedge. **"Technique absolutely *does matter*"**, and a well-designed programme includes movements chosen to build technical proficiency. That is the correct version of this argument — variability is expected and not feared, which is a different and much weaker claim than "form doesn't matter." Readers coming from the industry version of this debate should note the difference.

There is a companion argument about variety: new skill acquisition depends on it, but you can supply it through derivatives of the same lift rather than ten different exercises per session. And an aside that endurance-only participation in adolescence is associated with disadvantageous adaptations including lower bone mineral density — supported here by a link to another Barbell Medicine article rather than to a paper.

## Sloppiness worth knowing about

Three things, all minor individually but they bear on how carefully to quote this piece.

- **The RPE conversion changes the unit.** Lesinski prescribes **5 sets** of 6–8; Miles's converted prescription reads "**5 exercises** of 6–8 repetitions at 7–9 RPE." Five exercises of 6–8 reps is a very different session from five sets of one movement, and the substitution is not flagged.
- **Rest is quoted two ways.** The Lesinski dose is given as "3–4 minutes rest" in the opening and "2–3 minutes" in the Other Programming Variables section — with a later note that if absolute strength is the goal, rest may extend to 4–5 minutes.
- **A double negative inverts a sentence.** "No metric has not shown strong *predictive* power on an individual level" — the surrounding argument makes clear the intended meaning is that no metric *has* shown such power, which is also the defensible reading of the literature.

The article also refers to figures, sample week programmes and instructional videos that do not survive into the text version, so the specific programme it builds toward is only partly recoverable here.

**Flagged:** the one citation that does not fit its sentence is the link supporting "mechanisms of sports injury are complex" — it points to a consensus statement on the psychological and sociocultural influences on injury risk, response and recovery. That statement genuinely supports multifactorial complexity, but it is a psychosocial document rather than a general review of injury mechanisms, and with only five sources in the article it carries more weight than it should. Separately, the entire dosing recommendation rests on a single meta-analysis whose prescription the author himself says cannot be executed — a limitation he states clearly, and which the reader should carry forward rather than treating "5 × 6–8 at 80–89%" as an established youth training dose.
