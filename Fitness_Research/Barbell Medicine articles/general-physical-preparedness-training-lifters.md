# GPP for Lifters: Conditioning, Upper Back, Trunk and Arms

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "General Physical Preparedness (GPP) Training For Lifters" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/general-physical-preparedness-gpp-training-for-lifters/)) · Leah Lutz · 2020-11-06 · 4,359 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- GPP is their slot for everything a barbell programme leaves out: conditioning, upper back, trunk and direct arm work. Their lifters typically run **two or more GPP sessions weekly, with two to four conditioning bouts**.
- **LISS before HIIT for people with no aerobic base.** Their reason is specific and cited: HIIT is less effective as conditioning without an aerobic base ([Wilson 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/)), so building anaerobic capacity first can leave aerobic development worse off.
- For HIIT they select modality on four criteria — low risk, low skill, sustains 20–30 s of hard cardiorespiratory work, low residual fatigue — and that reasoning lands on **cycling and rowing**, not running, kettlebells or barbell complexes.
- **Lat pulldowns beat weighted chins for upper-back hypertrophy** on a volume-per-unit-fatigue argument, and chins beat pulldowns for nothing except chins. Stated as principle, not evidence.
- Direct ab work is defended as cheap rather than necessary: heavy squats, presses and deadlifts already train the trunk ([Comfort 2011](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/Fulltext/2011/01000/An_Electromyographical_Comparison_of_Trunk_Muscle.22.aspx)), but a prone bridge gets similar abdominal activation to a 6RM squat for much less fatigue ([van den Tillaar 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29922376/)).

## What GPP is for

The framing is GPP against SPP. Special physical preparedness develops sport-specific ability; general physical preparedness lays groundwork — cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular endurance, hypertrophy, and work capacity in the sense of tolerating a larger workload. For a powerlifter, weightlifter or strongman, those qualities are largely non-specific to competition performance, which is exactly why they need their own slot rather than emerging from the main lifts.

The organising question is honest and useful: **GPP should target the gaps your sport training leaves**. In barbell sport that means aerobic development plus direct upper back, trunk and arm work — tissue that is stressed indirectly by the competition lifts but rarely as a training target. It can also carry rehabilitation-flavoured work (Copenhagen planks and lateral lunges for adductors, Nordic curls and eccentric-focused leg curls for hamstrings).

The scheduling is deliberately loose. GPP is usually its own session, but it can be attached to a main training day or split across the week — cardio after day one, upper back after day two, abs after day three — typically to preserve a rest day. They note, in passing and without hedging, that they want people active every day including rest days.

The claim that this volume is not excessive is anchored to the physical activity guidelines ([Piercy 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30418471/)), with the correct emphasis that those are minimums and more activity brings more benefit.

## Conditioning: the LISS-first argument

This is the most substantive section. They place conditioning on an intensity spectrum from LISS to HIIT, approximated by heart rate, %VO2max, RPE or pace, and give a workable practical anchor: at or slightly below **RPE 6–7, or ~70% of max heart rate**, most efforts are aerobic. They then immediately undercut their own heart-rate anchor — the 220-age formula came from Haskell's review of ten studies in the 1970s including people with heart disease, and "is widely regarded to not be very accurate for exercise applications." Giving a rule of thumb and its provenance in the same breath is the sort of thing this source does well.

On the fat-loss question they refuse the popular answer: HIIT is often sold as superior for weight loss, and the cited meta-analysis ([Keating 2017](https://www.u-cursos.cl/medicina/2017/0/DPPAEF/1/foro/r/(2017)_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_interval_training_versus_moderate-intensity_continuous_training_on_body_adiposity.pdf)) found similar body-adiposity outcomes for interval and moderate continuous training with neither clearly better. Choice between them therefore falls to conditioning background, purpose, time and equipment, goal, injury history and preference — and they list those six as the actual decision inputs.

**The LISS-first recommendation is where they take a position.** For someone with no conditioning history who is already lifting — that is, already doing something very anaerobic — they start with LISS. The reason given is that improvements in anaerobic function may actually worsen aerobic development without concurrent aerobic training, citing Wilson's 2012 concurrent-training meta-analysis for the point that HIIT is less effective as conditioning without an aerobic base. Note what that citation is: a meta-analysis of the *interference* literature, used here for a directional claim about HIIT's dependence on aerobic base. It supports the general shape of the argument better than it supports the specific sentence.

Prescriptions are concrete. LISS at **3–5.9 METs**, and the guideline targets: 150–300 min/week moderate intensity or 75–150 min/week vigorous, plus resistance training on two or more days. Progression is start at ~20 min at a pace where you can speak in short sentences but not sing, add 5 minutes every 3–4 weeks up to 30, then raise intensity. HIIT starts at **5–7 intervals of 20–30 s all-out with 90–100 s complete rest**, adding a set every 3–4 weeks, with 15 rounds as a sensible ceiling.

The modality comparison is the best-reasoned passage in the article, and it is reasoning rather than evidence. Running is possible at high intensity but demands more skill for safe sprinting and leaves high residual fatigue in non-runners. Kettlebells and farmer's walks are highly anaerobic, need equipment, produce grip fatigue that bleeds into other training, and drive a smaller cardiorespiratory response than lower-skill options. Barbell complexes hit local muscular failure before the cardiorespiratory system is properly loaded. Cycling and rowing win on all four criteria, with rowing costing slightly more via grip and low back. Their aside that even Usain Bolt cannot hold 100% for more than about seven seconds is offered as reassurance rather than data.

## Upper back, trunk and arms

**Upper back.** The position: for hypertrophy, volume matters, and you cannot accumulate as much volume at adequate load with chins and especially weighted chins without generating unwanted fatigue — so **lat pulldowns are the better hypertrophy choice**. For strength, chins improve chins; barbell rows sit marginally closer to the deadlift, but if you want a bigger deadlift, deadlift more. Ten acceptable options are listed and explicitly treated as interchangeable, with a warning against over-crediting any GPP movement's transfer to the barbell lifts. Sets and reps: 3–5 × 12–15 at RPE 7–8, or a timed AMRAP.

**Trunk.** They prefer isometrics because that is how the trunk functions under a bar, while noting barbell athletes miss frontal-plane and rotational work. The argument for training abs at all is a cost argument, not a necessity argument, and the two citations do the work exactly: the compound lifts do train the trunk (Comfort 2011, an EMG comparison), and a prone bridge produces similar abdominal activation to a 6RM squat with substantially less fatigue (van den Tillaar 2018). Both are **acute EMG studies, not training outcomes** — activation is not hypertrophy or strength, and the conclusion they draw ("just train your abs, without a hardline stance") is appropriately modest for that evidence. Prescription: 1–2 exercises per week, rotated every 3–5 weeks, RPE 6–7+, isometrics held to within 30 s of failure.

**Arms.** Direct biceps and triceps work as supplementary stress, deliberately not a bodybuilding arm day, because competition and supplemental lifts have first call on recovery. Selection criteria: targets the muscle, large range of motion with both phases, not especially fatiguing at volume. Prescription: 3–6 × 10–20 at RPE 7–9, or an AMRAP capped at RPE 6–7 per set so acute fatigue does not limit accumulated volume. Supersets are endorsed as a time saver.

## What is evidence and what is house practice

Almost everything above the exercise lists is house practice. Five references support: the activity guidelines, the HIIT-versus-LISS fat-loss null, the aerobic-base claim, and the two trunk EMG papers. Nothing supports the GPP frequency, the pulldown-over-chin ranking, the rep prescriptions, or the rotation schedule. The article does not claim otherwise, and its recurring justification is adherence — freedom of exercise choice aids adherence, so pick work you will keep doing. That is a defensible coaching principle stated without a citation.

**Flagged:** "having freedom of choice has been shown to help adherence to training" is phrased as an evidence claim and carries no reference.

**Things they sell:** this article is a description of how Barbell Medicine's own programming and coaching structure GPP days, and it links their HIIT and tendinopathy content. It does not push a specific template, which is unusual for the source.
