# Collagen Supplements and Tendinopathy: A Trial of 20 People, and the Placebo Group Did Better

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Collagen Protein and Tendinopathy" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/collagen-protein-and-tendinopathy/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2021-10-08 · 3,550 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Collagen is a poor dietary protein.** It contains zero tryptophan, so its PDCAAS is **0**; add tryptophan and it would still only reach **0.39**, against **1.00** for whey ([Phillips 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27708684/)). Collagen is ~30% of total body protein and tendon is ~65–80% collagen, mostly type I — but that anatomical fact is the entire basis of the supplement's marketing logic.
- The central mechanistic objection: **amino acids do not choose where they are used.** Collagen raises blood hydroxyproline, glycine and proline after ingestion ([Skov 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31086034/)), but absorbed peptides and amino acids are directed by need, not by source tissue.
- The article reviews one trial in detail ([Praet 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30609761/)): **n=20**, double-blind placebo-controlled **crossover**, 2 × 3 months, 2.5 g hydrolysed collagen peptides twice daily versus 2.5 g maltodextrin, on top of a daily eccentric calf programme.
- **The raw result favours placebo.** At 3 months the collagen group's mean VISA-A moved 58.5 → 60.8; placebo moved 55.9 → 62.8. The minimum clinically important difference is **6.5 points** ([McCormack 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26491614/)) — only placebo cleared it. Only a linear mixed model produced a collagen advantage (12.6 vs 5.3 points).
- Feigenbaum's conclusion: any effect attributable to collagen supplementation is "very small or non-existent", consistent with a 2018 meta-analysis of **69 studies** finding no supplement with a clinically important long-term effect on joint pain ([Liu 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-097333)).

## Why collagen is a bad protein and a plausible-sounding supplement
Over 20 collagen types exist; types I, II and III dominate in humans ([Exposito 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20386646/)). Type I is in bone, ligament, tendon and skin ([Di Lullo 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11704682/)); type II in joint cartilage; type III in skin, vessels and organs. Supplemental collagen is ground fish, chicken, pig or cow tissue, containing up to 19 amino acids with high hydroxyproline, glycine and proline.

The protein-quality argument is the cleanest part of the article. PDCAAS scores a protein by its limiting amino acid relative to a reference protein, multiplied by digestibility. Collagen's limiting amino acid is tryptophan, present at zero, so the score is zero by construction. Feigenbaum is careful to note that hydrolysed collagen peptides *are* absorbed intact, which is a real point in the supplement's favour that he does not suppress — he simply observes it does not follow that they are routed to tendon.

## The trial, in numbers
**Subjects:** 20 patients with mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy — 7 unilateral, 13 bilateral. Symptom duration ranged from under a month to nearly **30 years**, and the data were not reported in a way that allows checking whether duration moderated the result. Lifetime risk of Achilles tendinopathy is quoted as **24% in elite and 9% in recreational athletes** ([Olewnik 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29955614/), [Li 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27885357/)).

**Design:** double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover. Collagen or placebo for 3 months, then switch, with VISA-A and contrast-enhanced ultrasound at baseline, 3 and 6 months. Bloods (total cholesterol, triglycerides, uric acid) ruled out familial hypercholesterolaemia and gout as contributors; none were present.

**Intervention:** 2.5 g hydrolysed collagen peptides twice daily, one dose 30 minutes pre-exercise; placebo was 2.5 g maltodextrin matched for packaging, taste and texture. Supplement adherence 89 ± 15% and 91 ± 9%.

**Exercise (both arms):** eccentric calf raises — concentric on the good leg, eccentric drop on the injured one, performed with knee straight and bent to bias gastrocnemius and soleus — **2 × 90 repetitions daily for six months, performed despite pain**. No weight-bearing sport for four weeks; running permitted once single-leg hopping caused under 2/10 pain. Exercise adherence 84% and 78%.

**Outcome measure:** VISA-A, 0–100, eight questions on pain, function and activity tolerance, where 100 is asymptomatic and 70 is described as the ceiling for someone with Achilles tendinopathy. Test–retest r = 0.93, inter-tester r = 0.90 ([Robinson 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11579069/)); MCID 6.5 points; reliability generalisation meta-analysis at [Palazón-Bru 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569746/).

**Results:** raw means as above. Linear mixed modelling gave 12.6 (collagen) vs 5.3 (placebo) in period one; in period two, 5.9 for those crossing from collagen to placebo and **17.7 for those crossing from placebo to collagen**. Baseline to six months: 18.5 vs 23.0 points, with the placebo-first sequence significantly better. Blinding was imperfect — 60% (6/10) and 55% (5/9) correctly identified collagen; only 40% (4/10) and 44% (4/9) correctly identified placebo.

**Vascularisation:** both groups' tendon vascularity fell significantly, with **no between-group difference at any time point** — consistent with the exercise programme or with natural history rather than the supplement.

## The vascularity digression, and why it is inconclusive
The article spends real space on whether neovascularisation matters. Normal tendon is poorly vascularised with little adult turnover, whereas tendinopathic tendon shows abnormally high collagen turnover for years beforehand ([Heinemeier 2018](https://doi.org/10.1096/fj.201701569R)). The new vessels are disorganised and leaky, plausibly producing a hypoxic environment — supported by elevated HIF-1α and its target genes in tendinopathic and ruptured tendon tissue ([Pufe 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15998338/)) — and **97.3%** of symptomatic Achilles tendons in one ultrasound study showed substantial neovascularisation, correlated with pain scores ([Yang 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22975033/)).

Feigenbaum lands this correctly: these are cross-sectional and histological associations, the direction of causation is unresolved, and **"we don't have a strong reason to significantly change the management of tendinopathy based on tendon vascularization alone."** That is the right conclusion and unusually restrained given how neatly the mechanism would have supported a story.

## Where the critique is strong, and where it overreaches
**Strong.** The crossover-order argument is the sharpest observation in the piece: if collagen were uniquely effective, the group starting with worse scores should improve more, and the opposite happened — the placebo-first group did better overall. The unblinding data support a positive-expectation effect. The choice of maltodextrin rather than an isonitrogenous protein placebo means the trial cannot separate "collagen" from "any additional dietary protein", and he notes this is endemic to the literature, which mostly uses water or sugar controls and mostly lacks control groups altogether. He also notes that almost none of this research looks at collagen-rich *foods* as opposed to supplements.

**Overreach, flagged.** Feigenbaum treats the raw mean-change-versus-MCID comparison as the honest analysis and the linear mixed model as a rescue manoeuvre ("only through additional statistical techniques"). That is unfair in one direction: mixed models are the standard handling of repeated measures with missing data in a crossover, not a post-hoc trick, and comparing a *group mean change* to an MCID — a within-person threshold — is itself a weak inference. His conclusion survives this, because the crossover-order finding and the blinding failure do the real work, but the specific dismissal of the model is not sound methodological criticism. A more serious design objection he does not raise: a crossover with no washout is a poor fit for a condition where a six-month eccentric loading programme runs concurrently and its effects carry over.

**Recommendation versus evidence.** The article's own key-point summary says there is "some low-quality evidence suggesting supplemental collagen may improve tendinopathy and/or joint pain in the short term, but not in the long term", while the body concludes the effect is very small or non-existent. Those are not quite the same claim, and the second is closer to what he actually shows.

**Commercial note.** Barbell Medicine sells a supplement line. This article argues against a supplement category — the conclusion runs *against* the commercial incentive, which is worth recording in a library where the opposite is the norm. The only internal links are to their own free tendinopathy guide.
