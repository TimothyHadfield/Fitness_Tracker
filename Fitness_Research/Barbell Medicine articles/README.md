# Barbell Medicine - article notes

Notes on 155 articles from barbellmedicine.com, written mostly by practising physicians and
physiotherapists. **This is the only source in the library qualified to write about injury,
pain and rehabilitation**, and that is why it is here — 24 notes cover injury and rehab and 4
cover pain science, filling what was the thinnest area in the whole collection.

**2,171 resolved papers** from 2,986 unique reference links across 665,000 words of source
material. The full bibliography is [RESEARCH-CITATIONS.md](RESEARCH-CITATIONS.md).

51 further articles were excluded, 43 of them a single templated cluster from March 2023 that
repeats the same ten-heading skeleton and the same sentences across dozens of pages.

## The citations are better here than anywhere else in the library — where they work

Every other source gives you a reference list for a whole video and leaves you to infer which
paper supports which claim. This one puts a numbered marker on the sentence. **76 articles carry
a verified marker-to-reference mapping covering 1,804 individual markers**, so a claim links to
its paper exactly rather than by inference, and the notes exploit that.

Three warnings, all earned by reading the corpus rather than assumed.

**A verified mapping proves the numbering is consistent, not that the right paper was cited.**
The notes found a hypertrophy claim citing a ketogenic-diet review, a biceps claim citing a study
of the horse, a bone-density claim propped up by a two-person case study, and printed PubMed IDs
resolving to a fruit-juice titration paper and to polyolefin nanofibers. Where an article prints
both a number and an inline link, **the link has been right and the number wrong every time.**

**Some articles never used numbered citations at all**, and that is different from broken
numbering. They cite by inline author-year link, or by a lumped list at the bottom, or — for 18
of these 155 — not at all. Each note says which it is.

**The reference lists are sometimes internally corrupt**: the author and title text printed
beside a PubMed ID does not always match the paper that ID resolves to. Where the notes caught
this, they list the paper the link actually resolves to and say so.

## What to know before reading

**They argue a position, and it is a contested one.** Barbell Medicine align with modern pain
science: pain is a poor readout of tissue damage, imaging findings are common in people with no
symptoms, "good form" carries far less weight than the industry claims, and most injuries are
managed by modifying load rather than resting. That is mainstream in physiotherapy research and
heterodox in the gym. The notes present it as their position with its evidence, and say where it
is stated more absolutely than the cited work supports.

**Almost every exercise ranking here rests on activation data, not measured growth** — the same
weakness the video sources have. Across this entire corpus only three exercise claims are backed
by hypertrophy outcomes: bench angle, the seated hamstring curl, and overhead triceps extension.
What makes it notable is that they *know*: their lifting-belt article cites Vigotsky explicitly
on why EMG amplitude cannot be read as recruitment, and it is the best-argued piece in the
corpus. Other articles then rank exercises on EMG anyway.

**They sell things** — templates, coaching, consultations and a supplement line. Where an article
recommends something they sell, the note says so plainly. It runs both ways: several articles
argue *against* supplement categories they profit from.
