# A Chest-and-Back Day With No Evidence for Its Exercise Choices, and Real Evidence for Its Supersets

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "5 Exercises For Chest and Back Workout Day" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-chest-and-back-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine (unattributed staff) · 2024-07-12 · 4,609 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **The five-exercise selection — bench press, chin-ups, dips, one-arm dumbbell row, dumbbell flyes — has no supporting data of either kind.** No hypertrophy outcome study, no activation study, no citation is attached to the list, the order, or the set-and-rep prescriptions. It is coaching judgement presented as a recommendation, which is fine, but it is not what the title implies.
- **The only cited exercise claim in the whole article is about the bench press incidentally loading the back**, and it rests on three **activation and biomechanics** studies ([Tucci 2011](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2011.3418), [Król & Gołaś 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001816), [Cudlip 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2022.2046937)) — sEMG and joint-loading, not growth.
- **The one genuinely evidenced claim is about structure, not exercises**: pairing chest and back as agonist-antagonist supersets saves time without compromising adaptations ([Weakley 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002179); [Burke 2024/2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scispo.2025.07.011)). That is the actual reason to read this article.
- They are **admirably restrained on two contested points**: dip torso angle ("it's unclear whether or not this produces different effects in strength and size") and the mind–muscle connection ("the muscles are being active whether you're consciously trying to squeeze them or not... most of these claims are mostly speculative").
- The anatomy section is loose and partly mis-cited — a **fibre-type histochemistry paper** is used to support a claim about the trapezius's origin, and one reference in the list is never cited in the text at all.
- **The most heavily monetised article of the three chest listicles**: three inline "Find your Hypertrophy Program" call-to-action blocks interrupt the body text, plus a long closing pitch for templates and coaching.

## What the article is actually recommending

Bench press 3–4×5–8 at RPE 7–8; chin-ups 3–4×5–8 at RPE 7–8; dips 2–4×6–10 loaded or 8–15 bodyweight; one-arm dumbbell row 2–4×8–12 at RPE 8–9; dumbbell flyes 2–4×10–15 at RPE 8–10. Movements are labelled primary / secondary / tertiary. All of it is uncited.

The programming rationale is stated up front and is the article's real thesis: **chest and back are an agonist–antagonist pair**, so fatigue carries over less between them than it would in a biomechanically similar pairing (bench press plus dumbbell press). They predict similar hypertrophy either way but a possible strength advantage to the antagonist arrangement. That prediction is uncited; the superset half of it is not.

## Evidence type, stated plainly

**Hypertrophy outcome data: none in this article.** No study cited here measured muscle size or strength change as a result of the exercises being recommended.

**Activation data: three studies, all attached to a single sentence.** Tucci 2011 measured selected shoulder muscles during submaximal isometric wall and bench press tasks; Król & Gołaś examined how barbell weight changes bench press structure; Cudlip 2022 compared upper-extremity muscle activity and joint loading between standard and powerlifting (arched) bench technique. Together they support the modest claim that the trapezius and latissimus dorsi contribute to a bench press, especially an arched one. **They say nothing about whether benching grows your back**, and the article does not claim they do.

**Structural/programming data: two studies, and these are the ones that matter.** Weakley 2020 examined superset configuration effects on bench press kinetics, kinematics and perceived exertion; Burke and colleagues' "Less time, same gains" compared superset with traditional set training on muscular adaptations and found comparable results in less time. This supports the practical recommendation: pair bench with chin-ups and dips with rows, minimal rest within the pair, 2–4 minutes between supersets.

**Strength-transfer data: weak.** The claim that strength training "has proven to improve power, endurance, and speed" is supported by a meta-analysis of **variable versus traditional resistance training** on maximal strength and power (Andersen 2022) and a 2003 study of **concurrent endurance and strength training** (Balabinis). Neither paper is a test of that general claim; this is a **loose attribution**, though not a wrong one in spirit.

## Where they are unusually careful

Two passages are better than the genre's norm and both cut against popular gym claims:

- **Dip torso angle.** The common teaching is that a vertical torso targets triceps and shoulders while a forward lean targets chest. They report the claim, then say it is speculative and unclear whether it changes strength or size outcomes, and recommend trying both. No citation is offered in either direction — which is the honest position when no outcome data exists.
- **Mind–muscle connection.** They allow it may help technique by changing positioning and range of motion, then state flatly that muscles are active regardless of conscious effort to squeeze them. This is **noticeably more conservative than the internal-focus claims elsewhere in this library's YouTube sources**, and worth remembering as a point of disagreement between sources.

A third passage is worth flagging for a different reason. Under "Get your Form and Technique Right" they write that as long as an exercise is performed repeatedly with an efficient movement pattern, "your unique technique will work just fine", and advise against limiting yourself to one movement pattern. **This is Barbell Medicine's modern position on technique** — and it directly contradicts the strong form-determines-adaptation argument in their own 2017 article *The Grip Problem*. The catalogue has changed its mind, and the two articles sit in the library unreconciled.

## Anatomy section: use with care

The anatomy tour is written for a lay reader and drifts. The trapezius does not span "from the base of the neck to the waist" (it ends around T12); the rhomboids do not run "down to the mid-waist" (roughly C7–T5); "without the teres minor muscle, our arms would pop right out of our shoulders" is not a real consequence.

More consequentially for a citation library: the claim that the trapezius "has the most extensive origin than any other muscle in our bodies" is cited to **Lindman 1990, a study of fibre-type composition of the human male trapezius by enzyme histochemistry** — a paper about fibre types, not about origin extent. That is a **mis-attribution**, and it survived the offset check because the numbering itself is intact. Reference [9] (McPherron, on increasing muscle mass to improve metabolism) appears in the list but is **never cited in the body text**.

## Commercial disclosure

Three "Find your Hypertrophy Program" blocks are embedded in the article body — after the intro, mid-list, and before the references — each linking to a routing funnel. The closing section is roughly 500 words of pitch for coaching, templates and community. The training content is free and does not require any of it.

## Relationship to the other chest articles

This is the third of a near-duplicate set alongside *Best Bodyweight Chest Workout Without Equipment* and *The 7 Best Chest Workout With Dumbbells*. **The shared material — anatomy tour, movement-category labels, RPE 7–9 prescriptions, "we recommend changing exercises every 6–10 weeks" — repeats across all three.** What is unique here is the agonist–antagonist superset evidence; what is unique in the dumbbell article is the sEMG caveat and the admission that common set and proximity-to-failure recommendations are invented; what is unique in the bodyweight article is the only actual hypertrophy trial data (push-ups vs bench press). Reading all three adds little beyond reading those three parts.
