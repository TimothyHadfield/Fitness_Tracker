# The Food Environment Was Built to Beat You, and Mostly It Does

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How the Modern Food Environment Makes Overeating the Default" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-the-modern-food-environment-makes-overeating-the-default/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2026-07-28 · 4,634 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The argument: US adult obesity went from ~15% in the late 1970s to >40% today ([NCHS 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/hestat/hestat111.htm)) without any change in the genome or the population's character, so **the explanatory variable is the environment**, not willpower.
- Three changes did the work — **bigger portions, more restaurant and delivery meals, more ultra-processed food** — and each raises intake without a decision being made. Pooled experiments: **doubling the portion in front of someone raises intake ~35%** ([Zlatevska 2014](https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.12.0303)), and the Cochrane review agrees ([Hollands 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD011045.pub2)). Average US daily intake rose ~**570 kcal/person between 1977 and 2006** ([Duffey & Popkin 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1001050)).
- The load-bearing experiment is **[Hall 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2019.05.008)**: 20 adults, inpatient, crossover, two weeks ultra-processed vs two weeks unprocessed, menus matched for calories offered plus protein, carbohydrate, fat, sugar, salt and fibre, ad libitum. People ate **~500 kcal/day more** and gained weight on the ultra-processed arm. **n=20 and four weeks total** — enormous effect, small trial.
- **Their strongest and most useful move is against their own side**: they say the observational harm data is graded low to very-low certainty, that NOVA is a blunt instrument (whey isolate and an Oreo land in the same bin), and that seed oils and food dyes are a distraction. The screen they propose is not "processed or not" but **does this food defend or defeat your fullness**.
- Practical prescription is environmental, not motivational: decide once at the shop, stock for satiety, cook more, add friction to the snacks, eat without a screen — and treat persistent appetite dysregulation as **a medical problem with medical tools** (GLP-1s, CBT, bariatric surgery).
- Ends by selling Barbell Medicine nutrition coaching.

## The premise, and how well it is supported

The framing question — did Americans lose their discipline in 1980? — is rhetorical, and the answer is well evidenced at the population level. The mechanism claim underneath it is stronger than the citation carrying it: **eating is mostly automatic**, triggered by the sight of food, the clock, container size and the people nearby rather than by hunger or deliberation. That rests on [Cohen & Farley 2008](https://www.cdc.gov/pcd/issues/2008/jan/07_0046.htm), a conceptual public-health paper, not on an experimental demonstration. The related line — "you can no more decide to be less hungry than you can decide to be less thirsty" — is **rhetoric outrunning the cited work**, though it is consistent with the appetite-regulation literature they draw on elsewhere.

The portion-size evidence is the sturdiest part. Young & Nestle documented marketplace portions against federal serving standards: **a typical cookie exceeds the standard by 700%, cooked pasta by 480%, a steak by 224%**, with burgers and sodas grown two- to fivefold since the 1970s. Zlatevska's meta-analysis gives the ~35% intake effect of doubling a portion. Rolls' chip-bag experiment is the mechanistic punchline: bigger bag, more chips eaten, and then **the same dinner three hours later** — no compensation. The Cochrane review confirms the direction while noting the evidence is about near-term consumption, which the article states.

On where food comes from: calories from food prepared outside the home have roughly doubled, more than six in ten adults eat restaurant or fast food on a given day, and each additional restaurant meal per week tracks with higher BMI ([Bhutani 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0890117116660772)) — that last is an **association in a Midwest cohort**, presented as such. Delivery is a ~$30bn industry with about two-thirds of Americans on a platform ([Stephens 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2020.00014)).

## Why ultra-processed food specifically

The mechanistic account is the **food matrix** ([Aguilera 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2018.1502743)): a whole food's fibre, water, structure and phytochemicals act together to slow eating and trigger stretch and hormonal fullness signals, and pulling one component out and dosing it alone loses most of the effect. Processing breaks the concert — refine the carbohydrate, strip the fibre, soften the texture, tune salt/sugar/fat to Moskowitz's "bliss point". Adding fibre or vitamins back does not rebuild it.

Then Hall's trial, which is presented well. The article is careful about what was matched: **calories *offered*, not calories eaten**, plus the macro and fibre profile, with subjects free to eat as much as they wanted. Because the paper menus matched, whatever moved intake was the processing. Two caveats the article does not raise: the ultra-processed menu was **more energy dense** in practice, and subsequent analyses attribute much of the effect to **energy density and eating rate** specifically. The article gestures at the second ("how fast the food could be eaten and how weakly it signaled fullness") but treats "the processing itself" as the variable, which is slightly broader than the data licenses.

## The tobacco history, and the addiction question

The industrial-history section — R.J. Reynolds buying Nabisco in 1985, Philip Morris buying General Foods in 1985 and Kraft in 1988, the 1999 Pillsbury meeting where Michael Mudd warned eleven food executives they were headed for tobacco's fate and General Mills' Stephen Sanger replied "Don't talk to me about nutrition. Talk to me about taste" — is sourced **entirely to one piece of journalism**, Michael Moss's 2013 *NYT Magazine* article. It is good journalism and the article names it, but four separate factual claims rest on a single non-peer-reviewed source. The checkout-lane audit is independent and cited properly: ~70% of food and drink at California store checkouts was unhealthy, rising to ~89% among snack-sized packages ([Falbe 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cdnut.2023.100075)).

On addiction, Feigenbaum lays out both camps and **states his own position rather than hiding it**. The Yale Food Addiction Scale puts roughly 14% of adults and 12% of children over the clinical bar ([Gearhardt 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075354)). One camp reads this as a drug-like hook; the other notes there is no nicotine-equivalent molecule and that much of the picture is the normal homeostatic response to restriction and weight loss. **He sides with the second** — the mechanism is an environment that overrides stop signals, not a chemical hook — while conceding the label may matter legally even if not mechanistically.

## Where they argue against their own conclusion

This is the section that makes the article worth keeping. The BMJ umbrella review pooling nearly ten million people reports **~50% higher cardiovascular mortality, ~55% higher odds of obesity, 12% higher type 2 diabetes risk per 10% increase in intake, ~22% higher depression risk** ([Lane 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310)) — and the article immediately says these are observational associations graded **low to very-low certainty** by the review itself, and that the most likely causal path is simply that these foods make you eat more.

They then dismantle the villain-hunting: NOVA sorts by processing rather than nutrients, which is "a bit like saying a bicycle and a 747 are the same because both contain metal". Seed oils get the opposite of the popular verdict — the largest pooled biomarker analysis links dietary omega-6 to **lower** cardiovascular risk ([Marklund 2019](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.038908)). **The Red Dye 40 claim in the same sentence carries no citation of its own.**

They apply the same scepticism to the lawsuit they opened with, noting Oklahoma's supreme court overturned the J&J opioid public-nuisance verdict in 2021, that the earlier fast-food suits failed, and that a federal court dismissed *Martinez v. Kraft Heinz* in June 2026 on causation grounds. **The evidence-versus-advocacy line is drawn clearly**, which is not the norm for this topic.

## The advice, and what it rests on

The recommendations are environmental design, and they are **clinical judgement rather than trial results** — no citation is offered for any of them, which is fine as long as it is understood:

- Decide once at the shop rather than every evening in the kitchen.
- Stock for satiety per calorie. Their packaged-food screen — **≥30% of calories from protein, or ≥1 g fibre per 10 g carbohydrate** — is an invented heuristic, not a validated threshold. Protein target ~1.6 g/kg bodyweight, from their own protein article.
- Cook at home more; use friction deliberately (opaque containers, high cupboards, high-satiety food at eye level); eat without a screen.
- If appetite signalling still fails, that is a medical problem: **GLP-1 medications, CBT and metabolic bariatric surgery all act on the same appetite machinery**, and using them is not cheating.

**Commercial disclosure:** the article closes on Barbell Medicine's nutrition coaching. The advice itself costs nothing to follow.
