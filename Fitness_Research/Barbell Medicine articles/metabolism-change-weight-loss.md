# Appetite Rises About Three Times Harder Than Metabolism Falls

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Does YOUR Metabolism Change with Weight Loss?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/does-your-metabolism-change-with-weight-loss/)) · Austin Baraki · 2018-05-18 · 1,062 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Metabolic adaptation to weight loss is real, and Baraki puts it at roughly **30 kcal/day of reduced expenditure per kilogram lost** — lose 10 kg and you burn about 300 kcal/day less than a person who was always that weight.
- The far bigger and far less discussed feedback loop runs through **intake**: the same weight loss drives appetite up by roughly **100 kcal/day per kilogram lost**, over threefold the expenditure effect.
- That number comes from a clever design the article names in the text: Polidori and colleagues used **canagliflozin**, which makes the kidneys dump about **90 g of glucose per day in urine**, to create a covert, placebo-controlled calorie deficit and then back-calculated intake from the weight trajectory.
- **153 canagliflozin vs 89 placebo patients over 52 weeks.** Weight fell then plateaued at a new equilibrium about **3.5 kg** lower; placebo lost under 1 kg. The plateau is the finding — it means intake had spontaneously risen to cancel a deficit the subjects did not know they had.
- "Metabolic damage" and "starvation mode" as used online are rejected outright: adaptation is real, a broken metabolism is not.

## Why the design matters
Most diet studies cannot separate the two feedback loops, because asking people to eat less makes intake the intervention. Here the deficit is imposed by the kidney at a near-constant rate, independent of behaviour and invisible to the subject, so any drift in body weight over 52 weeks is attributable to what appetite did in response. The intake figure is still **modelled rather than measured** — a validated mathematical model applied to the weight curve, not food records — which is a genuine limitation and one the article does not flag.

Also worth holding: the cohort is **type 2 diabetes patients on a glucose-lowering drug**, not healthy dieters. The direction of the effect is very likely general; the exact 100 kcal/kg coefficient should be treated as approximate outside that population.

## What it changes about dieting
The mechanism explains the standard real-world shape of a diet: good early progress, then a plateau as appetite quietly closes the deficit, then slow regain as the elevated drive to eat persists. On this account the plateau is **not** a failure of willpower appearing from nowhere, and it is not primarily the metabolism slowing — it is a neurohormonally driven increase in intake that the dieter does not perceive as a change in behaviour.

Baraki also corrects a misattribution that circulates widely: most of the reduction in expenditure is **non-exercise activity thermogenesis** — spontaneous fidgeting, walking, general movement falling off — rather than a collapse of resting metabolic rate. He references this to "elsewhere" in their own material rather than to a paper.

The paper's own conclusion, which the article quotes approvingly, is bleak: long-term maintainers succeed through "heroic and vigilant efforts" against a feedback system that never stops pushing.

## The behaviours they recommend
Against that, the article offers the **National Weight Control Registry** — over 10,000 adults who lost at least 30 lb and kept it off a year or more — and lists what those people report doing: 98% modified food intake, 94% increased physical activity with 90% averaging about an hour a day, 78% eat breakfast daily, 75% weigh themselves weekly or more, 62% watch under 10 hours of TV a week.

**Flag this clearly.** The registry is a **self-selected observational cohort of successes**, with no comparison group of people who did the same things and failed. Eating breakfast did not cause maintenance; breakfast-eaters are simply over-represented among maintainers. The article presents these as behaviours that "can help those who are dieting be successful" and recommends practitioners use them — a reasonable clinical heuristic, but stated more causally than a registry can support, and it sits immediately after a section that was rigorous about study design.

No Barbell Medicine product is recommended in this article; it closes by pointing at their own podcast episode on the topic.
