# Appetite Fights Back Three Times Harder Than Metabolism Slows

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Does YOUR Metabolism Change with Weight Loss?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/does-your-metabolism-change-with-weight-loss/)) · Austin Baraki · 2018-05-18 · 1,062 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Yes, metabolism changes with weight loss — but the article's real point is that **the intake side of the feedback loop is roughly three times larger than the expenditure side**, and almost nobody talks about it.
- The much-discussed adaptive drop in energy expenditure is about **30 kcal/day per kg lost**. Lose 10 kg and you burn ~300 kcal/day less than an equally-sized person who never dieted.
- The compensatory *increase* in energy intake is about **100 kcal/day per kg lost** — an "over threefold greater effect", from Polidori et al. 2016.
- That asymmetry explains the standard weight-loss trajectory: progress, then plateau as spontaneous intake erases the deficit, then slow regain as appetite stays elevated.
- Baraki draws a firm line between this real, quantified adaptation and **"metabolic damage" or "starvation mode"**, which he says exist "only on social media and not in the scientific literature."
- Most of the expenditure drop is attributed to reduced **NEAT** — moving around less without noticing — rather than a fall in basal rate.

## Why the Polidori study is the interesting one

The methodological problem with studying appetite compensation is that you cannot blind someone to a diet. Polidori's team got around this with a drug.

**Canagliflozin** is an SGLT2 inhibitor: it blocks renal glucose reabsorption so that glucose is excreted in urine. At 300 mg/day it produces a near-constant loss of about **90 g of glucose per day** — roughly 360 kcal out of the body, invisibly, with no behavioural change required. That makes it a placebo-controllable energy-balance manipulation, which diet studies cannot be.

The design and numbers:
- **153 subjects on canagliflozin vs 89 on placebo**, all with type 2 diabetes, over **52 weeks**.
- Treated group's weight fell and then **stabilised at a new plateau about 3.5 kg lower**; placebo lost under 1 kg.
- The plateau is the finding. If ~360 kcal/day is still being urinated out and weight has stopped falling, intake must have risen to match. The authors' validated mathematical model put the steady-state intake increase at **~350 kcal/day**, with no significant change in the placebo arm.
- Divided by weight lost, that is **~100 kcal/day per kg** — versus ~30 kcal/day per kg for the expenditure side.

The intake increase is inferred from a model, not measured directly — self-reported intake is too unreliable for this — so the number depends on the model being right. Baraki does not flag this, and it is the study's main limitation. The population is also **diabetic adults on a glucose-lowering drug**, which is not obviously generalisable to a lean lifter cutting for a meet.

## The clinical reading

Baraki's framing is that this is a **neurohormonal feedback system integrated by the brain**, proportional to weight lost, not a moral failing or a broken metabolism. He quotes the paper's closing line approvingly: long-term maintainers succeed "by heroic and vigilant efforts to maintain behavior changes in the face of increased appetite along with persistent suppression of energy expenditure."

That is the honest version. It is also a fairly bleak one, so the article ends with behavioural data from the **National Weight Control Registry** — 10,000+ adults who kept off ≥30 lb for ≥1 year:

- 98% modified food intake in some way
- 94% increased physical activity; 90% exercise about **1 hour per day**
- 78% eat breakfast daily
- 75% weigh themselves at least weekly
- 62% watch under 10 hours of TV per week

**Worth flagging:** the Registry is a self-selected observational cohort of successful maintainers. These are correlates of success, not proven causes of it, and the breakfast item in particular has not held up in randomised trials. Baraki presents them as behaviours that "can help those who are dieting be successful" and recommends practitioners use them — a recommendation, not a finding, and the article does not mark the difference.

## Where this sits against the rest of the library

This is a useful counterweight to the common framing elsewhere that a stalled cut means the metabolism has slowed. The claim here is the opposite: the metabolic slowdown is real but modest and quantifiable, and the larger, less visible effect is that the dieter starts eating more without registering it. That reframes a plateau as a self-monitoring problem before it is a physiology problem.

Nothing is sold in this article; it links a podcast episode and Barbell Medicine's other nutrition writing.
