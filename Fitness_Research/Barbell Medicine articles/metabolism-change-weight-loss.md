# Appetite Fights Back Three Times Harder Than Metabolism Slows

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Does YOUR Metabolism Change with Weight Loss?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/does-your-metabolism-change-with-weight-loss/)) · Austin Baraki · 2018-05-18 · 1,062 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Yes, metabolism changes — and the size of the change is known.** Energy expenditure falls by roughly **30 kcal per day per kilogram lost**. Lose 10 kg and you burn about 300 kcal/day less than a person who was always that weight.
- **The article's actual point is that this is the smaller half of the problem.** The compensatory rise in *intake* is about **100 kcal per day per kilogram lost** — over threefold larger than the expenditure adaptation everyone worries about.
- **The evidence is one clever trial.** Polidori 2016 used canagliflozin, a diabetes drug that blocks renal glucose reabsorption, to remove a **constant ~90 g of glucose per day in the urine** — an energy deficit the subject cannot perceive, and therefore placebo-controllable in a way that diet studies are not.
- **Numbers:** 153 on canagliflozin versus 89 on placebo, **52 weeks**. Weight fell and then stabilised at a new plateau about **3.5 kg lower**; placebo lost under 1 kg. A validated mathematical model put the steady-state rise in energy intake at about **350 kcal/day** in the drug group and no significant change in placebo.
- **What it explains:** the standard shape of a real diet — early loss, then plateau as intake spontaneously rises to cancel the deficit, then gradual regain while appetite stays elevated.
- **"Metabolic damage" and "starvation mode" are dismissed outright** as things that exist on social media and not in the literature. The reduction in expenditure is real, and Baraki says it is mostly mediated by falling non-exercise activity thermogenesis — you fidget and move less without deciding to.

## Why the drug design matters

The chronic problem in energy-balance research is that you cannot blind someone to how much they are eating, and self-reported intake is unreliable. Canagliflozin sidesteps it: the deficit is created downstream, at a fixed rate, without the subject's knowledge, against a placebo. **The compensatory eating is therefore observed rather than instructed**, which is what makes the 100 kcal/kg figure interesting.

## What to hold lightly

**The 350 kcal/day is modelled, not measured.** It comes from a validated mathematical reconstruction of intake from body-weight trajectories, not from food records. That is a strength relative to self-report and still a layer of inference.

**The population is diabetic patients on a glucose-lowering drug**, not dieters. The compensation may or may not transfer at the same magnitude to voluntary caloric restriction in non-diabetic people; the article does not raise the question.

**The behavioural advice is the weakest section and is not labelled as such.** The National Weight Control Registry figures quoted — 98% modified intake, 94% increased activity with 90% exercising about an hour daily, 78% eat breakfast, 75% weigh weekly, 62% watch under 10 hours of TV — come from a **self-selected registry of over 10,000 people who already succeeded**, holding ≥30 lb of loss for ≥1 year. These are correlates of success in an unrepresentative sample. Recommending them to practitioners as a "bottoms-up approach" treats them as causes, which the design cannot establish. Eating breakfast is the clearest example.

**The dismissal is stated more absolutely than needed.** Adaptive thermogenesis somewhat beyond what body-composition change predicts is a live research question; "starvation mode" as popularly described is the thing that fails, not the whole phenomenon. The article's own 30 kcal/kg figure concedes the point.
