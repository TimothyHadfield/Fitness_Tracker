# Ten Chest Exercises, One Real Ranking Claim — and It Says They're All the Same

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "10 Best Chest Exercises for Muscle & Strength" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-chest-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2023-10-26 · 6,959 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- The "10 best" is **not a ranking**. It is a list of ten exercises the authors like, presented in no evidential order, and they say so in the second sentence: the best chest exercises for you depend on fitness level, preference and individual response.
- The article's **one substantive ranking claim is a null**, and it is the rare case in this library where a ranking rests on measured growth rather than activation: bench angle does not meaningfully change pec hypertrophy or pressing strength ([Chaves 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32922646/), 8 weeks), and push-ups match bench press for both ([Kikuchi & Nakazato 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesf.2017.06.003); [Kotarsky 2018](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002345)).
- **Every within-exercise "targets X more" claim in the article is activation data or pure biomechanical reasoning**, and to their credit they mostly label it as such. Decline hitting the lower pec harder is EMG ([Glass 1997](https://doi.org/10.1519/00124278-199708000-00006); [Lauver 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2015.1022605)). Dips and torso lean: no citation, explicitly "speculative". Cable crossover range of motion: reasoning only.
- **Internal contradiction worth catching.** Having cited two null EMG studies and [Vigotsky 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001249) — the paper arguing EMG cannot be read as hypertrophic potential — they then assert, uncited, that pec and anterior deltoid excitation rise as the bench angle steepens and lower-pec excitation rises as it declines. That is the claim their own citations just undercut.
- The **practical prescription is a load-and-effort rule, not an exercise rule**: any chest exercise through a relatively long range of motion, within 3–4 reps of failure, at roughly 3–20 reps, is likely to produce similar results.
- The benefits section is broad and mostly well-cited, but includes **one popular-science book used as a physiology source** ([Epstein, *The Sports Gene*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sports_Gene)) and, unusually honestly, an admission that there is no evidence in either direction on chest training and breast appearance.

## What the article actually claims about exercise choice

The ten movements are flat barbell bench, dumbbell bench, incline bench, dips, decline bench, dumbbell flyes, standing cable flyes, cable crossovers, machine chest press, and push-ups. Each gets equipment, instructions and a "pro tip". None gets a comparative effect size, because none exists.

The comparative section sits before the list, and its conclusion is: **"most chest exercises are likely to produce similar results provided they work the muscle through a relatively large range of motion, using a load that gets the individual within 3 to 4 repetitions of failure within ~3 to 20 repetitions."** That single sentence carries the whole article's practical content. It is a claim about load, proximity to failure and range of motion — not about exercise identity — and it is consistent with the rest of this library's position on rep ranges and effort.

They also open the comparison with an unusual concession: claims that different chest variations produce different results, in overall growth or regional growth, are "either fabricated or speculation based on mechanistic data." That is the same criticism this library has been levelling at exercise rankings built on EMG and moment arms, made here by the source itself.

## Which rankings are backed by growth, and which by activation

This is the question that matters, so here is each claim with its evidence type.

**Bench angle (flat vs incline vs decline) — backed by hypertrophy outcome data, and the answer is "no difference".** Two activation studies found nothing: [Saeterbakken 2017](https://doi.org/10.1515/hukin-2017-0047) reported no significant differences in pec major, anterior or posterior deltoid, or lat excitation at 6RM across the three angles; [Christian 2023](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004250) found no significant differences in pec major, anterior or lateral deltoid excitation across flat, incline and decline, with barbells or dumbbells. Where other studies do find EMG differences, the article invokes Vigotsky 2017 to say those differences do not predict size or strength outcomes. Then the outcome evidence: Chaves 2020 trained untrained young men for **8 weeks** on flat only, incline only, or both, and found no meaningful differences in pec major hypertrophy or pressing strength. Under the incline heading, the specific belief that incline emphasises upper pecs and shoulders is called "not really supported by evidence" and pinned to those four references together.

**Push-ups vs bench press — backed by hypertrophy outcome data.** [Kikuchi & Nakazato 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesf.2017.06.003) took 18 men with at least a year of training, split 9 and 9, and had one group bench 3 sets to failure twice a week at **40% of 1RM** while the other did kneeling push-ups on the same scheme. Both gained about **3 mm of muscle thickness at the triceps and the pecs**, with no significant difference between groups. Kotarsky 2018 ran 4 weeks of push-ups vs bench press three times a week and found similar gains in 1RM bench and chest hypertrophy. The related biomechanical number — a standard push-up loads about **64% of bodyweight**, a kneeling push-up about **49%** — comes from [Suprak 2011](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181bde2cf), which is measurement, not an outcome.

**Machine vs free weight — backed by outcome data.** [Hernández-Belmonte 2023](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003271) is cited for machine training producing strength and size gains, with the caveat that strength improvements are largest in the tested modality. The specificity caveat is the important half.

**Decline emphasising the lower (sternocostal) pec and using less anterior deltoid — activation data only.** Glass 1997 and Lauver 2016 are both EMG studies of bench angle. The article states the limit itself: it is unclear that this variation produces different muscle size outcomes.

**Dumbbell flyes as an isolation choice — mixed, and neither strand is a growth comparison.** The rationale that single-joint work lets you target a muscle with less systemic fatigue comes from [Gentil 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0627-5), a review of acute effects and long-term adaptations of single- vs multi-joint exercises. The muscle-targeting claim — flyes lean on pec major, biceps and anterior deltoid, and use relatively little triceps compared with the bench press — is [Solstad 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33239937/), an EMG comparison. The recommendation to include flyes when size is the goal is therefore **advice, not a finding**.

**Dips at different torso angles — no evidence at all.** The claim that a vertical torso targets shoulders and triceps while a forward lean uses more chest is called "mostly speculative" by the authors, who then recommend trying both. That is the honest handling.

**Cable crossovers and the extended range of motion — reasoning only.** The suggestion that letting the hands cross increases serratus anterior and pec major training through greater humeral adduction and scapular protraction is anatomical inference with no citation.

**The uncited generalisation.** After all of the above, the article states that pec major (including its clavicular portion) and anterior deltoid excitation generally rise as the bench angle becomes more vertical, and middle and lower pec excitation rises as it declines. No reference is attached, it is activation language, and it sits against the two null EMG studies cited a paragraph earlier. **This is the article contradicting itself**, and it is the sentence a reader is most likely to carry away as a regional-targeting rule.

Net position for the library: of the ten exercises, **not one is ranked above another on measured growth**. The only outcome comparisons available are angle-vs-angle (null), push-up-vs-bench (null) and machine-vs-free-weight (equivalent, with modality-specific strength transfer). Everything remaining is EMG or anatomy.

## The benefits case

The article uses [Schoenfeld 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports7070177) for how to count set volume when multi-joint exercises train several muscles, then makes the participation argument: most adults know lifting is good for them but **three in four adults worldwide do no resistance training at all** ([Morrow 2004](https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.2004.10609156); [Garcia-Hermoso 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2022-106189), a meta-analysis of 3.3 million participants across 32 countries), against guidelines that recommend muscle-strengthening work for nearly everyone ([Piercy 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.14854)).

**The metabolic-rate myth is debunked properly.** Skeletal muscle is roughly 40% of adult body mass and accounts for about 80% of resting metabolic rate ([Frontera & Ochala 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00223-014-9915-y); [Yu 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/2050-6511-14-53)) — but a kilogram of muscle burns about **13 kcal/day** against **4.5 kcal/day** for fat ([Wang 2011](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajhb.21137)), so ten kilograms of muscle, which takes years, adds about **85 kcal/day**. They conclude the body-composition benefits of lifting come from elsewhere.

**Muscle imbalance** gets the pain-science treatment, and this is where the source's position shows. Asymmetry is normal adaptation, not pathology: tennis players carry roughly a quarter-inch of extra bone growth in the racket-side forearm and an elbow about 1 cm wider (sourced to Epstein's *The Sports Gene*, **a trade book rather than a study**); non-athletes carry more bone in their writing arm ([Walters 1998](https://doi.org/10.1385/jcd:1:4:359)); elite weightlifters have markedly thicker cruciate ligaments on MRI than untrained age-matched controls ([Grzelak 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00264-012-1528-3)). Their conclusion — small asymmetries do not matter for the average lifter, and screening people before letting them exercise constructs problems — is stated as position, and they name the exceptions where it does matter: when imbalance is the rate-limiting step for performance, and after muscle injury or ACL reconstruction. That is a clinician drawing the line in the right place. Note that the supporting citations are **cross-sectional imaging and anthropometry, not evidence that asymmetry fails to predict injury**; the conclusion is broader than the evidence shown.

**Sport transfer** is handled with the specificity principle. Bench press strength correlates with punch velocity in professional boxers ([López-Laval 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003362)) and club head speed in golf ([Sorbie 2021](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003788)), while performance on the NFL Combine's 225-lb bench test does not correlate with on-field performance ([Kuzmits & Adams 2008](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e318185f09d)). These are correlational, and the article says so implicitly by framing them as "correlates".

**Respiratory** claims are modest and appropriately hedged: pulmonary function improved in sedentary male smokers who did upper-body resistance training including the chest press ([Singh 2011](https://doi.org/10.4103/0970-2113.83971)), and in COPD, resistance training reduced systemic inflammation markers and improved strength, endurance, functional capacity and quality of life ([Vonbank 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rmed.2011.11.005), an RCT comparing three training modalities).

**Breast support** is the most candid passage. Claims that chest training lifts the breasts, or that training shrinks them, both get the same verdict: no scientific evidence supports either side. What exists is [Coltman 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2018.09.226), showing breast size acts as a barrier to exercise participation for many women. They label their own guess — that outside significant body-composition change, exercise probably does little to breast size or shape — as "admittedly speculative". That labelling is what makes this source useful.

**What they sell.** The conclusion is two calls to action, for their training programmes and for one-to-one coaching. Neither is woven into the technical advice, and the article's practical recommendation (pick what you like, train it near failure through a long range of motion) does not require either.
