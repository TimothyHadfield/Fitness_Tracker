# When Time Is Short, Aim for the Minimum Dose — Not the Perfect Week

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How to Exercise When You Have No Time" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-to-exercise-when-you-have-no-time/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2025-03-06 · 4,035 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **Detraining is slower than lifters fear.** Two to three weeks off does not meaningfully change strength ([Gavanda 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32509134/), [Hortobágyi 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8371654/)); even two to three months off costs roughly **10% of strength** ([Encarnação 2022 systematic review](https://doi.org/10.3390/muscles1010001)). Aerobic fitness falls faster, and muscle size is poorly characterised.
- **A "minimum effective dose" is a real thing with real numbers.** One to two sessions a week is associated with lower all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease ([Hamer 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28705222/), [Khurshid JAMA 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37462704/)). A single set per week improves strength, though multiple sets are better ([Krieger 2009 meta-regression](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19661829/)).
- **For a powerlifter specifically:** 3–6 sets each of squat, bench and deadlift per week, 1–5 reps, above 80% 1RM, is the identified minimum for maintaining 1RM strength ([Androulakis-Korakakis 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34527944/)).
- **Rest days are not a physiological requirement.** Three consecutive training days per week matched spaced sessions ([Yang 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29967584/)); small studies of daily training still showed gains. Schedule around your life, not around a recovery rule.
- **Their framing shift:** count volume over a **1–2 month** window rather than per week, and autoregulate by effort (RPE) rather than by a fixed weight. That is a recommendation, not a tested intervention.

## The detraining evidence, and what it licenses

The article's opening move is to lower the stakes. Missing sessions is not bed rest — and bed rest is the genuinely damaging case, with non-uniform strength loss and atrophy across muscle groups ([Marusic 2021 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33703945/)). For someone still walking around and working, short layoffs are cheap.

The specific claims are worth separating by study type. The "two to three weeks is fine" claim rests on a three-week detraining study in **adolescent athletes** and a 1993 study in **power athletes** — both small, both in trained populations, neither a general-population result. The "~10% over two to three months" figure comes from a systematic review of detraining after resistance training. The aerobic-decay and muscle-size claims are supported by a 1991 retraining study in heavy-resistance-trained women ([Staron](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1827108/)) and a **detraining meta-analysis in older adults** ([Grgic 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36360927/)) — an older-adult meta-analysis is a reasonable source for "not well characterised" but a weak one for a young lifter's expectations. The direction of the evidence is right; the precision implied by "approximately 10%" is not warranted across populations.

## How little is enough

The guideline baseline they quote (2.5–5 h/week moderate or 1.25–2.5 h vigorous aerobic, plus 2+ resistance days) works out to roughly 3–7 hours weekly. The article's argument is that falling short of that is not the same as doing nothing.

Two supports carry the mortality claim, and both are **observational**: a population study of 108,011 British adults on "weekend warrior" activity patterns and mental disorder, and an accelerometer-derived JAMA cohort on weekend-warrior activity and incident cardiovascular disease. These are associations in cohorts, not trials — appropriate for the claim as stated ("can reduce the risk"), but the causal language sits slightly ahead of the design.

The single-set claim is better grounded: Krieger's meta-regression on single versus multiple sets, plus [Schoenfeld's dose-response meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/) on weekly volume and muscle mass. Both point the same way — one set does something, more sets do more. That is consistent with the volume-response material already in this library from the YouTube sources.

"Exercise snacks" — isolated one-minute bouts of vigorous activity scattered through the day — are supported by exactly two small studies, both explicitly labelled pilots ([Perkin 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31687210/); [Fyfe 2022 pilot RCT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35751032/)), both **in older adults**. The article is honest that this applies to untrained people rather than someone already in a weight room, but two pilot studies is a thin base for a named strategy.

The powerlifting minimum-dose number is the tightest claim in the piece, and it comes from a single dedicated review of minimum effective dose for 1RM strength.

## The practical toolkit

Most of the article is programming advice with no citation behind it, and it should be read as coaching rather than evidence:

- **Pair exercises by setup** — squat/press, deadlift/row, RDL/floor press — so the second movement is lighter and needs fewer warm-up sets.
- **Time caps**: 20 minutes on the main lift, 10–15 on a compound supplemental, 10 minutes of as-many-sets-as-possible on isolation work. The cap does double duty as a fatigue governor on bad days.
- **EMOM / E*x*MOM**: E3MOM for 4–6 reps at RPE 6–7 on compounds; E2MOM for 6–10 reps on isolation work.
- **Compressed warm-ups**: rest only as long as it takes to change plates, fewer reps on later warm-ups.
- **Supersets**: rest 2–4 minutes after each pair.

**One uncited claim deserves flagging.** The article states that agonist-antagonist and alternate-peripheral supersets produce strength and hypertrophy comparable to traditional training, while agonist-agonist supersets produce less — and attributes the difference to preserved versus reduced volume. That is a specific empirical claim with no reference attached anywhere in the piece. It is plausible and mechanistically coherent, but the reader cannot check it.

## Rest periods and rest days

For hypertrophy work they land on **at least 2 minutes**, with diminishing returns past 3 — 5-minute rests do not beat 3-minute rests. The two papers under that sentence are [Ahtiainen 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16095405/) (short vs long rest, trained men) and [Senna 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21993029/) (rest interval length and perceived exertion). Both are small acute/short-term studies; the practical recommendation for time-crunched lifters — 2–3 minutes — is advice, not a finding.

On frequency, the honest line is "there is little data to suggest an optimal rest day frequency." Yang 2018 found consecutive-day training matched spaced training. The daily-training support is a **case series** of powerlifters and weightlifters doing daily 1RMs (Zourdos 2016) and [Dankel 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27875635/) on 21 consecutive days of strength-test familiarisation. Neither is a design that can establish "training every day is fine"; the article's own hedging ("some smaller studies") is appropriate, and the conclusion they draw — schedule by convenience — is the right size for the evidence.
