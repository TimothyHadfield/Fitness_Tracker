# Hypertrophy as a Managed Process, Not a Single Variable — Argued Entirely Without Citations

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "What Drives Hypertrophy? How Muscle Growth Works" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/what-drives-hypertrophy/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2023-03-18 · 1,488 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **This article cites nothing.** No numbered references, no inline links to research, no named studies. Every claim is stated on the brand's authority. That is unusual for this source and it should change how much weight the note carries.
- The argument is a demotion argument: hypertrophy is the product of **mechanical tension, volume, effort, fatigue management and consistency interacting**, and no single variable — least of all exercise choice — is the lever.
- **Exercise selection is explicitly named as "not the primary driver."** That lands on the same side as this library's adjudicated position, though by reasoning rather than by evidence.
- Their volume claim is the conventional one: more work produces more growth **up to the point it can no longer be recovered from**. No number is given for that point, and no study is offered for its existence.
- On effort: sets must be challenging, but **not every set needs to go to failure** — leaving a few reps in reserve is presented as the better trade against fatigue.
- The article ends by selling **Barbell Medicine's Hypertrophy I template**, which it presents as the way to manage these variables for you.

## What they say drives growth

Hypertrophy is defined at the tissue level as muscle protein synthesis exceeding breakdown, accumulated over repeated exposures rather than produced by any one session. Training is then framed as a stimulus that generates fitness and fatigue simultaneously, with the useful stimulus being whatever is well matched to the individual.

**Mechanical tension** is called a necessary component, achievable across a wide range of loads and rep schemes so long as sets are sufficiently challenging. Range of motion is folded in here: a larger range exposes the muscle to tension over more of its length, but they say differences between *similar* ranges are usually small. **Volume** is bounded by recovery. **Effort** is bounded the same way. **Consistency** is treated as the variable that makes the others possible, which is why they say scheduling and exercise choice matter at all.

## Where this sits against the rest of the library

Two of its claims are load-bearing for this library and worth naming precisely.

**Mechanical tension is the driver** — this library's adjudicated position, and Barbell Medicine agrees with it. But they never separate tension from *time* under tension, a distinction the library treats as decisive elsewhere, and they attach no evidence to the claim at all.

**Exercise selection is not the driver.** This library's finding is stronger and better supported: almost every exercise ranking it contains rests on EMG, moment arms or coaching feel rather than measured growth, and only about a dozen exercise-choice claims survive on hypertrophy data. Barbell Medicine reaches a compatible conclusion without doing any of that work — they simply assert that many movements can fill the same role.

**Advice, not evidence.** The recommendations to leave reps in reserve, keep exercises stable and cap volume at what you recover from are all reasonable clinical advice. None of them is presented with a study, an effect size or a subject count. Read this as a well-organised statement of the authors' position, not as a review.
