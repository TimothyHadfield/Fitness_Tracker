# How to Squat: A Technique Guide That Says Technique Is Mostly Preference

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How-To Squat Correctly: Technique, Benefits & Muscles Worked" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-to-squat/)) · Claire Zai · 2024-03-08 · 4,881 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- A long technique tutorial with **11 references**, only **six of which sit on a specific sentence**. Almost none of the form advice is cited. The article is honest about this in places — "there isn't one perfect squat", "find what works for you" — but the reader should notice the split.
- The cited claims are about **muscles worked, range of motion, and the health benefits of resistance training in general**, not about squat form. The form advice is coached preference presented as instruction.
- Their two most contested claims — that knees travelling past the toes is not a knee-injury risk, and that "a specific squat form is not correlated with injury or pain" — are stated **without a single citation** in an article that cites 11 papers elsewhere.
- On depth they are unusually careful: they recommend below parallel, then immediately concede there is "likely little difference from a health or hypertrophy perspective" between just-above and well-below parallel, and that the real argument for a fixed depth is **consistency of measurement**, not optimality.
- The only ROM citation ([Schoenfeld & Grgic 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/2050312120901559), a systematic review) supports "longer ROM generally correlates with more growth" — a general finding, not a squat-depth finding.

## The knees-past-toes claim, and how far it actually goes

The article's line: many people claim forward knee travel raises injury risk by "loading the knee joint", but "there is no evidence that performing squats this way increases the risk of knee injury or knee pain compared to other squat styles."

That is an absence-of-evidence claim, and as stated it is probably true — there is no prospective trial randomising lifters to knee-forward versus knee-back squats and counting injuries. But the article then converts it into a positive claim, that humans are "highly adaptable to training provided the dose is appropriate". **That second sentence is their clinical position, not a finding**, and it is doing most of the persuasive work. The same applies to "while a specific squat form is not correlated with injury or pain" in the troubleshooting section — a strong, unqualified, uncited assertion. It is consistent with the broader physiotherapy literature on movement quality and injury, but the article does not cite that literature, so a reader taking this note as evidence would be borrowing credibility from the eleven references attached to other claims.

**Flagged:** this is the pain-science overreach the library keeps seeing from this source. "Form predicts injury poorly in the studies we have" and "form is not correlated with injury" are different claims, and only the first is defensible from what is cited here — which is nothing.

## Depth: the honest section

They recommend aiming below parallel (hip crease below the top of the knee), then dismantle most of the usual reasons for it:

- No unique health or hypertrophy benefit to squatting *well* below parallel.
- Likely little difference between parallel, just-above, and just-below for health or hypertrophy.
- Strength gains are specific to the depth trained (their SAID-principle argument) — training below parallel improves the below-parallel squat, which matters for powerlifting and less elsewhere.
- The real case for picking a depth is that if you add weight while quietly reducing ROM, you cannot tell whether you got stronger or just changed the movement.
- "Squatting above parallel is certainly better than not squatting at all."

Their five listed causes of shallow squats — awareness, an over-vertical torso, wrong stance width, knees caving, and simple lack of strength — are diagnostic coaching, not evidence, but the last one is a useful clinical framing: if you can bodyweight-squat below parallel but not with a bar, the problem is load, not mobility.

## Two citation problems worth recording

1. The sentence "strength training has been shown to improve power, endurance, and speed as well" is marked `[4.5]` — a typo for [4,5]. Reference 4 (Andersen 2022) is a meta-analysis comparing **variable versus traditional resistance training** for strength and power; reference 5 (Balabinis 2003) is a small **concurrent endurance-and-strength** training study. Neither is a clean source for that sentence.
2. "Regular strength training has been **proven** to reduce the risks or symptoms of disorders like depression and anxiety." The Gordon meta-analyses measure **symptom reduction in RCTs**, not reduction in risk of developing the disorders. "Proven" and "risks" both overshoot; the caveat they add — that effect size is not tied to strength gained — is accurate and is the more interesting finding.
