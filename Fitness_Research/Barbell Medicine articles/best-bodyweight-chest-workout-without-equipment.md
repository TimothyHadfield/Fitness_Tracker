# Ten Push-Up Variations, One Real Claim: Push-Ups Can Replace the Bench Press

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Best Bodyweight Chest Workout Without Equipment (At Home)" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/10-best-chest-workout-without-equipment/)) · Barbell Medicine (unattributed staff) · 2023-11-28 · 4,656 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **The only claim in this article with hypertrophy outcome data behind it is the framing claim** — that push-ups build the chest about as well as the bench press. Two small training studies support it: [Kikuchi & Nakazato 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesf.2017.06.003) (18 trained young men, ≥1 year experience, low-load bench vs push-up, no significant between-group difference in hypertrophy or strength) and [Kotarsky 2018](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002345) (4 weeks, 3×/week, progressive calisthenic push-ups vs bench press, similar gains in bench 1RM and muscle thickness).
- **The ranking of the ten variations is not evidence-based and the article does not claim it is.** No citation is attached to the order, to the "primary / secondary / tertiary" movement labels, or to the rep and RPE prescriptions. These are coaching judgement.
- Where individual variations are justified at all, the justification is **activation data, not growth data** — surface EMG for wide-hand placement ([Kim 2016](https://doi.org/10.1589/jpts.28.446)) and a scoping review of shoulder EMG across push-up variants ([Kowalski 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/17585732211019373)).
- The one genuinely useful hard number: a standard push-up moves **~64% of bodyweight**, a kneeling push-up **~49%** ([Suprak 2011](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181bde2cf)). That is what makes push-ups programmable rather than just "do more reps".
- **The health-benefits half of the article is borrowed from elsewhere.** Its citations are cardiac-rehabilitation and osteoarthritis reviews that have nothing to do with push-ups, and one section heading ("Improved Bone Density") is supported entirely by osteoarthritis papers.
- The article closes by selling Barbell Medicine's Beginner and Hypertrophy II templates, coaching and supplements.

## What the outcome evidence actually says

The substantive claim — you can train the chest at home and not obviously give anything up — is well made and reasonably supported for the population studied.

Kikuchi & Nakazato randomised 18 young men with at least a year of resistance training to low-load bench press or push-ups and found no significant difference in hypertrophy between groups; both improved. Kotarsky ran a **four-week** progressive push-up programme against bench press training three times weekly and found similar improvements in 1RM bench press strength and chest muscle thickness. Both studies are small, short and in young men; four weeks is barely enough for measurable hypertrophy to separate from swelling and measurement noise. The direction of the finding is consistent with the wider low-load literature — that load matters less than proximity to failure — but two studies of this size do not establish equivalence, they establish the absence of a detected difference.

Barbell Medicine states this correctly in the body text ("data suggests that push-ups produce comparable levels of muscle activation and offer similar strength gains as the bench press") and does not oversell it.

## Where the ranking comes from, and where it doesn't

Ten variations are listed in order with rep ranges and RPE targets. **None of that ordering is cited.** The reasoning offered is mechanical and, on its own terms, sensible: decline push-ups shift load toward the clavicular pec and front delt; incline reduces the fraction of bodyweight lifted; offset and one-arm variations progress toward a genuine unilateral push; diamond narrows the grip and shifts demand to triceps; deficit push-ups extend range of motion at the shoulder.

Two variation-specific claims carry citations, and both are **activation studies**:

- **Wide hand placement** loads the serratus anterior more than a narrower grip — Kim 2016, an sEMG study.
- **A general EMG survey** — Kowalski 2021, a scoping review, reporting high pec major and triceps excitation in unstable push-ups and peak serratus amplitude in push-up-plus and incline variants.

Plyometric push-ups get a **kinematic** citation ([Moore 2012](https://doi.org/10.70252/ILNL1678)) for the unremarkable point that they involve higher velocity and force per rep. The article then adds that athletes use them to boost "endurance, speed, and strength" — that is an uncited extension well past a four-variation kinematics paper.

The deficit push-up claim ("better for hypertrophy thanks to the increased range of motion") is uncited here, though a long-range-of-motion preference is defensible from the wider literature.

**The honest summary is that this is an experience-based exercise menu with a well-evidenced headline and no evidence at the level of individual exercise ranking.** That is normal for the genre; it is worth stating plainly because the article's framing invites the reader to assume otherwise.

## The health-benefits section does not belong to this article

Roughly a quarter of the piece is a general case for resistance training, and its citations are recycled from Barbell Medicine's health content rather than drawn from anything about push-ups:

- **"Better Heart Health"** cites two meta-analyses of resistance plus aerobic training in coronary artery disease and a 1996 study of strength training after myocardial infarction, then adds a claim about blood pressure and cholesterol supported by a resistance-training lipid study in type 2 diabetes and Naci's network meta-analysis of 391 RCTs comparing exercise with antihypertensive drugs. Naci's analysis is dominated by endurance and mixed exercise; using it to support "resistance training improves blood pressure" **overstates what that paper shows.**
- **"Improved Bone Density"** is the clearest error. The heading promises bone; the two citations under it are a systematic review of resistance-training dosing in knee osteoarthritis and a systematic review of resistance training in musculoskeletal rehabilitation. **Neither is about bone mineral density.** The accompanying claim that lifting earlier in life "slows down or even stops further progression of the disease" is stronger than the cited rehabilitation review supports.

None of this is fraudulent, but a reader auditing the reference list will find that the numbered markers in this section point at real papers making adjacent, not identical, claims.

## Commercial disclosure

The final section recommends Barbell Medicine's own Beginner Template and Hypertrophy II Template, one-on-one coaching, and their supplement line. Stated plainly so the reader can weigh it: the article's practical conclusion is "push-ups work", which does not require buying anything, but the closing funnel is a product page.

## Relationship to the other chest articles in this library

This is one of several near-identical Barbell Medicine listicles. It shares its evidence base and its structure with *The 7 Best Chest Workout With Dumbbells* and *5 Exercises For Chest and Back Workout Day*, and it is the **only one of the three that carries hypertrophy outcome data specific to its own exercises** (Kikuchi, Kotarsky). The dumbbell article contributes the useful methodological caveats about sEMG and proximity to failure; the chest-and-back article contributes the superset evidence. Read this one for the push-up-versus-bench question and skip the health section.
