# Twelve Fixes for the Texas Method, All of Them Coaching Judgement

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "12 Ways To Skin The Texas Method" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/12-ways-to-skin-the-texas-method/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2015-02-24 · 4,155 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **This article cites nothing.** No numbered markers, no reference list, no linked papers — the only two outbound links are to a Starting Strength wiki page and a JTS blog post. It is practical programming content from coaching experience, and should be read that way.
- The template it modifies: a three-day week of **Volume Day** (5×5 squat and press, 1×5 deadlift), **Light Day** (5×2 squat at 80% of volume day, press work, chins, back extensions) and **Intensity Day** (squat 5RM, bench/press 3RM, power cleans).
- The twelve fixes cluster into four real ideas: **shorten or split volume day**, **periodise the rep scheme when weekly load increases stall**, **move exercises to where fatigue is lowest**, and **rewrite the template around a different sport**.
- The single most transferable fix is #2 — when weekly linear progression on intensity day stops, **step the reps down over an eight-week wave and reset 10–15% lighter**, rather than grinding the same scheme.
- This is 2015 Feigenbaum, and the voice is much closer to Starting Strength orthodoxy than Barbell Medicine's later output — power cleans defended, 5×5 assumed, and a tone that would not survive their later editorial standards.

## The evidential status of this article

Worth being blunt, because the rest of this source is unusually well cited. There is **no reference list, no citation marker anywhere in the text, and no linked paper**. Three named claims appear without any way to check them:

- Power cleans "improve motor learning overall in my estimation based on experience and the literature" — self-marked as opinion plus unspecified literature.
- "Dr. Zourdos *et al*. have found that speed work can be done up to as high as 90% of a 1RM and optimal power output can be maintained for single-effort athletes" — named investigator, no citation.
- A pointer to a JTS article by Mike Tuchscherer arguing classical speed work is really a device for accumulating volume — a blog post, and presented as one.

So: everything below is programming practice. Feigenbaum is generally careful to say it, opening the piece by saying these are approaches "which I have used both personally and professionally", and using the word *theoretically* explicitly when the rationale runs ahead of the observation. Read this as a coach's toolkit, not evidence.

## The four ideas underneath the twelve fixes

**Reducing the cost of Volume Day (fixes 1, 3, 5, 7).** The recurring complaint about the Texas Method is that day one is long and brutal — 25 squat reps, 25 bench reps and a heavy set of five deadlifts, all heavy enough to compromise the rest of the week.

- **#1, split it.** He notes the common suggestion that 5×5 squats degrade the 5×5 bench, moving bench to its own day, but says he has "not experienced this with others often enough to validate it" — an explicit refusal to adopt a popular fix on insufficient personal evidence. His preferred version keeps the original frequency and volume across two volume days.
- **#3, move the deadlift.** Deadlifts land at the end of a long session when both fatigue and motivation are worst. His fix is to open Light Day with them instead. Volume unchanged; only the placement moves. This is the cleanest fatigue-management idea in the article.
- **#5, make Volume Day dynamic-effort.** For lifters whose recovery is compromised — he lists advancing age, being "less male", poor conditioning, poor diet and poor sleep — he converts volume day to EMOM work at roughly 70–80%: squat 12 sets of 2, bench 8 sets of 3, deadlift 15 singles every 30 seconds. He is careful to distinguish this from Westside speed work (typically 50–60% with bands and chains), and to say the mechanism is accumulating recoverable volume, not speed. Light day's squat then rises to 80–100% of the new, lighter volume day load.
- **#7, pyramid the volume sets.** Instead of five sets across, work to one top set 5 lb above last week's, with the other four sets at 90–100% of it. Same total volume, one weekly rep PR, progression sustained a while longer.

**Periodising when the linear progression dies (fix 2).** The most broadly useful item. His claim is that nobody sustains weekly load increases at a fixed rep scheme indefinitely, so wave the reps down over eight weeks: volume day 5×5 → 4×5 → 3×5 → 3×4, light day 5×2 → 3×3, intensity day 5RM → 4RM → 3RM → five singles across. Deadlifts follow the same rep prescription for a single set. Load increases go on intensity day: 2.5–5 lb on bench and press, 5–10 lb on squats. **After week eight, take 10–15% off and restart at week one**, with a bigger deload if recovery demands it. He is explicit this is for people who have already been running the Texas Method a while, not lifters straight out of a novice progression.

**Exercise substitution (fix 4).** Power cleans are the most-substituted exercise in Starting Strength-family programmes, and he defends them as a rare objective way to train rapid force development. He then lists the cases where dropping them is legitimate: someone over 45 with no athletic background and no interest, a history of Achilles injury, lacking a training bar and light bumpers, or having genuinely tried with coaching and failed. Replacements keep the pulling volume: paused, tempo, halting, snatch-grip, beltless or deficit deadlifts, 2–3 sets of five at a challenging but non-grinding weight, no belt.

**Rewriting the template for a different athlete (fixes 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12).** Six variants: powerlifter (bench twice weekly, press demoted to a developmental lift, with a five-week peaking block ending in openers and meet day), bodybuilder (higher-volume 8×5 squats and bench, AMRAP finishers, an arm superset and ab block), four-day split, conditioning placement, CrossFit, and Olympic weightlifting.

Two are worth pulling out. **Fix 10, conditioning placement** is the most portable: put conditioning between days one and two, or after day three — because light day needs little immediate recovery and post-intensity-day work functions as active recovery while preserving a full rest day. His military PFT variant is fully worked: chins and abs as 7-minute AMRAPs plus HIIT at 20 s on / 1 min 40 s off for seven rounds on Tuesday, a run and max-rep test on Saturday.

**Fix 8, the bodybuilding variant**, contains a claim that would not appear in their later work: that the most optimal hypertrophy programme is "heavily biased towards the big lifts instead of isolation work" for a 3×/week trainee wanting 95% of the natural hypertrophy available to them. **This is stated as fact with no support**, and the 95% figure is invented precision. Later Barbell Medicine material treats exercise selection for hypertrophy far more permissively.

## What has aged, and what has not

**Aged well:** the fatigue-placement logic (#3), the periodisation wave (#2), the conditioning placement (#10), and the general stance that a template is a starting point to be modified for the person in front of you rather than a prescription to be endured. The refusal to adopt the squat-interferes-with-bench fix on insufficient personal evidence (#1) is a small but genuine piece of intellectual discipline.

**Aged badly:** the tone. Two passages gatekeep who counts as a powerlifter and who counts as a competitive CrossFitter, at some length and with contempt ("finishing 1412th out of 10,000 people makes you a non-competitive recreational CrossFit participant"). The "being *less* male" phrasing for recovery capacity is a 2015 artefact. The blanket claim that "strength programming in most CrossFit gyms is inappropriate almost all of the time" is asserted, not shown. None of this affects the programming, but it marks the article as an early piece from before the source developed the editorial voice the rest of this library sees.

**Things they sell:** unusually, nothing. There is no template link, no product placement, no coaching pitch — only an author bio listing Feigenbaum's credentials and competition lifts (640 squat, 430 bench, 725 deadlift at 198 lb). The commercial layer that saturates their later articles is absent here, which is consistent with a 2015 publication date.
