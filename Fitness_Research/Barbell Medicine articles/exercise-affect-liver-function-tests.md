# AST and ALT Are Muscle Enzymes Too — Why a Lifter's "Liver Panel" Misleads Doctors

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How Does Exercise Affect Liver Function Tests?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-does-exercise-affect-liver-function-tests/)) · Austin Baraki · 2023-03-06 · 1,168 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **AST and ALT are not liver-exclusive.** Both are also present in skeletal muscle, red blood cells and pancreas, so muscle damage — ordinary training damage, not just rhabdomyolysis — raises them while the liver is entirely healthy.
- **Reference ranges are contested and lower than most labs report.** Baraki gives normal ALT as **29–33 U/L for men and 19–25 U/L for women**, attributed to the American College of Gastroenterology guideline [1].
- **The magnitude and timeline come from one small experiment.** Fifteen healthy men without liver disease, not habitual lifters, did a **single resistance session**. AST and ALT rose dramatically alongside creatine kinase and myoglobin, **peaked at 3–4 days**, and declined toward normal only by **7–12 days**, with large between-person variation [2].
- **The clinical failure mode is over-investigation.** Clinicians who do not know this may launch further blood tests, imaging and potentially **liver biopsy** for an abnormality caused by the workout [3]. That is the article's central point and it is a clinician making it about clinicians.
- **The discriminators they name are the useful part:** alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin and coagulation tests are largely unaffected by exercise, so abnormalities *there* need urgent evaluation and must not be dismissed as exercise-related.
- **The recommended test is procedural, not diagnostic:** repeat the panel after a temporary break from training, expect substantial decline within **7–10 days**, and pair it with creatine kinase as a more muscle-specific marker.

## The clinical reasoning

The article is deliberately built as a two-step. First, establish that a "hepatic function panel" is a panel of *shared* markers, not liver-specific ones — AST and ALT leak from any damaged cell that contains them, and skeletal muscle contains a great deal. Second, establish that the timeline of that leak is long enough that a training lifter is rarely outside it.

The second step is where the evidence sits, and it is thin by design: **n = 15, untrained men, a single session, no control group.** It is enough to show the effect exists and roughly how long it lasts. It is not enough to give a population-level probability that any given elevated ALT is muscular, and the article does not claim one.

**What stops this being a dismissal.** Baraki is careful that exercise is a *possible* explanation, not the default. He lists the causes that must stay live — fatty liver disease, alcohol, viral infection, autoimmune and genetic disease, cancer, and drug injury including workout supplements and anabolic steroids — and says persistent or rising values despite a training break should raise concern rather than reassurance. He also advises against checking these tests indiscriminately "just to know", which is a genuinely unfashionable position in a fitness audience.

**Advice versus evidence.** The 7–10 day retest window is clinical judgement extrapolated from the 7–12 day recovery observed in fifteen men. It is sensible; it is not a validated protocol, and the article does not pretend otherwise.

**Where to go next.** Barbell Medicine later published a much longer case-based treatment of the same problem, with the full panel-by-panel reasoning, the ALT/AST half-lives and the albumin-and-INR discriminators.
