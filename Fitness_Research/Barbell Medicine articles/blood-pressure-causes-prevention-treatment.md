# Blood Pressure: Lifting Does Not Cause Hypertension, and Four Quarter-Dose Pills Beat One Big One

## Bottom line
- **Lifting weights does not cause hypertension.** Holding your breath against a heavy load produces a large momentary pressure spike; the article's position is that this does not raise long-term *resting* blood pressure, and that the repeated transient stress is a useful cardiovascular stimulus that lowers resting pressure over time. This is the claim their audience most needs, and it is the one sentence in the article carrying **no citation at all**.
- **Exercise can match medication.** Combining strength and aerobic training lowers blood pressure more than either alone, and to a degree comparable with commonly used drugs — cited to Naci 2019, a network meta-analysis of **391 randomised controlled trials** comparing exercise and antihypertensive drugs on systolic pressure.
- **Low doses of several drugs beat a big dose of one.** Blood pressure drugs show steep diminishing returns: doubling a single drug from standard dose adds only about **1.5 mmHg** on top of the ~8.7 mmHg the standard dose delivers, while **four drugs at a quarter dose each produce about 22.5 mmHg** — with fewer side effects than high-dose monotherapy. (Chow 2021; Wang 2025.)
- **White-coat and masked hypertension both matter.** People with white-coat hypertension carry higher cardiovascular risk than the persistently normotensive (Cohen 2019) and are much more likely to develop sustained hypertension over the following decade (Mancia 2013). Masked hypertension — normal in clinic, high in daily life (Trudel 2019) — is the more dangerous one because it goes undiagnosed.
- **A new guideline recommendation worth knowing:** all patients with newly diagnosed high blood pressure should be screened for aldosterone disorders (Adler 2025). That is a change from prior practice, where screening was reserved for resistant or young-onset cases.

## What the numbers are

Resting pressure below 120/80 is normal; 120–129 systolic with diastolic under 80 is "elevated"; 130–139/80–89 is stage 1; above 140/90 is stage 2. Half of US adults are affected. Blood pressure is the leading contributor to premature death worldwide, accounting for 13% of global deaths annually. The dose-response between usual blood pressure and vascular mortality comes from Lewington 2002 — a meta-analysis of individual data on **one million adults across 61 prospective studies**, which is about as strong an observational base as epidemiology produces.

Most people with high blood pressure have no symptoms, which is the entire argument for screening. Baraki is explicit that blood pressure is "one of the few, regular medical screenings we recommend for all people" — notable from a group otherwise sceptical of routine testing.

## The clinical reasoning on lifting

This is the section the rest of the library should care about, and it is worth separating what is argued from what is cited.

The physiology is uncontested: a Valsalva against a heavy load produces a large, brief intrathoracic and arterial pressure spike. The inferential step — that these spikes do not translate into elevated *resting* pressure, and in fact act as a hormetic stimulus that lowers it — is asserted without a supporting reference in this article. The conclusion is well supported elsewhere (Barbell Medicine has a separate article on resistance training and hypertension), but as written, the reassurance a hypertensive lifter most wants rests on the author's clinical judgement rather than a paper on the page.

The practical upshot is unambiguous: **people who already have hypertension can and should still strength train.**

## The modifiable causes, with the numbers attached

**Body fat, especially abdominal.** Visceral fat releases hormones that increase sympathetic tone and vascular constriction while increasing renal salt and water retention. Sustained weight loss of **5–10% of body weight** significantly lowers blood pressure (Stevens 2001, the Trials of Hypertension Prevention phase II). The rough rule offered — one pound lost, roughly one point of pressure — is explicitly labelled a rule of thumb. Response varies, and some people need additional strategies to hold the reduction long term (Sjöström 2000; Laaksonen 2003). They prefer a **waist target to a weight target**: below 40 inches (101 cm) for a man of European descent, 31.5 inches (80 cm) for a woman of Asian descent, with other cut-offs by demographic in a linked article.

**Physical activity.** The 2018 US Physical Activity Guidelines (Piercy 2018): 150–300 minutes/week moderate aerobic *or* 75–150 vigorous, **plus** strength training of all major muscle groups on two or more days. Fewer than a quarter of US adults meet them.

**Diet.** Reducing sodium while increasing potassium generally improves health and lowers cardiovascular risk (Cook 2014; Neal 2021 — a salt-substitution trial with hard cardiovascular endpoints). USDA targets: under 2,300 mg sodium, 4,700 mg potassium per day. Baraki's honest aside is that milligram targets are useless for telling people what to eat, and the operative advice is to replace highly processed foods with vegetables, fruit, legumes, nuts, whole grains, dairy and lean protein. The DASH diet lowers blood pressure in people with and without hypertension and **independent of whether weight loss occurs** (Filippou 2020, a meta-analysis of RCTs).

**Sleep.** Roughly 7–7.5 hours. The specific clinical action is to screen everyone with high blood pressure for obstructive sleep apnoea using the **STOP-BANG** tool and refer for sleep testing if the score warrants — treating apnoea improves blood pressure and often reduces medication needs.

**Genetics.** About **30–50% of the variation** in blood pressure is explained by genes, and people at the genetic end of that distribution frequently cannot reach target with diet and exercise alone. The article is careful, and correct, to frame this as not a failure of effort.

**Drugs that raise it.** Stimulants, NSAIDs, oral contraceptives, anabolic steroids and EPO, and contaminated supplements.

## The medication section is the strongest part

Rather than the usual gesture at "talk to your doctor," this gives the actual reasoning. Preferred first-line agents: thiazides (hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone) and mineralocorticoid antagonists (spironolactone); calcium channel blockers (amlodipine, nifedipine); ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers. Beta blockers, loop diuretics, alpha blockers, hydralazine, clonidine and nitrates are second-line with specific niches.

The dose-response argument is the payoff. Blood pressure drugs deliver most of their effect at low dose; escalating the dose adds little effect and a lot of side-effect risk. The comparison table (Wang 2025):

| Regimen | Systolic reduction |
|---|---|
| One drug, standard dose | 8.7 mmHg |
| One drug, double standard dose | +1.5 mmHg |
| Two drugs, standard dose | 14.9 mmHg |
| Two drugs, double standard dose | +2.5 mmHg |
| Three drugs, quarter dose each | 11.2 mmHg |
| Four drugs, quarter dose each | 22.5 mmHg |

They do not oversell it: more pills is more complexity and more burden, particularly for someone already on several medications, and the trade-off is a real one for the prescriber and patient to make. The framing — "no one is giving out awards for going through life without having a prescription to fill" — is aimed squarely at the lifting audience's reflexive resistance to medication, and it is the right target.

**Athletes:** all diuretics are banned by anti-doping agencies, and beta blockers are banned in precision sports (they calm tremor and focus, and blunt maximum heart rate). A Therapeutic Use Exemption is required if either is genuinely needed.

## What they sell

**Nothing.** This article links only to their own free articles — waist measurement, how to measure blood pressure, the cholesterol series, the beginner prescription — and to public resources like the STOP-BANG tool and the dietary guidelines. There is no template pitch and no coaching pitch. For a Barbell Medicine article that is unusual and worth recording.

**Flagged:** two places where the writing outruns what is cited on the page. "Lifting weights does not cause hypertension" is stated as flat fact with no citation attached, in an article that otherwise links a source for nearly every empirical claim. And "people with white coat hypertension benefit from blood-pressure lowering treatments" is an inference: the meta-analysis cited alongside it (Cohen 2019) compares event rates across groups observationally rather than testing a treatment, so the benefit is reasoned, not demonstrated. Both conclusions are defensible; neither is established by the source sitting next to it.
