# Barbell Medicine — the whole source in one file

A synthesis of all 155 notes in this folder, organised by the decision you are making rather
than by the site's categories. Every claim links to the note it comes from, and every note links
to the papers behind it.

[README.md](README.md) is the index of individual notes.
[RESEARCH-CITATIONS.md](RESEARCH-CITATIONS.md) is the bibliography — 2,171 resolved papers.

## Why this source is here

Four other sources in this library cover hypertrophy. **None of them covers injury, pain or
medicine**, because none of them is qualified to. This one is written mostly by practising
physicians and physiotherapists, and 60 of these 155 notes are clinical: injury and rehab, pain
science, lab interpretation, cardiovascular risk, pregnancy, ageing, GLP-1 agonists.

For those topics this is not one view among several. It is the only view in the library, which
raises rather than lowers the bar on saying how well evidenced each claim is.

## The one thing to understand before reading anything else

**This source contains two completely different kinds of writing, and they are not equally
trustworthy.**

The **bylined clinician essays** — Feigenbaum, Baraki, Miles, Inglis, Zai and colleagues — are
careful. The lifting-belt article separates what is shown from what is assumed better than
anything else in this library. The cholesterol pieces quote studies' own limitations sections
back at people citing their abstracts. The sarcopenia article spends its longest section
explaining why the metric its own industry sells is unreliable.

The **unbylined "best X exercises" listicles** are search-optimised content. They contain
duplicated reference entries, a biceps claim cited to a study of horse anatomy, a bone-density
heading supported by osteoarthritis reviews, and technique instructions no coach would write.
They sit on the same domain, in the same visual template, with the same air of authority.

**Check the byline.** It is the single most useful filter for this source.

## The three questions to keep asking

**Is this evidence, or is it a recommendation?** These are clinicians, and clinical advice
routinely outruns its evidence for good reasons. But the two arrive in the same voice here. The
asymptomatic-imaging findings are strongly evidenced; the rehab dose attached to them — sets,
reps, tempo, the pain-rule thresholds — is almost entirely uncited expert opinion. Several
articles say so themselves. The notes mark the boundary in every section.

**Was growth measured, or activation?** Across all 40 exercise articles, **three claims** are
backed by measured hypertrophy: bench angle, the seated hamstring curl, and overhead triceps
extension. Everything else is EMG, moment arms or bare anatomy. What makes this notable rather
than ordinary is that they *know* — several articles cite Vigotsky on why EMG cannot be read as
recruitment and then rank exercises on EMG a few paragraphs later.

**Does the citation support the sentence?** The inline `[n]` markers make this checkable here in
a way it is not for the video sources, and checking it found a lot: a hypertrophy claim citing a
ketogenic-diet review, printed PubMed IDs resolving to a fruit-juice titration paper and to
polyolefin nanofibers, a one-page editorial cited for meta-analysis effect sizes, reference lists
whose printed author and title do not match the paper their own link resolves to. **Where an
article prints both a number and a link, the link has been right and the number wrong.**

## The short version

- **For an injury: learn the red flags, then stop chasing the diagnosis.** Past cauda equina
  symptoms, the concussion list, the Ottawa Ankle Rules, and a locked or effused knee, the label
  rarely changes what you do. Cut load far harder than feels reasonable, keep training everything
  else, and track the load at which pain appears rather than waiting for pain to vanish.
- **Structural findings on a scan are normal.** 43% of over-40s have knee osteoarthritis on
  imaging without symptoms; 30% have meniscus tears; 65% have SI joint degeneration. This is the
  best-evidenced argument in the source.
- **Pain is a poor readout of tissue damage** — an identical 48 °C stimulus rates 4 to 100 across
  people. That is established. Several of the stronger statements built on it are not.
- **Your abnormal blood test is probably muscle.** Creatinine, liver enzymes and CK all rise with
  training and muscle mass. But above **1.5 mg/dL creatinine** always investigate, and cystatin C
  settles it.
- **Don't screen yourself when you have no symptoms** — not vitamin D, testosterone, thyroid,
  cortisol, or whole-body scans. A physician position, argued from guidelines, by people who sell
  coaching.
- **Protein: 1.6–1.8 g/kg and stop optimising.** Their own articles give four different numbers
  and never reconcile them; the lowest is below what their own cited meta-analysis supports.
- **Equipment does less than claimed but isn't useless.** Belts raise intra-abdominal pressure
  and do not weaken the trunk; whether anyone actually lifts more in one has never been studied.
- **Most programming variables are levers on time, not growth.** Their effort prescription (RPE
  6–8 on compounds) sits below what the rest of this library concluded, and is asserted rather
  than cited.

## Injury and rehabilitation

No other source in this library covers injury or rehab, so this is not one view among several —
it is the only one. That raises the bar on separating what is demonstrated from what is a
clinician's practice pattern. These articles mix the two freely, and the split is not where you
would guess.

### The approach, before any diagnosis

Twenty-four articles across seven authors and six years say the same four things.

**Modify the load, don't stop.** Rest is rejected in every single condition here —
[tendinopathy](barbell-medicine-guide-tendinopathy.md),
[ankle sprain](manage-ankle-sprain.md), [heel pain](guide-heel-pain.md),
[tennis elbow](lateral-elbow-pain-lifters.md),
[sciatica](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md),
[spondylolysis](train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md),
[post-op knee](post-operative-knee-rehabilitation.md),
[concussion](concussion-guide-symptoms-treatment-recovery.md). The reasoning is
capacity: rest detrains the tissue, so you return to the same demand with less tolerance. It is
sound, and mostly uncited. The direct evidence is narrow — a Cochrane review of immobilisation
versus functional treatment in acute lateral ankle ligament injury (Kerkhoffs 2002), and Maetz
2023, a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs in midportion Achilles tendinopathy comparing
loading against passive modalities. Everywhere else it is inference from those two plus
detraining physiology.

**Pain is a poor readout of tissue damage.** Their foundational claim, and its citation base is
thinner than the confidence suggests: in
[recovering from injury](recovering-injury-embrace-process.md) it rests on two editorials
(Moseley & Butler 2015; Lewis & O'Sullivan 2018), and in the sciatica guide on two
conceptual/definitional papers (Moseley 2007; Cohen 2018). The empirical support is really the
imaging data below, not the pain-science literature.

**Imaging findings are common in people with no symptoms.** This is the best-evidenced thing in
the whole section — see the numbers two headings down.

**Symptoms, not force capacity, set the ceiling.** Their one portable invention: re-anchor RPE
from "could I do another rep?" to "*should* I?" It appears in the sciatica guide,
[rehab mistakes](biggest-mistakes-re-making-lifting-rehab.md), the tendinopathy guide and
[meniscus](second-opinion-meniscus-injury.md) — uncited in all four, and the most useful idea
here.

### I hurt — when do I see someone?

Read this first; the red flags are the part of this source that is neither optional nor
contested.

- **Cauda equina** — new bowel or bladder problems, or altered sensation in the groin or saddle
  area. Emergency surgery. Flagged in the sciatica guide,
  [sciatica exercises](simple-exercises-help-sciatica-pain.md) and the spondy article, before any
  reassurance.
- **Shoulder pain with chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness or left-arm radiation** — that is a
  cardiac screen, not a shoulder screen ([shoulder pain guide](barbell-medicine-guide-shoulder-pain.md)).
- **Concussion**: limb weakness, true vision loss or diplopia, asymmetric pupils, seizures, LOC
  over a minute, altered mental status, >2 vomits, amnesia beyond 30 minutes, escalating headache
  or agitation, neck tenderness — worse over 60 or on anticoagulants. Urgent care now.
- **Ankle**: the Ottawa Ankle Rules screen for fracture, not severity. Lay version: if you cannot
  bear weight, get evaluated.
- **Knee**: a clear mechanism plus swelling or inability to weight-bear; effusion obscuring the
  kneecap border; true locking or persistent loss of range.
- **Back or leg pain** with unexplained weight loss, fever or cancer history; progressively
  worsening radicular symptoms; inability to bear weight after trauma.

### Does this need a scan?

Almost always no, and here the evidence is real. Every study below is **cross-sectional
prevalence imaging in people without symptoms** — supporting one claim (a finding is weak
evidence it caused your pain), not the stronger one (findings never matter).

| Finding | Population | Prevalence without pain |
|---|---|---|
| Rotator cuff, labral tears, OA (Barreto 2019) | 123 people, **both** shoulders scanned, pain in one | near-equal both sides; full-thickness supraspinatus tears and glenohumeral OA ~10 points more common on the painful side |
| Knee OA on MRI (Culvenor 2019, SR+MA) | asymptomatic uninjured adults over 40 | up to **43%** |
| Meniscus tear (Horga 2020) | 230 knees, mean age 44, 3.0 T | **30%** |
| Meniscal change (Pappas 2016) | asymptomatic collegiate basketball players | **50% preseason → 62% postseason** |
| Meniscal abnormality (Beals 2016, SR) | ~300 asymptomatic athletes | ~one third |
| Spondylolysis | asymptomatic adolescents / adults | **3–7% / 11.5%** |
| SI joint degeneration (Eno 2015) | pain-free adults | **65.1%**, 30.5% substantial |

Two of these do more than prevalence. Barreto scanned both shoulders in the same person, which
controls for age, occupation and training history — a strong within-subject design, and the best
single study in the section. Pappas is longitudinal within a season: the same asymptomatic
athletes accumulated more meniscal findings without developing symptoms. That is a genuine
dissociation, not a snapshot.

The spine adds a temporal argument the others cannot: herniations mostly resorb, and **larger
herniations are more likely to resorb than smaller ones** (Chiu 2015; Zhong 2017), size does not
track symptom severity (Karppinen 2001), and symptoms resolve without the scan changing (Benson
2010, 7-year follow-up of conservatively treated massive prolapses). Beutler's 45-year follow-up
and Andrade's systematic review found no association between spondylolysis and back pain. Plantar
fascia thickening also occurs in painless feet and changes with running, so imaging cannot
confirm heel pain either.

**Where they overreach:** the shoulder guide rests 6,710 words on that one study, and its own
10-point gap cuts against the strong reading. [Hip pain part I](training-hip-pain-part-i.md)
asserts imaging "commonly reveals abnormalities in all athletes" with no citation at all. The
sciatica guide builds the identical argument across 15 numbered references with a verified
mapping — same conclusion, twentyfold the evidential base.

### Should I stop training? What the dose actually looks like

Almost none of the prescription is evidenced, and several articles say so outright. The spondy
article: "treating this diagnosis falls more in the realm of expert opinion than rigorous
scientific evidence." [Hip pain part II](training-hip-pain-part-ii.md) concedes there is **no
research on heavy slow resistance training for hip tendinopathy at all** — the protocol is
extrapolated from Achilles and patellar trials (Beyer 2015; Kongsgaard 2009, 2010). The rehab
mistakes article carries **zero citations across 3,639 words**.

The uncited prescriptions, in one place, so you can see them for what they are:

- **"Tolerable"** = mild-to-moderate discomfort that does not spike to debilitating levels
  during the session or in the following **24–48 hours** (shoulder and sciatica guides).
- **Pain thresholds**: ≤3–4/10 during heavy slow resistance (tendinopathy); ≤4/10 during and 24
  hours after, two breaches in a row → cut volume (elbow); symptoms rising no more than
  **2 points out of 10** (meniscus); pain-out-of-10 plus RPE summing to ≤10 (spondy).
- **Heavy slow resistance**: 3-0-3 tempo, RPE 8, 2–3×/week, at the *start* of the session, held
  **12–16 weeks** because tendons adapt slowly.
- **The load cut is bigger than you think**: a 300 lb squat for 5 becomes **12 reps at 95 lb**
  with a 3-second tempo. Taking 25 lb off the bar is not a load reduction.
- **Progression**: ≤10% load increase per session, double progression through 8–12 reps, and a
  de-escalation order for tempo — normalise the concentric first, the eccentric last.

Every number above is clinical default. The rehab mistakes article is admirably plain about it:
"there is nothing special about any of the sample starting programs provided here." The nearest
supporting evidence sits a step away — Eckard 2018, a systematic review of 57 studies
establishing the training-load/injury relationship, with session RPE best supported.

The evidenced treatment findings are mostly *negative*: injections, PRP, corticosteroid, dry
needling, kinesiotaping, ice, cupping, scraping and shockwave show at most small short-term
effects across heel pain, elbow, ankle and OA. Karanasios 2021 (SR+MA, **2,123 subjects, 30
trials**) found exercise beats passive treatment in lateral elbow tendinopathy — with effects the
authors call **small**, which the article never quotes.

### Surgery, and coming back from it

The strongest evidence in the section. [Osteoarthritis](osteoarthritis-symptoms-causes-treatment.md)
carries Skou 2015 (NEJM RCT) and its 2018 two-year follow-up: among patients judged eligible for
knee replacement, **75% in the non-surgical arm declined the operation at 12 months, 68% at 2
years**. Read precisely — that is deferral under good conservative care, not exercise beating
arthroplasty; in the trial surgery produced greater improvement with more serious adverse events,
and Baraki says so. For sciatica, Peul 2007 (NEJM RCT, **283 patients**, disabling sciatica of
6–12 weeks) plus Fernandez 2016 (SR+MA): surgery buys faster relief, similar long-term outcomes.
Arthroscopic partial meniscectomy is no better than conservative care long-term (Abram 2020,
SR+MA; Rotini 2023, SR of SRs), and the AAOS position statement against arthroscopic debridement
for knee OA has not changed practice.

After surgery the message inverts: the failure mode is **under-loading**. Post-ACLR return to
sport runs 81% any level, 65% pre-injury level, **55% competitive** (Ardern 2014, >7,500
athletes); 83% in elite athletes (Lai 2018); 23% reinjury under 25 (Wiggins 2016, SR+MA). Only
**13.9% of athletes met all return-to-sport criteria and 27.8% the strength criteria** (Toole
2017), and squat depth is impaired seven years post-op (Sanford 2016). All observational — they
show outcomes are often poor, not that better rehab causes better ones, and Miles hedges
accordingly. Grindem 2016 (Delaware-Oslo cohort) is the actionable one: each additional month
before return lowers reinjury odds. Vlok 2022, a scoping review of **117 exercises in 41
studies**, found the exercise descriptors that determine strength gains largely missing from
published ACLR programmes.

[When symmetry matters](symmetry-matters-look-instances-where-indexes-matter.md) is the most
rigorous article here. Limb symmetry below 85% predicts hamstring injury 4× in 100 professional
footballers (Fousekis 2011) and 2.4× in rugby, 3.4× below 80% (Bourne 2015) — prospective
cohorts, and Miles says plainly that **no cited study shows raising LSI lowers reinjury**. He
raises Goodhart's law against his own metric, notes LSI inflates when the uninvolved limb also
detrains (Wellsandt 2017), and labels his gym-floor substitute unvalidated. Meanwhile 56% of
physical therapists and 60% of sports-medicine physicians clear athletes on manual muscle testing
alone (Greenberg 2018, 2019) — an instrument bounded by the clinician's own strength, which
cannot resolve an 80% index.

### Where they outrun the evidence, and what they sell

The recurring error is stating **absence of demonstrated benefit as demonstrated absence**: "no
evidence that any modality speeds up the healing process" (ankle), "no physiological
justification" for passive modalities (tendinopathy), "none of these have demonstrated a benefit
beyond placebo" (heel), "no passive intervention outperforms the body's own healing process"
(post-op knee) — all uncited or over-read. [Core stability](core-stability-matter.md) rests
"absolutely no scientific evidence" on a single narrative critical analysis (Wirth 2017), which
is also the only source for the same claim in the sciatica articles. [Running and knee OA](running-knee-osteoarthritis.md)
calls recreational running "protective" from cross-sectional prevalence data while its own source
(Alentorn-Geli 2017) found 13.3% OA prevalence in competitive runners versus 3.5% in recreational
— a dose signal the headline erases. [Training calves](case-training-calves.md) repeats it
smaller: healthy runners made 200% bodyweight plantarflexor torque against ~130% in those with
unilateral Achilles tendinopathy, deficient on *both* limbs (O'Neill 2019) — cross-sectional, so
"strengthen calves to protect the Achilles" outruns the design. The concussion guide states
causally that longer rest worsens symptoms, from a largely paediatric literature. And several
citations are simply wrong: the arrhythmia claim in
[DOMS](t-straighten-arms-after-workout.md) links to a body-composition paper, "Stovall 2010"
links to a different review (hip part I), and the elbow corticosteroid sentence links to an
exercise-versus-passive meta-analysis.

They are unusually rigorous in four places: admitting no evidence exists for their own hip
protocol; volunteering that most ankle bracing trials compare bracing to *immobilisation*, so
they show bracing beats rest; labelling the knee-sleeve mechanism "reasonable hypotheses"
([knee sleeves](knee-sleeves.md)); and flagging Dye 1998 as **n = 1** while still using it.

**Commercial routing is near-universal.** Most of these 24 articles close by directing readers to
Barbell Medicine's paid Pain & Rehab consultations; the low back and knee rehab templates are
pitched in the articles working through exactly those conditions; the spondy article sells The
Bridge; the knee sleeve article recommends SBD and Rogue through referral links. The advice is
mainstream physiotherapy and is not bent to fit the product — but "skip the scan and self-manage"
points toward a coaching subscription rather than a clinician. Three sell nothing:
[power after injury](train-power-after-injury.md),
[youth resistance training](resistance-training-youth-part-i.md) and
[recovering from injury](recovering-injury-embrace-process.md). And it runs both ways: the
concussion guide attaches its creatine link to a paragraph saying creatine has not been shown to
help.

## Pain science, and how far it stretches

Four notes carry the source's intellectual core — [the SI joint note](si-joint-movement-cause-hip-low-back.md), [the crepitus note](crepitus-expectations-vs-reality.md), [the pain-in-training note](pain-training.md) and [the placebo and nocebo note](placebo-nocebo-effects-pain.md) — arguing one position: pain is a protective output shaped by biological, psychological and social inputs rather than a gauge of tissue damage, structural findings are common in people with no symptoms, and what a person is told about their body measurably changes what they feel. Mainstream in physiotherapy research, heterodox in a gym, well evidenced at the core. The problem is narrower: in four sentences it is asserted absolutely, and the absolute version is not what the papers show.

### The asymptomatic-findings argument, worked properly

**How much the SI joint moves.** Goode's 2008 systematic review of three-dimensional measurement studies pooled roughly **8° about the Z-axis, 4° Y, 2.2° X**. Two radiostereometric studies by Kibsgård — implanted tantalum markers, the reference standard for skeletal micromotion, in patients with **long-standing pelvic girdle and SI pain**, during the single-leg stance and straight-leg raise manoeuvres clinicians use as tests — found **under 1°**. Population and manoeuvre match the clinical claim — stronger than cadaver or healthy-volunteer data.

**Whether anyone can detect it.** Motion palpation of the SI joint gave **48% agreement and kappa −0.06** (Robinson 2007). Kappa corrects for chance: zero means no better than guessing, negative means worse. Not poor reliability — the absence of a signal. Palpating the PSIS, the largest landmark available, gave **kappa 0.27** (Cooperstein 2016, systematic review) — "fair", nowhere near the 0.6–0.8 demanded of a test that directs treatment; Nolet 2021 generalises it beyond the SI joint. Preece 2008 gives the anatomical reason from cadaveric pelvises: **up to 11° and 16 mm of side-to-side landmark asymmetry** in one specimen, more than the joint's entire measured range, so felt asymmetry cannot be read as displacement.

**Whether treatment moves it.** Tullberg 1998, roentgen stereophotogrammetry sensitive to **0.2 mm**, found **under 1 mm of translation and under 1°** after a manual technique. Nothing is being reset.

**And the prevalence figure it turns on.** Eno 2015 found SI joint degeneration in **65.1% of pain-free adults, 30.5% of it substantial** — the same shape as Brinjikji 2015 on asymptomatic spinal degeneration, Culvenor on knee OA at MRI, and Barreto 2019's bilateral shoulder scans. A finding present in two-thirds of people who feel fine cannot confidently be named the cause when someone hurts. Sound inference, and the best card here.

### What pain tracks instead

The direct evidence is a spread, not a correlation: 321 adults given an identical **48 °C** heat stimulus rated it from **4 to 100 out of 100** (Fillingim 2017). Crepitus makes the point structurally — McCoy found it in **99% of 250 normal knees**. Pazzinatto's observational samples of **584 and 361** people, matched on Kellgren-Lawrence severity to isolate crepitus itself, found slightly worse *self-reported* function and quality of life in the crepitus groups and **no difference in objective function or strength** (de Oliveira Silva's 165 women with patellofemoral pain versus 158 controls likewise: **OR 4.19**, no functional correlate). Perception and performance come apart. Beliefs, by contrast, track outcomes and are clinician-seeded — Setchell's survey found **89% of people with persistent low back pain had learned their negative beliefs from healthcare professionals**. The defensible summary is [the pain-in-training note](pain-training.md)'s: imaging findings and lifting posture predict symptoms poorly, fearful beliefs predict outcomes well.

### Placebo established, nocebo borrowed

These are not equally supported, and the source treats them as if they were. Placebo in pain is firm. FIDELITY (Sihvonen 2018) randomised degenerative meniscus tears to real surgery or a sham — anaesthetised, scoped, scarred, told they had been treated — and found no significant difference in pain or function at two years; both groups improved. Ashar 2024 gave **101** chronic low back pain patients a saline injection while **telling them it was a placebo**, and still found significant pain improvement at one month with functional neuroimaging. That open-label result matters most: it removes deception as a precondition, which is what makes the clinical advice possible at all. The source is disciplined about the boundary — placebo moves subjective outcomes, not blood lipids or HbA1c.

**A clinical error to record.** The article repeatedly calls FIDELITY's active arm "meniscus repair surgery". It was **arthroscopic partial meniscectomy** — removal of torn tissue, not repair. Different operation, different rationale, and the distinction matters to anyone reading it against their own knee. The counts given (144 total, 70 vs 74) do not match the published trial and should be checked before quoting.

Nocebo is weaker. The flagship claim — nocebo stronger and more persistent than placebo — rests on Kunkel 2025, whose title says plainly "in healthy individuals" and which used experimental pain. A legitimate paradigm, but not the same evidential object as chronic pain in patients, and the article crosses the gap without a hedge. Saunders 2024, a real systematic review and meta-analysis of social learning as a nocebo route, is summarised past readability. And the sham-surgery effect size — **17 studies, 535 patients**, over half clinically improved — is secondhand from Hartman 2009, a *brief narrative review* also carrying the pricing and treatment-intensity claims: the weakest load-bearing citation in the piece.

### The four sentences that outrun the evidence

**"The joints themselves essentially do not move."** Established: SI motion is small, unreliably detected, unchanged by manipulation. Overstated: the cited studies measured real, non-zero motion — *too small to palpate*, not absent. Cost: a claim any anatomist can falsify discredits the reliability evidence under it, the part that matters.

**The slide from "clinicians cannot reliably detect motion" to "this is not a pain source."** Different claims. The article's own Laslett material concedes that posterior pelvic pain is real, that provocation-test *clusters* have diagnostic value against anaesthetic block, and that the missing piece is a gold standard for localising it — not proof of innocence. Cost: someone with genuine SI-region pain reads a negative finding about *palpation* as a verdict on their symptoms.

**"All treatments for pain are mediated by placebo effects."** Asserted twice, once as a section heading, with **no citation** — the article's thesis and its least defended sentence, and unfalsifiable as stated. The defensible version: any treatment for a subjective symptom carries a placebo component of unknown size. Cost: it flattens the distinction the same article elsewhere gets right, between real physiological benefit plus placebo and placebo only — the one its own ethics checklist runs on.

**"Glutes being weak, 'asleep', or forgetful are not based in scientific plausibility"** — from [the hip pain note](training-hip-pain-part-i.md), same pattern. It rests on two small EMG studies (Dwyer 2013 in hip osteoarthritis, Marshall 2011 in low back pain during standing) finding *increased* gluteal activation in people with pain. Those undercut "pain switches the glutes off"; they do not make weakness implausible. The defensible version, from the same article: manual muscle testing and functional screens cannot detect what they claim to.

The pattern is consistent: a well-evidenced negative finding about *measurement* restated as a positive claim about *reality*. And no study cited across these four notes shows that reassurance changes outcomes — that protocol is clinical judgement on descriptive evidence, not a tested intervention.

## Health, labs and medicine

Material no other source here touches, written by practising physicians — and where the gap between *what the evidence shows* and *what we recommend* is widest. Several of these articles carry no citations at all.

### My blood test came back abnormal

The most useful cluster here: a lifter's out-of-range creatinine, transaminases and CK are routinely muscle-derived rather than disease.

**Creatinine.** [It is a muscle metabolite used as a kidney test](abnormal-labs-exercise-part-i-kidney-function.md) — the breakdown product of creatine in skeletal muscle — so a rise means either filtration fell or muscle mass rose, and CKD-EPI cannot tell which. The ceiling: even people who train will not exceed **1.5 mg/dL (133 µmol/L)** without impairment, and values above it **"should always be evaluated further"**, with muscularity permitted only after other causes are excluded. **1.4–1.5 against a 0.7–1.2 range** is the realistic muscularity case; below it, impairment is still possible. Two things discriminate: a **prior value** (0.8 → 1.4 warrants evaluation even if muscle was gained in between — trajectory beats threshold), and **cystatin C**, a filtration marker unaffected by muscle mass, so it stays normal beside a muscular lifter's creatinine-driven "abnormal" eGFR while real impairment raises both. *Evidence:* the physiology. *Recommendation:* the 1.5 threshold and the compare-to-a-prior sequence — conventional, and untested as a pathway.

**Liver enzymes, and a defect worth recording.** AST and ALT are not liver-exclusive; [both leak from skeletal muscle](exercise-affect-liver-function-tests.md), and the magnitude rests on one experiment — **Pettersson 2008, n=15 untrained men, one session, no control**. The [longer case-based article](elevated-liver-enzymes-after-exercise-means.md) adds the timeline that traps a regular lifter (still rising at 48 h, peak 4–7 days, baseline only at 10–12 days), the discriminators that hold (**albumin and INR are unaffected by exercise**; a normal INR effectively excludes acute liver failure), and a physician survey finding **56% did not list exercise-induced muscle damage in their differential**.

But it gives **three different escalation thresholds**: the body sets "substantially less plausible" at **>5× ULN**, the FAQ sets urgent evaluation at **>10×**, and the patient scripts invoke **<3×** as the expectant-observation range. In an article whose whole purpose is telling a reader whether to worry, that is a real defect — someone taking the FAQ number could sit on a 7× elevation. Its key takeaway also claims **100% of resistance trainees show elevation** on the strength of fifteen untrained men, and **its reference list is internally corrupt** — at least five entries' author and title point at a different paper than the PMID beside them. Its recommendation — rest a week, retest with CK and LDH, before imaging or referral — is sensible judgement with no trial behind it. The article links to their **paid Medical Consultations**.

**CK.** [Rhabdomyolysis is a clinical diagnosis, not a lab value](rhabdomyolysis-causes-treatments-return-sport.md). The classic triad appears in **fewer than 10% of cases**. **CK >5× ULN is sensitive but not specific** — below it you can be confident it is absent, above it you have learned little, because athletes routinely exceed it in normal training, and discharge is not decided on it either. The four-phase return protocol is explicitly labelled expert opinion.

### My cholesterol is high

The four-article lipid series is the best-cited work here. The organising claim is **cumulative exposure — "the lower, the longer, the better"** — which licenses their [rejection of the 10-year risk window for younger people](cholesterol-causes-prevention-treatment.md), a policy preference, not a guideline position. **ApoB is the target** (under 90); **HDL-C is demoted to a marker**, since trials raising it do not reduce events. Diet reduces to **fibre ≥30 g/day** and fat *type*, not fat total.

[Part 2](cholesterol-myths-misconceptions.md) is where the evidence is densest: LDL-C tracks plaque linearly inside the normal range (**1,779 people with no conventional risk factors; plaque 11% at LDL-C 60–70, 64% at 150–160**), and **1 mmol/L of lowering yields 22–24% fewer events regardless of how achieved**. Its weakness is asymmetric scrutiny — null saturated-fat studies get three pages of dissection, supporting ones none — and its **"18–20% vs <10% of calories" threshold** is an established breakpoint in neither cited source.

The [action plan](lower-heart-disease-risk-action-plan-cholesterol.md) carries the drugs: CTT's **170,000 participants across 26 trials, 25–30% fewer events per 38 mg/dL**, each statin doubling buying only ~6%, so **early combination beats dose escalation**. It rejects red yeast rice, citrus bergamot and OTC fish oil — notable from a company with a supplement line. But the sentence closing its case for aggressive therapy — **"a 1% reduction in plaque volume is associated with an 18% reduction in major cardiovascular events"** — has no source at all, and is a surrogate relationship doing an outcome's work. [Lp(a)](lipoprotein-levels-testing-treatment-heart-disease.md) is **>90% genetic, fixed by age 5, test once**; rule out below 30 mg/dL, rule in above 50; **no approved therapy lowers it and reduces events**, so management is compensatory. It calls Lp(a) "causal" flatly, then concedes the hypothesis is untested — and links twice to their **paid physician consultation**. [Statin muscle symptoms](statins-muscle-they-affect-strength-growth.md) run 1–5% above placebo in blinded trials against 10–20% in clinic; STOMP (**420 adults, six months, atorvastatin 80 mg**) found no strength difference — but that article also claims no difference in *hypertrophy*, which STOMP never measured, and seven of its references link to unrelated papers.

### My blood pressure is high

[Naci 2019 — a network meta-analysis of 391 RCTs, 39,742 subjects](effect-resistance-training-high-blood-pressure.md) — is the whole basis. In hypertensive subgroups exercise lowered SBP **8.96 mmHg** against **8.8** for the drug trials; resistance training alone **−7.83**; across all 197 exercise trials including normotensives, only **−4.84**. Two caveats travel with that headline. **8.96 vs 8.8 is not a difference**, and it is an **indirect comparison across separate populations** — the drug trials were larger, older and started ~18 mmHg higher; nobody has been randomised to exercise or a drug. And the outcomes differ in kind: the drug literature has stroke and mortality endpoints, the exercise trials measured **millimetres of mercury over weeks**. "Exercise slightly beats medication" is a surrogate comparison, not a substitution. The [longer guide](blood-pressure-causes-prevention-treatment.md) is unusually good on drugs — doubling one agent adds ~1.5 mmHg on top of 8.7, while **four drugs at quarter dose deliver ~22.5 mmHg** with fewer side effects. But "lifting weights does not cause hypertension", the sentence its audience most wants, is the one empirical claim in it with **no citation**. [Measurement technique](measure-blood-pressure-correctly.md) is the standard procedure done properly: cuff size is the biggest error you control, and a high reading without symptoms is a primary-care problem, not an emergency.

### I'm on a GLP-1

[The "40% muscle loss" headline is largely a measurement problem](glp-1-muscle-loss-ozempic-wegovy-mounjaro.md), and the argument is good: DXA "lean soft tissue" contains water, glycogen, organs and intramuscular fat, assumes a fixed 73% hydration that breaks during 15% weight loss, and moves 1–2% from glycogen or creatine manipulation alone. The STEP-1 **placebo** group also lost DXA lean mass despite minimal weight change. Where function was measured it held or improved (SEMALEAN: fat −18%, lean −5%, **grip +4.5 kg**).

Two cautions. **Their Table 1 is a model presented as measurement** — it decomposes a 3 kg lean-mass loss into liver glycogen, liver fat, intramuscular fat and **only 200–500 g of contractile muscle**, with a footnote conceding these are "bounded estimates" from compartment modelling, and a stated total (1.5–3.9 kg) that does not pin to the 3 kg it decomposes — the article's most quotable claim is its least measured one. And **SEMALEAN is a single-arm cohort with no control**, carrying most of the weight for "function improves." No pair-fed human trial exists, so "the drug is catabolic" is untested; the one pair-fed comparison is mostly mice. Their protein target, **1.2–1.6 g/kg**, is extrapolated from non-GLP-1 deficit literature and clashes with the ISSN stand, which treats 1.6–2.4 as the floor. On stopping, [three randomised withdrawal trials converge](gain-weight-back-after-ozempic-data-shows.md): roughly two-thirds regained within a year, SURMOUNT-4's placebo arm regaining ~14% of body weight. Their reframe is fair — lifestyle does not hold weight off better, it holds off less weight. Both articles route to **Coaching, the paid Beginner Template and Whey Rx**.

### Should I get my testosterone checked? (Mostly: no)

[The most valuable clinical content in the batch is a refusal](where-priorities-improve-health.md). The seven priorities are ordinary; what distinguishes the article is the flat instruction against **routine lab or imaging screening in asymptomatic people — naming vitamin D, testosterone, thyroid, cortisol, stress tests and whole-body scans** — plus no sleep trackers and no default keto or fasting. A physician position on the harms of over-testing, argued from USPSTF, in a market whose business model is selling biomarker panels. (Its "~90% of what matters" headline is invented, and coaching is linked three times.)

The testosterone articles carry it through. [More than half of men meeting deficiency criteria at one visit are normal on a repeat draw with no treatment](low-testosterone-men-symptoms-causes-testing-trt.md) (MMAS, >1,700 men). Requiring symptoms *plus* labs drops prevalence from **38.7% to 2–6%** — the six-fold spread overprescribing lives in. Only **three sexual symptoms** cluster reliably with biochemical hypogonadism; fatigue and low mood, the complaints that drive men into clinics, are worthless as discriminators. Most adult cases are **secondary and reversible**: visceral fat (**−147 ng/dL with obesity; +84 for 10% weight loss**), sleep restriction (**−10–15% at 5 h/night for a week**), opioids — and **sleep apnoea**, where exogenous testosterone worsens the actual problem. The [sleep apnoea guide](basic-guide-sleep-apnea.md) makes the same case. TRAVERSE (**5,246 men; MACE 7.0% vs 7.3%**) retires the cardiovascular scare without showing benefit. The [companion piece](low-testosterone-causes-diagnosis-management.md) has the best line on harm — spermatogenesis suppressed within 10 weeks, **4–10% never fully recover** — but is unsourced throughout and defers roughly ten times to their **paid Testosterone Course**.

### Measuring the body, and the metabolic chain

[BMI's real failure is under-diagnosis](bmi-accurate-bmi.md), not the muscular outlier: **~97% specificity, ~42% sensitivity at 30** (25 studies, ~32,000 people), catching only 36% of men and 49% of women who genuinely carry excess fat — a positive is trustworthy, a negative is not. It survives on outcome data (**239 prospective studies, >10 million people**), not precision. Pair it with [waist measured at the iliac crest](measure-waist-circumference.md), where the NHANES cut-points come from — though that article's bolded 40″/34″ is undercut by its own footnote conceding 37″/31″ screens better. [The BRI article](new-number-health-body-roundness-index-replace.md) misstates its own formula — BRI is **waist and height only** — so its case that BRI accounts for ethnicity fails. [The visceral-fat piece](visceral-fat-vs-weight-waist-matters-more.md) is better, and its winning theory undercuts its own headline: visceral fat is a **marker** that subcutaneous storage capacity has been exceeded, with fatty liver doing the damage. That chain runs through the **personal fat threshold** in [type 2 diabetes](know-about-type-2-diabetes-part-1.md) — one model presented as the model, with no effect sizes or designs — to [fatty liver](fatty-liver-disease-causes-symptoms-treatments.md), whose most actionable sentence is that **ALT and AST are normal in more than half of MASLD patients** and do not exclude advanced fibrosis. That article's numbers are almost entirely uncited.

### Getting older, and being pregnant

[Sarcopenia has moved off mass onto strength](sarcopenia-symptoms-prevent.md): mass declines 0.5–1.0%/year after 50, strength 1.5–3.0%, because the loss is neural. Its "walking is not enough" is sound size-principle reasoning stated as established finding, with no study comparing walking to lifting. [Osteoporosis](basic-guide-osteoporosis.md) is the opposite extreme — **it cites nothing at all**, including its one load-bearing safety claim, that high-intensity lifting rebuilds bone in older patients without causing fractures. Such trials exist; a reader cannot check that this rests on them. [Strength tracked cancer mortality where self-reported lifting did not](strength-cancer-related-mortality-enough-exercise-actually.md) in one NHANES analysis (n=2,773) — but a dynamometer beats a two-question self-report through measurement error alone.

[Training through pregnancy](exercise-during-pregnancy-safety-training-guide.md) is safe in **uncomplicated, average-risk** pregnancies, on ACOG plus a 2025 BJSM meta-analysis pooling 47,619 participants; the Valsalva fear is answered by two small acute studies (one a **22-woman** 3D Doppler) that test the mechanism directly but measure surrogates. Guidelines say **RPE 4–6**; the article prescribes **RPE 6–8** from a non-pregnant meta-analysis. Its worst gap: the sentence introducing conditions to stop for is followed by nothing, and no warning-signs list appears anywhere. [Cycle-phase periodisation is not supported](women-menstrual-cycle-athletic-exercise-performance.md) — cycling estradiol runs ~3× post-menopausal levels and the dip lasts days not decades, and all four periodisation studies are confounded by tapers and contralateral crosstalk. [The sex-and-gender article](shades-gray-sex-gender-fairness-sport.md) is strongest on the history of failed sex testing; its stretch is using within-sex testosterone variation to argue about the between-sex gap. Two loose ends: [exercise triggers migraine in 22% of sufferers yet every intervention reduced frequency](exercise-affect-headaches.md) in a 21-trial network meta-analysis, and [CBD has good evidence only for two rare paediatric epilepsies](science-cbd-oil-health-performance.md), none for performance, with **only 31% of 84 tested products accurately labelled and THC in 21%**.

## Exercise selection, technique and equipment

### Almost nothing here is ranked on growth, and they know it

Forty articles cover which exercise to pick. **Three claims in the set are backed by measured hypertrophy.** Bench angle, from [Chaves 2020](bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) — 8 weeks, untrained young men, flat vs incline vs both, no meaningful difference in pec major hypertrophy or pressing strength. The seated hamstring curl, from [Maeo 2021](best-glute-hamstring-exercises.md) — 12 weeks, MRI muscle volume, greater hamstring growth training at long muscle lengths. And overhead triceps extension over the pushdown, from [Maeo 2022](best-triceps-exercises-cable.md), triceps hypertrophy "substantially greater" in the shoulder-flexed position. Everything else — every tier list, every "targets the upper pecs", every ordering of six rear delt exercises — rests on EMG, moment arms, bare anatomy, or nothing at all.

The other outcome trials that appear are equivalence results, not rankings: push-ups match the bench press ([Kikuchi 2017, Kotarsky 2018](best-chest-exercises-muscle-strength.md)), machines match free weights with strength transfer specific to the mode trained ([Hernández-Belmonte 2023](best-bench-press-alternatives-build-chest.md)), cable and barbell preacher curls grow the biceps equally ([Nunes 2020](best-chest-biceps-workout.md)), hip thrust and back squat produce similar gluteal growth ([Plotkin 2023](best-leg-day-workout-routine-exercises.md), a preprint), bodyweight and barbell squats raise quad strength similarly ([Wei 2023](best-quad-exercises-workouts-massive-thighs.md)). Their own evidence says exercise identity matters less than the industry sells — which is, in their better moments, what they say.

**What makes the gap damning rather than merely disappointing is that they can state the rule and then break it in the same document.** The lifting-belt article cites Vigotsky's paper on reading sEMG amplitudes and concludes that EMG cannot be used to infer motor unit recruitment, and that more excitation meaning more growth "remains yet to be proven" ([belts](weightlifting-belt-they-work.md)). It is the best-argued piece in the corpus. Then:

- [Best chest exercises for hypertrophy](best-chest-exercises-hypertrophy.md) cites Vigotsky under the incline dumbbell press to say excitation differences do not predict outcomes — then, roughly 6,000 characters later, justifies a 15-degree decline dumbbell press on Glass & Armstrong 1997, a pure EMG study of bench angles.
- [10 best chest exercises](best-chest-exercises-muscle-strength.md) cites two null EMG studies plus Vigotsky, then asserts uncited that pec excitation rises as the bench steepens and lower-pec excitation rises as it declines — the regional rule a reader will carry away.
- [Best upper chest exercises](best-upper-chest-exercises-bigger-pecs.md) states the caveat twice in prose while its title, structure, exercise descriptions and conclusion assume the opposite throughout.
- [Best lower chest exercises](best-lower-chest-workout-exercises-massive-pecs.md) cites Vigotsky's paper by its own title — *hypertrophic potential cannot be inferred* — then ranks ten exercises on EMG.
- [Best glute and hamstring exercises](best-glute-hamstring-exercises.md) writes the caveat cleanly, then justifies five of eight entries with activation papers.
- [Best rear delt exercises](best-rear-delt-exercises-workouts.md) puts it in the clearest terms of any article here — the data "doesn't show that the differences are predictive of actual outcomes" — and ranks six exercises with **not one posterior-deltoid growth study in its list.**

Feigenbaum's [front-vs-back squat piece](bar-placement-squat-matter.md) is the honourable exception: he frames sEMG as a surrogate endpoint, shows it failing in one direction and holding in another, and concludes only that the *absence* of a large activation difference makes a large outcome difference unlikely — a weaker and better claim than the listicles make on the same evidence.

### Two kinds of article, and you must know which you are holding

The corpus splits cleanly. **The clinician-bylined essays** — Feigenbaum on [belts](weightlifting-belt-they-work.md), [bar placement](bar-placement-squat-matter.md) and [the grip problem](grip-problem.md), Ben Inglis on [range of motion](range-motion-considerations.md), Claire Zai on [the squat](squat-correctly-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) and [the bench press](bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) — argue positions, quote sources against their own interest, and mostly sell nothing.

**The unbylined "Barbell Medicine" listicles** of the 2023–24 cluster are search-optimised content with a reference list attached. The tells are structural. Lists duplicate entries wholesale: seventeen numbers resolving to thirteen papers in [wrist wraps](wrist-wraps.md), twenty-two to eighteen in [chest hypertrophy](best-chest-exercises-hypertrophy.md), six to four in [cable chest](best-cable-chest-workout-exercises.md), one protein review under four numbers in [leg day](best-leg-day-workout-routine-exercises.md). Citations land on the wrong papers: a biceps claim on [muscle architecture in the horse](best-chest-biceps-workout.md), a golf club-head-speed claim on a case report about chest pain, a trapezius-origin claim on a [fibre-type histochemistry paper](exercises-chest-back-workout-day.md), a bone-density heading on [osteoarthritis reviews](best-bodyweight-chest-workout-without-equipment.md). Non-sources appear: an [ACE industry PDF](best-chest-biceps-workout.md), and a consumer lifestyle website in the [traps article](best-trap-exercises-workouts-bigger-back.md).

Then the errors a coach or clinician would not make. [Inner chest](best-inner-chest-exercises-workouts.md) credits the dumbbell flye with acting as "a chest opener, which helps reduce back pain" — uncited, and against their own pain-science position. [Lower chest](best-lower-chest-workout-exercises-massive-pecs.md) tells you to reverse a fly "when the dumbbells touch your chest". [Arms and shoulders](arms-shoulder-workout-bigger-biceps-shoulders.md) gets the preacher curl backwards, claiming shoulder flexion lengthens the biceps long head.

### Equipment: the belt article is genuinely good

[What lifting belts do](weightlifting-belt-they-work.md) pulls every study comparing belted to unbelted — 36, twenty occupational and sixteen in the gym — and sorts them properly.

**Shown.** Belts raise intra-abdominal pressure in five of seven lifting studies (Harman 1989: 175 mmHg belted vs 156 beltless at 90% 1RM deadlift; Lander 1992: 25–40% higher), additively with a Valsalva. Belts do **not** meaningfully alter trunk sEMG — McGill's participants never exceeded 15% of an isometric MVC either way, and Escamilla found a swap between obliques and rectus abdominis, not suppression. Belts do **not** decondition the trunk: construction workers wearing lumbar belts for two to three months got *stronger* on direct strength and endurance testing.

**Failed.** Belts do not reduce low-back injury rates, lost work days or compensation claims across large occupational trials. Feigenbaum then quotes NIOSH against his own interest — the studies "cannot be used to either support or refute" the effect — which is insufficient evidence, weaker than his own preceding sentence. About 90% of belt-wearing gym-goers say they use one to prevent injury; that is the belief the data do not carry. All of it is occupational, and the transfer to barbell lifting is asserted rather than argued.

**Assumed and never measured.** Trunk stiffness, the proposed mediator, is not measured in any cited study. Nor has any study tested whether anyone lifts more weight with a belt — the case is proxies (15.5% faster concentric at 90% 1RM squat; RPE two points lower on an unblindable 80% 1RM deadlift; one null). He names placebo as part of the mechanism, which is candid. Flag: the article's own recap drops the hedging and states the stiffness mechanism flatly.

[Wrist wraps](wrist-wraps.md) is the same shape, thinner: no reliable change in grip strength across four studies, no 1RM change in the only head-to-head (n=18), consistent *subjective* stability and measurable joint position sense, and no study of wraps against injury rates in any barbell sport. Their conclusion — wraps for people who already have wrist pain, not as prophylaxis — is clinical judgement on an adjacent taping RCT, and they say so. **Knee sleeves get no treatment at all**: the [squat guide](squat-correctly-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) lists shoes, wraps, belt, chalk and sleeves uncited and says none is essential.

**Commercial disclosure.** The belt article discloses that Pioneer, one of three brands recommended, sponsors their podcast — the right way to handle it. The wrist wrap article carries an **affiliate-tagged Rogue link** and repeated SBD links with prices.

### "Good form": their position, and a contradiction they never reconcile

Their position is that form carries far less weight than the industry claims. The **REP model** ([glutes and hamstrings](best-glute-hamstring-exercises.md), [chest and triceps](chest-triceps-workout-routine-exercises.md)) calls the only formal definition of proper technique in the literature circular — it defines correct technique by injury reduction while the premises about velocity, plane and range are unestablished — and replaces it with Repeatable, Efficient, and meeting the exercise's Points of performance, explicitly declining to prescribe neutral spine or knees behind toes. That is a reasoned position, not a demonstrated result: nothing cited tests whether coaching it lowers injury rates.

The overreach is consistent. [The squat guide](squat-correctly-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) asserts that "a specific squat form is not correlated with injury or pain" **with no citation, in an article that cites eleven papers elsewhere** — and "form predicts injury poorly in the studies we have" is a different claim. [The bench press guide](bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) argues the arch is not dangerous from mechanism alone, and calls Valsalva cardiovascular risks "unsubstantiated" on a citation that only shows people reflexively breath-hold. [Arms and shoulders](arms-shoulder-workout-bigger-biceps-shoulders.md) calls the shoulder-damage claim about overhead work "made-up" and avoiding lockout "wholly made up" — on citations covering injury base rates, not those claims. The upright-row sections in [shoulders](best-shoulder-exercises.md) and [traps](best-trap-exercises-workouts-bigger-back.md) reframe impingement correctly as a symptom cluster rather than a structural diagnosis, then slide to "not uniquely injurious in practice" — an injury-incidence claim with no injury data.

**The contradiction is dated.** [The Grip Problem](grip-problem.md) (2017, edited by Mark Rippetoe) makes a strong form-determines-adaptation argument: deadlifting with a rounded back means the erectors "weren't required to do their job today", so they will not be able to tomorrow. [The chest-and-back article](exercises-chest-back-workout-day.md) (2024) says that as long as an exercise is performed repeatedly with an efficient movement pattern, "your unique technique will work just fine". The catalogue changed its mind and both remain live, unreconciled. Read the 2017 piece as a Starting Strength artefact; its grip mechanics stand independently.

### The physics error

[Barbell Forces: Levers, Moments, and Torque](barbell-forces-levers-moments-torque.md) (2012) gets the definitions right, and the useful claim — bar position trades torque between joints, the front squat lengthening the distal-femoral moment arm and the low-bar squat the spinal and proximal-femoral ones. **Its central argument is then backwards.** It repeatedly claims lengthening these levers *helps* the lifter: "a longer lever will increase the torque and thus allow for heavier weights to be moved," complete with an Archimedes quote. These are *resistance* moment arms; lengthening one increases the torque the muscles must overcome, making the lift harder. Mechanical advantage comes from the muscle's own moment arm, never mentioned. The article cites nothing.

### Where they meet the library's adjudicated positions

**Range of motion.** [Inglis 2022](range-motion-considerations.md) refuses "how deep" as one question and splits it into strength, hypertrophy, pain and health. On lengthened partials he lands where the library has since settled — they "may produce similar results compared to full ROM", and given the thin evidence he advises against using them for a significant portion of training. That is *equal, not better*, reached in 2022 for the right reason. Two details worth keeping: Kubo 2019's depth advantage was an adductor and glute advantage, quads statistically indistinguishable (4.9% vs 4.6%); and Goto 2019's 48.7% vs 28.2% partial-ROM result is passed along uncritically, despite a mechanism — intramuscular hypoxia — that does not belong in a muscle-length argument.

**Regional hypertrophy.** No article here measures a region of the pectoralis major, before or after training. [Inner chest](best-inner-chest-exercises-workouts.md) says in its first paragraph that the inner chest is not a separate muscle and isolating it is unlikely — then sells a high-to-low cable fly for "the lower portion" and a hex press for squeezing the muscles together. [Lower chest](best-lower-chest-workout-exercises-massive-pecs.md)'s only genuine regional-growth citations, Ema 2013 and Earp 2015, are about the **quadriceps** — which makes regional growth possible in principle, not evidence that a decline press grows the lower pec.

**Depth.** [The squat guide](squat-correctly-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md) concedes no unique health or hypertrophy benefit to squatting well below parallel, and says the real argument for a fixed depth is **consistency of measurement** — otherwise you cannot tell whether you got stronger or shortened the movement.

### What they sell

[How to choose exercises](choose-exercises-hypertrophy.md) and [the bench press for hypertrophy](bench-press-hypertrophy-technique-programming.md) each close on the Hypertrophy I template; [chest hypertrophy](best-chest-exercises-hypertrophy.md) pitches four. The heaviest-monetised listicles — [chest and back](exercises-chest-back-workout-day.md), [arms and shoulders](arms-shoulder-workout-bigger-biceps-shoulders.md), [chest and triceps](chest-triceps-workout-routine-exercises.md), [chest and biceps](best-chest-biceps-workout.md) — embed three identical "Find your Hypertrophy Program" blocks in the body with campaign tracking. [Shoulders](best-shoulder-exercises.md) and [traps](best-trap-exercises-workouts-bigger-back.md) put a paid-coaching pitch *inside* the shoulder-pain paragraph. And [leg day](best-leg-day-workout-routine-exercises.md), [pull day](best-pull-day-workout-routine-exercises.md) and [push day](best-push-day-workout-routine-exercises-muscle.md) each demolish the supplement category with USPSTF and AHA-grade evidence, then sell their own pre-workout and whey isolate in the next paragraph, uncited.

## Programming, effort and recovery

Thirty-six articles that divide sharply by quality of evidence. The recovery material — detraining, overtraining, HRV — is among the best-sourced writing in this library. The programming material is often the opposite: several articles cite nothing, several are product catalogues.

### Starting from zero

[The Beginner Prescription](beginner-prescription.md) sets the house dose: 2+ sessions a week, 2–3 hard sets per exercise, most work at RPE 6–8, 2–4 minutes rest, load added by 1–5% *after* performance improves. Its clearest failure: "more exercise leads to greater improvements" is linked to two low- versus high-*load* meta-analyses, which cannot carry it.

[The strength training guide](strength-training-guide-get-stronger.md) generalises it — the same prescription at six and at ninety, four adaptations in parallel from session one, four inputs: ~60% 1RM untrained and ~80% trained (Rhea 2003, 140 studies), 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week (uncited), 2–4 sessions, progression matched to the lifter. Its best-evidenced passage is LIFTMOR: 101 postmenopausal women, 8 months, supervised 5×5 at 80–85% 1RM, lumbar spine BMD **+2.9% versus −1.2%**, knee extensor strength +37% versus +5%, one minor adverse event in 2,600+ sessions. [Youth resistance training](recommendations-youth-resistance-training.md) rests its whole dose on one meta-analysis (Lesinski 2016), then argues it is not executable as written. [At home](exercise-home.md) and [with no time](exercise-have-no-time.md) prescribe an effort target rather than a movement.

### Progressing: load follows adaptation

[Progressive loading](progressive-loading.md) and its restatement, [Beyond Progressive Overload](beyond-progressive-overload-work-smarter-not-harder.md), argue that overload does not add stress — it adds *stimulus* to hold stress constant as fitness rises. The bar is reactive, not proactive. The framing paper is Impellizzeri 2019 on external versus internal load; the number is Lambrianides 2022, a mean of **4.3 weeks to a demonstrable strength increase, range 1 to 12** — so weekly load increases outrun the adaptation. The diagnostic rule is clean: someone genuinely stronger adds weight *without* the RPE rising. Three cautions: the intensity floors drift unexplained between the two articles (65–75% and 85–90% in 2022, ~70% and ~85% in 2025); the singles floor rests on Androulakis-Korakakis 2018, a *pilot study* in a handful of powerlifters, reused for the same claim in at least four articles; and no cited study compares reactive against scheduled loading.

[Novice, intermediate, advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md) is the strongest version: >9,000 powerlifters (Latella 2023) show a smooth logarithmic curve from year one to year ten with no inflection at any tier boundary, and day-to-day 1RM variation of ~3–5% (Grgic 2020) means a 500 lb squatter's noise band is ±25 lb against monthly gains of 10–15 lb. Ahtiainen 2003 — eight strength athletes versus eight non-athletes, identical 21-week programme, 3.9% versus 20.9% — is mostly denominator arithmetic. [Understanding stalls](training-expectations-understanding-stalls.md) adds the rule — three weeks minimum, a "regression" stipulated at ~15% and labelled arbitrary — and cites nothing; neither does [returning to the gym](returning-gym.md).

### How hard to train — the clash worth reading

Their effort prescription is **RPE 6–8 on compound lifts and RPE 8–10 on isolation, even in a hypertrophy programme** ([strength vs hypertrophy](strength-vs-hypertrophy-training-similarities-differences.md); [strength vs bodybuilding](strength-training-vs-bodybuilding-similarities-differences.md); [intermediate powerlifting programs](intermediate-powerlifting-programs-increase-strength-size.md)). That is 2–4 RIR on the main lifts: below the 1–3 RIR band this library adjudicated, above Menno Henselmans' argument that 4–6 RIR can match 0 RIR when total reps are equated, and the split itself is used by nobody else here. It is **asserted, not argued** — no citation supports the compound/isolation split anywhere. The implicit logic is fatigue budgeting (compounds expensive per unit of per-muscle stimulus, isolation cheap); Menno's position only rescues RPE 6–8 if you add sets to equate reps, which they never require.

They are also inconsistent with themselves: their chest-and-triceps article prescribes RPE 6–8 across the board while their shoulder article prescribes RPE 8–10 for the same class of isolation movement, and [how many chest exercises](many-chest-exercises-per-workout.md) argues for ~4–5 reps from failure, calling the Physical Activity Guidelines' "within 1 rep of failure" instruction made up. The best-supported strand is the strength half: two Pareja-Blanco velocity-loss trials (~8 weeks, professional soccer players and resistance-trained men) where lower velocity loss matched or beat higher loss with less fatigue — training-outcome studies with a measurable proxy, but two papers from one lab treated as settled.

### Can anyone rate RPE accurately?

[What is RPE in lifting](rpe-lifting.md) is 4,831 words carrying **one citation and no reference list**. Its answer to the subjectivity objection is modest — not "lifters are accurate" but "you don't need to be", because the useful load band is narrow. The citation is Helms 2017, *Self-Rated* Accuracy of RPE-Based Load Prescription in Powerlifters, attached to "trainees can implement RPE to a reasonable degree of accuracy within a few weeks." Self-rated accuracy measures confidence, not accuracy; wrong design for the claim. Against it: Halperin 2022 finds under-prediction of reps to failure by **2–5 reps**, and Barbosa-Netto 2021 found over 40% of lifters got 6–10 extra reps at their self-declared 10-rep weight, 26% got 19–20. At 4 reps @ RPE 8, a 2–5 rep error is the difference between the prescription and a warm-up. Their own [novice/intermediate/advanced](novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md) article cites Halperin correctly and adds Malleron 2025 finding no trained-versus-untrained difference — the corpus contains the correction to its own RPE article, unreconciled. Periodic calibration against true failure appears nowhere.

### How long to rest

[The ultimate guide to rest periods](ultimate-guide-rest-periods-maximize-workouts-strength.md) recommends **2–5 minutes for strength, 2–4 for hypertrophy**, both stated before the evidence review and neither upper bound tested by anything cited. The evidence: Schoenfeld 2016 (23 trained men, 8 weeks, 3 min beat 1 min — squat 1RM +15% vs +7.5%, bench +12.7% vs +4%); de Salles 2010 (36 men, 16 weeks, leg press +22/+34/+42% at 1/3/5 min, volume not equated, which they say); González-Hernández 2023 finding no difference between 3 and 5 minutes. The gap: a 2025 article rests its hypertrophy case on **Grgic 2017, a review of six studies in mostly untrained men**, and never mentions the 2024 network meta-analysis this library covers, which found >60s beat ≤60s with every rest band's credible intervals overlapping and ~90 seconds possibly sufficient. Presenting a 2–4 minute floor as firmer than that is the clearest case here of a recommendation outrunning the literature. Their novice-needs-longer-rest claim is sourced to a sprint-running review and a study of enzymes in children — neither about lifters.

### Cardio, and what to do with it

[Concurrent training](concurrent-training-interference-effect-cardio-kill-gains.md) is one of their better articles. The interference effect traces to Hickson 1980 in untrained men, and three later meta-analyses (Petré 2021, Schumann 2022, Lundberg 2022) found no significant compromise. They demote the mTOR/AMPK story — all three signalling citations are acute single-session studies — and reframe interference as a total-workload problem, agreeing with House of Hypertrophy on headline and mechanism. Two differences matter. On **modality** they cite two reviews pointing opposite ways (running worse than cycling; cycling worse than running) and conclude there is no basis to choose — a better account than the received view that running interferes most. On **same-session ordering** they recommend conditioning after lifting on shared days, while House of Hypertrophy weights a 2021 meta-analysis in trained individuals showing strength gains lowered by same-session but not separate-session cardio — very likely Petré 2021, which they cite for the null only. Their own quoted Schumann caveat on explosive strength, "especially when performed in the same session," points the same way; [GPP for lifters](general-physical-preparedness-training-lifters.md) puts LISS before HIIT for anyone with no aerobic base.

### What a layoff costs

[How fast do you lose muscle and strength](fast-lose-muscle-strength.md) is the best article in this batch. The reference number is Wall 2014: two weeks in a full leg cast cost **8.4% of cross-sectional area and 22.9% of strength**. The organising idea is that the *reason* you stopped predicts the damage better than the days — missing sessions, immobilisation, bed rest and critical illness are four different situations, and nearly every frightening statistic comes from the last two (ICU: rectus femoris CSA −17.7% in ten days). They set the noise floor first, at ~2–5% between-day variation, treating ~5% as the threshold for real change. Three weeks off in adolescent athletes and two weeks in powerlifters and D1 footballers produced no significant loss; four weeks cost resistance-trained older men ~17%; two to three months costs 7–12%. Older adults who trained two years then stopped for three still finished **14% above baseline on leg press and 9% on bench**, against +82% and +34% for continuers. The maintenance and return sections carry **no citations at all**, and are where the Time Crunch template sits.

### Whether recovery metrics are worth tracking

[Are you really overtrained](overtrained-evidence-shows.md) argues no controlled resistance-training study has produced overtraining syndrome. A systematic review of 22 overload studies found 10 reported zero performance decline and no reliable biomarker (Grandou 2020). The failed attempts are the substance: three athletes squatting a true 1RM daily for 30 days all improved (473→500 lb, 275→303 lb, 484→530 lb); Raastad 2001's daily submaximal leg work produced **+6% 1RM**. Only Fry 1994 — 11 men, 10 sets at 1RM daily for 14 days — dropped 1RM ~12 kg, with recovery in 2–8 weeks, short of the months OTS requires. No biomarker works: self-report beat hormones, resting heart rate and HRV across 56 studies (Saw 2016), and the testosterone-to-cortisol ratio moved *opposite* to prediction.

This contradicts Nippard's overtraining video on four points: the 2–3 week definition (functional overreaching on the consensus definition), supercompensation itself (via Kiely 2018), the volume-versus-intensity attribution (Nippard: volume; they read Raastad and Fry as intensity), and HRV monitoring. **Both cite Fry 1994 for opposite conclusions** — Nippard as proof overtraining is real, Barbell Medicine as the strongest attempt on record that still failed the criteria — and both cite Saw 2016, which Nippard uses for a self-survey before recommending HRV as "the more objective marker."

[Understanding HRV](understanding-heart-rate-variability-strength-training.md) is the most rigorous piece here. The two head-to-head trials in resistance training both came back null: 21 older women over 7 weeks and 22 young men racing to 20 sessions, with no difference in strength, hypertrophy, functional tests or non-responder count, despite the HRV groups training more (27 of 35 sessions versus 21; 20 sessions in five weeks versus seven). The decisive datum is Chen 2011: in national and international weightlifters, 1RM performance returned to baseline at **~30 hours while HRV took ~60**, so HRV would have pulled an athlete from a session they were ready for. High HRV is not simply better (atrial fibrillation, on Mendelian randomisation), and under 5% of the parameters wearables report have been validated (Doherty 2024) — Apple Watch and WHOOP underestimate HRV; the Polar H10 holds against EKG. They then recommend a 5–7 day rolling average used only to *add* a session, never skip one: coaching judgement stacked on a null result, labelled experimental. Their speculation that the higher-volume HRV group "would see better results in the long-term" is supported by nothing in either trial. What they endorse instead is session RPE trending upward across two or more weeks at constant relative load.

### Set-extension methods

[Myo-reps](myo-reps.md) is precisely specified — one activation set of ~12–30 reps to RPE 8, then 3–5 rep back-off sets on the same load with 20–30 second rests — and honestly framed as time-efficient volume on a lower-priority lift, not as superior. But its stimulus story runs on **muscle damage and metabolic stress**, both largely retired elsewhere in this library (Damas 2016; lactate infusion failing to raise anabolic signalling; Dankel 2016, where post-set cuffing *reduced* growth). The author hedges the mechanism as theoretical, so this is dated framing rather than error. **No comparison against volume-matched straight sets is cited anywhere.** [Blood flow restriction](introduction-blood-flow-restriction-training.md) grades its mechanisms honestly, while carrying affiliate-tagged links to two cuff products.

### What is not evidence here

Several articles cite **nothing**: [what drives hypertrophy](drives-hypertrophy-muscle-growth-works.md) (no references, no links, closing on a template sale), [12 ways to skin the Texas Method](ways-skin-texas-method.md), [the intermediate/advanced template](general-strength-training-template-intermediate-advanced.md), [arms for GPP](train-arms-gpp.md), [improving your rowing](ways-improve-rowing.md), and [their HIIT article](high-intensity-interval-training.md), which sells the cited version behind a membership. Others are product catalogues: [five intermediate powerlifting programs](intermediate-powerlifting-programs-increase-strength-size.md) and [two best Olympic weightlifting programs](best-olympic-weightlifting-programs-maximum-performance.md) rank seven of their own templates with no comparative data of any kind. The reasoning inside them sometimes argues against interest — [Olympic vs powerlifting](olympic-lifting-vs-powerlifting-differences.md) concedes weightlifting builds no more strength than ordinary training (Morris 2022), [the snatch and clean article](incorporate-snatch-clean-into-program.md) justifies them by enjoyment rather than adaptation, and [endurance athletes](strength-training-endurance-athlete-part-iii-programming.md) tells athletes with RED-S flags not to add load at all. Hold [strength standards](strength-standards-bodyweight-age-sex.md) loosely — its marker-to-reference mapping is broken — and note that [the specificity article](pendulum-specificity-application-part-i.md) calls its own central argument reasoning, not a finding. [Training motivation](science-training-motivation.md) alone argues adherence is the binding constraint: enjoyment had the largest effect on six-month attendance (β=0.83) across 575 gym members with turnstile data.

## Nutrition

### Four protein numbers from one publisher, and none of them cite the others

Barbell Medicine give at least four daily protein targets across these nineteen articles, spanning better than a two-fold range, with no article acknowledging that any of the others exist.

**1.3–3.1 g/kg/day** is the 2021 house guide ([protein guide](barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md)): 1.3–2.2 g/kg for maintaining or gaining, 2.3–3.1 g/kg for people who are lean *and* dieting, training 5+ days a week, or anabolically resistant. The bottom is well founded — Morton 2018, 49 studies, 1,863 adults, breakpoint ~1.6 g/kg. The top rests on **one source**, Helms 2014, a review titled *A Case for Higher Intakes*, whose endpoint is minimising lean-mass loss rather than hypertrophy and whose underlying trials compare higher protein against *inadequate* protein. They show too little is bad; they do not show 3.1 beats 1.8, and the article does not say so.

**1.6 g/kg** appears in [how to eat a healthy diet](eat-healthy-diet.md) and as the floor in [training while losing weight](train-while-losing-weight.md), both traced correctly to Morton's ~1.62 breakpoint. This is their best-supported number. The healthy-diet article also gives 3.1 g/kg for physique athletes in a deficit with **no citation at all**.

**1.4–1.6 g/kg** is the current figure in [protein on Ozempic](protein-ozempic-much-need.md), the 8,500-word [protein and weight loss](protein-weight-loss-maximizing-fat-loss-preserving.md) guide and [working out but gaining weight](reasons-re-working-out-but-gaining-weight.md). **It is the lowest number in the whole library** — below the adjudicated 1.6–1.8 g/kg, below the ISSN's ≥1.6–2.4, below Henselmans, and below the 1.4–2.2 g/kg quoted earlier in the same article. Its anchor is the **2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines' general-population band of 1.2–1.6**, not a deficit trial. In the weight-loss guide the two references arguing for higher intakes, Morton 2018 and Helms 2014, are printed but **never attached to a sentence**; 19 of its 49 references are never cited at all.

**1.6 g/kg of target body weight** turns up in [creatine on Ozempic](creatine-ozempic-prevent-muscle-loss.md) — a smaller denominator again for anyone with obesity, so a lower gram figure wearing the clothes of the second number. The 2012 archive swung the other way: [To Be A Beast](to-be-a-beast.md) sets a muscle-gain template at 1.1–1.25 g/lb (**2.4–2.8 g/kg**), then argues on the same page against high protein on ammonia-toxicity grounds while conceding nobody knows whether dietary protein raises ammonia harmfully at all.

### What a reader should actually do

Take **1.6–1.8 g/kg of current body weight**, deficit or not, and stop optimising. That is the library's adjudicated band and it sits inside Barbell Medicine's own best-sourced figure. Below it you are trusting a general-population guideline over the meta-analysis they cite themselves; above 2.0 g/kg you are buying insurance, not effect — nobody has run 1.6 against 2.4 in a moderate deficit. Their GLP-1 triage order survives all of this: **training first, calories second, total protein third, distribution a distant fourth**. If a drug has taken your appetite and you are at 0.6 g/kg, the move that matters is 0.6 → 1.2, not 1.4 → 1.6.

### Per-meal distribution: right in 2014, wrong in the same article

[Seven rules to optimize protein intake](rules-optimize-protein-intake.md) killed the absorption ceiling early and correctly: tracer work shows essentially complete absorption, ~110–120% of intake once enterocytes secrete their own protein into the lumen, and rate is not extent. [Protein slows digestion? Nope](protein-slows-digestion-nope.md) does the same for gastric emptying, though it too cites nothing.

The same 2014 article then builds a system that has not held: a 3–4 g leucine per-meal threshold, a **3–5 hour refractory period**, "3–5 meals tops", and the claim that protein eaten inside that window "would contribute nothing to MPS and then, by definition, be stored away" as glycogen or fat. That is **unsourced** — the article cites nothing whatsoever — and treats an acute MPS measurement as a full accounting of protein fate.

The later bands soften without abandoning the idea: **~20–45 g** per meal in the protein guide, **25–40 g** in the weight-loss guide (uncited, quietly raising the ISSN's lower bound from 20 g). Stating a band still implies a ceiling, and the verdict here is that there is none.

### Creatine: the right meta-analysis, the wrong unit

[Creatine: benefits, side effects](creatine-benefits-side-effects-safe-consumption.md) cites Burke 2023, the direct-measurement meta-analysis, and reports it as "a couple of pounds or so in a year." Burke pooled ten studies using site-specific ultrasound and MRI and found **0.10–0.16 cm of muscle thickness at four sites, pooled effect size 0.11**, the authors calling the practical significance possibly minimal. Feigenbaum reports neither the effect size nor that verdict, and converts a regional thickness result into a whole-body mass unit the paper never measured.

Everything else is restrained — "modest" effects, the ISSN 2017 stand cited only for dosing rather than its "most effective ergogenic supplement available" line, agnostic on endurance, unconvinced on cognition: markedly less enthusiastic than the ISSN. [Creatine on Ozempic](creatine-ozempic-prevent-muscle-loss.md) argues against its own shelf — no trial has tested creatine during GLP-1 therapy, and the effect is **mediated entirely by training** (~1–2 kg lean mass and 5–10% strength over 8–12 weeks with lifting, near nothing without). **They sell creatine.** The contamination section (~20% of supplements contaminated, 30–50% mislabelled) is properly cited and then links their own line, naming PeriRx as what to buy.

### Collagen: the best appraisal in the corpus, against a category they profit from

[Collagen protein](collagen-protein-work.md): PDCAAS **0**, because collagen has no tryptophan and the score is set by the limiting amino acid, against 1.00 for whey; 30 g collagen failed to stimulate muscle protein synthesis where 30 g whey succeeded; and Benito-Ruiz 2009, n=250, gave collagen a **4.7 mm** advantage on a 100 mm pain scale against a minimal clinically important difference of **13.7 mm**.

[Collagen and tendinopathy](collagen-protein-tendinopathy.md) has the best evidence appraisal in these notes. Praet 2019: **n=20**, double-blind crossover, 2 × 3 months, 2.5 g hydrolysed collagen peptides twice daily on top of 2 × 90 daily eccentric calf raises. Raw VISA-A moved 58.5 → 60.8 on collagen and 55.9 → 62.8 on placebo against an MCID of 6.5 — only placebo cleared it. Three observations do the work: the **crossover-order artefact** (the placebo-first sequence did better overall, the opposite of a real collagen effect), **unblinding** (60% and 55% correctly identified collagen, only 40% and 44% identified placebo), and the **maltodextrin control**, which cannot separate "collagen" from "any extra dietary protein". One correction: he calls the linear mixed model a rescue manoeuvre, and mixed models are standard for repeated measures in a crossover. The qualification is that **whey is the comparator they favour and they sell whey** — in the 2025 article "whey protein isolate" links to their own product page.

### Red meat: meticulous on cancer design, casual on cohort modelling

[The science of red meat](science-red-meat-health.md) labels the cancer evidence exactly: all observational; IARC's ~17% per 100 g/day red meat and ~18% per 50 g/day processed meat are relative risks; against a US lifetime colorectal risk of ~4.5% (39 cases per 100,000) that is roughly **7 extra cases per 100,000**. It even reports the deflationary consistency figure — only **7 of 14 cohorts and 7 of 15 case-control studies** found a positive association for *unprocessed* red meat.

The saturated-fat section then presents Li 2015's substitution estimates — 5% of energy from saturated fat replaced by PUFA → ~25% lower coronary risk, by sugar or refined carbohydrate → **no significant change** — as "the data suggest", without noting these are **substitution models inside a prospective cohort**, not trials: the least certain numbers in the piece, delivered with the most confidence, right after the most careful design labelling in the corpus. Capping processed meat at "under 50 g/day" compounds it — that is the *exposure unit at which the risk was measured*, not a safe ceiling. No product is sold here.

### Where they argue against their own side

[Why weight regain is not a failure of willpower](weight-regain-not-failure-willpower.md) is built as a falsifiable claim: BMI heritability of **40–70% of between-person variance** from 540 Danish adoptees and 93 identical twin pairs reared apart, with a careful paragraph explaining that heritability describes differences between people rather than a verdict on your own body. Sumithran 2011: 50 people, ~13.5 kg lost, self-rated hunger *higher than pre-diet* a year later. Fothergill's Biggest Loser cohort ~700 kcal/day below baseline at six years — from **14 contestants**, which the article never says. The strongest item is Wright 2023: over 12,000 siblings, where the "smarter is leaner" correlation survives between strangers and **shrinks to zero between siblings raised in the same family**.

[The modern food environment](modern-food-environment-makes-overeating-default.md) rests on Hall 2019 — **20 adults, inpatient crossover, two weeks per arm, menus matched for calories offered plus protein, carbohydrate, fat, sugar, salt and fibre, ad libitum**; people ate ~500 kcal/day more on the ultra-processed arm. Enormous effect, tiny trial. Then the section that earns the article its place: they report the BMJ umbrella review's ~50% higher cardiovascular mortality and ~55% higher obesity odds across nearly ten million people and **immediately say the review itself grades that low to very-low certainty**; they call NOVA a blunt instrument (whey isolate and an Oreo land in the same bin); and they give seed oils the opposite of the popular verdict, citing the biomarker analysis linking omega-6 to **lower** cardiovascular risk. [Does your metabolism change](metabolism-change-weight-loss.md) supplies the number underneath: expenditure falls ~**30 kcal/day per kg lost** while intake rises ~**100 kcal/day per kg lost**.

### Salt and fibre: the uncited tail

The [salt newsletter](newsletter-word-salt.md) is 712 words with **zero references** and two factual failures. **"Sea salt has about 10% as much sodium as table salt" is wrong by roughly tenfold** — both are predominantly sodium chloride by mass, and crystal size changes sodium per *teaspoon*, not per gram. "Most who train seriously will become hyponatremic after training" is an unhedged prevalence claim with nothing behind it. And advising a healthy person to *add* salt extrapolates from an absence of evidence for restriction, which is not evidence for supplementation. The [fibre guide](dietary-fiber-guide-viscosity-fermentability-gut-health.md) is the cleanest reframing in the set — drop soluble/insoluble for **viscosity and fermentability** — and the least sourced, four links across 2,229 words.

### The commercial layer, plainly

WheyRx recurs throughout, sometimes in the sentence carrying the recommendation: linked mid-paragraph in the [protein guide](barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md); named at the close of Rule 6 in the [2014 rules](rules-optimize-protein-intake.md), straight after whey is asserted superior to every other source; named twice in [protein on Ozempic](protein-ozempic-much-need.md); named in the [weight-loss guide](protein-weight-loss-maximizing-fat-loss-preserving.md) as meeting supplement criteria that same article had just set; and linked twice in [how to eat a healthy diet](eat-healthy-diet.md), whose most rigorous section reports ~20% of supplements contaminated before recommending two of their own.

It cuts both ways. Recommendation 4 of the protein guide states intakes to 4.4 g/kg are safe but improve nothing. The weight-loss guide spends 8,500 words concluding the flagship satiety mechanism for its own product does not hold up. Both collagen articles argue against a category they could stock. And their supplement criteria are checkable against any brand, including theirs.

## Where this source departs from the rest of the library

- **Effort.** They prescribe RPE 6–8 on compounds even for hypertrophy and RPE 8–10 on isolation.
  That sits below the 1–3 reps-in-reserve this library adjudicated, above Menno Henselmans' 4–6
  RIR, and the split prescription is used by nobody else here. It is asserted rather than cited,
  and it is inconsistent between their own articles.
- **Protein in a deficit.** Their 1.4–1.6 g/kg is the lowest figure in the library — below the
  adjudicated 1.6–1.8, below the ISSN's ≥1.6–2.4, below Henselmans. It is anchored to a
  general-population dietary guideline rather than a deficit trial, and their own reference list
  contains the meta-analysis that says otherwise.
- **Overtraining.** Four direct contradictions with Jeff Nippard's treatment, including both
  sources citing Fry 1994 for opposite conclusions, and disagreement on whether overtraining takes
  weeks or years, whether volume or intensity drives it, and whether HRV is worth tracking.
- **Rest periods.** Their 2025 article rests on a six-study meta-analysis and never engages the
  2024 network meta-analysis this library covers, in which all the rest bands overlap.
- **Regional hypertrophy.** They argue — correctly, and against most of the library — that incline
  bench is not shown to preferentially build the upper chest. Then several of their own listicles
  assume the opposite.
- **Range of motion.** Their 2022 position that lengthened partials produce *similar* results to
  full range, with advice against using them widely, is where the trained-subject trials landed
  two years later. This is the source ageing best on a contested question.

## Known problems in this material

- **The elevated-liver-enzymes article gives three different escalation thresholds** — >5× the
  upper limit of normal in the body text, >10× in the FAQ, <3× in the patient scripts. A reader
  taking the FAQ figure could sit on a 7× elevation. Cite that article only with the caveat.
- **The barbell-physics article inverts its own argument**, treating a longer resistance moment
  arm as magnifying the lifter's force. It cites nothing.
- **Reference lists are sometimes internally corrupt** — printed author and title text not
  matching the paper the adjacent PubMed ID resolves to. Found in the liver-enzyme, statins and
  overtraining articles among others. The links have been right; the printed citations wrong.
- **13 of 155 articles failed the marker-verification check**, and a further group never used
  numbered citations at all. Each note says which case it is; no numbering was ever repaired by
  guessing.
- **18 notes cover articles that cite nothing whatsoever**, several of them making clinical or
  epidemiological claims. The osteoporosis article's safety claim — the whole point of the piece —
  names no study at all.
- **Everything sells something.** Templates, coaching, consultations, a supplement line. The notes
  record it in each case, in both directions: several articles argue against supplement categories
  the company profits from, and the collagen and cholesterol pieces are the clearest examples.
