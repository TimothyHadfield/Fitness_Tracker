# Working Out and Gaining Weight: Calories, Water, and a Scale You Are Reading Wrong

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "3 Reasons Why You're Working Out But Gaining Weight" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/working-out-but-gaining-weight/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2025-08-07 · 3,256 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Three causes, in order of importance: **eating too many calories, water retention, and misreading a single scale weight**. Nothing exotic, and the article says so.
- **Exercise is a poor weight-loss lever and a good health lever, and these are separate claims.** Long-term exercise triggers compensatory reductions in other components of energy expenditure, so the deficit you create is smaller than the calories you burned — seven citations back that [16–23]. Their conclusion: cutting 500 kcal of intake produces more weight loss than adding 500 kcal of exercise.
- **You cannot raise metabolic rate usefully.** RMR is ~80% determined by lean body mass [24], and adding lean mass barely moves it: 18 elite rugby players gained **2.0 kg of lean mass over 14 weeks with no significant RMR change** [34]. The only reliable pharmacological route is DNP, which kills people.
- **Two per cent of total body water in a 70 kg person is nearly two pounds** [35], and regular training makes the body retain more water. This is the mechanism behind most "I started lifting and gained 4 lb in a week."
- **Weight has a weekly rhythm** — heaviest Sunday and Monday, lightest Friday [39] — with typical variation of **1–2% of bodyweight** [40–42]. Their recommendation: judge energy balance on a **two-to-four-week rolling average**, never a single reading.
- **The protein advice here (1.4–1.6 g/kg) contradicts their own protein guide**, which recommends 2.3–3.1 g/kg for lean people actively losing weight. The library's position is 1.6–1.8 g/kg; this article sits just under it and their 2021 guide sits far above it. Both are Barbell Medicine.

## Reason 1: calories, and why "just eat less" is not simple

The framing is standard energy balance: TDEE = resting energy expenditure + diet-induced expenditure + activity-induced expenditure, contributing roughly **60% / 10% / 30%** [9]. The useful additions are the two caveats.

First, **individual variation is enormous**. The response to an identical energy deficit or surplus varies more than **five-fold** between people, mostly genetically — evidenced by the Bouchard identical-twin studies, the 1990 overfeeding trial [10] and the 1994 constant-intake exercise trial [11]. These are small, old, and still the cleanest demonstrations available that the same calories do different things to different bodies.

Second, **the body defends against the deficit**. Restriction reduces energy used for metabolic processes and activity while raising hunger and cutting fullness [12–14], the combination the article calls metabolic adaptation. DeBenedictis 2020 [14] is cited for the appetite half. This is framed as why adherence fails, not as an excuse — and it is the article's explanation for how GLP-1 agonists work: they reduce hunger enough to make the deficit sustainable.

**On exercise and expenditure the article presents both sides and refuses to pick.** Pontzer's constrained energy model [15] holds that total expenditure is defended within a narrow band; the additive model holds that exercise calories simply add. Their verdict — "the answer is somewhere in the middle," with compensation attenuating the extra burn and producing only modest TDEE changes — is supported by a block of eight references [16–23] including a systematic review of energy compensation predictors (Riou 2015 [20]), a dose-response trial in sedentary postmenopausal women (Church 2009 [21]), and Melanson 2013 on compensatory behavioural adaptations [23]. This is unusually honest handling of a live controversy.

**The RMR section is the strongest.** Roughly 80% of RMR tracks lean body mass [24], with about 20% of the variance unexplained by lean mass, race, sex, age, measurement error or environment and attributed to genetics [25,26]. Weight loss is estimated at ~75% fat and ~25% lean tissue [27]. Using Hall's figures — **19 kcal/kg/day for lean tissue and 4.5 kcal/kg/day for fat** [28] — a 10 kg loss at that composition predicts an RMR drop of about **81 kcal/day**; anything beyond that is adaptive thermogenesis [29]. And the muscle-versus-fat point is handled properly: a pound is a pound; the densities are **1.06 kg/L for muscle and 0.92 kg/L for fat** [32,33], so a kilogram of fat takes up more space. That is the whole of the "muscle weighs more" claim.

## Reason 2: water

Humans are about 60% water, ranging 45–75% by body composition; fat-free mass is 70–80% water and fat mass only 10%. A **2% shift in total body water in a 70 kg person is close to two pounds**, and Bunt 1989 [35] is cited for larger fluctuations in some people, particularly women. Regular exercise increases water retention as an adaptation, especially in heat and humidity — so the scale can rise in the first weeks of training with no change in tissue at all.

The hydration recommendations are clinical advice rather than trial findings, and are presented as such: establish a euhydrated reference by weighing first thing on three consecutive mornings after the bathroom and before eating or drinking; start workouts within **1%** of that; avoid losing more than **2%** during; do nothing special afterwards unless training twice a day, in which case replace **~1.5 L per kilogram lost, at 0.4–1.5 L/hour**. The evidence cited underneath is that hypohydration of ~2% or more impairs sweat rate, sweat onset and performance [36,37], plus the NATA fluid replacement position statement [38].

## Reason 3: the scale is fine, your reading of it is not

The distinction drawn is the right one: scales are accurate and precise, which does not make any single weight informative. A weekly rhythm exists — heavier on Sundays and Mondays, lighter on Fridays [39] — with variation of roughly 1–2% of bodyweight, modified by season and holidays [40–42], including a JAMA Network Open cohort of Australian adults [42]. The practical instruction is a rolling average over **two to four weeks** taken under similar conditions, and to make diet decisions from that trend only.

## What they recommend, and where it sits

The take-home actions: cut **200–300 kcal/day**; eat **1.4–1.6 g/kg protein** from lean, minimally processed sources; change the food environment; meet the exercise guidelines for lifting and conditioning; use a rolling average; and keep exercising regardless of what the scale does.

**Against the library's position on protein (1.6–1.8 g/kg), 1.4–1.6 sits just below the band** — a defensible number, and notable mainly because it disagrees with Barbell Medicine's own [protein guide](barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md), which recommends 2.3–3.1 g/kg for people who are lean and actively losing weight. Someone reading both articles gets a two-fold spread from one publisher, and neither cross-references the other. The library's verdict — that the upper half of the cutting range has no head-to-head trial behind it — makes the lower figure the better-supported one, which is the opposite of what the more detailed article recommends.

**Flagged, three things.**
1. **A clear mis-citation.** Reference [17], inside the block supporting compensatory reductions in energy expenditure, is Labbok 1997, a multicentre study of the **Lactational Amenorrhea Method** as contraception. It has nothing to do with energy expenditure. The surrounding citations are appropriate; this one is an error.
2. **Reference [27] is cited for a rule it exists to criticise.** Heymsfield 2014 is titled "Weight loss composition is one-fourth fat-free mass: a **critical review and critique** of this widely cited rule." The article uses it to support the 75/25 figure. It does add "though this varies significantly amongst individuals," which is roughly the paper's point, but the citation is doing the opposite of what its source argues.
3. **The DNP section carries no citations at all** — the history, the uncoupling mechanism, the 1930s use, the annual toxicity cases, and the comparison against GLP-1 agonists are all unreferenced in an article that otherwise cites 41 sources.

Minor: the satiety claim [3] rests on an RCT in patients with type 2 diabetes, a narrower population than the general statement it supports.

**Sells:** three free Barbell Medicine calculators (TDEE, calorie, macronutrient) are linked in the recommendations, along with their beginner programme article and four of their own podcast episodes, and the piece closes by directing readers to their support email. Nothing paid is named, but the advice routes the reader into their own tools at every action step.
