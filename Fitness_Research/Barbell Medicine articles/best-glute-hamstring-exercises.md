# Eight Glute and Hamstring Exercises, One of Which Is Backed by Measured Growth

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The 8 Best Glute and Hamstring Exercises" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-glute-and-hamstring-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-02-28 · 6,902 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- Eight exercises, tiered rather than ranked: **primary** (squat, deadlift), **secondary** (hip thrust, Romanian deadlift), **tertiary** (step-up, split squat, hamstring curl, hip extension). Pick 4–5 per week — one primary, two secondary, one to two tertiary — at RPE 7–8 (2–3 reps in reserve), 8–9 for hamstring curls.
- **Of the eight, exactly one selection is supported by measured hypertrophy: the seated hamstring curl**, on [Maeo 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000002523), a 12-week training study that found greater hamstring growth when the muscle was trained at long lengths. Every other exercise-level claim in the article rests on **EMG** (muscle excitation) or on narrative reviews and coaching articles.
- The article says this itself, and well: EMG "doesn't correlate well with strength and hypertrophy results", so "modestly higher EMG levels of one exercise… are not particularly convincing" ([Vigotsky 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00985)). It then does not apply the caveat to its own eight entries.
- **The one hypertrophy outcome trial that would test the hip-thrust hype is cited — under the wrong exercise.** [Plotkin 2023](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.06.21.545949) found hip thrust and back squat training produced *similar* gluteal hypertrophy and similar deadlift carryover. It is attached to the back-extension paragraph, not to the hip-thrust paragraph whose "very useful for… the size of the gluteus maximus" claim it directly bears on.
- Injury framing is the house position and is defensible: 2–4 injuries per 1,000 training hours, most resolving in under two weeks. But the citations in that section are visibly misaligned, and one paragraph is duplicated verbatim with a different set of reference numbers.
- Their **REP model** (Repeatable, Efficient, Points of performance) deliberately refuses to prescribe "neutral spine" or "knees behind toes". Presented as a reasoned position, not as demonstrated injury prevention.

## The eight, and what each ranking actually rests on

**Squats (primary, 3–4 × 4–6).** The claim that squats "carry over to sport" cites [Myer 2014](https://doi.org/10.1519/ssc.0000000000000103), a *Strength & Conditioning Journal* proposal for assessing squat technique — a review article, not a transfer trial. **Activation/expert-opinion, not outcome.** The article separately says bar position (high vs low) makes little difference to hypertrophy or general strength, which is the honest reading.

**Deadlifts (primary, 3–4 × 4–6).** The muscles-and-force-transfer description cites [Del Vecchio 2018](https://doi.org/10.15406/mojypt.2018.03.00042), a narrative piece in a low-tier journal; the squat-vs-deadlift mechanics claim cites [Martín-Fuentes 2020](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0229507), an **EMG systematic review** of deadlift variants. **Activation, not outcome.**

**Hip thrusts (secondary, 3–4 × 6–10).** "Very useful for improving hip extension strength and power as well as the size of the hip extensors" cites [Contreras 2011](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0b013e31822fa09d) — a technique article by the exercise's main promoter — and [Delgado 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003290), an **EMG comparison** of squat, RDL and hip thrust during hip extension. **Activation, not outcome**, and the size half of that sentence is not supported by either paper.

**Romanian deadlifts (secondary, 3–4 × 6–10).** "A great variation to train the hamstrings, glutes and erectors for both strength and size" cites Martín-Fuentes again — the same **EMG review**. **Activation, not outcome.**

**Step-ups (tertiary, 2–4 × 8–12 per leg).** "Proven to have similar levels of muscle excitation as… squats, deadlifts and hip thrusts" cites [Neto 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32132843/), a systematic review of **gluteus maximus EMG**. Here the article's wording is at least accurate — it says *excitation*, not growth — but "proven" is heavy for a review of acute signal amplitudes. **Activation, not outcome.**

**Split squats (tertiary, 2–4 × 10–15 per leg).** The claim that split squats "engage [the hip extensors] more than regular squats" carries **no in-text citation at all**. The reference list does contain [Arakawa 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36542838/) — greater hip moments in rear-foot-elevated split squats than back squats at matched relative load — which is the moment-arm study that would support it, but it is never cited in the body. **Moment arms at best, and unlinked.**

**Hamstring curls (tertiary, 2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9).** The seated variation is recommended over lying and standing because it "has been shown to produce great results for muscle hypertrophy", citing [Maeo 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000002523) — a 12-week resistance training study using MRI-measured muscle volume, comparing training at long versus short hamstring lengths, in which the long-length (seated) condition produced greater hamstring hypertrophy. **This is the only exercise selection in the article backed by measured growth**, and note what it is actually evidence *for*: muscle length during the movement, not the leg-curl machine as such.

**Hip extensions / 45-degree back extension (tertiary, 2–4 × 10–20).** Recommended as a low-skill, low-load option "useful in helping those with low back sensitivity", citing [Plotkin 2023](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.06.21.545949) — which is a hip-thrust-versus-back-squat hypertrophy trial and says nothing about back extensions or low back pain. As above, this is the article's one glute *outcome* study, and it is filed against the wrong claim. Note it was a **bioRxiv preprint** when cited.

## The EMG caveat, written and then not used

The "How to Choose the Right Exercises" section is the strongest part of the article. It names the failure mode directly — claims like "hip thrusts are the *best* exercise for glute hypertrophy" are "generally supported by EMG studies, which measure muscle excitation", and excitation has no established link to muscle protein synthesis or to measured growth ([Vigotsky 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00985)). It concludes there is no universally best exercise and recommends training a muscle through several ranges of motion and rep schemes.

That is exactly right, and it is in tension with the seven preceding sections, five of which justify an exercise with an EMG paper. The recommendation that survives the caveat is the variety recommendation, not the tier list. **Read the tiers as a coaching heuristic — training economy, fatigue cost, skill demand — not as an evidence ranking.**

## The injury section, and its citation drift

The claims: most resistance training runs **2–4 injuries per 1,000 hours**; most injuries are not catastrophic, resolve without specialist care, and have **average symptom duration under two weeks**; **19% of injuries in men's professional football over 21 seasons were hamstring injuries**.

The sources behind those numbers are [Keogh & Winwood 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0575-0), an epidemiological review across weight-training sports, and [Raske & Norlin 2002](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465020300021701), a retrospective survey of elite Swedish weight and power lifters. Both support the rate figures. Three problems, stated plainly because they affect how much weight to put on this section:

1. **The paragraph is duplicated.** The same two sentences appear twice, a few lines apart, once with markers [16]/[15] and once with [17]/[18].
2. **The symptom-duration claim is attached to [Matsunaga 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbmt.2021.05.013)**, a study of gluteus maximus fatigue and muscle synergies during single-leg landing. That paper cannot support it.
3. **The 19% football figure is attributed to Keogh & Winwood**, a weight-training injury review, which is not where that number comes from.

The mapping extraction for this article passed its offset check, so these are the article's own attributions, not a parsing artefact. The underlying rate claims are still well supported by Keogh & Winwood and Raske; the wiring is what is loose.

## Technique: the REP model, and the circularity argument

They quote the standard definition of proper technique from a 2009 systematic review — correct speed, resistance, plane and range "to maximize adaptations and minimize risk of injury" — and correctly call it **circular**: it defines proper technique as whatever reduces injury, while the premises that particular velocities, planes and ranges reduce injury are not established. Their counter-example is that injury rates are similar in high-velocity and low-velocity barbell sports; weightlifters report 1.0–1.7 injuries per athlete per year against bodybuilders' 0.3–1.8 ([Colado & García-Massó 2009](https://doi.org/10.3810/psm.2009.06.1716)). *Note: the quoted 2009 review is Colado & García-Massó, but the article's marker on the quote points to Raske & Norlin — off by one.*

Their replacement, the **REP model**: technique should be **Repeatable** (ROM, joint angles, velocity and tempo broadly similar rep to rep, accepting genuine within-person variability — [Bartlett 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/14763140701322994), [Nordin & Dufek 2017](https://doi.org/10.1123/jab.2016-0097)), **Efficient** (no force production that doesn't move the bar), and meeting the exercise's **Points of performance**. It explicitly declines to prescribe neutral spine or knee position, on the grounds that those positions "do not appear to be uniquely risky independent of dosing".

**This is their position, and it is a recommendation rather than a demonstrated result.** The cited work supports movement variability being normal; it does not test whether coaching the REP model lowers injury rates against coaching neutral spine. The stronger reading — that specific positions are not risky — is one the cited papers do not reach.

## Overtraining

Overtraining syndrome is weeks-to-months of depressed performance ([Meeusen 2006](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461390600617717)); it appears rare and hard to induce with resistance training ([Grandou 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01242-2), a systematic review that is largely a methodological critique of the literature). Their illustration is a **case series** in which well-trained powerlifters and weightlifters worked up to a daily squat 1RM for a month and all improved. A case series with no control group is the weakest design in the article, and they use it appropriately — as a reason not to *worry* about overtraining, not as a programme recommendation.

## What they sell

The article ends by directing readers to Barbell Medicine's coaching and to their **injury and rehab coaching** service, and links throughout to their own blog and templates. The exercise recommendations are not obviously shaped by that, but the low-back-sensitivity framing around hip thrusts and back extensions leads directly to the rehab product.
