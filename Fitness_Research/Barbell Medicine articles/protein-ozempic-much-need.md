# Protein on a GLP-1: The Problem Is Total Intake, Not Protein Preference

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Protein on Ozempic: How Much Do You Need?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/protein-on-ozempic/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2026-05-11 · 2,863 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The headline number — GLP-1 users averaging **0.6 g/kg/day, about 38% of a 1.6 g/kg target** — comes from a **single conference abstract**: 116 users, 5,741 days of app-logged food records, presented at the 2026 European Congress on Obesity. Unpublished, self-reported, commercial dataset. It carries the whole article.
- The mechanism is not protein avoidance. **Protein stayed at ~20% of Calories, the same share as non-users**; total intake fell (1,102 vs 1,281 kcal/day) and meals were skipped. People are eating less of everything.
- Their target is **1.4–1.6 g/kg/day**, anchored to the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines' 1.2–1.6 g/kg band. **This is the lowest protein figure in this library** — below the ISSN's 1.4–2.0 floor in practice, below Henselmans' 1.8, and below the library's adjudicated 1.6–1.8 g/kg.
- The **triage order is the most useful thing in the article**, and it is explicitly a clinical judgement rather than a finding: training first, total Calories second, total protein third, distribution a distant fourth.
- Overreach: *"Without resistance training, the protein you do eat is mostly irrelevant for muscle preservation"* is stated absolutely, and nothing cited **in this article** tests it. The supporting argument is offloaded to their separate GLP-1 muscle-loss piece.
- **They sell the fix.** Whey Rx (their own whey isolate) is named twice, plus their macronutrient calculator and physician-led nutrition coaching.

## The gap, and what actually causes it
GLP-1 receptor agonists slow gastric emptying and blunt central appetite signalling. The article's contribution is to show what that does to a food log rather than to a hunger scale: three observable behaviours — total intake down, macronutrient *proportions* unchanged, meal skipping up across breakfast, lunch and dinner. That matters practically, because it means "eat more protein, less carbs" advice is aimed at the wrong problem. The problem is that a meal did not happen.

Feigenbaum adds a clinical caveat that the population data hide: in their coaching practice the appetite trajectory has no reliable timeline. Some users are worst in the first fortnight, some only after a dose escalation, some cycle. This is offered as observation, and labelled as such.

He also notes most users in the dataset reported little or no dietary guidance from a clinician. That is the article's implicit argument for coaching — reasonable, and also the thing they sell.

## How much, and where it sits in this library
The recommendation is 1.4–1.6 g/kg/day, reasoned from a **population guideline** (the DGA band of 1.2–1.6) rather than from a trial in dieting, training adults. That is worth marking, because the same authors' evidence-linked passage points the other way: the two papers cited for the "is more than 1.6 better?" debate are Morton's 2018 meta-analysis (the source of the ~1.6 g/kg plateau) and Helms 2014 (the case for *higher* intakes in resistance-trained athletes in a deficit).

Feigenbaum's answer is that the question is academic here, since 88% of the dataset was under 1.2 g/kg — the relevant move is 0.6 → 1.2, not 1.4 → 1.6. That is a fair triage argument. But **as a number it clashes with this library's adjudicated position of 1.6–1.8 g/kg**, and with the specific finding that a moderate deficit does not license *lowering* the target. Read it as a floor for a low-appetite population, not as a competing estimate of the requirement.

The one place they raise the per-meal emphasis is **anabolic resistance**, which they attribute to comorbidity and inactivity rather than to age itself — 30–40 g per meal matters more for these users. That is consistent with the library's position that the 20–30 g ceiling is dead but that distribution buys a few percent in specific populations, not in general.

## The triage, and the tactics
Training first: two to four resistance sessions a week are argued to do more for muscle retention than the difference between 1.0 and 1.6 g/kg. Calories second: 1.0 g/kg at 1,800 kcal beats 1.6 g/kg at 900. Protein third. Distribution fourth, and the honest reason given for spreading it is behavioural — people who eat protein often end up with a higher daily total, not that the mechanism pays.

The practical tactics are all appetite-shaped rather than physiology-shaped, and they are the article's real content: front-load 40–50 g into the meal where appetite still works (usually breakfast); eat the protein on the plate first; keep a 20–40 g liquid backup for days when solids fail (cold tolerates better than warm, sipped better than gulped); pick lean, protein-dense sources because total Calories are the binding constraint. Collagen is ruled out as a protein source, correctly, on muscle protein synthesis grounds.

For food aversions — commonest with chicken, eggs and dairy — the advice is rotation for two to four weeks rather than persistence. Pure clinical experience, presented as such.

**Too fast / too slow** is a prescriber conversation, not a supplement one: symptoms of low energy availability on one side, and on the other a dose that is too low or an older agent, referenced to the STEP-1 and SURMOUNT-1 trials.
