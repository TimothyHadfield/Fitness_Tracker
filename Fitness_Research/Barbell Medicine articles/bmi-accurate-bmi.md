# BMI's Real Failure Is Under-Diagnosis, Not the Muscular Guy Who Reads Obese

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Is BMI Accurate? A Physician's Guide to BMI" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/is-bmi-accurate/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2026-08-09 · 4,705 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **BMI is highly specific and poorly sensitive.** Pooling 25 studies and ~32,000 people, when BMI called someone obese it was right ~90% of the time; when someone genuinely had excess adiposity, BMI caught only about half. At a cutoff of 30 the split sharpens: **~97% specificity, ~42% sensitivity** ([Okorodudu 2010](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2010.5)). In >13,000 US adults the same pattern held — BMI ≥30 flagged excess fat correctly in ~95% of men and ~99% of women, but caught only **36% of men and 49% of women** who actually had it ([Romero-Corral 2008](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2008.11)).
- **The gym's favourite complaint is the small error.** Misclassification runs overwhelmingly toward missing fat, not inventing it — and in ~5,000 adults, people above BMI 30 carried the same cardiometabolic risk whether lean mass was high or low ([Colpitts 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.13605)). A lean muscular outlier is settled in a minute with a tape measure.
- **BMI survives on outcome data, not precision.** 239 prospective studies, >10 million people, four continents: mortality lowest at BMI 20–25, rising above and below ([Global BMI Mortality Collaboration 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30175-1)). In 57 studies and ~900,000 adults restricted appropriately, each **5-point rise above ~22.5–25 carried ~30% higher all-cause mortality**, and BMI 40–45 cost **8–10 years of median survival**, comparable to smoking ([Prospective Studies Collaboration 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(09)60318-4)).
- **The cutoffs are admittedly arbitrary.** The 1995 WHO report that fixed 25 and 30 described them as chosen for convenience from largely European-descent populations; the WHO's own 2004 consultation recommends **23 and 27.5** for South, Southeast and East Asian populations ([WHO Expert Consultation 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(03)15268-3)).
- **The recommendation:** BMI plus waist circumference (or waist-to-height under 0.5), then labs if flags are up. This matches the 2025 *Lancet Diabetes Endocrinology* Commission ([Rubino 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(24)00316-4)), which they follow closely and cite properly.

## The screening-test framing, and why it does the work
The article's organising move is to treat BMI explicitly as a screening test and then apply the standard sensitivity/specificity vocabulary to it. Once you do that, most of the public argument dissolves, because a screening test is allowed to be imprecise in one direction as long as you know which direction.

BMI's direction is clear and consistent across both cited datasets: **a positive is trustworthy, a negative is not.** Cross 30 and you almost certainly do have excess adiposity. Sit below it and you may or may not — roughly half of people with genuinely excess fat are sitting there, reassured. The clinical consequence they draw is that a normal BMI should never close an inquiry when other flags are up (family history of type 2 diabetes, high-risk ethnic background, sedentary pattern, large waist).

This is the correct reading of the two accuracy papers and it is the article's strongest section. It is also a specifically clinical argument — a screening test is judged by what it is used for, not by whether it is a good measurement — and it is the kind of reasoning this library gets from no other source.

## Why the misses cluster where they do
Three explanatory ideas carry the argument, and only the third is cited.

**Normal-weight obesity ("skinny fat")** — a BMI in the healthy range with a high fat percentage, especially visceral. BMI measures size, not composition, so it is blind to this. These people carry insulin-resistance, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia and cardiovascular risk tracking overt obesity.

**Visceral versus subcutaneous fat, and the personal fat threshold.** Subcutaneous fat is the safe depot; visceral fat, packed around liver, pancreas and intestines, is metabolically active and inflammatory. The personal fat threshold model says each person has a roughly genetically set subcutaneous storage limit — under it, a fairly high body-fat level may do little harm; over it, fat spills over into liver (fatty liver, hepatic insulin resistance) and pancreas (impaired beta-cell function). That spillover, not a scale number, is what tips someone into type 2 diabetes, and it explains why the threshold sits at different weights for different people.

**Age.** BMI's sensitivity for detecting high body fat against DXA is said to fall to roughly a third in people over 60 and to keep dropping — sarcopenic obesity is precisely the state a normal BMI hides.

**Flagged:** the personal fat threshold model, the visceral/subcutaneous risk contrast, the older-adult sensitivity figure, the waist thresholds (94/80 cm concerning, 102/88 cm markedly higher risk) and the 2023 AMA policy are all **presented without citation**. These are not fringe claims — most are well supported in the wider literature — but the article's ten references sit almost entirely under its accuracy and mortality claims, and the uncited material is disproportionately the material that supports its own recommended workflow. In an article whose thesis is about the discipline of evidence, that asymmetry is worth naming.

## The "BMI is racist" question, handled better than most
Feigenbaum splits the criticism in two, and this is genuinely careful reasoning.

The **weak version** blames Quetelet and the formula. He rejects it: Quetelet devised a population statistic around 1832 to describe "the average man" as part of social physics, from European male measurements, and never intended individual diagnosis ([Eknoyan 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/ndt/gfm517)). Faulting the arithmetic for a job it was never assigned is a category error. Keys revived it in 1972, testing it against other weight-for-height indices in >7,000 men, named it, and endorsed it **for population research** while saying plainly it was imperfect for individuals and that underwater weighing was better but too slow and costly for surveys ([Keys 1972](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9681(72)90027-6)).

The **strong version** blames the 20th-century institutions that took a European-derived population tool, imposed arbitrary universal cutoffs, and applied it to individuals worldwide. He accepts this, and points to the ethnicity-specific Asian thresholds as the remedy already in place.

He then makes the sharper point: the remedy the criticism supports is *pair BMI with waist circumference and clinical context*, not *discard BMI*. And because BMI's real failure is missing hidden fat, a reflexive "throw it out" narrative catches fewer at-risk people, working against the group it means to help.

**Flagged, mildly:** "BMI is neither racist nor sexist" is a rhetorical framing rather than a finding, and the sexist half is never addressed at all despite the sensitivity figures differing substantially by sex (36% in men versus 49% in women in Romero-Corral). The critique deserved the same two-part treatment the race question got.

## What they recommend, and how it differs from what they showed
The three-step workflow — pair BMI with waist, stratify overall risk, let labs and history be the tiebreaker — is **clinical advice**, not a demonstrated result. No study cited here tests whether the workflow improves outcomes over BMI alone. What is cited is that the 2025 Commission recommends exactly this: BMI as a screening and population tool only, with excess adiposity confirmed by direct fat measurement or at least one additional anthropometric measure, and adiposity assumable above BMI 40. The Commission's clinical/preclinical obesity distinction — excess adiposity already impairing an organ versus excess adiposity with function preserved — is reported accurately.

The suggested lab panel (fasting glucose, ideally fasting insulin, lipids with triglycerides and HDL, and the triglyceride-to-HDL ratio as an insulin-resistance marker) is uncited and is the most clearly "we recommend" element in the piece. Fasting insulin and TG:HDL are reasonable clinical practice, not guideline-mandated screening.

**Also worth noting:** the article does not report an effect size for waist circumference's added predictive value over BMI — it asserts waist-to-height "can match or beat BMI as a predictor" without a citation, in a piece that is otherwise scrupulous about the strength of BMI's own outcome record. The companion article on the [body roundness index](should-bri-replace-bmi.md) covers the same ground with 27 references.

## Commercial note
The article closes by directing readers to Barbell Medicine's free BMI calculator and then to their paid coaching service. Internal links point to their Vital 5 action plan, waist-measurement guide and sarcopenia article. The calculator is free and the clinical advice is not distorted toward the sale, but the piece is search-optimised content ending in a coaching funnel and should be read as such.
