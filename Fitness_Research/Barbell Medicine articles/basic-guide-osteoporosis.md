# Osteoporosis Is a Call to Load the Skeleton, Not to Protect It

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "A Basic Guide to Osteoporosis" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/a-basic-guide-to-osteoporosis/)) · Austin Baraki · 2021-11-05 · 1,614 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- Bone is **living, remodelling tissue** that adapts to load and atrophies without it — the same logic as muscle. Baraki explicitly rejects the "wear and tear / bones wear out" model, and uses disuse and spaceflight as the demonstration.
- **Osteoporosis is asymptomatic.** Aches and pains in bones or joints are not a sign of it. It is found by screening or by a fracture, and there is no symptom in between.
- **Screening is targeted, not universal.** The standing recommendation he gives is DEXA for **all women over 65**; younger women and men need a specific risk factor to justify it, and for most people testing is "a waste of time and resources".
- **Load specificity matters for bone.** Swimming, aqua-aerobics and cycling do not deliver the forces bone responds to; nor does very light weight for very high reps. His prescription is heavier loads for fewer reps, applied to spine, shoulders, hips and legs — with **no particular exercise or program required**.
- **The framing argument is the article's real payload:** telling patients their bones are "brittle" or "fragile" produces fear and avoidance, which is the opposite of the behaviour that helps. "You are not destined to be fragile."

## Risk factors and diagnosis
The risk list is conventional and clinically useful: older age, smoking, alcohol, very low body weight, and female sex — particularly after menopause or after the ovaries stop working or are removed. For men he gives a concrete threshold: **testosterone below roughly 200 ng/dL**, or treatment with androgen blockers for prostate cancer.

Secondary causes he names: severe vitamin D deficiency, thyroid and parathyroid disorders, kidney disease, intestinal disease. On medications he draws a distinction most patients get wrong — **long-term oral steroids (e.g. prednisone) and high-dose inhaled steroids contribute; nasal steroid sprays for allergies do not.**

Where diet, exercise and habits have been addressed and osteoporosis persists, he sends the reader to a doctor to estimate fracture risk with [FRAX](https://www.sheffield.ac.uk/FRAX/) and weigh vitamin D and anti-osteoporosis drugs — noting only that some prevent breakdown and others promote building, without naming classes. Explicitly: needing medication "should not be viewed as failure".

## What the diet advice actually is
A general healthy-eating template rather than anything bone-specific: plenty of fruit and vegetables, plenty of fibre, palm-sized protein portions at each meal, unsaturated fats from oily fish and nuts, little animal saturated fat, little or no ultra-processed food. The only bone-targeted elements are **calcium and protein**, with calcium sources listed as leafy greens, dairy, tofu, beans and fortified foods. No intakes, no thresholds, no numbers.

## Where the evidence is missing
**This article cites nothing at all** — no reference list, no linked studies, no named papers. That is a real limitation for a clinical guide, and it lands hardest on two claims.

**"Exercise may be the most important thing people can do to reduce the risk of osteoporosis."** This is stated flatly and is the article's strongest claim. It is very likely too strong. In established osteoporosis, pharmacotherapy produces substantially larger reductions in fracture risk than exercise does, and exercise trials typically move bone mineral density by low single-digit percentages. Exercise has good evidence for **reducing falls**, which is a genuinely important and partly separate mechanism, and the article does mention fall risk — but the sentence as written invites a reader to substitute lifting for treatment, which the rest of the article does not actually endorse.

**"We have studies of high-intensity weightlifting programs in older patients with osteoporosis showing that they can re-build their bone strength without causing dangerous fractures during exercise."** This is the load-bearing safety claim of the whole piece and it is **completely uncited**. The reader is asked to take on trust both that such trials exist and what they found. This library has already encountered a bone-density claim in this corpus propped up by a two-person case study, so an uncited "we have studies" is exactly the sentence that needs a reference and does not have one.

The distinction to hold: **the evidence-based part is "loading builds bone and disuse loses it", which is uncontroversial.** The part that is clinical advice rather than demonstrated evidence is "therefore heavy lifting is the most important intervention and is safe in people who already have osteoporosis" — reasonable, consistent with what the field believes, and not supported by anything the article shows you.

## Products
The article points readers to Barbell Medicine's own **[Beginner Prescription](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-beginner-prescription/)** as a starting point. It is a free article rather than a paid template, but it is Barbell Medicine's standard entry funnel toward their paid programmes and coaching, and the reader should know that. The recommendation is otherwise agnostic — he is explicit that machines, dumbbells or barbells all work and that there are no magic programs.
