# Osteoporosis Is a Loading Problem as Much as a Drug Problem — Argued Without a Single Citation

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "A Basic Guide to Osteoporosis" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/a-basic-guide-to-osteoporosis/)) · Austin Baraki · 2021-11-05 · 1,614 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **The framing claim: bones are living, remodelling tissue that adapts to load and atrophies without it** — not a finite resource that "wears out." Baraki uses disuse (sedentary living, spaceflight) as the demonstration.
- **Osteoporosis is asymptomatic.** Aches and pains in bones or joints are not a sign of it. Absent screening, it is usually found after a fracture.
- **Load specificity is the practical message.** Swimming, aqua-aerobics and cycling will not build bone the way lifting does, and "lifting very light weights for very high repetitions will not provide the same benefit as lifting heavier weights for fewer repetitions." No specific programme is required — anything loading spine, shoulders, hips and legs, on machines, dumbbells or barbells.
- **They push back on "brittle" and "fragile" as clinical language**, arguing it produces fear that stops people doing the one thing most likely to help. This is the nocebo position Barbell Medicine argues elsewhere in this library, applied to bone.
- **The article cites nothing at all.** Including for its single load-bearing evidential claim.

## The clinical content, condensed

**Risk factors:** older age, smoking, alcohol, very low body weight. Women more than men, especially after menopause or earlier loss of ovarian function. Men with **testosterone below 200 ng/dL** or on androgen-deprivation therapy for prostate cancer. Also severe vitamin D deficiency, thyroid and parathyroid disorders, kidney disease, intestinal disease, and **long-term oral steroids (prednisone) or high-dose inhaled steroids** — with the useful specific that **nasal steroid sprays for allergies do not cause problems**.

**Screening:** not everyone. "Most people are at such low risk that testing is a waste of time and resources" — a restraint claim worth noting from a source that sells fitness services. The standing recommendation given is **DEXA for all women over 65**; younger women and men with relevant conditions should decide individually with a doctor.

**Management:** a general healthy dietary pattern (fruit and vegetables, fibre, palm-sized protein at each meal, unsaturated fats, little animal saturated fat, little ultra-processed food), with attention to calcium and protein; stopping smoking; cutting alcohol. Then loaded exercise. Then, where needed, **FRAX** to estimate fracture risk, vitamin D, and anti-osteoporosis medication — some antiresorptive, some anabolic — used alongside diet and loading. Their line that needing medication "should not be viewed as failure, but simply as another tool" is a good corrective to the lifestyle-purist reading their audience is prone to.

## Evidence versus recommendation

**The load-bearing claim is unsupported as written.** "We have studies of high-intensity weightlifting programs in older patients with osteoporosis showing that they can re-build their bone strength without causing dangerous fractures during exercise" — no study named, no reference given. This is the sentence that licenses the whole heavy-lifting recommendation for a fearful reader, and the one place a citation was genuinely needed. Such trials do exist; a reader cannot check that this claim rests on them.

**"Exercise may be the most important thing people can do"** is a ranking, not a measured comparison. Pharmacotherapy has hard fracture-endpoint RCT evidence; exercise trials here mostly report **bone mineral density**, a surrogate. As a recommendation it is reasonable; as a comparative fact it outruns the evidence.

**The light-versus-heavy claim is asserted**, and a reader coming from this library's hypertrophy material may think it contradicts the finding that roughly 5 to 35 reps grow muscle comparably. It does not — bone responds to peak strain magnitude and rate, muscle to tension near failure. But the article gives no evidence for its version, so hold it as mechanism.

**Sells:** links to their free **Beginner Prescription** and to a Barbell Medicine article on youth resistance training. No paid product is pitched, though the Beginner Prescription is an entry point to their templates.
