# Weight Regain Is Physiology, Not Character

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Why Weight Regain Is Not a Failure of Willpower" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/why-weight-regain-is-not-a-failure-of-willpower/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2026-07-21 · 4,849 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The argument is structured as a falsifiable claim and a test: **if obesity were a conscious fight against temptation, it would track the things discipline produces. It does not.** The evidence they bring is heritability, post-diet physiology, and a sibling-comparison study of cognitive ability.
- **Heritability of BMI is roughly 40–70% of between-person variation** [5], from adoption [3] and twins-reared-apart [4] designs. Their handling of this is unusually careful: heritability describes variance between people, not the share of *your* body that is genetic, and susceptibility only shows up if the environment triggers it.
- **After weight loss the body raises hunger and lowers the burn, and holds both there.** [Sumithran 2011](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1105816) [9]: 50 people, ten weeks on a 500–550 kcal formula diet, ~13.5 kg lost; a year later hunger hormones were still up, satiety hormones still down, and self-rated hunger was **higher than before the diet started**.
- **Metabolic adaptation persisted for six years in the Biggest Loser cohort** [2] — resting metabolism ~610 kcal/day below baseline at the end of the 30-week competition, ~700 kcal/day below at six years, some hundreds below what body size alone predicts.
- **GLP-1 drugs are the argument's strongest move**: STEP 1 [18] gave ~15% loss over 68 weeks versus ~2% on placebo, SURMOUNT-1 [19] ~21% at the top tirzepatide dose, against ~3% average maintained loss at five years from lifestyle alone [15,16,17]. Their inference — that if turning one appetite dial makes eating this much easier, appetite was the thing running the show — is rhetorically effective but is inference, not measurement.
- **Where they say effort belongs:** design the food environment, keep muscle with protein and resistance training, and treat the physiology with medication when warranted. Two of those three are recommendations, not findings.

## The genetics argument, done well

The adoption study [3] — 540 Danish adult adoptees — is the cleanest natural experiment available: adoptees share genes with parents they never lived with and a kitchen with parents who fed them for eighteen years. Adult weight tracked the biological parents; the adoptive parents had close to no relationship with it. The twin study [4] — 93 identical pairs reared apart — produced BMI correlations of roughly 0.66–0.70, close to twins raised together.

The paragraph immediately after is the best-written thing in the article, and it corrects the error this evidence usually produces: 40–70% is the share of *differences between people* that genes explain, not a verdict that most of your body is genetic and the rest is your fault. Heritable is not fixed. Genes set the guardrails; environment decides where inside them you land.

The population argument follows: US adult obesity went from about 13% in the early 1960s to around 40%, severe obesity from about 1% to nearly 10%. Two generations is not enough time for a population's DNA to move, so the change is environmental. **Citation problem:** that sentence is marked `[8]`, which is Speakman's set-point/settling-point theory paper — it does not contain 2021–2023 US prevalence data. Reference [6], the NCHS prevalence report that plainly does, is **never cited anywhere in the article**. The prevalence figures are correct; the marker points at the wrong paper.

They also prefer a **defended range** with an upper and lower guardrail over the older single set point [8], and say the range model is "still debated" — the right level of confidence for a theoretical framework.

## What is evidence here, and what is rhetoric

**Evidence.** Sumithran [9] on persistent hormonal adaptation. Fothergill [2] on persistent metabolic adaptation. Wright 2023 [12], the sibling comparison — more than 12,000 siblings across four longitudinal studies, where the population-level "smarter is leaner" correlation survives between strangers and **shrinks to indistinguishable from zero between siblings raised in the same family**. That is the single strongest piece in the article against the character story, because it removes shared background rather than arguing around it.

**Rhetoric.** Taft [11] and Churchill are anecdotes. They illustrate the claim; they cannot test it, and the article's framing ("nobody looks at Taft or Churchill and concludes they lacked willpower") is persuasion. The bodybuilder-and-ultra-endurance-athlete point is a clinical observation with no citation. The NBA-height analogy comes from a popular book [7], and the one-in-six figure it rests on is a widely repeated estimate rather than a measured statistic.

**Numbers the article omits.** The Fothergill metabolic-adaptation result — the emotional centre of the piece — comes from **14 contestants**. The article never says so. They do give the honest caveat, citing Hall's own reinterpretation [10], that the contestants' large sustained exercise increases may account for part of the suppression and the figure may not transfer to an ordinary diet. That is a real concession, properly cited, and it is to their credit.

**Flagged.** For the claim that a smaller version of the same effect follows ordinary moderate weight loss, they cite one systematic review [22] and describe the direction as "remarkably consistent." That review's title is a question — *Does adaptive thermogenesis occur after weight loss in adults?* — which is a softer framing than the sentence it is supporting. The direction claim is probably right; "remarkably consistent" is stronger than one review titled as an open question will carry.

## GLP-1s and the muscle-loss claim

They cite receptor localisation and food-cue brain activity in humans [13] and arcuate-nucleus mechanism work [14] for how the drugs act, then the outcome trials [18,19]. The comparison against lifestyle [15,16,17] — about half reach 5% loss within a year, fewer than a quarter reach 10%, ~80% of lost weight back by five years, ~3% average maintained — is the context that makes the drug effect look as large as it is, and it is honestly sourced to three named trials and a meta-analysis.

Two claims in this section are weaker than the surrounding material:

- **"Muscle loss is mostly a measurement problem."** They assert that DXA overstates lean loss because it cannot separate muscle from water, glycogen and intra-tissue fat. That sentence carries **no citation**, and it is a substantive technical claim. The functional evidence they do cite [20] is SEMALEAN: a year of maximum-dose semaglutide with grip strength improving without training and sarcopenic obesity falling from about half to about a third of patients. That supports *function held up*, not *DXA overstates lean loss*.
- **"Enough protein and a little resistance training preserve most of the muscle that matters"** is uncited here, and the article links to their own GLP-1-and-muscle article rather than to a study. It is a reasonable clinical position, consistent with the protein and training material elsewhere in this library, but it is presented as established.

To their credit they explicitly refuse the "hormones, not calories" framing: the hormones govern hunger, satiety and food behaviour, and **none of them act independently of energy balance**. That keeps this article consistent with the rest of the library rather than in conflict with it.

## The agency problem, and what they sell

They raise the strongest objection to their own thesis — that telling people their weight is set by genes and environment can produce fatalism and shame [21] — and answer it with a distinction rather than a dodge: you cannot will yourself less hungry any more than less thirsty, so effort aimed at the dinner-table fight is aimed at the lever that barely moves; agency is real but belongs upstream. That is a clinical recommendation, clearly labelled as their view ("we think").

The article closes by linking to Barbell Medicine's paid online physician consultations, their training programmes (named in the "hold onto muscle" section), and their coaching service. The recommendation to "treat the physiology when it warrants it" routes to a consultation they sell. The underlying position is mainstream obesity medicine, but the reader should see the route.
