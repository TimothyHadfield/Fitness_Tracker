# When Training Hurts: Find the Load You Can Tolerate, Not the Rest Day

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Pain in Training: What To Do?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/pain-in-training-what-do/)) · Austin Baraki · 2019-12-09 · 3,652 words
**Topic:** Pain science

## Bottom line
- The article's organising claim is that **pain is a protective output influenced by biological, psychological and social inputs, not a readout of tissue damage**. The strongest empirical support offered is the spread of pain ratings to an identical noxious stimulus: 321 adults given a standardised 48 °C heat stimulus rated it anywhere from **4 to 100 out of 100** ([Fillingim 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27902569/)).
- **Absolute rest is the approach they explicitly reject** for routine training aches — it detrains you, so you return wanting loads you are no longer adapted to. This specific claim is asserted without a citation in this article.
- The practical core is the **"entry point"**: find a dose of the movement that leaves symptoms *improved or stable over the following 24–48 hours*. Anything that markedly worsens symptoms during or after the session is too much.
- Their ordered levers: **cut absolute load first** (and further than feels reasonable — the worked example takes a 365 lb squatter down to **85–95 lb for sets of 12–15 with a 3-0-3 tempo**), then alter range of motion, then change the exercise. Frequency starts at **2–3 non-consecutive days per week**.
- "Pain free" is explicitly ruled out as a short-term goal. Expect fluctuation; expect **months, not days**, for tendinopathy or radiculopathy.

## What the cited evidence actually supports
Baraki opens against what he calls the "body-as-machine" model — hurt equals harm, so painful activity should be avoided. The papers he attaches to this are mostly about *beliefs*, not tissue: [Bunzli 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28704621/) on how people with back pain make sense of pain-related fear, and [Setchell 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29149847/), a cross-sectional survey of what people believe causes their own persistent back pain. These establish that the mechanical model is *widely held*, which is the claim he makes with them. They do not by themselves establish that it is wrong.

The work carrying the heavier load is elsewhere. On technique and lifting posture he cites two systematic reviews published either side of the article: [Saraceni 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31775556/), a systematic review with meta-analysis asking whether lumbar flexion during lifting is related to low back pain, and [Nolan 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31730537/), a systematic review asking whether lifting technique differs between people with and without back pain. Both find the link is far weaker than the industry assumes. On imaging he cites [Brinjikji 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25430861/), a systematic review of spinal degeneration findings in *asymptomatic* people, plus a meta-analysis ([Zhong 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28072796/)) and a systematic review ([Chiu 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25009200/)) on spontaneous resorption of lumbar disc herniations. On the cost of clinician language, [Darlow 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24218376/) on the enduring impact of what clinicians say, and [Woby 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17218132/) on self-efficacy mediating between pain-related fear and outcome.

That is a reasonable evidence base for a specific, defensible claim: **imaging findings and lifting posture predict symptoms poorly, and fearful beliefs predict outcomes well.**

**Flagged overreach.** The article moves from that to "it is difficult — if not impossible — to establish physical positions that are inherently 'bad' across the population". That is a stronger claim than the cited reviews support: they show technique does not separate people with pain from people without it *at group level*, which is not the same as showing no position carries risk for any individual under any load. Baraki half-concedes this himself two paragraphs later ("there are absolute limits to tissue tolerance"), and pins the residual risk on high-velocity unpredictable field-sport movements rather than the gym — a plausible clinical intuition, offered without evidence. The dismissal of cadaver studies ("corpses can't adapt") is a fair point about external validity stated as if it settled the matter.

## The entry point, in numbers
The rule is a *symptom trajectory* rule, not a pain-threshold rule: pick a stimulus that leaves you the same or better 24–48 hours later. The most common failure he names is under-reducing — the lifter who drops 365 to 315, still hurts, and refuses to go to 225 or 135 or the empty bar. Their stated preference is to **undershoot deliberately**.

Two mechanisms force load down without changing the exercise: push reps up to 12–15, and add tempo (a 3-count eccentric and 3-count concentric, or an isolated 6-count eccentric). The worked example — 365 × 5 becoming 85 × 15 at 3-0-3 — is roughly a 75% load reduction, which is the point being made.

If nothing is tolerable at any load, they modify range of motion (block/pin deadlifts, box or pin squats, board/floor/pin presses), with the caveat that **a shortened range is not a licence to load heavier**. If that fails, change the exercise, including to unilateral work. They deliberately use a *variety* of bilateral and unilateral movements because people compensate away from a painful side.

Sample template for non-specific knee pain in the squat, 2–3 non-consecutive days: 3-0-3 tempo low bar squat 12 @ RPE 6/7/8, then 3-0-3 split squat 15 @ RPE 6/7/8; day two the same with high bar squat and weighted lunge.

## Progression and expectation management
Increments are deliberately vague and they say so: 2.5–5 lb, or 5–10%, described in the article itself as "arbitrary suggestions" given to people who want a number. If symptoms are flat, they may hold load constant for several sessions to let desensitisation happen before adding weight. As load rises, reps come down (12 → 10 → 8 → 6) and tempo accelerates back to normal.

Symptom flares are attributed to dose *and* to biopsychosocial inputs — sleep, stress, concurrent illness — which is consistent with the model but also makes the framework hard to falsify: any flare can be explained either by too much load or by a bad week.

For recurrent symptoms after a successful cycle, they blame the programme, and name the pattern: rigid models with excessive intensity, excessive volume, no fatigue management, and hyper-specific exercise selection. Their fix is RPE-based autoregulation plus more exercise variation.

**This is recommendation, not evidence.** Almost nothing in Part 2 — the entry-point rule, the 24–48-hour window, the tempo strategy, the 2–3 exposures per week, the ordering of the levers — carries a citation. It is coherent clinical reasoning from clinicians who do this daily, and it is broadly what contemporary physiotherapy guidance says, but it is presented in the same voice as the referenced pain-science material and should be read as a different kind of claim.
