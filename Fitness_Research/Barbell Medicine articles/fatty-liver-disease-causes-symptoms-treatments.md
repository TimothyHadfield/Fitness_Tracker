# Fatty Liver Disease: A Metabolic Condition That Happens to Sit in the Liver

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Fatty Liver Disease: Causes, Symptoms, and Treatments" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/fatty-liver-disease/)) · Austin Baraki · 2025-08-05 · 4,175 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- MASLD (formerly NAFLD) now affects an estimated **25% of adults worldwide**, with **5–6%** at the steatohepatitis stage. It is the most common chronic liver disease and the fastest-growing cause of liver-related death, and most people who have it do not know.
- **Fibrosis severity, not fat or inflammation, is the prognostic variable.** Death risk from liver disease is **50–80× higher** with advanced fibrosis than with MASH and no significant fibrosis. Everything in diagnosis and treatment is organised around that fact.
- **Normal liver enzymes do not rule it out** — ALT and AST are normal in more than half of MASLD patients and correlate poorly with severity. This is the single most actionable clinical point in the article.
- Weight loss is dose-responsive: **≥10% body weight loss produced MASH resolution in 90%** and fibrosis regression in 45%; ~7% loss cuts liver fat by about 45%. Steatosis is easy to reverse, established fibrosis is not.
- **Saturated fat looks worse than sugar for liver fat specifically** — an isocaloric diet at 20% of energy from saturated fat raised liver fat ~40%, while the same energy from simple sugars did not. Baraki does not let this become an anti-sugar claim.

## The disease, and the reframing

The organising claim is that MASLD is **not a liver disease that causes metabolic problems, but a metabolic disease that manifests in the liver**. The nomenclature change matters here — non-alcoholic fatty liver disease was defined by what it was not, metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease by what it is.

The staging, which the rest of the article depends on:

- **MASL (simple steatosis)** — fat in >5% of hepatocytes, with or without mild inflammation.
- **MASH** — steatosis plus hepatocyte inflammation, degeneration and cell death.
- **Fibrosis → cirrhosis** — progressive scarring; cirrhosis is irreversible.

Alcohol-related fatty liver is separated out by threshold: more than 20 g ethanol/day for women and 30 g/day for men, or more than 14 and 21 standard drinks/week respectively.

Prevalence figures given: ~25% of adults worldwide, 5–6% with MASH, 7.6% of children generally but **34% of children attending paediatric obesity clinics**, and around $100 billion in annual US cost. Cardiovascular disease, not liver failure, is the leading cause of death in people with MASLD — a point that follows directly from the reframing.

**Pathophysiology.** The "two-hit" model is presented and then explicitly demoted as incomplete. First hit: energy surplus drives fatty acid delivery and *de novo* lipogenesis while adipose insulin resistance releases free fatty acids, overwhelming the liver's capacity to oxidise or export them on VLDL. Second hit: oxidative stress from metabolising that excess damages hepatocytes and triggers inflammation. Baraki then says the real picture is multiple parallel factors — genetics, gut microbiome, metabolic, inflammatory and fibrotic pathways. Stating a teaching model and immediately marking it as insufficient is the sort of thing this source does well. Insulin resistance sits at the centre via three routes: failure to suppress hepatic glucose production, failure to suppress adipose lipolysis, and promotion of *de novo* lipogenesis. The MASLD–type 2 diabetes relationship is explicitly bidirectional, with hepatokines named as the liver-to-periphery signal.

The **Personal Fat Threshold** concept carries the explanation of lean MASLD: subcutaneous adipose has individually variable storage capacity, and once exceeded, fat deposits ectopically in liver, muscle and pancreas — so a normal-BMI person with low storage capacity can exceed their threshold at a lower body weight. The one linked prevalence figure supports this: lean or non-obese MASLD is estimated at **19% globally** ([Ye 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32413340/)), a systematic review and meta-analysis. Baraki adds that these people often carry excess abdominal fat and so may not be truly lean.

**Risk factors with numbers:** up to 80% of people with obesity may develop MASLD; 60–70% of people with type 2 diabetes have it, and diabetes more than doubles the risk of advanced fibrosis and liver-related death. Genetics influences susceptibility in 20–70% of cases while known variants explain only 10–20% of heritability. Premenopausal women's gluteofemoral subcutaneous storage pattern is described as relatively protective — with the correct hedge that the protection is relative and easily overwhelmed.

## Diagnosis: the clinically load-bearing section

Most people are asymptomatic; some report fatigue, malaise or right-upper-quadrant pain. Diagnosis is usually incidental, from enzymes or imaging done for something else.

Then the correction that matters: **liver enzymes are normal in more than half of MASLD patients and correlate poorly with severity, so normal ALT and AST do not rule out MASLD or even advanced fibrosis.** Given how many people are told their liver tests are fine, this is the most useful sentence in the article.

Because fibrosis is what predicts outcomes, the workup targets fibrosis: non-invasive scores ([FIB-4](https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/2200/fibrosis-4-fib-4-index-liver-fibrosis), [APRI](https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/3094/ast-platelet-ratio-index-apri)) that are good at *ruling out* advanced fibrosis at very low values, then imaging (ultrasound, elastography, MRI). Biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing MASH and staging fibrosis but is not routine given cost, invasiveness and risk. Once diagnosed, staging plus cardiovascular risk assessment.

## Treatment: what is evidence and what is recommendation

**Weight loss** is the primary lever, target ≥5% and preferably ≥10%. The described 2021 meta-analysis found a dose-response across behavioural programmes, drugs and bariatric surgery: everyone losing ≥10% saw reduced disease activity, 90% achieved MASH resolution, 45% showed fibrosis regression.

**Diet composition** matters beyond calories, and the specific comparisons are the interesting part:
- **Saturated fat** — overfeeding 1,000 kcal/day of SFA for three weeks raised liver triglyceride more than the same surplus from unsaturated fat or simple sugars. Isocalorically, 20% of energy from SFA raised liver fat ~40%; 20% from simple sugars did not.
- **Monounsaturated fat** — a 28%-of-energy MUFA diet, half from extra-virgin olive oil, reduced liver fat 18% over 12 weeks in people with prediabetes and MASLD.
- **Polyunsaturated fat** — low-SFA/high-PUFA diets consistently lower liver fat; omega-3 EPA/DHA enhances liver fat reduction and fat oxidation.
- **Added sugar** — explicitly less potent than saturated fat under isocaloric conditions, but still limited, because in a hypercaloric context it drives energy surplus, *de novo* lipogenesis, VLDL production and remnant cholesterol.
- **Fibre** — the evidence is called epidemiological, and the 30–35 g/day target in the action plan is a recommendation on top of association.
- **Coffee** — consistently associated with lower MASLD and fibrosis risk; optimal type and dose "has not been established in prospective randomized trials."

The recommendation drawn is to substitute unsaturated for saturated fat. **The sugar handling is the piece's best judgement call** — the isocaloric data would license a strong "sugar is not the problem" line, and Baraki declines to take it.

**Exercise** improves insulin sensitivity, body composition, inflammation and cardiovascular function, with a stated dose-response for volume, and — importantly — **liver benefit occurs regardless of weight loss**. The prescription is the physical activity guidelines (150–300 min/week moderate or 75–150 vigorous, plus resistance training on ≥2 days), with higher and moderate intensity described as comparable.

**Drugs.** Vitamin E for MASH in people without diabetes, insufficient evidence in diabetes or advanced fibrosis. Pioglitazone for insulin sensitivity. GLP-1 and related agents carry the article's two other linked references — [semaglutide in the NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2413258) (Sanyal 2025, phase 3 in MASH) and [retatrutide in Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-024-03018-2) — plus tirzepatide, all described as showing dose-response between weight loss and reduced liver fat, inflammation and fibrosis. Resmetirom, a thyroid hormone receptor-beta agonist, is named as the first approved drug for MASH with moderate-to-advanced fibrosis. **Bariatric surgery** is called the most potent treatment: improvement in ~90% of patients, fibrosis regression, extended lifespan, possibly reduced liver cancer risk.

**Supplements** get the sharpest treatment: silymarin and phosphatidylcholine have preliminary suggestions of benefit, but robust trial evidence on MASLD outcomes is lacking, effects are far smaller than lifestyle or approved drugs, and supplements are poorly regulated and frequently contaminated.

**Things they sell:** Barbell Medicine runs a supplement line, which makes the supplements section notable in the other direction — it argues against the category. The article links their own Beginner Prescription programme as the on-ramp to the exercise guidelines, and three of their podcast episodes.

**Flagged:** every number above the three linked references is uncited. The claims are conventional and this is a physician writing in his own field, but a reader wanting to verify the 50–80× fibrosis mortality figure, the 90%/45% MASH-resolution numbers, or the 40% saturated-fat effect has no paper to go to.
