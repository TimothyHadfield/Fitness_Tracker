# Exercise Triggers Migraines in Some, But Regular Training Reduces Their Frequency

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How Does Exercise Affect Headaches?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-does-exercise-affect-headaches/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2023-02-27 · 971 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- A **new** headache that starts during exercise is a reason to see a physician, not to self-manage. Guidelines call for brain imaging — MRI plus MRA, or CT plus CTA — to rule out structural and vascular causes.
- **Primary exercise headache (PEH)** is the diagnosis when no cause is found. It skews young, is slightly more common in women, and its risk factors are heat, altitude and sustained strenuous effort ([Sjaastad 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14510926/); [ICHD-3 beta](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23771276/)).
- PEH is rare next to migraine, which affects **12–15% of adults worldwide** and ranks **second only to low back pain** as a global cause of disability ([Ashina 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33773613/)).
- Exercise is a self-reported migraine trigger in **22%** of patients in the largest dataset — a prospective series of **1,207 patients aged 13–80** ([Kelman 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17403039/)).
- Despite that, a **2022 systematic review and network meta-analysis of 21 controlled trials** found **all** exercise interventions reduced migraine frequency ([Woldeamanuel 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-022-01503-y)). This is the article's strongest evidence and its central claim.

## Telling the two problems apart
Headaches split into primary (no identifiable cause — tension-type, migraine, cluster) and secondary (trauma, tumour, infection). PEH sits in the primary group but is defined by timing: during or immediately after exercise, with no cause found. The clinical reasoning matters — you cannot call a headache primary until imaging has excluded the secondary causes, which is why the article puts evaluation before reassurance.

PEH and migraine overlap in people but not in presentation. Most exercise headaches lack the migraine signature of nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia and unilateral pain ([Sjaastad 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14510926/); [Hanashiro 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26466691/)). A throbbing one-sided headache arriving with a hard set is more likely migraine provoked by exertion than PEH.

## The avoidance trap
Some migraine sufferers find exercise brings pain on or worsens it, and stop exercising as a result ([Irby 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26643584/); [Nadelson 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16483514/); [Martins 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16750801/) — the last of these is explicitly about **kinesiophobia**, fear of movement, in migraine). This is the same avoidance pattern Barbell Medicine argues against in musculoskeletal pain, applied to headache: the trigger is real, the avoidance is costly, and the preventive benefit accrues to people who keep training.

## What exercise actually does
Two n=1 **case reports** describe a migraine being aborted by exercise ([Darling 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1774180/); [Strelniker 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19211193/) — the latter in *Medical Hypotheses*, not peer-reviewed in the usual sense). Curiosities, not evidence, and the article treats them as such.

The prevention result is the substantive one. The proposed mechanism is a raised migraine triggering threshold in regular exercisers, and the authors are explicit that **the required frequency and intensity are unknown**. They also note exercise helps the conditions that travel with migraine — depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, self-image ([Irby 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26643584/)).

## Evidence versus recommendation
The evidence supports: exercise reduces migraine frequency. The recommendation goes further — that everyone with a headache disorder should meet or exceed the [US physical activity guidelines](https://health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-09/Physical_Activity_Guidelines_2nd_edition.pdf), barring an untreated condition making it unsafe. A reasonable clinical extrapolation from a meta-analysis that could not specify a dose. **Where it slips**: "all exercise interventions were effective" is stated flatly, when a network meta-analysis of 21 trials carries wide per-modality uncertainty — aerobic versus strength was the source paper's actual comparison, and the article does not report which won.
