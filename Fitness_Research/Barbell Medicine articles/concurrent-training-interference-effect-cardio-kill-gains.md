# Cardio Does Not Kill Gains, But Too Much Training Does

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Concurrent Training and the Interference Effect: Will Cardio Kill Your Gains?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/concurrent-training-and-the-interference-effect/)) · Nick Occhipinti · 2025-11-25 · 4,036 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- The interference effect — reduced strength, size or power when cardio is added to lifting — traces to a single 1980 study ([Hickson](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7193134/)) in untrained men. Three later meta-analyses ([Petré 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33751469/), [Schumann 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/), [Lundberg 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35476184/)) found **no significant compromise to strength or hypertrophy** versus lifting-only programmes.
- Their reframing is the useful part: whatever interference exists is **a total-workload problem, not a metabolic-pathway problem**. Adding conditioning on top of an already-maximal lifting load will cost you performance the same way adding more lifting would.
- The mTOR/AMPK story is explicitly demoted. They accept acute AMPK activation can transiently inhibit mTOR, and then say the long-term outcome data does not follow from it — a clean example of refusing to let a mechanism outrank an outcome.
- **Explosive strength is the live exception**, and they quote Schumann's own caveat on it, but then set it against a 2025 trial ([Feuerbacher](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70025)) finding no penalty to explosive strength either. They do not resolve the disagreement.
- Modality does not decide it: one review found running worse than cycling, another found cycling worse than running, and they conclude the evidence gives no strong basis to choose. **This clashes with two other sources in this library** (see below).

## What the article actually argues

The structure is mechanism → outcome → practice, and the ordering carries the argument. Two mechanisms are put up. The first is signalling: resistance training drives muscle protein synthesis through mTOR, endurance training activates AMPK to restore cellular energy, and the two are held to be mutually antagonistic. They cite [Rose 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16210351/) for mTOR-pathway inhibition when cardio precedes lifting, [Babcock 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22492813/) for blunted satellite-cell response to acute resistance exercise, and [Spaulding & Yan 2022](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-physiol-060721-095517) for the AMPK side.

Then they knock it down. Acute AMPK activation can transiently inhibit mTOR immediately post-workout, but "long-term data suggests this effect does not significantly compromise muscle growth when proper recovery and programming are used." All three of those citations are acute, single-session studies; the conclusion is drawn from the training-outcome literature instead. That is the right order of evidence and they say so.

The second mechanism is **total training load exceeding recovery capacity**, framed through fitness-fatigue. Their worked case: a lifter training five days a week who suddenly adds 150 minutes of conditioning to meet the physical activity guidelines has added a large block of volume that may exceed what they can recover from — and in that scenario results *would* suffer in the short term. Their point is that the same thing happens if you add the volume as more lifting. Interference in this account is not a property of cardio; it is a property of doing too much.

On outcomes, three meta-analyses are cited together for no significant compromise. They then quote Schumann verbatim, including the part against them: hypertrophy and maximal strength are unaffected, "however, explosive strength gains may be attenuated, especially when aerobic and strength training are performed in the same session." Feuerbacher 2025 is offered against this, finding no reduction in maximal strength, explosive strength or hypertrophy. Two papers disagree, and the article leaves it there rather than adjudicating — honest, but the reader is left without a bottom line on power.

## The clinical-to-practical gap

Everything from here is recommendation, not evidence, and the article mostly labels it. Three cases:

**Untrained beginners** should do both, to or beyond the guidelines. Conditioning after lifting on shared days, at roughly 60–80% max heart rate, at least 150 min/week. The evidence supports this one: interference is least likely where adaptation headroom is largest.

**Competitors** peaking for a powerlifting or weightlifting meet should taper conditioning over the final 4–6 weeks — their example steps 200 min/week down to 30 across five weeks. No citation. It is a resource-allocation argument, and a sensible one, but it is coaching judgement.

**Strength-focused intermediates** get a graded six-week ramp to 150 min/week: 60 min over two sessions, then 90 over three, then 150. Also uncited.

They are unusually good about marking the weakest claim in the piece. On preferring cycling, erging or elliptical over running and swimming for these lifters, they write that both running and swimming "seem to produce a bit more fatigue than other options… though this is mostly based on experience, not scientific evidence." That is exactly the distinction the reader needs.

**Flagged as overreach:** the CrossFit Games section. A 256 kg competition squat by an elite Games athlete demonstrates that the two qualities coexist in a self-selected outlier; it does not tell you anything about the trade-off for anyone else, and the article half-admits this before using it as a demonstration anyway. The article also asserts strength gains follow a staircase and that day-to-day performance varies within ~5% of baseline, with no citation for either number.

**Things they sell:** the article links a 100+ page programming eBook sold as part of their Low Fatigue Strength template, plus their training-programme catalogue, from inside the programming discussion. The advice is not distorted by it, but the placement is a sales placement.

## Where this sits against the rest of the library

Three existing notes cover the same question, and the agreement is broad but not total.

**Agreement on the headline.** [House of Hypertrophy's "combine cardio and weights"](../House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/combine-cardio-weights-should.md) reaches the same verdict from the same literature: updated meta-analyses find no compromise, and Wilson's 2012 meta-analysis (which did find interference) is superseded. Both sources reject the signalling mechanism on the same ground — it does not survive in humans.

**Agreement on power as the exception.** Both note it. HoH is firmer: power adaptations *are* compromised, and sprinters, jumpers and Olympic lifters should care. Barbell Medicine cites the 2025 trial against that and does not commit.

**A real clash on modality.** Barbell Medicine cites Lundberg 2022 (interference more pronounced with running than cycling) and Sabag 2018 (cycling worse than running for lower-body performance and muscle gain) and concludes the evidence is directionless. HoH says subgroup analyses on running and cycling did not change results — also a null. But [Jeff Nippard's Q&A](../Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/drop-sets-dumb-cardio-kill-gains-stretch.md) states flatly that running interferes more than cycling or swimming. That is the older received view, and the Barbell Medicine pair of contradictory reviews is the better account of why nobody should assert it confidently.

**An omission worth noting.** HoH gives real weight to a 2021 meta-analysis restricted to trained individuals finding strength gains lowered by *same-session* cardio but not by separate sessions — very likely Petré 2021, which Barbell Medicine cites for the null result only. Barbell Medicine then recommends putting conditioning in the same session, after lifting. Their own quoted Schumann caveat points the same way ("especially when… performed in the same session"). The library's other source would say: separate the sessions where you can.

**A third source pushes further.** [HoH's capillary-argument video](../House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/cardio-increase-muscle-growth-here.md) argues cardio may *help* hypertrophy via capillary density, on one 14-person untrained single-leg study. Barbell Medicine makes no such claim. Their restraint is the better call.
