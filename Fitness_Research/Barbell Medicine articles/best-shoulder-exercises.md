# Six Shoulder Exercises Chosen on Activation Data, and a Good Argument About Upright Rows

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Best Shoulder Exercises" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-shoulder-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-03-19 · 5,111 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- Six recommendations: **overhead press, high-incline dumbbell bench press, upright row, one-arm cable lateral raise, bent-over rear delt fly, face pull.** Every rationale given for these choices is **electromyography or anatomy**. The reference list contains no study comparing any of these exercises for muscle growth.
- **The genuinely valuable section is on upright rows and shoulder impingement**, where they use the modern pain-science reframing correctly: impingement is now defined as a spectrum of clinical findings, not damage to a named structure, so "this exercise causes impingement" is a category error. Their conclusion is that upright rows are not uniquely dangerous — start conservatively, load progressively.
- **They also refuse a claim they could easily have sold**: "there is no data to support a specific 'push to pull' ratio or to confirm that face pulls are uniquely beneficial." That is more honest than the genre norm.
- **The mapping is broken for this article.** Marker numbers run past the end of the parsed list, and the article's own list visibly drifts — the closing VO₂max claim is marked [39], but reference 39 is a duplicate of 38 and is about post-exercise oxygen consumption. **No claim-level attribution was possible.** The references below are a reading list for the article as a whole.
- Roughly half the article is a general "benefits of resistance training" essay barely connected to shoulders. Some of it is good myth-correction (muscle mass and metabolic rate, EPOC); one claim about osteoarthritis progression clearly outruns its evidence.

## The mapping problem, stated plainly

The marker-to-reference check failed for this article, on three counts: the highest inline marker is [39] while only 34 references parse; five markers ([15], [16], [28], [29], [30]) point past the list as parsed; and the numbering is not a clean 1..N.

The drift is visible in the article itself. The final claim — that endurance athletes who added weight training gained 12.9% in VO₂max against 6.8% for endurance training alone — carries marker [39]. Reference 39 in the article's own list is LaForgia et al. on excess post-exercise oxygen consumption, a verbatim duplicate of reference 38. The study actually being described is Balabinis 2003 on concurrent endurance and strength training, which sits at 16. Similarly, the sentence about lower risk of type II diabetes, cancer and heart disease carries [27], and reference 27 is a study of myofibrillar protein abundance in resistance-trained men.

## The six exercises and what kind of evidence sits behind them

| Exercise | Prescription | Stated rationale | Evidence type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overhead press | 3–5 × 4–6, RPE 7–8 | Loads anterior delt; implement choice makes little difference | **EMG** ("similar levels of electrical activity" across implements) |
| High-incline DB bench press (45–60°) | 2–4 × 6–10, RPE 8–9 | Steeper angle loads anterior delt and upper pec more | **EMG** (bench-angle activation studies) |
| Upright row | 2–4 × 10–15, RPE 7–8 | Shoulders and upper back; impingement concern addressed | Clinical reasoning + EMG on grip width |
| 1-arm cable lateral raise | 3–5 × 10–15, RPE 8–10 | Lateral delt plus rotator cuff; cable gives tension early in ROM | **EMG** (rehab-exercise and lateral-raise activation studies) |
| Bent-over rear delt fly | 3–5 × 10–15, RPE 8–10 | Posterior delt; also loads middle delt | **EMG** + anatomy |
| Face pull | 3–5 × 10–15, RPE 8–10 | Posterior delt, external rotators, traps | **Anatomy**; benefit claim explicitly disclaimed |

**Say it directly: this ranking is activation-based, not outcome-based.** The reference list contains at least eight distinct EMG papers — overhead press implement stability, bench angle (two), upright row grip width, shoulder rehabilitation exercises, lateral raise variations in competitive bodybuilders, bands versus free weights in single-joint upper-body work, and hand position during horizontal abduction — and no training study comparing shoulder exercises for hypertrophy. This is the same pattern the rest of this library has found in every "best X exercises" article, and it is worth noting that **the same authors, in their chest hypertrophy article, cite Vigotsky's argument that EMG amplitude cannot be read as hypertrophic potential.** That caution does not appear here.

Two specific claims are weaker than they read:

- **The lateral raise "trains the lateral deltoid, as well as muscles of the rotator cuff like the infraspinatus and subscapularis."** A cable lateral raise is shoulder abduction; the infraspinatus and subscapularis are rotators acting mainly as humeral head stabilisers here. Activation during the movement is real; describing them as trained by it is a stretch.
- **"By doing more volume without generating too much fatigue, it is likely that fitness adaptations like strength will improve."** This is a reasonable coaching inference, but the citation trail behind it in this article runs through activation studies, not volume-dose training studies. It should be read as **recommendation, not finding** — and note that this is precisely the "isolation work lets you add cheap volume" argument they make in their chest articles too.

The prescriptions themselves are opinion: no cited work supports 3–5 sets of 10–15 at RPE 8–10 for lateral raises over any other scheme. **Worth flagging across the series:** the isolation work here is prescribed at RPE 8–10 (0–2 reps in reserve), while their chest-and-triceps article prescribes RPE 6–8 across the board. Neither article argues for its choice, and the two are not consistent.

## The upright row section is the clinical content

This is the part that justifies the article's existence in a library that already has activation-based exercise rankings from other sources.

The standard gym claim, which has a genuine source in the strength and conditioning literature, is that upright rows narrow the subacromial space and risk impingement. The article states the claim, then does something the genre almost never does: it questions the diagnostic concept underneath it. **Impingement classically meant the rotator cuff or subacromial bursa being irritated between the acromion and the humeral head. Current usage defines it as a spectrum of clinical findings — shoulder pain and dysfunction — rather than injury to identified anatomy.** If the label names a pattern of symptoms rather than a mechanical pinch, then "this movement causes impingement" is not a mechanical prediction that can be made about an exercise in the abstract.

Their conclusion follows: upright rows are not uniquely dangerous; start conservatively with load and volume and progress over time.

**Presented as their position, this is well founded** and consistent with the direction of the subacromial pain literature. Two caveats worth keeping:

- The reframing is a claim about *diagnostic labelling*, and they use it to reach a claim about *exercise safety*. Those are close but not identical. "Impingement is a symptom cluster, not a structural diagnosis" does not by itself establish that no exercise carries elevated risk for anyone; it establishes that the usual mechanical argument for singling out the upright row is weak. As written, the article slides from the first to the second without marking the gap.
- The recommendation to load conservatively and progress gradually is sensible clinical practice, offered without evidence attached. That is fine — it should just be read as advice.

**Commercial note, and it sits awkwardly:** the paragraph immediately after the impingement discussion is "If you have shoulder pain already, consider getting in touch with us. Our coaching team includes physicians and rehab specialists." A pitch for paid coaching placed inside the clinical passage. The reader can weigh it; the argument does not depend on it, and the article closes with a further pitch for training templates and an email-list discount.

## The second half: general benefits, mixed quality

More than half of this "best shoulder exercises" article is about resistance training in general.

**Well handled.**
- *Strength is specific.* Improvement on an overhead press 1RM will be roughly proportional to how similar the training was to that test. Heavy overhead pressing beats light dumbbell benching for that outcome. They then note that training broadens force production generally — power, endurance — while predicting, correctly, that transfer scales with similarity.
- *Fibre number is broadly fixed; growth is fibre enlargement.* Standard and correct; the hyperplasia question is acknowledged as unsettled rather than dismissed.
- *Muscle does not meaningfully raise metabolic rate.* The best passage in the section, with real numbers: **1 kg of muscle costs roughly 13 kcal/day, 1 kg of fat 4–5 kcal/day.** Gaining 10 kg of muscle — which takes years — buys about **100 kcal/day**. This directly contradicts a common fitness-industry claim.
- *EPOC is small.* They describe the mechanism, note that untrained people show larger EPOC than trained, and conclude that its contribution to total daily energy expenditure is minor.

**Overreach.**
- **"It seems that starting to lift earlier not only mitigates the loss of strength caused by osteoarthritis but also delays or even stops the advancement of the condition."** Resistance training reliably improves pain, strength and function in knee osteoarthritis, including in advanced disease. There is no good evidence that it halts structural progression. **This is the clearest case in the article of a conclusion outrunning its evidence**, and it is exactly the kind of claim these authors are usually careful about.
- **"Weight lifting has been confirmed to reduce waist circumference by decreasing abdominal fat."** "Confirmed" is too strong for a network meta-analysis of exercise prescriptions in adults with obesity.
- **The 12.9% versus 6.8% VO₂max figures** are presented as a settled result. They come from a small early-phase concurrent-training study; that is a single-study effect size reported to one decimal place, not an established magnitude.
- **"Hypertrophy is delayed until the person adjusts to it. That might not occur at all if the training dose is much too high."** The first half reflects the muscle-damage/protein-synthesis literature reasonably. The second half — that excessive dosing can prevent growth entirely — is asserted without support, and the same unsupported claim appears in their chest hypertrophy article.
