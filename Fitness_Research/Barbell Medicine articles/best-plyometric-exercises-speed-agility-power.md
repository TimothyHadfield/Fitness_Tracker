# Plyometrics Are for Athletes Who Need Speed, Not Lifters Who Want Size

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The 8 Best Plyometric Exercises for Speed, Agility and Power" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-plyometric-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-01-22 · 5,744 words

## Bottom line
- The most useful thing here is the **scoping claim, not the exercise list**: plyometrics "aren't absolutely necessary for most people". Recreational trainees and health-driven lifters are told to spend the time on resistance training and conditioning instead, unless they enjoy jumping. Only athletes in sports rewarding jump height, change of direction and sprint speed are told to include them. That is a rare instance of an article in this genre arguing against its own topic.
- **They state plainly that plyometrics are a poor hypertrophy tool** — reduced eccentric loading and short time under tension — and that high-velocity, eccentric-light training preferentially grows type II fibres while bodybuilding-style work grows both type I and type II. They then flag honestly that whether that fibre-type split is training-driven or selection bias (the sport choosing the athlete) is unknown.
- **The eight exercise picks are backed by neither hypertrophy nor activation data.** Four upper-body (med-ball chest pass/shot put, rotational med-ball throw, underhand med-ball throw, explosive push-up) and four lower-body (box jump, broad jump, drop jump, single-leg hop). The supporting studies attached to individual movements are either **predictive/correlational** (rotational throw velocity predicts bat and pitch velocity), **validation of a test** (explosive push-ups as a field measure of upper-body power), or a **training study of a different modality** (12 weeks of medicine-ball training in young female handball players). None compares the listed exercise against an alternative for any adaptation.
- **Where outcome data does appear, it is at the level of the modality, not the movement**: meta-analyses and an umbrella review support plyometric training improving vertical jump, sprint time and muscle power; a 2023 meta-analysis in highly trained youth soccer players supports combined strength-plus-plyometric training over either alone; a systematic review of cluster-randomised trials supports plyometric-containing injury-prevention programmes reducing ACL injury incidence, more clearly in males and in non-contact injuries.
- The fat-loss FAQ is the strongest-reasoned section in the piece and cuts against the fitness-industry line: **plyometrics have not been reliably linked to weight loss**, EPOC contributes little to total daily energy expenditure, and 1 kg of muscle burns roughly 13 kcal/day against 4.5 for fat — so 10 kg of muscle, which takes years, adds about 85 kcal/day.

## The eight exercises, and what supports each

Reps and sets are borrowed from Olympic lifting practice: few reps per set, multiple sets, long rests, quality of speed over accumulation of volume. Prescriptions given:

| Exercise | Prescription | Load |
|---|---|---|
| Med-ball shot put / chest pass | 3 × 6 | ~10 lb, thrown fast and hard |
| Rotational med-ball throw | 3 × 15–20 | 5–10 lb |
| Underhand med-ball throw | 3 × 6–8, alternating height and distance | 5–10 lb |
| Explosive push-up | 3 × 5–8 | Bodyweight |
| Box jump | 3 × 6, height raised each set to a comfortably landable max | Bodyweight |
| Broad jump | 3 × 6, maximum distance each rep | Bodyweight |
| Drop jump | 3 × 6, progressing drop-land → drop-hop → drop-jump | Bodyweight |
| Single-leg hop | 3–4 × 5–10 m, alternating legs before single-leg | Bodyweight |

**Med-ball shot put / chest pass.** Justified on coordination and on conditioning the scapular and humeral stabilisers to the eccentric demands of throwing — a mechanistic assertion with nothing measured behind it. The cited support is a 12-week medicine-ball *training programme* in young female athletes that improved throwing distances across med-ball tests. That is an outcome study, but the outcome is a med-ball throw test after med-ball training, which is close to testing the training itself.

**Rotational med-ball throw.** Backed by a Spalding University study of an NCAA Division III college baseball team, in which rotational med-ball throw velocity predicted bat swing velocity, batted-ball velocity and pitching velocity better than broad jumps or lateral-to-medial jumps. **This is a correlational, predictive finding, not a training effect** — it shows the throw is a good *test* of rotational power, not that training it transfers to the diamond. The article does not draw that distinction.

**Underhand med-ball throw.** No supporting citation at all; the section is instructions only.

**Explosive push-up.** The cited work establishes explosive push-ups as a valid field test of upper-body power and a useful way to monitor training programmes. Again: a test-validation study is used to justify including an exercise, which is a different claim.

**Box jump, broad jump.** The box jump section carries no study. The broad jump section carries one, and it is used to *debunk* rather than to support: a trial of three warm-up routines in trained and untrained men and women found warm-up of any type had little effect on standing broad jump performance, while maximum strength was strongly related to jumping ability. Two things worth keeping: warm-up-to-jump-better is not supported, and strength is the stronger correlate of jump performance.

**Drop jump.** The one exercise with methodological detail. Two descent techniques are described — step-off (stepping down with the dominant leg while the training leg stays on the box) and drop-off (leaning forward until both feet leave the platform together) — and a kinetics study is cited finding the **drop-off technique produced less inter-limb kinetic asymmetry** than step-off. A further recommendation appears: drop jumps on sandy surfaces or from platforms above 50 cm (19.6 in) to trigger lower-body pre-activation and raise post-landing latency-response activation levels. **Flag:** that is a muscle-activation rationale for a box-height prescription, with no jump or sprint outcome behind it, and 50 cm is a substantial drop height to recommend on those grounds.

**Single-leg hop.** Described as low-level preparation for more advanced work. The only supporting link is to a *Men's Health* article about men over 40 — not a study.

**Net verdict on the ranking:** eight placements, none supported by measured hypertrophy (which the article does not claim), and none supported by a comparison against alternative exercises for power, speed or agility either. Two are supported by studies validating the movement as a *test*. One rests on kinetic asymmetry. The rest are convention.

## What plyometrics do and do not do

The article defines plyometrics via the stretch-shortening cycle — rapid force production exploiting elastic energy, boosting musculotendinous stiffness and power — and credits the naming to Purdue track coach Fred Wilt in 1975. Practical framing: they are fatiguing because of the force involved, so put them early in a session, and start at one or two sessions a week of two or three exercises before adding frequency, volume or intensity.

**Against hypertrophy.** Their argument has three legs: plyometrics are predominantly concentric, so the eccentric component that pairs with the concentric in traditional lifting is reduced; they are fast, so time under tension is short and metabolite accumulation lower; and eccentric-light high-velocity training (Olympic lifting is their comparison) appears to preferentially hypertrophy type II fibres while merely maintaining type I. The first two are mechanism arguments, not outcome comparisons. The third is an observation about training populations, and they say so — the fibre-type difference may be training or may be self-selection into the sport. **This is the correct level of confidence and is worth noting because the same article elsewhere makes far firmer claims from thinner material.**

**For performance.** The claims here rest on real synthesis evidence: plyometric training improves vertical jump height, sprint time and muscular power in healthy people and athletes; a sprint-performance meta-analysis notes dose-response variation with programme length, volume and intensity; Olympic weightlifting variants produce broadly similar improvements in change of direction and muscular power, so coaches may substitute. The most interesting single result they report is transfer to a non-jumping sport: **prepubertal male swimmers who replaced eight weeks of sport-specific swim drills with plyometric jump training improved short-distance swimming performance.** Small, young, specific population — but a genuine randomised transfer finding.

**For strength.** Understated correctly: plyometrics improve high-velocity strength (power) but are less likely to improve maximal strength or hypertrophy than traditional resistance training. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in high-level, highly trained male youth soccer players found **combined strength-plus-plyometric training** improved maximal strength, power and speed measures more than either modality alone.

**For rehabilitation.** Their clinical section is brief but sound: after ACL reconstruction, plyometrics can be used during rehabilitation both to re-expose the athlete gradually to explosive demand and to restore speed- and agility-related fitness lost post-operatively. The cited trial compared low- versus high-intensity plyometric exercise during ACL rehab — i.e. the question is dose, not whether to use them.

**For injury prevention.** Injury-prevention programmes that include plyometric exercises reduce ACL injury incidence relative to programmes without them, per a systematic review of cluster-randomised trials, with the effect more evident in males and in non-contact ACL injuries. They recommend both plyometrics and traditional resistance training together, and state the mechanism is unknown — presumed to run through strength, motor control and tolerance to sport demands. **This is a recommendation stated as a recommendation.**

## The fat-loss FAQ, which is better than the rest of the article

Asked whether plyometrics are good for fat loss, they answer no: plyometrics have not been reliably linked to weight loss. They then dismantle two standard claims:

- **EPOC.** Excess post-exercise oxygen consumption rises with exercise volume, intensity and muscle mass involved, and is higher in untrained than trained people because the trained have adapted the costly metabolic processes. But whether EPOC raises total daily energy expenditure "hasn't been tested", and current research shows its contribution to TDEE is small. **Flag:** one sentence in this passage goes the other way, asserting that EPOC from a blended strength-and-hypertrophy programme "has proven to decrease visceral adipose tissue". That claim is far stronger than the surrounding paragraph and than the metabolic literature supports, and it sits directly beside the admission that EPOC's effect on TDEE has not been tested.
- **Resting metabolic rate.** The stated numbers: **1 kg of muscle uses about 13 kcal/day, 1 kg of fat about 4.5**. Gaining 10 kg of muscle — years of work — raises total daily energy expenditure by roughly 85 kcal. Their conclusion is that "muscle burns calories" is a real but negligible mechanism.

What they retain is that strength training builds muscle, lowers regain risk, reduces waist circumference, and may improve sensitivity to satiety cues. This is a section where their advice runs *below* the popular claim rather than above it.

**Two quality problems worth recording.** The FAQ answering "what plyometrics should I do to jump higher?" lists box jumps, depth jumps, squat jumps, lateral bounds, medicine ball overhead throws — and **bent-over row**, which is neither plyometric nor a jump. And the article's mid-section on drop jumps prescribes surfaces and drop heights with more specificity than any of its cited work supports.

## What they sell
The conclusion routes to Barbell Medicine's training templates and to custom coaching, described as delivered by "certified professionals". There is no supplement or equipment pitch here, and no product is recommended inside the technical sections.
