# The Menstrual Cycle Does Not Reliably Change Strength — and the Magnitude Argument Is Why

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Women's Menstrual Cycle and Athletic Exercise Performance" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-menstrual-cycle-estrogen-and-performance-part-i/)) · Claire Zai · 2021-04-23 · 7,797 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- The article sets a formal null — *there is no reliable difference between menstrual cycle phases on strength-sport performance* — walks the evidence, and concludes it cannot be rejected. **The practical recommendation is: do not prospectively periodise training on cycle phase; auto-regulate and adjust to what actually happens.**
- The strongest argument in the piece is about **magnitude, not direction**. Estrogen genuinely matters for muscle — ovariectomised rats lose cross-sectional area and protein turnover, and postmenopausal women show higher protein breakdown with lower muscle mass. But cycling estradiol (roughly 33–87 pg/mL follicular, 65–242 pg/mL luteal) runs about **three times higher than the average of the first five post-menopausal years**, and the cyclic dip lasts days, not decades. Chronic depletion and monthly fluctuation are not the same exposure.
- **Muscle protein synthesis rates do not differ between cycle phases** (Miller 2006), which is the mechanistic bridge that would have to hold for a phase effect on hypertrophy to exist.
- The performance studies are small, use mostly single-joint or grip proxies, and **disagree**. Two found a mid- to late-follicular strength advantage of about 10–12%; the two best-controlled studies — which used superimposed electrical stimulation to rule out central fatigue and measured hormones on each test day — found nothing, and no correlation between hormone concentrations and force.
- **The four periodisation studies are all confounded the same way**: within-subject limb comparisons vulnerable to contralateral crosstalk, and testing weeks that fell at different training volumes, so an apparent phase effect is indistinguishable from a taper.
- **Where they overreach**: they write "we cannot reject this hypothesis and therefore we accept it," which is not how a null works; they describe the periodisation evidence as "weak but converging evidence pointing to a benefit" when one of the four studies found nothing at all; and the nocebo argument that closes the piece — telling women their cycle limits their training may seed self-fulfilling expectations — is plausible and entirely uncited.

## The physiology, and why the article bothers with it

Roughly the first third is endocrinology: the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, GnRH driving LH and FSH, LH and FSH acting on the ovaries to produce estrogen and progesterone, and negative feedback holding the whole system in range. Estradiol is the estrogen of interest. It circulates bound to albumin and sex hormone binding globulin, and SHBG-bound estrogen is unavailable to tissue. Three receptor classes are described — nuclear ERα (breast, uterus, ovary, bone, liver, adipose), nuclear ERβ (bladder, ovary, colon, adipose, immune tissue), and membrane estrogen receptors implicated in cardiac recovery and DNA repair.

The reason for the detour is that this is where the phase-periodisation hypothesis comes from: estrogen receptors sit in muscle, tendon, ligament and bone, so it is *reasonable* to expect cyclic estrogen to move training outcomes. The article's job is to show that reasonable expectation is not borne out.

They also make a general endocrinology point that travels well beyond this topic: the fitness industry's belief that hormones must sit inside narrow "optimal" ranges is wrong. Reference ranges are wide, regulation is redundant, and the body uses allostasis to hold concentrations where they need to be. Relaxin is raised and then dismissed in a sentence — it increases tendon laxity in the luteal phase but is framed as an injury-risk hormone, and gym sports have low injury risk. **That dismissal is asserted, not argued.**

## Does exercise move the hormones, and does it matter?

Three acute studies say the hormonal response to a lifting session differs by phase.

**Kraemer 1995** — 11 women, 5 self-reporting luteal and 6 follicular, doing 4 sets of 5. Luteal estradiol ran **~80–136 pg/mL** against **27–34 pg/mL** in the follicular phase, and the luteal group peaked **16 minutes into the session**. **Consitt 2002** reported similar findings; **Walberg-Rankin 1992** found it only in a calorie deficit.

**Nakamura 2011** adds cycle regularity as a moderator. Women with normal cycles in the mid-luteal phase had higher resting estradiol and progesterone than women with abnormal cycles, and showed significant post-training rises in both. The same women showed no change when tested in the early follicular phase, and **women with abnormal cycles showed no post-training change in either phase**.

The article then does the thing that makes it worth reading: it points out that **none of these studies checked whether the hormone changes correlated with strength, hypertrophy or any training outcome**, and that measuring circulating hormones during exercise is confounded by plasma volume shifts from sweating, changes in clearance and metabolism, and even posture. Taken at face value, the findings still license nothing about training.

## Chronic depletion vs monthly fluctuation — the core argument

Since ovariectomy is not available as a human experiment, the evidence comes from rodents and from menopause.

**Ovariectomised rats** show reduced metabolic function and impaired insulin sensitivity, and at the muscle level reduced cross-sectional area and size, lower rates of protein remodelling and turnover, and slower recovery of muscle mass after enforced inactivity (Kitajima & Ono 2016). Mechanistically, estrogen is proposed to increase the number of actin-myosin cross-bridges, raising force per fibre, and to protect myosin from oxidative damage (Lowe 2010). Estrogen replacement restores strength and size to control levels.

**Postmenopausal women** have higher rates of both muscle protein breakdown and synthesis and lower muscle mass than men or premenopausal women (Smith & Mittendorfer 2016) — a pattern consistent with breakdown outpacing synthesis. A review (Collins 2019) suggests estrogen preserves muscle by reducing apoptosis, and the article is careful to attribute that to **chronic** depletion rather than acute cyclic fluctuation. Chronic loss also blunts the training response in a way not seen between men and premenopausal women; declining physical activity across the lifespan is offered as a co-explanation (Enns & Tiidus 2010).

**Then the magnitude argument.** Estradiol in menstruating women runs about **32.7–87.2 pg/mL in the follicular phase and 65.4–242.4 pg/mL in the luteal phase** — roughly three times the average level of the first five post-menopausal years, which continues falling thereafter (Anttila 1991). For the cyclic dip to do what menopause does, it would have to be far larger and last far longer.

**And the mechanistic check.** Muscle protein synthesis rates do not differ significantly between cycle phases (Miller 2006), and men and women show similar protein turnover through young and mid-adulthood — implying a wide estrogen range compatible with normal muscle function.

This is a good argument, well constructed, and it is the part of the article most worth keeping.

## The performance studies, with their numbers

- **Kishali 2006** — survey across judo, taekwondo, volleyball and basketball. Women medalled regardless of phase and showed no significant performance differences, **despite reporting feeling significantly better during the first 14 days** of the cycle. Self-report and outcome diverge.
- **Phillips 1996** — 12 untrained women, 10 trained women, 5 trained women on oral contraceptives, 6 untrained men. Force measured by squeezing a transducer between thumb and index finger; phase by basal body temperature and urine LH. Both trained and untrained women rose about **10%** from the start to the end of the follicular phase. But **plasma estrogen did not correlate with relative muscle force**, and the within-cycle variation was **±1.2 lb against a 24 lb mean**.
- **Sarwar 1996** — 20 sedentary women (10 on oral contraceptives), quadriceps MVIC with superimposed electrical stimulation plus handgrip, weekly across two cycles. Phase was standardised on an **assumed 32-day cycle with ovulation inferred, not measured**. Mid-cycle (days 12–18) quadriceps strength was significantly higher, by up to **13.5 lb (11.7%)** in untrained women — against a within-sample standard deviation of about **11.2 lb**. The stimulation matters: it rules out central nervous system inhibition as the mechanism.
- **Elliott 2003** — 7 women, blood drawn at the same time of day on day 2 and day 21, pinch force measured the same way as Phillips. **No strength difference despite large hormone variation.**
- **Janse de Jonge 2001** — 19 healthy women with regular cycles; isometric quadriceps with superimposed stimulation plus isokinetic knee flexion and extension at multiple angles, with estradiol, progesterone, LH and FSH drawn at every test session. **No differences across phases, and no correlation between any performance metric and any hormone concentration.** Small, but the best-controlled design in the set, and the article says so.
- **Romero-Moraleda 2019** — 13 female triathletes, none on hormonal contraceptives. Half-squat 1RM, then 20/40/60/80% 1RM at maximal velocity on three randomised test days spanning early follicular, late follicular and mid-luteal. Phase by temperature, LH strips and menses; **hormones not measured**. No differences in peak power, mean power, peak velocity or mean velocity.
- **Weidauer 2020** — 50 women (25 on oral contraceptives), knee extension, knee flexion and grip across early follicular, ovulatory and mid-luteal, with hormones measured. **No effect of hormone levels on strength**, though strength was slightly higher in the follicular phase — leading the authors to propose a **non-hormonal** cause for whatever phase differences appear.

**The confound the article uses well:** Birch & Reilly 2002 tested maximal isometric lifting strength (deadlift at knee height) in 10 untrained women and found no difference between cycle phases — but a **~13 lb difference between testing at 6 am and 6 pm**. Time of day, itself likely a habituation effect, moved strength more than cycle phase did.

The honest summary the article gives: the barbell-relevant data is limited to proxies — pinch force, grip, isolated knee extension, half squats — and there is no consensus on strength-sport performance or training adaptation, largely because the data does not exist.

## Periodising by phase: four studies, four confounds

- **Reis 1995** — 7 untrained women, each acting as her own control, one leg on menstrual-cycle-triggered training (leg press 3×12, three times a week in the follicular phase, once a week in the luteal) and the other every third day. Both legs improved; the MCTT leg improved more. **No p-values reported.** The MCTT leg also carried reduced volume immediately before every test, which is a taper. And within-subject limb comparisons are vulnerable to contralateral crosstalk. The article's one positive note: the researchers did manage a workable at-home phase-identification method using body temperature and ovulation strips.
- **Sung 2014** — 20 untrained women, one leg high-volume in the follicular phase, the other high-volume in the luteal. The follicular-high leg gained more strength and size. But the rate of change was similar in every week except the first two and the last two, so **the effect is concentrated in initial exposure and the taper**, not in the phase manipulation. Crosstalk again.
- **Sakamaki-Sunaga 2016** — 14 untrained women, arms trained on opposite schedules, 1RM dumbbell curl at baseline, 4, 8 and 12 weeks, phase by basal body temperature. Both arms gained cross-sectional area and strength; **no difference between arms**. This is the clean negative result, and it is the one the article's summary line glosses over.
- **Wikström-Frisén 2017** — 59 resistance-familiar women (32 on oral contraceptives) in three groups: follicular-high (5×/week then 1×/week), luteal-high (the reverse), and a 3×/week control, over four months. Squat jump, countermovement jump, isokinetic peak torque and lean body mass measured pre and post. Groups 1 and 3 improved squat jump, countermovement jump and hamstring peak torque, but **not quadriceps torque**; the luteal-high group did not improve. The confound is decisive: **each group entered post-testing at a different training volume** — five sessions that week for group 1, one for group 2, three for group 3 — so the follicular group tested off a de facto taper and the luteal group off a heavy week.

Two further limits apply to all four: **none measured hormone levels**, and most assumed a 28-day cycle, when cycles range roughly 18–40 days (Fehring 2006) and vary with age and BMI (Bull 2019). Ovulation strips can separate follicular from luteal but cannot resolve early/mid/late without daily hormone assays, which most people will not pay for.

The article's own summary — "weak but converging evidence pointing to a benefit of periodized training" — **is more generous than its own analysis warrants**, given that one of the four found nothing and the other three are explained by taper and crosstalk. The paragraph then argues the effect away, which reads as the authors' actual view.

## Contraceptives, meta-analyses, and the position they land on

Oral contraceptives are hard to study because formulations differ in hormone type and dose, making cross-study comparison unreliable. Most studies show no strength difference between users and non-users. A meta-analysis (Elliott-Sale 2020) found oral contraceptives **may slightly reduce exercise performance, with very small group-level effects and wide inter-individual variation** — which the article reads as a case for individual management rather than a blanket recommendation. Symptom dulling may itself improve training tolerance (Knowles 2019). Their practical framing, that managing the conditions contraceptives treat probably matters more to training than any direct performance effect, is a clinician's framing and a fair one.

Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses — McNulty 2020, Thompson 2020, Blagrove 2020, Carmichael 2021 — agree the evidence is **inconclusive**, and McNulty is explicit that most of it is low quality. The forest plot the article reproduces has a pooled estimate near the line of no effect.

Their four stated reasons for accepting the null: no reliable or repeatable phase-induced differences; high inter-individual variability in response to phase, contraceptives and training alike (Ahtiainen 2016); other inputs — sleep, nutrition, programming — being more reliable and more modifiable; and the cost of accurate tracking, both financial and in **nocebo risk**.

The clinical advice that follows is the useful part, and it is advice rather than evidence: do not automatically start the luteal phase at lower intensity, frequency or volume; do not deload by default on symptoms; use an auto-regulated programme and monitor the individual's actual response; a coach's job is to find the pattern if one exists, not to plan around an assumed one; severe pain or discomfort in training is not something to train through; team-sport athletes should train to the competition schedule and to the group.

**Where it becomes advocacy.** "Athletes are human first, female second," "women are resilient," and the argument that telling women to periodise by period constructs a barrier that reduces the chance they train at all — these are position statements, not findings, and the nocebo mechanism in particular is asserted without a single citation despite being the article's fourth pillar. The underlying point, that the intervention has costs and the evidence does not justify them, stands on the evidence already presented; the framing is doing extra work.

**What they sell.** Unusually for this source, no product is pitched. The article does recommend working with a coach who auto-regulates, which is a service they sell, but it is not presented as a purchase.
