# The Menstrual Cycle Does Not Reliably Change Strength — and the Magnitude Argument Is Why

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Women's Menstrual Cycle and Athletic Exercise Performance" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-menstrual-cycle-estrogen-and-performance-part-i/)) · Claire Zai · 2021-04-23 · 7,797 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- The article sets a formal null — *there is no reliable difference between menstrual cycle phases on strength-sport performance* — and concludes it cannot be rejected. **The recommendation: do not prospectively periodise training on cycle phase; auto-regulate and adjust to what actually happens.**
- The strongest argument is about **magnitude, not direction**. Estrogen genuinely matters for muscle — ovariectomised rats lose cross-sectional area and protein turnover, postmenopausal women show higher protein breakdown with lower muscle mass. But cycling estradiol (roughly 33–87 pg/mL follicular, 65–242 pg/mL luteal) runs about **three times the average of the first five post-menopausal years**, and the cyclic dip lasts days, not decades. Chronic depletion and monthly fluctuation are not the same exposure.
- **Muscle protein synthesis rates do not differ between cycle phases** (Miller 2006) — the mechanistic bridge that would have to hold for a phase effect on hypertrophy to exist.
- The performance studies are small, mostly single-joint or grip proxies, and **disagree**. Two found a follicular advantage of about 10–12%; the two best-controlled — using superimposed electrical stimulation to rule out central fatigue, with hormones measured on each test day — found nothing, and no correlation between hormone concentrations and force.
- **The four periodisation studies are confounded the same way**: within-subject limb comparisons vulnerable to contralateral crosstalk, and testing weeks falling at different training volumes, so an apparent phase effect is indistinguishable from a taper.
- **Where they overreach**: "we cannot reject this hypothesis and therefore we accept it" is not how a null works; "weak but converging evidence pointing to a benefit" of periodisation is generous when one of the four studies found nothing; and the closing nocebo argument is plausible but entirely uncited.

## The physiology, and why the article bothers with it

Roughly the first third is endocrinology — the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, negative feedback, estradiol bound to albumin and SHBG, and the three receptor classes (nuclear ERα, nuclear ERβ, membrane ER). The detour exists because this is where the periodisation hypothesis comes from: estrogen receptors sit in muscle, tendon, ligament and bone, so it is *reasonable* to expect cyclic estrogen to move training outcomes. The article's job is to show that reasonable expectation is not borne out.

They also make a general endocrinology point that travels well beyond this topic: the fitness industry's belief that hormones must sit inside narrow "optimal" ranges is wrong — reference ranges are wide, regulation is redundant, and allostasis holds concentrations where they need to be. Relaxin is raised and dismissed in a sentence, on the grounds that gym sports have low injury risk. **That dismissal is asserted, not argued.**

## Does exercise move the hormones, and does it matter?

**Kraemer 1995** — 11 women, 5 self-reporting luteal and 6 follicular, doing 4 sets of 5. Luteal estradiol ran **~80–136 pg/mL** against **27–34 pg/mL** in the follicular phase, peaking **16 minutes into the session**. **Consitt 2002** reported similar findings; **Walberg-Rankin 1992** found it only in a calorie deficit.

**Nakamura 2011** adds cycle regularity as a moderator. Women with normal cycles in the mid-luteal phase had higher resting estradiol and progesterone than women with abnormal cycles and showed significant post-training rises in both; the same women showed no change in the early follicular phase, and **women with abnormal cycles showed no post-training change in either phase**.

The article then does the thing that makes it worth reading: **none of these studies checked whether the hormone changes correlated with strength, hypertrophy or any training outcome**, and measuring circulating hormones during exercise is confounded by plasma volume shifts, clearance and metabolism, and posture. Taken at face value, the findings still license nothing about training.

## Chronic depletion vs monthly fluctuation — the core argument

Ovariectomy is not available as a human experiment, so the evidence comes from rodents and from menopause.

**Ovariectomised rats** show impaired insulin sensitivity and, at the muscle level, reduced cross-sectional area, lower protein remodelling and turnover, and slower recovery of muscle mass after enforced inactivity (Kitajima & Ono 2016). Estrogen is proposed to increase actin-myosin cross-bridge number, raising force per fibre, and to protect myosin from oxidative damage (Lowe 2010); replacement restores strength and size to control levels.

**Postmenopausal women** have higher rates of both muscle protein breakdown and synthesis and lower muscle mass than men or premenopausal women (Smith & Mittendorfer 2016) — consistent with breakdown outpacing synthesis. A review (Collins 2019) suggests estrogen preserves muscle by reducing apoptosis, and the article carefully attributes that to **chronic** depletion, not cyclic fluctuation. Chronic loss also blunts the training response in a way not seen before menopause, though declining physical activity across the lifespan is a co-explanation (Enns & Tiidus 2010).

**Then the magnitude argument.** Estradiol in menstruating women runs about **32.7–87.2 pg/mL in the follicular phase and 65.4–242.4 pg/mL in the luteal phase** — roughly three times the average of the first five post-menopausal years, which keeps falling thereafter (Anttila 1991). For the cyclic dip to do what menopause does, it would have to be far larger and last far longer.

**And the mechanistic check.** Muscle protein synthesis rates do not differ significantly between cycle phases (Miller 2006), and men and women show similar protein turnover through young and mid-adulthood — implying a wide estrogen range compatible with normal muscle function. This is the part of the article most worth keeping.

## The performance studies, with their numbers

- **Kishali 2006** — survey across judo, taekwondo, volleyball and basketball. Women medalled regardless of phase with no significant performance differences, **despite reporting feeling significantly better during the first 14 days**.
- **Phillips 1996** — 12 untrained women, 10 trained, 5 trained on oral contraceptives, 6 untrained men. Force by thumb-index transducer squeeze; phase by basal body temperature and urine LH. Women rose about **10%** across the follicular phase, trained and untrained alike — but **plasma estrogen did not correlate with relative force**, and within-cycle variation was **±1.2 lb against a 24 lb mean**.
- **Sarwar 1996** — 20 sedentary women (10 on oral contraceptives), quadriceps MVIC with superimposed electrical stimulation plus handgrip, weekly across two cycles, phase standardised on an **assumed 32-day cycle with ovulation inferred, not measured**. Mid-cycle (days 12–18) quadriceps strength was significantly higher, by up to **13.5 lb (11.7%)** in untrained women — against a within-sample SD of about **11.2 lb**. The stimulation rules out central nervous system inhibition as the mechanism.
- **Elliott 2003** — 7 women, day 2 vs day 21, hormones measured, pinch force as in Phillips. **No strength difference despite large hormone variation.**
- **Janse de Jonge 2001** — 19 women with regular cycles; isometric quadriceps with superimposed stimulation plus isokinetic knee flexion and extension at multiple angles, with estradiol, progesterone, LH and FSH drawn at every session. **No differences across phases, and no correlation between any performance metric and any hormone concentration.** Small, but the best-controlled design in the set.
- **Romero-Moraleda 2019** — 13 female triathletes, no hormonal contraceptives; half-squat 1RM then 20/40/60/80% at maximal velocity on three randomised test days across early follicular, late follicular and mid-luteal. Phase by temperature, LH strips and menses; **hormones not measured**. No differences in peak or mean power or velocity.
- **Weidauer 2020** — 50 women (25 on oral contraceptives), knee extension, knee flexion and grip across three phases with hormones measured. **No effect of hormone levels on strength**, though strength was slightly higher in the follicular phase — leading the authors to propose a **non-hormonal** cause for whatever differences appear.

**The confound the article uses well:** Birch & Reilly 2002 tested maximal isometric lifting strength (deadlift at knee height) in 10 untrained women and found no difference between phases — but a **~13 lb difference between testing at 6 am and 6 pm**. Time of day, itself likely habituation, moved strength more than cycle phase did.

Their honest summary: the barbell-relevant data is limited to proxies — pinch force, grip, isolated knee extension, half squats — and there is no consensus on strength-sport performance or adaptation, largely because the data does not exist.

## Periodising by phase: four studies, four confounds

- **Reis 1995** — 7 untrained women as their own controls, one leg on menstrual-cycle-triggered training (leg press 3×12, three times weekly in the follicular phase, once weekly in the luteal), the other every third day. Both improved; the MCTT leg more. **No p-values reported.** That leg also carried reduced volume before every test, which is a taper, and within-subject limb comparisons are vulnerable to contralateral crosstalk. One positive: the researchers managed a workable at-home phase-identification method using body temperature and ovulation strips.
- **Sung 2014** — 20 untrained women, one leg high-volume in the follicular phase, the other in the luteal. The follicular-high leg gained more strength and size — but the rate of change was similar in every week except the first two and last two, so **the effect sits in initial exposure and the taper**, not the phase manipulation. Crosstalk again.
- **Sakamaki-Sunaga 2016** — 14 untrained women, arms trained on opposite schedules, 1RM dumbbell curl at baseline, 4, 8 and 12 weeks. Both arms gained cross-sectional area and strength; **no difference between arms**. The clean negative result, and the one the article's summary line glosses over.
- **Wikström-Frisén 2017** — 59 resistance-familiar women (32 on oral contraceptives) in three groups over four months: follicular-high (5×/week then 1×/week), luteal-high (the reverse), and a 3×/week control. Groups 1 and 3 improved squat jump, countermovement jump and hamstring peak torque, but **not quadriceps torque**; the luteal-high group did not improve. The confound is decisive: **each group entered post-testing at a different training volume** — five sessions that week, one, and three respectively — so the follicular group tested off a de facto taper and the luteal group off a heavy week.

Two limits apply to all four: **none measured hormone levels**, and most assumed a 28-day cycle, when cycles run roughly 18–40 days (Fehring 2006) and vary with age and BMI (Bull 2019). Ovulation strips separate follicular from luteal but cannot resolve early/mid/late without daily assays.

The article's own summary — "weak but converging evidence pointing to a benefit of periodized training" — **is more generous than its own analysis warrants**, given one null result and three taper-and-crosstalk explanations. It then argues the effect away, which reads as the authors' actual view.

## Contraceptives, meta-analyses, and the position they land on

Oral contraceptives are hard to study because formulations differ in hormone type and dose. Most studies show no strength difference between users and non-users, and a meta-analysis (Elliott-Sale 2020) found they **may slightly reduce exercise performance, with very small group-level effects and wide inter-individual variation** — a case for individual management, not a blanket rule. Symptom dulling may itself improve training tolerance (Knowles 2019). Their framing — that managing the conditions contraceptives treat matters more to training than any direct performance effect — is a clinician's framing and a fair one.

Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses — McNulty 2020, Thompson 2020, Blagrove 2020, Carmichael 2021 — agree the evidence is **inconclusive**, and McNulty is explicit that most of it is low quality. The forest plot reproduced in the article has a pooled estimate near the line of no effect.

Their four stated reasons for accepting the null: no reliable or repeatable phase-induced differences; high inter-individual variability in response to phase, contraceptives and training alike (Ahtiainen 2016); other inputs — sleep, nutrition, programming — being more reliable and more modifiable; and the cost of accurate tracking, both financial and in **nocebo risk**.

The clinical advice that follows is advice, not evidence: do not automatically start the luteal phase at lower intensity, frequency or volume; do not deload by default on symptoms; auto-regulate and monitor actual response; a coach's job is to find the pattern if one exists, not to plan around an assumed one; team-sport athletes should train to the competition schedule and to the group.

**Where it becomes advocacy.** "Athletes are human first, female second," and the argument that telling women to periodise by period constructs a barrier to training at all, are position statements rather than findings — and the nocebo mechanism is asserted without a citation despite being the article's fourth pillar.

**What they sell.** Unusually for this source, no product is pitched. It recommends working with a coach who auto-regulates, which is a service they sell, but not as a purchase.
