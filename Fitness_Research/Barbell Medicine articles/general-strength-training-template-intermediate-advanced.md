# A Three-Day RPE Template, and the Clearest Statement of Why RPE Beats Percentages

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "General Strength Training Template for the Intermediate/Advanced" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/scivationstrong/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2016-05-19 · 3,917 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **This article cites nothing.** It is a written-out training block plus the coaching reasoning behind it. Every claim is Feigenbaum's opinion or coaching experience, and he flags most of them as such ("it is my opinion that…", "I've found…").
- **It was commissioned.** The programme was written for **Scivation**, a supplement company, who released the workouts on Instagram; the article is the long-form explanation. It directs readers to Scivation's Instagram at the end. That is the commercial relationship in this piece — it does not push a Barbell Medicine product.
- **The template is three days, competition-lift-led:** each session opens with a single at RPE 8 on one of the big three, then repeated sets of 5 at RPE 8, then a specific supplemental variant at ascending RPE 7/8/9, then a lighter supplemental at RPE 6/7/8 for higher reps.
- **The best content is the RPE case.** Percentages are anchored to a 1RM that is "only accurate on the day it's performed"; RPE self-corrects for sleep, stress, nutrition, motivation and accumulated fatigue on the day. He also states the downsides honestly — a real learning curve, and chronic over- or under-raters.
- **Explicitly not for novices**, defined by rate of progress rather than load: a novice is someone who can still add weight every session. If you have never run a linear progression, run one first.

## The programme

**Workout 1** — Competition squat: 1 @ RPE 8, then 5 @ RPE 8 × 3–5 sets. 2-count paused bench: 4 @ 7, 4 @ 8, 4 @ 9 × 3–4 sets. Romanian deadlift: 7 @ 6, 7 @ 7, 7 @ 8 × 4 sets.

**Workout 2** — Competition bench: 1 @ RPE 8, then 5 @ RPE 8 × 4–6 sets. 2-count paused squat: 4 @ 7, 4 @ 8, 4 @ 9 × 3–4 sets. Press: 7 @ 6, 7 @ 7, 7 @ 8 × 4 sets.

**Workout 3** — Competition deadlift: 1 @ RPE 8, then 5 @ RPE 8 × 3–5 sets. Touch-and-go bench: 4 @ 7, 4 @ 8, 4 @ 9 × 3–4 sets. Front squat: 7 @ 6/7/8 × 4 sets. Dumbbell incline bench: 8 @ 6/7/8 × 4 sets.

Plus **GPP** 1–2 days a week, time-capped so the lifter cannot overdose it: 25–30 min steady-state cardio at RPE 6 (conversational), 6–8 min upper-back work with no sets to failure, 6–8 min trunk work (preferably isometric), and mobility only if needed.

The design logic he gives: sets of 4–5 permit heavy loads, accumulate volume and intra-set fatigue, and still allow technical focus, where a set of 10 limits load and degrades attention. The **preceding single** does two jobs — it rehearses the first-rep mechanics that matter at a meet, where you only get one attempt, and it calibrates the day's working load. Supplemental lifts sit at 6–7 reps to add volume for hypertrophy and to keep total training stress in check during a developmental block. The set *ranges* (3–5, 4–6) are deliberate: on a day where the single and first work set move well, take the extra sets; on a bad day, do not.

Later blocks, he says, move toward RPE 9 with occasional 10s — these first four weeks are for volume exposure.

## The RPE argument

The history is given accurately: Gunnar Borg's original 6–20 scale from the 1960s, where each increment maps to a subjective effort level and multiplying by ten approximates heart rate, developed for aerobic exercise physiology rather than lifting. **Mike Tuchscherer** of Reactive Training Systems is credited by name as the person who brought RPE into strength training as a load-selection tool, and Feigenbaum notes he was Tuchscherer's client and friend — a credit-where-due passage that is unusually explicit.

His three advantages: RPE **accounts for daily variability** (a 1RM is accurate only on the day it is set, so percentages derived from it drift out of date); it **communicates the intended training stress for the context**, since being 16 weeks versus 14 days from a meet should change the volume/intensity balance; and it **builds the lifter's self-awareness**, which matters most for competitors, where attempt selection depends on reading your own capacity on the day.

His two disadvantages, stated without spin: **poor RPE gauging** is normal at first, with both under- and over-estimation, and some people chronically mis-rate in one direction — remedied by practice, honesty after the set, and video. Second, **planned linear progress is harder to visualise** — though he argues RPE and linear progression are not mutually exclusive; keep adding weight weekly if you can, and let RPE be the fallback when the planned jump does not happen.

**The definition of a novice is the sharpest thing in the article.** A novice is not defined by how much they lift but by the **sustainable rate of load increase** with other variables held constant — someone who can still add weight every session. The corollary he states bluntly: if you have never run a linear progression, you are a novice until proven otherwise, and this programme is not for you.

## Equipment, exercise order, and the "it depends" sections

**Belts, wraps and straps.** Competition lifts get competition form and equipment — belt, wrist wraps, knee sleeves, paused bench, specialty bars if your federation uses them. On supplemental lifts he is permissive about belts (particularly for a lifter with a back-injury history where a belt makes training more productive) and wrist wraps.

He is more restrictive on **knee wraps and straps**, and the reasoning is mechanical: knee wraps assist out of the hole and reduce the squat's training effect; straps reduce grip demand and, by permitting double-overhand, remove the need to manage a mixed grip — where the supinated side's latissimus dorsi sits at a less favourable mechanical advantage, producing the familiar "windmill" of that side of the bar away from the lifter. **All of this is mechanical reasoning without supporting data**, and the "reduces the training effect" claims in particular are assertions, not findings. His overall position is pragmatic: use them when you have to, including to train around knee pain or torn hands.

**Exercise order.** In this developmental phase each competition lift gets priority in its own session, and the competition deadlift is spaced as far as possible from the squat to keep squat fatigue off it. Approaching a meet he would reverse both choices deliberately — bench directly after squats, deadlift nearer the squat — to condition the lifter to the meet's actual fatigue sequence. The trade-off is development versus specificity, resolved by how much time remains. Where to put everything else (cardio, arm work) gets an honest non-answer: the further from competition, the more can be added, provided recovery holds.

## The worked training day

The load-finding walkthrough is the most practically useful section, because it shows the method rather than the theory. He works a 500 lb squatter up: empty bar × 5 for 5 sets, 95, 135, 225, 275 × 5, 325 × 3, 375 × 1, 425 × 1 at ~RPE 6, 445 × 1 at ~RPE 7, 460 × 1 at RPE 8 — then 405 × 5 @ 8, 405 × 5 @ 8.5, 405 × 5 @ 9, scrapping the fifth set because RPE has climbed.

Then he does something better on the bench. He *tries* to predict the 2-count paused bench load from RPE charts and a touch-and-go 1RM, reaches a messy 240–250 lb estimate, and abandons the exercise: predicting the weight "is getting REALLY complicated, especially if we don't know our 1 rep max for that movement. So is there an easier way? Sure." The answer is to ramp from the empty bar until a set lands on the target RPE — bar, 95, 135, 185, 215 @ 7, 230 @ 8, 240 @ 9, then back-offs at 235 @ 9 and 230 @ 8.5 (repeated, because it came in low). Warming up **through** RPE 7 and 8 is what makes the top set findable.

His warm-up stance is also stated plainly and without evidence: if you can reach the required positions, skip foam rolling and mobility work and start squatting with the empty bar, on the reasoning that squatting prepares you for squatting better than stretching does. Reasonable, untested, and presented as his view.
