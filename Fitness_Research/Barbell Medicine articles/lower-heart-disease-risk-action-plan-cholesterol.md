# The Cholesterol Action Plan: ApoB, Lifetime Exposure, and Early Combination Therapy

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How-To Lower Your Heart Disease Risk: Action Plan for Cholesterol" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/lower-your-heart-disease-risk/)) · Austin Baraki · 2025-12-27 · 6,242 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **The organising claim is cumulative exposure, not a snapshot**: "the lower, the longer, the better." Risk is a function of how long ApoB-containing particles have been elevated, which is why a low 10-year score in a 45-year-old can coexist with a high lifetime risk.
- **ApoB over LDL-C.** ApoB counts atherogenic particles rather than the cholesterol mass they carry, and it is the particle that enters the artery wall. Where the two disagree — commonly in diabetes — ApoB is the more reliable. Target: **below 90 mg/dL for most people**, lower for high risk.
- **Early combination therapy over dose escalation.** Each doubling of a statin dose buys only ~6% more LDL-C lowering, so a low-dose statin plus ezetimibe (~20% additional reduction) reaches targets faster and with fewer side effects than pushing one drug to maximum.
- **Diet does two things well**: raise the polyunsaturated-to-saturated fat ratio, and get to 30 g+ of fibre a day. Everything else in the nutrition section is secondary to those two.
- **Supplements get a near-uniform rejection**, including from a company that sells supplements — red yeast rice, citrus bergamot and over-the-counter fish oil are all turned down for regulatory, dosing and outcome reasons. Only phytosterols get qualified support.
- **This article has no numbered reference list.** Everything is inline hyperlinks, so no claim-level numbered mapping exists — see References.

## What to measure
The standard panel is treated as a foundation rather than an answer. Non-fasting samples are adequate for most purposes; only triglycerides are materially affected by fasting. **HDL-C is explicitly demoted** to a marker of good health rather than a treatment target — interventions that raise it do not protect against heart disease, and extremely high levels (above roughly 97 mg/dL in men, 135 in women) are paradoxically associated with *increased* mortality, a finding the article links to two prospective cohorts ([Madsen 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28419274/)). Very low HDL-C shows up in diabetes, liver disease and anabolic steroid use.

On LDL, they make the useful point that particle *size* is a distraction: all LDL particles are small enough to enter the artery wall.

**ApoB** is the centrepiece. Discordance between LDL-C and ApoB is common enough to matter clinically ([Sniderman 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38700053/)), and in discordant cases ApoB wins. The causal argument leans on Mendelian randomisation: people with genetic variants producing lifelong low ApoB have much lower risk than people who lower ApoB later in life — which is simultaneously evidence that ApoB is causal and evidence that duration of exposure is the variable that matters. The test costs under $30.

**Lp(a)** is genetically determined and largely fixed, so it should be measured **once in a lifetime** to set a baseline. As of 2025 no approved therapy targets it directly; the practical consequence of a high result is to manage every other risk factor as though the patient were high-risk.

## Interpreting the numbers
Non-HDL-C (total cholesterol minus HDL-C) is offered as the free proxy for ApoB when ApoB is unavailable, because it captures LDL, Lp(a) and remnants. **Remnant cholesterol** gets more attention than it usually receives: it is calculated as TC − HDL-C − LDL-C, is flagged by triglycerides above 150 mg/dL, is described as a hallmark of insulin resistance rather than an isolated lipid finding, and is less responsive to standard drugs. Their thresholds: **below 20 mg/dL optimal, above 30 mg/dL very high**. Their claim that remnant particles can carry up to 40 times more cholesterol than a standard LDL particle is stated without a source.

The risk-assessment section is the most clinically conventional part: 10-year calculators for ages 40–79, with the explicit caveat that they systematically underestimate young people, and 30-year/lifetime estimates preferred for ages 20–59. A list of risk-enhancing factors follows, including family history of premature disease, chronic kidney disease or diabetes, hs-CRP above 2.0 mg/L, chronic inflammatory disease, premature menopause, adverse pregnancy outcomes and South Asian ancestry. Familial hypercholesterolaemia is flagged at LDL-C persistently above 190 mg/dL or ApoB above 130.

**Their target table:** LDL-C <100 (low/average risk) or <55 (high risk); non-HDL-C <130 or <70; ApoB <80 or <50; triglycerides <100 for everyone. These are aggressive relative to some guidelines and are presented as desirable rather than mandated — though a reader should note the non-HDL-C target appears as "<100–130" in the text, "<130" in the table, and "below 100" in the action box, which is an internal inconsistency, not a nuance.

**On coronary artery calcium**, the reasoning is the sharpest in the article. CAC detects only calcified plaque, which is the late, stabilised form; soft non-calcified plaque is the more rupture-prone kind and requires CT coronary angiography to see. So the test's meaning inverts with age: **in a young adult, CAC greater than zero is highly informative** (plaque present before it should be) while zero is weak (young plaque has not had time to calcify); **in an older adult, CAC of zero is highly informative** while a positive score is expected. In someone with established disease it is irrelevant. This is genuinely good clinical teaching and not the way CAC is usually explained to lay readers.

## Diet and exercise
Two levers do the work. **Fat quality** — saturated fat slows cholesterol clearance from the blood; polyunsaturated and monounsaturated fats prompt the liver to clear it faster, so the P:S ratio summarises the effect, and replacing saturated with unsaturated fat is described as the single most potent dietary move for LDL-C. **Fibre** — at least 30 g/day, working mainly through soluble fibre binding bile acids so the liver must pull cholesterol from the blood to make more.

Dietary cholesterol is deliberately demoted to third place, with two reasons it still matters: genetic hyper-responders, and the fact that statins increase intestinal cholesterol absorption. There is also a practical detail most articles miss — **unfiltered coffee (French press, espresso) raises LDL via diterpenes**, so paper-filtered coffee is a free swap. They note that excess body fat can limit the effectiveness of other lipid-lowering interventions ([Sundfør 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31216575/)).

Exercise: exceed the standard guidelines — 150–300 minutes moderate or 75–150 vigorous aerobic activity per week, plus resistance training at least twice weekly.

**The one section where the reasoning outruns the evidence** is the CAC-in-athletes passage. High-volume aerobic activity raises CAC scores; the article interprets this as plaque *stabilisation* — soft plaque converting to safer hard plaque — and therefore not a negative finding. The supporting links are a narrative review ([Sung & Sung 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11547064/)) and two small plaque-composition studies ([Madssen 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25248813/), an intravascular ultrasound study; [Vesterbekkmo 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36562212/), a HIIT randomised trial). Those are consistent with the interpretation but do not establish it, and the flat statement that "a higher CAC score in a high-performing athlete does not carry the same risk as the same score in a sedentary individual" is stated more confidently than that evidence base supports.

## Drugs
This is the strongest section and the one that justifies the article's existence, since it is written by a practising physician and reviewed by another.

- **Statins** upregulate hepatic LDL receptors. The dose-response anchor is the Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' meta-analysis of **over 170,000 participants across 26 trials**: every 38 mg/dL (1 mmol/L) of LDL-C lowering produces a **25–30% reduction in major cardiovascular events**, proportional to the absolute reduction achieved ([CTT 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21067804/)). High-potency options (rosuvastatin, atorvastatin) can reach 65% lowering at maximum dose, but each doubling adds only ~6%.
- **Pitavastatin** gets an unusual amount of space: fewer drug interactions, neutral or favourable effect on blood glucose where other statins modestly raise it, neutral effect on Lp(a) where other statins raise it 10–20%, and the lowest rate of muscle symptoms.
- **New-onset diabetes** is handled honestly but slightly overstated. They say the risk is "almost exclusively" in people who already have prediabetes or metabolic risk factors, and that higher cardiorespiratory fitness largely mitigates it ([Radford 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25568952/), [Kokkinos 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41031997/)). "Almost exclusively" is stronger than the cited review supports; "concentrated in" would be accurate.
- **Ezetimibe** — 10 mg daily, ~20% additional LDL-C reduction, well tolerated, and mechanistically complementary because it closes the absorption "back door" statins open. Moderate statin plus ezetimibe versus high-intensity statin monotherapy is supported by a 2025 meta-analysis ([Yasmin 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40492183/)).
- **Bempedoic acid** — 25% alone, +18% added to a statin, 40% with ezetimibe; liver-restricted, so fewer muscle symptoms.
- **PCSK9 inhibitors** — ~50% LDL-C lowering plus **18–36% Lp(a) lowering**, with outcome evidence from FOURIER ([Sabatine 2017](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1615664)) and ODYSSEY Outcomes ([Schwartz 2018](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1801174)) showing further benefit on top of high-intensity statins.
- **Prescription omega-3s** — the strongest evidence is for high-dose EPA as icosapent ethyl (REDUCE-IT, [Bhatt 2019](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792)); carboxylic acid formulations did not show clear benefit (STRENGTH).
- **Older agents** — fibrates for triglycerides above ~500 mg/dL to prevent pancreatitis, with no compelling standalone cardiovascular benefit; niacin rejected outright (failed outcomes, liver failure risk with high-dose extended-release); cholestyramine largely obsolete.

**The overreach to flag:** "A 1% reduction in plaque volume is associated with an 18% reduction in major cardiovascular events" is presented as justification for aggressive combination therapy, with **no source at all**, and the word doing the work is "associated" — this is a surrogate-endpoint relationship being used to argue for a treatment intensity. The drug recommendations elsewhere rest on hard outcome trials; this one does not, and it is the sentence that closes the argument.

## Supplements, and what they sell
Worth recording plainly because Barbell Medicine sells a supplement line: **this article recommends against almost every cholesterol supplement on the market.** Red yeast rice is rejected because its active compound is chemically identical to lovastatin with worse dosing control and contamination risk. Citrus bergamot has modest lab-value effects but no outcome evidence. Over-the-counter fish oil lacks evidence for the general population. Only **phytosterols** get support: 2–3 g/day lowers LDL-C by 9–12% by reducing intestinal cholesterol absorption 30–45%, with the sitosterolemia caveat affecting about 0.05% of people.

What they do sell here is a **free downloadable Cholesterol Action Plan PDF**, promoted four times and containing an automated "Lipid Scorecard and Hidden Risk Calculator" — a lead magnet rather than a product — plus online physician consultations, linked three times, and several of their own podcast episodes.
