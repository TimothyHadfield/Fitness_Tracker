# Cholesterol, From First Principles: What the Panel Measures and What Actually Moves It

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Cholesterol - Causes, Prevention & Treatment" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/a-basic-guide-to-cholesterol/)) · Austin Baraki · 2021-12-04 · 3,812 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **Risk is cumulative exposure, not today's number.** Their central claim is that heart disease risk tracks how high blood lipoprotein levels have been *multiplied by how long*, which is why treating late captures far less benefit than treating early ([Robinson 2018](https://doi.org/10.1161/jaha.118.009778)). About 40% of events occur before age 65, per the article.
- **The number to read off a standard panel is non-HDL-C** (total cholesterol minus HDL-C), not LDL-C. Target under 130 mg/dL for generally healthy people, as low as possible for high-risk people, and they state flatly there is no blood level that is "too low."
- **ApoB is better still** — it counts particles rather than the cholesterol they carry — and their complaint is that most doctors still won't order it despite it being cheap.
- **HDL-C is a marker, not a target.** Trials that raise HDL-C have not reduced events, and genetic-variant studies find no causal effect (Voight 2012, Mendelian randomisation). LDL passes both tests where HDL fails both. This is the cleanest piece of reasoning in the article.
- **The two dietary levers they actually push are fibre (≥30 g/day) and fat *type*, not fat total.** Replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat beats replacing it with carbohydrate, even high-fibre carbohydrate ([Li 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26429077/)). They explicitly decline to demonise total fat or dietary cholesterol.
- Notably, the article sells nothing. No template, supplement or consultation is pitched; the only links out are to their own free articles.

## Why cholesterol matters at all
The framing is mechanical rather than moralistic. Cholesterol is essential — every cell can make what it needs, and infants who cannot make it have severe birth defects — so the problem is never cholesterol per se but *lipoproteins in the blood over time*. Lipoproteins below roughly 70 nanometres can cross the artery wall; LDL particles run 20–30 nm, so **every** LDL particle, large or small, clears that threshold. That single fact does the work later used to dismiss the "particle size matters" argument.

They are careful to say risk is probabilistic, not deterministic, and use the smoking analogy: not every smoker gets lung cancer, and the risk is real anyway. They also concede up front what critics point at — heart attacks in people with unremarkable numbers, early deaths in people with low numbers — and promise to explain them in the third part of the series rather than waving them away.

The strong version of their claim is that even with normal blood pressure, no diabetes, no smoking and no inflammation, higher cholesterol still raises risk. That rests on two named links, Yusuf 2004 (INTERHEART) and Friera 2017 (the PESA imaging cohort). Both are observational: a case-control study of risk factors and a subclinical-atherosclerosis imaging cohort. They support "independent association," which is what is claimed here; the causal weight is carried elsewhere, by the genetic and trial evidence they only gesture at in this instalment.

## Reading the panel
Four components, with their commentary:
- **Total cholesterol** — everything on all lipoproteins; not very useful alone.
- **HDL-C** — associated with lower risk, but correlation only, and interventions that raise it don't help.
- **Triglycerides** — a real contributor, largely set aside here except that above 500 mg/dL it becomes an urgent problem in its own right.
- **LDL-C** — the main, though not the only, particle that enters the vessel wall.

Their simplification: **TC − HDL-C = non-HDL-C**, which captures all the potentially harmful cholesterol from a cheap panel and predicts better than LDL-C alone. Worked example given: 200 − 50 = 150 mg/dL. The high-risk pattern they flag is the diabetic triad — high LDL-C, high triglycerides, low HDL-C together.

**ApoB** is presented as the superior test because it counts the particles that do the damage rather than the cargo, with Sniderman 2021 cited for the case. They also state that most other advanced lipid tests add little beyond this — a usefully deflationary point given how much of the market sells them.

## What actually moves the number
**Genetics.** Given first, and given real weight. LDL-C above 190 mg/dL raises suspicion of familial hypercholesterolaemia, though it does not confirm it. Their clinical framing is worth quoting in substance: people with genetically high cholesterol usually cannot get to a low-risk level with diet and exercise, this is not a failure of effort, and medication is typically necessary. Combined with the cumulative-exposure model, they argue such people are the *most* urgent to treat, because their exposure clock started at birth and ran undetected for decades.

**Diet.** Two targets only. Fibre at 30 g/day minimum from fruit, vegetables, legumes and whole grains, with more being better — the dose-response framing is theirs. And fat *type*: saturated fat from animal sources raises cholesterol and risk, they say, "particularly when saturated fat calories start to exceed 18–20% of total calories," a specific threshold given without a citation attached to it. The substitution hierarchy is the useful part and it *is* cited — [Li 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26429077/), a prospective cohort, found replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat outperformed replacing it with carbohydrate. Note this is cohort data, not a feeding trial; the feeding-study evidence is cited separately as Clarke 1997 (BMJ), which is a meta-analysis of controlled feeding studies on blood lipids, not on events. The distinction is not drawn in the article.

They deliberately decline to recommend low total fat, and decline to make dietary cholesterol a first-line target. A low-carb, higher-fat diet is explicitly acceptable to them if fibre is adequate and fats are mostly unsaturated.

**Body fat.** Waist circumference is the recommended measure, not scale weight. The recommendation to reduce waist to the low-risk range is given as a **strong recommendation** — that is an advice claim, not a demonstrated effect size, and no study is attached to it. They add an explicit anti-stigma position and endorse anti-obesity medication and bariatric surgery as legitimate treatment.

**Other causes.** Check thyroid function: hypothyroidism occurs in roughly **1.4–13% of patients with high cholesterol** ([Tagami 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21505266/), a multi-centre prevalence study), and treating it can lower cholesterol. Review medications that may raise it. Anabolic steroids are named as harmful to the lipid profile and not recommended.

## What comes next, and where they depart from guidelines
Their clearest heterodoxy is on risk estimation: **they reject the 10-year risk window as shortsighted for younger people** and favour a 30-year or lifetime estimate, citing Thanassoulis 2018, which compared a long-term-benefit approach with standard risk-based statin eligibility. This follows directly from the cumulative-exposure model, but it is a *policy* preference of theirs, not a guideline position as of writing — and it would make more young people statin-eligible. They say so, implicitly, and it is worth naming explicitly.

Practical sequence for low-risk people with mild elevation: fix the fat substitutions, get fibre to 30 g, reduce waist circumference, then re-test lipids and/or ApoB in **2–4 months**. For persistent elevation or high risk, medication, discussed with a doctor; drugs are deliberately left out of scope here. Their closing line on this is a clinical-attitude claim rather than an evidence claim, and a fair one: "No one wins a prize for avoiding the use of medicines and living at higher risk than necessary."
