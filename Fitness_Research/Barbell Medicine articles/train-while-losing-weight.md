# Training in a Deficit: Strength Survives, Muscle Gain Doesn't, and Exercise Barely Moves the Scale

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How-To Train While Losing Weight" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-to-train-while-losing-weight/)) · Austin Baraki · 2025-04-19 · 4,762 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Strength is essentially deficit-proof; lean mass is not.** A meta-analysis of 52 studies found energy deficiency impairs resistance-training gains in **lean mass but not strength**, with severity of the deficit not predicting strength outcomes either ([Murphy & Koehler 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14075)). The authors' own explanation is that the strength gains are neural or microarchitectural rather than hypertrophic — which is the correct reading and also the caveat.
- **Exercise alone barely moves weight over the long run.** Long-term (>6 month) exercise-only weight loss lands somewhere between **0 and 2 kg**, with some people gaining. The 1-year Oslo Diet and Exercise Study: exercise-only lost **1.3 kg**, diet plus exercise **6.5 kg** ([Anderssen 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2006.00631.x)). A 10-month trial in men with obesity: exercise-only lost **nothing**, diet plus exercise **~9 kg** ([Dengel 1996](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1996.81.1.318)).
- **They dismantle a meta-analysis on their own side of the argument.** [Jayedi 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.52185) reported 0.52 kg and 0.56 cm waist per 30 min/week of aerobic exercise — Baraki points out it is built mostly on trials of ≤12 weeks, and that its own long-term entries show the effect fading. That is a rigorous move and the best thing in the article.
- **"Anabolic rescue":** resting muscle protein synthesis falls in a deficit regardless of training status, but adding resistance training normalises it and raises it by **~30%** ([Pasiakos 2010](https://doi.org/10.3945/jn.109.118372); [Areta 2014](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.00590.2013)).
- **Recommendation:** 200–500 kcal/day deficit, **0.25–0.50 kg/week**, ≥1.6 g/kg protein, keep training as it is. All reasonable; the "no modification needed" part is an absence-of-evidence claim, and the article contradicts it two paragraphs earlier.

## The exercise-alone argument, and why it is well made
Two mechanisms are offered for why exercise underperforms as a weight-loss lever: people subconsciously move less outside training and metabolism drops slightly, restoring balance; and the exercise volume required to shift energy balance meaningfully runs to **two or three times** the Physical Activity Guidelines, which most people don't meet in the first place.

The evidence chain is honest. Appetite counter-regulation is quantified ([Polidori 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.21653); [Blundell 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.12257)), energy compensation during exercise interventions is systematically reviewed with the finding that **shorter interventions show less compensation** ([Riou 2015](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu7053677)) — which is exactly the mechanism that makes short-trial meta-analyses over-read. And the between-person variation is the twin work: up to **five-fold differences** in response to identical overfeeding or identical exercise at constant intake, largely genetic ([Bouchard 1990](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199005243222101), [1994](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1550-8528.1994.tb00087.x)).

The most telling citation is [Goodpaster 2010](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2010.1505): diet for a full year with exercise added only in the last six months produced the **same** one-year weight loss as diet plus exercise throughout.

**The conclusion drawn is the right shape:** exercise is essential for health and performance, its direct effect on long-term weight is muted, and the deficit comes from diet. They do not use this to argue against exercise, and they explicitly flag responders who do better — via improved dietary adherence or less compensation.

## Strength during restriction — what the studies actually are
Three trials carry the claim, and their populations matter.

**Untrained, aggressive deficit.** 40 young untrained men with overweight, **4 weeks at ~40% below maintenance** with lifting and HIIT: leg press **+98%**, bench **+32%**, max push-ups **+32%**, relative VO2max **+15%**. This is [Longland 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/) — which the article does not name and which does not appear in its numbered list. **Two caveats it omits:** the trial was designed as a high-versus-lower-protein comparison, and untrained men doing supervised training six days a week for four weeks is the single most favourable case for "strength improves in a deficit" that exists.

**Controlled, short, moderate.** 24 untrained adults confined to a research facility for two weeks on a **750 kcal/day deficit** or maintenance. The deficit group lost 1.3 kg with **no detriment** to muscular endurance, strength, five-mile run time or Wingate performance, and no difference from controls ([Zachwieja 2001](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2001-13822)).

**Elite, and the best-designed of the three.** 24 elite Norwegian athletes randomised to fast (1 kg/week) or slow (0.5 kg/week) weight loss to the same endpoint. The slow group lost **31% of fat mass** versus 21%, and **gained 2% lean mass** where the fast group was unchanged. 40 m sprint unchanged in both; squat 1RM up similarly in both; bench up in both but **more in the slow group** ([Garthe 2011](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.21.2.97)). This is the study that justifies the rate-of-loss recommendation, and it is a genuine RCT in the population that matters.

**Credit where due:** Baraki states plainly that there is "relatively little data" for highly trained strength-power athletes on modest deficits, and that it is unclear whether a sub-500 kcal deficit reduces strength gains in the short to medium term. Most sources assert here; he declines to.

The longer-run caveat he adds is the important one: over time a deficit may cost strength **indirectly**, by reducing the muscle gained. His aside that undifferentiated hypertrophy (leg extensions) would not transfer to a 1RM squat the way squatting does is coaching judgement rather than a cited finding, but it is well-reasoned.

## Muscle size, and the honesty about its ceiling
The mechanism is anabolic rescue: resting MPS drops under restriction in trained and untrained alike, and resistance training restores and raises it ~30%. The outcome data show simultaneous muscle gain and fat loss in untrained people, people with obesity, and older adults.

The older-adult trial is worth its numbers: 36 subjects aged 60–80, six months, diet-only versus diet plus lifting; both lost 2.5–3 kg and 2.4–2.7 kg of fat, but **lean body mass rose 0.5 kg only in the lifting group** ([Dunstan 2002](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.25.10.1729)). **Flagged:** that paper's population is older patients with **type 2 diabetes**, which the article does not mention — a relevant restriction on generalisability. Similarly [Hoffman 1990](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/abstract/1990/08000/the_effects_of_self_selection_for_frequency_of.3.aspx) is college footballers self-selecting training frequency during a 10-week winter block, not a controlled deficit trial.

The bottom line they draw is appropriately deflationary: a wide range of people **can** gain some muscle while losing weight, but it will almost certainly be **less** than at maintenance or in a surplus, particularly for trained lifters. That agrees with Murphy & Koehler and with the rest of this library.

## Cardiorespiratory fitness, and the LEA boundary
The VO2max section is the most technically careful writing here. Weight loss shrinks cardiac mass and left ventricular size, and reduces oxygen-hungry muscle, so **absolute** VO2max and oxygen pulse can fall — but the drop may vanish once corrected for fat-free mass, i.e. in **relative** terms ([Weiss 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001074); [Hunter 2012](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e31824f236c); [Sothern 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11094863/)). They then volunteer that the scaling of VO2max to body mass is itself contested and point to Nevill's allometric work — a caveat that weakens their own tidy conclusion, offered unprompted.

Elite endurance performance appears maintained under negative energy balance ([Fudge 2006](https://doi.org/10.1079/BJN20051608); [Burke 2023](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003169); [Brady 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002488)).

The limit is Low Energy Availability, with the standard thresholds: **<30 kcal/kg fat-free mass high risk, 30–45 moderate, >45 no risk** ([Lane 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina55100665)). Severe LEA is a risk factor for RED-S ([Gallant 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02130-0)). They state that both remain active areas of research and that their understanding is subject to change, and they link the LEAM-Q and LEAF-Q screening tools. This is the appropriate level of confidence and it is rare.

## The practical advice, and one internal contradiction
- **200–500 kcal/day deficit, 0.25–0.50 kg/week** for people prioritising performance; a larger deficit is explicitly "acceptable" for people with overweight or obesity prioritising health. That second clause is clinical judgement, not a cited result, and it is the right call for a physician to make.
- **≥1.6 g/kg body weight protein** ([Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608)), more for leaner or harder-training people ([Helms 2014](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2013-0054)) — consistent with the ISSN stand and their own [protein and weight loss](protein-weight-loss-maximizing-fat-loss-preserving.md) article.
- **Avoid low/very-low carbohydrate diets while training** ([Burke 2017](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP273230), elite race walkers, plus a 2024 review). The same evidence appears in their push-day article, where this library already flagged that race-walking economy is thin support for a lifting recommendation.
- **150–300 min moderate or 75–150 min vigorous aerobic activity per week** ([Piercy 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.14854)), with a 4–6 week ramp for the currently inactive.

**Flagged (internal contradiction).** The key-points list says "no special training modifications are necessary while eating less," and the resistance-training section repeats that programming needn't change. But the muscle section says "hypertrophy-focused training, where volume takes precedence over the absolute weight lifted, can be helpful with maintaining muscle mass in a deficit" — which is a training modification. Neither claim is supported by a trial comparing modified against unmodified programming in a deficit; no such comparison is cited. The honest version is "we have no evidence that modification helps," which is what they say elsewhere ("there is little to no evidence for specific exercises, repetition ranges, or programs to aid weight loss").
