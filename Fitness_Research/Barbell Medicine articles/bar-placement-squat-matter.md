# Front Squat vs Back Squat: One EMG Study, No Growth Data, and an Honest Account of Why That Matters

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Does Bar Placement In The Squat Matter?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/does-bar-placement-in-the-squat-matter/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2020-08-21 · 6,038 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- The conclusion — front and back squats produce essentially the same strength and hypertrophy — **rests entirely on activation (sEMG) and joint-angle data. No study cited compares the two squats for measured muscle growth or for transfer of strength to a third test.** Feigenbaum says this himself, and the article is largely a lecture on why that limitation exists.
- The single study analysed is Yavuz et al. (2015): **12 healthy men**, 1RM front and back squat, seven-muscle sEMG normalised to MVIC, plus 2D kinematics. **The only statistically significant EMG difference was vastus medialis** (front 55.4% vs back 48.8% of MVIC); the only kinematic difference was hip angle (front 95.9° vs back 79.5° — a more upright torso). Erector spinae, glute max and both hamstrings did not differ.
- Front squat 1RM averaged **85.0 ± 15.7 kg vs 109.2 ± 25.5 kg** for the back squat. Feigenbaum's headline point is that **near-identical muscle excitation occurred despite ~28% less absolute load** — so the number on the bar is not the training stimulus.
- His treatment of sEMG as a *surrogate endpoint* is the most rigorous thing in this batch. He shows it failing in one direction ([Jenkins 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26159316/) found higher EMG at 80% than 30% 1RM, yet [Schoenfeld 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200)'s meta-analysis of 21 studies found equal hypertrophy) and holding up in another (rectus femoris EMG is far higher in leg extension than leg press — [Ema 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-016-3363-5) — and growth data agree — [Ema 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-013-2700-1), [Fonseca 2014](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000000539)). His rule: **only large EMG differences are worth taking seriously.**
- **The study the entire article analyses is not in the article's reference list.** Neither are Yavuz, Jenkins 2015, Goto 2005 or Green 1999, all cited inline; Evans 2002 appears twice. This is a sloppy bibliography attached to an unusually careful argument.

## Is this ranking backed by growth data or activation data?

Activation data, and the article says so. Feigenbaum frames the whole exercise explicitly: sEMG is "a type of *surrogate endpoint*, e.g. a measurement of a variable that may or may not correlate with an important outcome". His analogy is LDL and blood pressure standing in for cardiovascular events. He does not claim a hypertrophy or strength result for front vs back squat — he claims that *the absence of a large activation difference makes a large outcome difference unlikely*. That is a defensible inference and a weaker claim than most exercise-selection content in this library makes on the same kind of data.

He also spells out the limits of the tool itself. sEMG measures muscle *excitation* — depolarisation detected at the skin — not muscle *activation*, the proportion of fibres actually contracting, which depends on excitation-contraction delay and ion kinetics ([Vigotsky 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00985), [Zatsiorsky 2012](https://doi.org/10.5040/9781492595298)). MVIC normalisation produces numbers that are, in his words, "unlikely to be accurate" but "likely to be fairly precise" — 55% is more than 49%, but 55% does not mean 55% of fibres are working. He notes the methodological requirements (shaving, skin prep, gel, ≥2 cm inter-electrode spacing to limit cross-talk).

The two-sided evidence check is worth keeping. Against sEMG as a hypertrophy surrogate: Jenkins had 9 women and 9 men do leg extensions, 3 sets to failure at 80% 1RM and, a week later, at 30% — EMG was higher at 80%, yet the load literature shows no hypertrophy difference when sets go to failure. For sEMG between *different exercises*: Ema 2016 found much higher rectus femoris excitation in a single-joint leg extension than in leg press, and separate training data (Ema 2013, Fonseca 2014) found single-joint work grows rectus femoris while multi-joint work does not. For strength, the only supporting evidence he offers is [Calatayud 2015](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000000589) — 30 college students, band-resisted push-ups vs bench press, 5×6 twice weekly for 5 weeks at a fixed 6RM load — where equal pectoral and anterior deltoid EMG was accompanied by equal 1RM and 6RM bench gains. He flags the protocol's oddity himself ("Yep, these folks did 5 sets of 6 with a '6RM' twice weekly for 5 weeks") and concedes the EMG-to-strength link "probably needs more research before it is well established" ([Vigotsky 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00985)).

## What the Yavuz study actually shows

Twelve healthy males with some front and back squat experience and no injury history. Feigenbaum's complaint is that nothing else was reported — no age, bodyweight, height or training history — and that only group means are given, so EMG cannot be correlated with 1RM at the individual level. Bodyweight has to be back-calculated: the participants lifted 138% and 105% of bodyweight, implying ~79 kg.

Depth was defined as "knee angle of 90°", set with adjustable stoppers. Feigenbaum reads the figure as showing squats **above parallel** and argues this is the study's most consequential limitation: deeper squats would likely produce larger hip and knee angle differences, and possibly larger EMG differences, between the two variations. That caveat should travel with the conclusion.

Electrodes covered rectus femoris, vastus medialis, vastus lateralis, erector spinae, gluteus maximus, biceps femoris and semitendinosus, synchronised to ~120 fps video with markers at right ankle, knee, hip and bar end for 2D kinematics. Only two significant findings emerged: vastus medialis excitation (55.4% front vs 48.8% back) and hip angle (95.89° front vs 79.46° back). Everything else — including the posterior chain, where the folk expectation is that the back squat loads it more — showed no significant difference. [Gullett 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19002072/) is offered as corroboration that leg and trunk muscles are comparably active in both.

Feigenbaum's practical reading: front squats build front squat strength, back squats build back squat strength, and given the joint-angle similarity, carryover between them should be high. For anything that is *not* a squat, the two look equivalent. The framing device is a thought experiment about identical twins, one front squatting and one low-bar back squatting for a year — his answer is no meaningful difference in leg press 1RM or leg cross-sectional area, so preference should decide, and using both variations avoids early specialisation.

**A title-to-content mismatch worth flagging:** the article is called "Does Bar Placement In The Squat Matter?" and the twins are described as front vs *low bar* back squat, but the Yavuz data compare front squat to what Feigenbaum himself calls the **high bar** back squat. No cited study compares high bar to low bar at all. Low bar is asserted into a conclusion the data do not cover.

## The background half: adherence, specificity, and the hypertrophy inputs

Roughly the first third is a general exercise-selection framework, and it is more interesting than usual because it foregrounds behaviour. Self-selected exercise type and intensity improve adherence via affective response and self-efficacy ([Baz-Valle 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0226989), [Williams 2008](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.30.5.471), [Neupert 2009](https://doi.org/10.1123/japa.17.1.1)); movement variability aids motor learning ([Wu 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3616), [Dhawale 2017](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-neuro-072116-031548)); early specialisation is associated with more injury and worse long-term outcomes in youth athletes ([Post 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546517690848), [Bell 2018](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-0657)). He notes the ACSM-derived guideline of 8–10 exercises for major muscle groups, ≥2 non-consecutive days per week, at loads producing volitional fatigue in 8–12 reps ([Westcott 2009](https://doi.org/10.3810/psm.2009.06.1709)), and states plainly that **no specific movement or rep range carries a unique health benefit** — a heavier squat 1RM buys you a heavier squat 1RM.

The physiology section makes one claim that sits against gym orthodoxy: motor unit recruitment is roughly similar across light (<50% 1RM) and heavy (>85%) dynamic efforts, with the difference lying in rate coding ([Potvin & Fuglevand 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005581) — note this is a computational model, not human data; [van den Tillaar 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0217044) found similar quadriceps activation from 50% to 90% 1RM in single squat reps). Rate coding also rises at light loads near failure, but those adaptations skew toward sustaining force rather than maximising it ([Vila-Chã 2010](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.01213.2009)).

The hypertrophy-inputs list — mechanical loading, motor unit recruitment, range of motion, metabolic stress, muscle damage, volume — is standard, with two honest hedges. He notes that mechanical tension and excitation are not the same thing, citing [Simpson 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28138986/), in which six weeks of static stretching alone increased calf size. And on metabolic stress he says outright that "causality remains speculative". On failure proximity he cites [Pareja-Blanco 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12678) and [Sampson 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12445) as showing equivalent outcomes training well short of failure, then still lands on ~4–5 reps in reserve or closer as the practical zone — a recommendation, not a finding.

**Where it outruns the evidence.** The Key Points claim "front squats and back squats have nearly identical sEMG amplitudes at submaximal *and* maximal loads", but Yavuz tested 1RM only; the submaximal half rests on Gullett. The twins verdict extends a 12-subject, above-parallel, single-session 1RM EMG comparison to a year of training and to a variation (low bar) never measured. And "it doesn't really matter what the absolute load being lifted is, but rather how hard the muscles are working" is supported here by [Carpinelli 2008](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/241485398) and [Jungblut 2009](https://doi.org/10.2478/v10036-009-0033-2), two commentary pieces rather than trials.
