# A Chest-and-Biceps Day Built on Pairing Logic, With Almost No Outcome Data Under the Exercise Picks

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The 7 Best Chest and Biceps Workout" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-chest-and-biceps-workout/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2023-12-29 · 5,972 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- The one genuinely useful idea here is the **pairing rationale**, not the exercise list: chest and biceps are an "alternate-peripheral" pair whose exercises barely overlap, so fatigue carryover is smaller than for chest+triceps or chest+shoulders. They are explicit that whether this produces *more* growth than another pairing is "up for debate" — a rare and correct hedge.
- **Not one of the seven exercises is chosen on hypertrophy outcome data.** Six are picked on anatomy and convention; the seventh — the concentration curl — is picked on an EMG activation study, and that study is a non-peer-reviewed industry PDF from the American Council on Exercise [14].
- Where the article *does* cite hypertrophy outcome trials, it uses them for programming variables rather than exercise choice: push-ups vs bench press over 4 weeks gave near-identical 1RM and muscle-thickness gains [3]; range of motion generally beats partials [9] except at long muscle lengths [10]; cable vs barbell preacher curls grew the biceps equally despite opposite torque profiles [11].
- **Reference quality is poor in a way that matters.** The claim that incline dumbbell press "isolates the clavicular head" cites a push-up training study [3]. The golf club-head-speed claim cites an emergency-medicine case report about chest pain [2]. The biceps antagonist claim cites a study of muscle architecture **in the horse** [22]. The article's own conclusion lists five exercises, two of which (diamond push-ups, machine chest press) never appear in the body, and drops all three biceps exercises.
- It sells hard: three identical "Find your Hypertrophy Program" call-outs, plus injury-rehab coaching, fitness coaching and nutrition coaching. The programming advice and the product are the same thing.

## What is actually being recommended

Seven movements, each with a set/rep scheme and an RPE target, sorted into Barbell Medicine's usual primary/secondary/tertiary movement categories:

| Exercise | Category | Prescription |
|---|---|---|
| Flat barbell bench press | Primary | 3–4 × 5–8, RPE 7–8 |
| Incline dumbbell bench press | Secondary | 2–4 × 6–10, RPE 7–8 |
| Push-ups | Tertiary | 3 sets to RPE 7–8 (stated elsewhere as 10–30 reps) |
| Cable crossover | Tertiary | 2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9 |
| Preacher curl | Tertiary | 3–4 × 8–12, RPE 8–9 |
| Dumbbell curl | Tertiary | 3–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9 |
| Concentration curl | Tertiary | 3–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9 |

Compounds first, isolation later, larger muscle group first. Rest two to three minutes between sets, on the basis of two Grgic systematic reviews finding that rest intervals under two minutes blunt strength [36] and, less consistently, hypertrophy [37].

## Which rankings are backed by growth data, and which by activation

This is the question the list does not answer for itself, so here it is exercise by exercise.

**Bench press — convention, not comparison.** No study is cited comparing the bench press to anything else for chest growth. The two citations attached to it are about *transfer to sport*: bench press strength correlates with punch performance in professional male boxers [1], and — supposedly — with golf club head speed, except the reference given for the golf claim is Smereck's case report of a young adult presenting with acute chest pain after benching [2]. That is a misattribution, not a weak citation.

**Incline dumbbell press — anatomy, and a misattributed reference.** The claim that it "particularly isolates the clavicular head" is footnoted to Kotarsky's push-up training trial [3], which has nothing to do with incline pressing. The related claim that the incline path is "50% longer than the average" has no reference at all. Upper-chest bias by inclination is a plausible moment-arm argument, but nothing here supports it.

**Push-ups — the one exercise with a real training trial behind it.** Kotarsky 2018 [3] compared four weeks of progressive calisthenic push-ups against bench pressing, three times per week, and found near-identical improvements in 1RM bench strength and chest muscle thickness. That is genuine outcome data, and it is the strongest empirical claim in the article — though four weeks in previously untrained participants is a short window and small change scores are easy to equalise. The other two push-up citations are not about muscle at all: Yang 2019 [4], a prospective cohort of active adult men (firefighters) in which those completing >40 push-ups had markedly lower cardiovascular event risk than those completing <10, is **observational and confounded by fitness**, and the article states it as though push-up capacity were causal; and Villareal's NEJM trial of aerobic vs resistance vs combined training in dieting obese older adults [5] is stretched to support "push-ups burn more calories".

**Cable crossover — activation and kinematics only.** The supporting work is Signorile 2017 [6], comparing muscle activation and kinematics between cable and selectorised machines, plus a conference abstract by Richardson 2016 that is listed **twice**, as references 7 and 8. None of it measures growth. "Continuous tension throughout the range maximises muscle engagement" is a mechanism claim with no outcome behind it. The chart of low/mid/high cable positions targeting upper/lower/inner chest is regional-emphasis folklore with no citation whatsoever.

**Preacher curl — the best-cited item, and the citation undercuts the ranking.** Pallarés 2021 [9] (ROM meta-analysis) and Kassiano 2023 [10] (lengthened partials review) are used correctly: fuller ROM generally beats shorter, but partials performed at long muscle lengths may match full ROM. Nunes 2020 [11] then randomised cable versus barbell preacher curls — opposite torque-emphasis profiles — and found **hypertrophy essentially equal either way**, with a strength advantage for the cable version only when tested at long muscle lengths. Read plainly, that reference argues the preacher-curl variant barely matters, which sits awkwardly under a list ranking specific curl variants.

**Dumbbell curl — modelling, not measurement.** Both citations [12, 13] are biomechanical analyses: a finite-element model of the biceps under increasing dumbbell load (a preprint) and a modelling paper on the non-linear relationship between load and muscle force. Neither involves training anyone. The practical takeaway offered — do dumbbell curls in higher-volume, lighter sets — does not follow from either.

**Concentration curl — pure EMG, and weak EMG at that.** The article says "research suggests concentration curls are the prime exercise to isolate your biceps", and the research is a single American Council on Exercise study [14] ranking eight biceps exercises by surface EMG amplitude. It is sponsored by the certifying body, published as a PDF rather than in a peer-reviewed journal, and measures activation, not growth. Barbell Medicine's own lifting-belt article cites Vigotsky's paper on why sEMG amplitude cannot be read as recruitment or as a proxy for hypertrophy — a caution they apply to belts and not here.

**Net:** zero of the seven placements rest on measured hypertrophy. One (push-ups) has a short training trial showing it is *not worse* than the bench press. One (concentration curl) rests on activation data. The rest are anatomical reasoning.

## The programming material, which is better than the list

Two claims are handled well. On **exercise order**, they state the evidence honestly: exercises performed earlier gain more *strength*, while for *hypertrophy* there is no clear signal that order matters [23, 24]. They then recommend compounds first anyway — and label that as their preference driven by the strength benefit, which is the correct way to mark an advice claim that outruns its evidence.

On **injury risk**, the numbers are roughly the ones this library sees elsewhere: about 2–4 injuries per 1,000 participation hours, or 1–2 per lifter per year [31, 32], most resolving within about two weeks, with lifting compared favourably against contact and non-contact sport. They add the counterweight that not exercising carries its own injury risk [33, 34], citing Little 2013 [35], a 12-month incidence study in previously sedentary older adults, for the striking claim that non-exercisers had the same injury rate as people training several hours per week. Note the attribution slip: the injury-rate figure is footnoted partly to Pojednic 2018 [31], a paper about the gap between clinicians and fitness professionals, when Keogh & Winwood [32] is the actual epidemiology.

The **volume** section is the weakest reasoning in the piece. "Too high a dose gives you too many muscle protein breakdowns, which won't help you increase muscle size" is not what the cited work says; reference 25 is a developmental-biology paper on cellular heterogeneity in muscle development, and Damas 2018 [26] argues almost the opposite — that early-phase damage markers confound hypertrophy measurement rather than describing a breakdown-versus-synthesis dial the lifter can tune. **Flag:** this is presented as mechanism when it is a plausible-sounding gloss the references do not support.

Their **progressive loading** framing is worth keeping: they deliberately avoid "progressive overload" because people read it as "training must get harder", and prefer that reps rise as fitness rises, matched to the individual. That is a coaching position, not a finding.

The **hydration** passage (0.4–1.5 L/hour during exercise; replace 100–150% of body-mass loss when recovery is under four hours) is uncited and reproduced almost verbatim twice in consecutive paragraphs — one of several signs this is a lightly-edited content-marketing page rather than a clinician-authored one, despite the byline.

## Where the advice and the product line up
The three inline CTAs route to a "Find your Hypertrophy Program" quiz with campaign tracking parameters. The injury section routes to paid injury-and-rehab coaching, the conclusion to templates, fitness coaching, nutrition coaching and an email list. None of it is hidden, but the article's structure — individual exercises matter less than structured programming, and here is our programming — is a sales argument as much as a training one.
