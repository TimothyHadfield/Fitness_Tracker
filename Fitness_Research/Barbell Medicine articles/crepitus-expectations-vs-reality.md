# Knee Noise Is Nearly Universal, and What It Means to You Matters More Than What It Is

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Crepitus: Expectations vs. Reality" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/crepitus-expectations-vs-reality/)) · Austin Baraki · 2019-02-27 · 1,832 words
**Topic:** Pain science

## Bottom line
- **Crepitus — snapping, cracking, popping from a joint — is close to universal.** McCoy et al. examined 247 symptomatic and 250 normal knees and found **99% of the normal knees had crepitus** at evaluation [2]. A finding present in essentially everyone cannot by itself signal disease.
- It is **not nothing, either**, and Baraki does not pretend otherwise: Lo et al. found subjective knee crepitus in asymptomatic people predicted later development of symptomatic tibiofemoral osteoarthritis [3]. He keeps the inconvenient paper in rather than dropping it, which is the article's best feature.
- Where crepitus tracks with worse patient-reported outcomes, it **does not track with measurable ones**. Pazzinatto et al., matching on radiographic (Kellgren-Lawrence) severity across two observational samples of 584 and 361 people, found slightly lower self-reported function and knee-related quality of life in the crepitus groups but **no difference in objective knee function or strength** [5].
- So the loading is on **interpretation**. The qualitative work says people hear the noise as their joints wearing out, feel old and anxious, get embarrassed when others hear it, and stop doing things [7] — textbook fear-avoidance [8].
- The management recommendation is education and reassurance, plus practical tricks to reduce hypervigilance (ambient noise, a knee sleeve to muffle the sound) — **explicitly without telling the person the sleeve provides "support" or "stability"**, because that narrative reintroduces the belief you are trying to remove.

## What the evidence actually is
The evidence base here is old, observational and qualitative — no trials. Physicians once listened to joints with a stethoscope (*joint auscultation*), trying to correlate volume, frequency and quality of the noise with pathology, going back to Blodgett in 1902 [1]. Baraki's judgement is that this fell out of use and was probably useless anyway.

The modern studies are cross-sectional and cohort. de Oliveira Silva et al. compared 165 women with patellofemoral pain against 158 pain-free women: crepitus was substantially more common in the pain group (**odds ratio 4.19**) but showed **no relationship with function or other self-reported clinical outcomes** [4]. A cross-sectional association of that size tells you crepitus and pain co-occur; it tells you nothing about direction, and the absence of any functional correlate is the more interesting half of the result.

Pazzinatto is the sharpest paper in the set because it controls for radiographic severity, isolating crepitus itself. The gap it finds is between how the knee is *perceived* and how it *performs*. Baraki links this to Stanton et al. on the spine, where felt stiffness in chronic back pain is a protective perceptual inference rather than a readout of measured stiffness [6] — the same perception/measurement divergence in a different joint.

## Where the beliefs come from
Robertson et al.'s qualitative study is the centre of the article [7]. Participants were preoccupied with the **meaning** of the noise: damage, "premature aging", degeneration, feeling old. They were embarrassed when others could hear it, especially in quiet rooms, and other people's reactions — wincing, commenting, "*you should get that checked out*" — worked as a social amplifier of the sense of danger. Several stopped hobbies outright.

Two further sources of belief get named. **Family**: knowing a relative with knee problems led people to assume their crepitus was the same process with the same fate. Baraki's clinical suggestion is to ask the person whether they know anyone with similar issues, since that is often where the model came from. **Clinicians**: Setchell et al.'s cross-sectional survey found **89% of people with persistent low back pain had learned their negative beliefs from healthcare professionals** [9], and Darlow et al.'s 2018 qualitative study of knee osteoarthritis beliefs found patients holding a literal "wear and tear", "bone-on-bone", "missing cartilage" model — which, the authors note, participants held with certainty even while their own symptoms were stable or improving in ways the model could not explain, a discordance they did not register [10]. Robertson's 2010 editorial makes the practical version of the point: loud crepitus of non-arthritic origin mostly produces anxious, hypervigilant patients who need reassurance [11].

**The clinical reasoning, stated plainly:** if the noise is near-universal, if it does not predict measurable function, and if the belief it triggers reliably produces avoidance, then the treatable target is the belief. That is a coherent argument, and Baraki is careful to say it survives even if Lo is right — *even if* crepitus does forecast future symptomatic osteoarthritis in some people, that would still not be a reason to stop training.

**Flagged:** two claims outrun what is cited. "Increased activity throughout life can help maintain function, strength, range of motion, and quality of life" and "*inactivity* appears to be a greater risk factor for worsening pain and disability due to osteoarthritis" are both asserted without a reference in this article — plausible and broadly supported elsewhere, but not evidenced here, and the second is doing real work in the argument. Separately, note that no study cited shows that reassurance changes outcomes in people with crepitus. The reassurance protocol is clinical judgement built on descriptive evidence, not a tested intervention.

**No product is pitched in this article** — unusual for the source, and worth noting.
