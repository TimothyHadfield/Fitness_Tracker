# Plantar Heel Pain: Manage the Load, Distrust Almost Everything Else

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Guide to Heel Pain" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/guide-to-plantar-heel-pain/)) · Derek Miles · 2022-05-27 · 2469 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- Call it **plantar heel pain**, not fasciitis: "-itis" asserts an inflammatory mechanism the tissue findings don't support, and the neutral label leaves room for load and capacity contributors.
- **Diagnosis is clinical** — morning-first-steps pain plus tenderness on palpation. Thickened fascia on ultrasound also occurs in people with no pain and changes with running as normal adaptation, so imaging can't confirm it.
- The driver is a **recent change in loading** — mileage, standing hours, a shoe swap, a walking holiday — not the absolute amount.
- **Most cases resolve within about a year untreated**, which should discipline how you read every uncontrolled treatment claim here.
- Load management and foot/ankle strengthening first; stretching and orthotics for short-term relief only; injections, dry needling and passive modalities not recommended; shockwave a low-confidence add-on.

## Why "fasciitis" is disputed, and why imaging doesn't settle it
The reasoning: the name encodes a mechanism, the mechanism gets treated as established, and treatment follows the name — if it's inflammation, you inject a steroid. Their position is that the inflammatory framing was never well supported, and plain plantar heel pain fits a multi-contributor condition better.

The imaging argument is the strongest part of the piece: a finding common in people *without* symptoms cannot confirm a diagnosis in people *with* them, so history and palpation outrank the scan, and X-ray or CT is reserved for trauma. Radwan 2016 and Alabau-Dasi 2022 are the only studies named in prose. Notable gap: **heel spurs** are never mentioned, though the fascia-thickness argument would apply to them directly.

## What contributes to it
Listed: BMI above 27, excessive running, a sedentary lifestyle, and jobs with prolonged standing or walking — stated flatly, with no study design, sample size or effect size. Risk factors of this shape typically come from case-control or cross-sectional work, so association is established and causal direction is not; sedentariness and a BMI of 27 aren't independent.

Biomechanics is handled better: limited ankle dorsiflexion is the classic culprit, and the article says more recent work has not found it a strong contributor. That fits their wider line that structural and postural variables predict pain poorly.

## What to actually do
**Step 1 — adjust recent load changes.** Find the change and scale it back; transient triggers (a holiday) resolve on their own. Where the change is a work requirement, this step isn't available.

**Step 2 — strengthen the foot and ankle.** Stretching the plantar fascia and calf has evidence for **short-term** relief; study protocols typically use 10-second holds for 10 repetitions. The article is explicit that this does not lengthen the tissue and that early relief may just be novel loading. Strengthening: calf raises with a rolled towel under the toes to tension the fascia, 2–3 sets of 15 double-leg progressing to single-leg, then external load (soleus, seated calf, leg-press, donkey raise), bodyweight being too little stimulus for a strong athlete. Sample programme, 2–3x/week: calf raises 3 sets at 2 RIR; single-leg deadlift and curtsy lunge 3x8 capped near 25 lb; stretch as needed, 4–5 sets of 10-second holds.

**Step 3 — footwear.** No shoe is shown better than another and custom orthotics aren't necessary. An orthotic is just another way to modify load, useful short-term; over-the-counter is suggested over custom. The preference for a rigid shoe during a flare is explicitly labelled anecdotal.

**Step 4 — everything else.** Magnetised insoles and topical wheatgrass cream are the named examples of treatments that beat nothing. Corticosteroid, botox, PRP and dry-needling injections show at most small short-term effects, no substance superior to another, plus adverse-event risk — not recommended. Instrument-assisted soft tissue work and kinesiotaping have no good evidence, and "breaking up adhesions" is called unsubstantiated. Shockwave has low-level support with conflicting meta-analyses, so it's an add-on, not first-line.

## Evidence versus recommendation
**Evidence:** resolution within a year; short-term benefit from stretching and orthotics; injections and passive modalities not meaningfully beating placebo; dorsiflexion not a strong contributor. **Recommendation:** the strengthening protocol — exercise selection, sets, reps, frequency, progressions — is clinical reasoning borrowed from tendinopathy loading, not a cited trial result. It is the article's central recommendation and the part with the least evidence behind it.

**Selling:** the article closes by pointing readers to Barbell Medicine's paid injury-rehab coaching.

Two claims are stated more absolutely than the evidence supports: "none of these have demonstrated a benefit beyond placebo" is a stronger negative than a literature this heterogeneous can deliver — absence of demonstrated benefit is not demonstrated absence; and "most cases get better on their own over the course of a year" carries no source, proportion or interval while doing heavy work in the argument.

**Commercial disclosure:** the article closes by directing non-responders to Barbell Medicine's paid Pain and Rehabilitation coaching service, and links to their own calf-training and tendinopathy articles. No supplement or template is pushed; the sale sits at the point where the free advice fails.
