# Osteoarthritis Is a Whole-Person Condition, Not a Worn-Out Part

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Osteoarthritis: Symptoms, Causes & Treatment" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-barbell-medicine-guide-to-osteoarthritis/)) · Austin Baraki · 2020-08-14 · 4,233 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **Imaging and symptoms come apart.** Up to **43% of people over 40 show knee OA features on MRI with no pain at all** (Culvenor 2019, systematic review and meta-analysis). Pain can improve substantially without joint structure changing, and roughly **20% of patients still have knee pain after total knee replacement**.
- **Routine loading does not cause OA; trauma does.** ACL tears raise later OA risk ([Paschos 2017](https://doi.org/10.5312/wjo.v8.i3.212)), but ordinary training does not harm cartilage (Bricca 2019, systematic review of RCTs) — and elite weightlifters had **thicker** knee cartilage than controls, thicker the earlier they started ([Grelak 2014](https://doi.org/10.1002/ca.22393)).
- **Exercise defers surgery in people already listed for it.** In patients with moderate-to-advanced OA judged eligible for knee replacement, a non-surgical exercise programme led **75% to decline the operation at 12 months and 68% at 2 years** ([Skou 2015 NEJM RCT](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa1505467); [Skou 2018 two-year follow-up](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joca.2018.04.014)).
- **Guidelines already say exercise first; practice does not follow.** Five citations document the gap between the ACR/AAOS/AAFP first-line recommendations and what clinicians actually deliver.
- **Most of what is offered instead is weak.** Oral analgesics, opioids, topicals, corticosteroid/PRP/hyaluronic acid injections and glucosamine are grouped as offering "transient, minor, or no benefits over placebos" (Hunter 2017, *JOSPT* editorial), as are passive rehab modalities.
- **The advice** is start low, go slow, accept tolerable symptoms, and deliberately re-approach avoided movements. That last part is a clinical judgement, not a tested protocol.

## The argument: from joint to person

Baraki's structure is unusual and effective. He takes [Bunzli 2019](https://doi.org/10.1097/CORR.0000000000000784) — a qualitative study of patients scheduled for knee replacement, *all* of whom believed their knee was "bone on bone" from "wear and tear" and avoided painful activity out of fear of further damage — and organises the article around the three beliefs that study surfaced, quoting patients directly. Each theme then gets a factual rebuttal.

The underlying position is the "whole-person" reframing of OA (Caneiro 2020, *BJSM* call to action): the joint is one input among biological factors (bony change, genetics, prior trauma, other conditions including excess body fat) and psychological and social ones (poor sleep, fear, depression, anxiety, stress, low self-efficacy), which together reduce physical activity and life engagement and so worsen symptoms and disability ([Rejeski 1998](https://doi.org/10.1002/art.1790110205); [Martel-Pelletier 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrdp.2016.72); Wang 2015; [Spector 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joca.2003.09.005)). Baraki's stated reason for preferring this framing is not purely descriptive: it gives more intervention targets than "replace the joint".

He also makes a deliberate language choice — refusing the word "degeneration" and calling radiographic OA a normal age-related change, "analogous to the development of grey hair or skin wrinkles." That is a rhetorical move as much as a clinical one, and it is worth naming as such.

## Theme 1: "It's bone on bone"

The rebuttal rests on the imaging-symptom mismatch, and Culvenor's asymptomatic-MRI meta-analysis is the load-bearing citation. Note the study design: this is prevalence data in asymptomatic uninjured adults, which establishes that findings are common without pain — it does not establish that findings never matter. Baraki is careful about this: "this is not to say that these age-related changes can't play a role."

Crepitus gets the same treatment: common in pain-free people with normal function, and not a signal of ongoing damage. No citation is given for the crepitus claims here; he links to a separate Barbell Medicine article.

## Theme 2: "I loaded it too much"

This is the section a lifting audience will care most about, and it is the best-evidenced. The machine analogy fails because living tissue adapts. Traumatic injury is conceded as a genuine risk factor. Routine loading is not: Bricca's systematic review of RCTs on exercise and articular cartilage supports the null, and Grelak's MRI comparison of elite weightlifters against controls supports something stronger — a dose-dependent *thickening* with earlier training onset.

Handle Grelak carefully. It is a small cross-sectional imaging comparison, not a training intervention, so healthy-athlete selection could produce that result without any adaptation occurring. Baraki presents it as fitting the adaptation model, which it does, but it cannot demonstrate it.

For treatment rather than prevention, progressive strength training improves muscle strength, pain intensity, physical function and self-efficacy even in advanced arthritis, supported by a five-reference stack running from a systematic review of resistance training in musculoskeletal rehab ([Kristensen 2012](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.2010.079376)) to an RCT of high- versus low-resistance training in knee OA ([Jan 2008](https://doi.org/10.2522/ptj.20060300)) and a meta-analysis of strengthening or aerobic exercise for pain relief ([Tanaka 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269215513488898)). Self-selected running is associated with improved knee pain and no structural progression in Osteoarthritis Initiative data ([Lo 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10067-018-4121-3)) — observational, so self-selection is doing some work there.

## Theme 3: "It only goes downhill"

Progression is variable; many people do not worsen over years. Inactivity is among the most prevalent risk factors for functional decline in arthritis ([Dunlop 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/art.20968)), which gives a modifiable target.

The Skou trials are the strongest thing in the article and deserve to be read precisely. These are patients *eligible for* knee replacement, randomised to surgery plus non-surgical care versus non-surgical care alone; the headline figure is the proportion in the non-surgical arm who chose not to have the operation within 12 months and 2 years. It is a claim about deferral and preference under good conservative care, not a claim that exercise outperforms arthroplasty on pain and function — in the trial, surgery produced greater improvement, with more serious adverse events.

Baraki does not obscure this. He states that replacement is "generally successful surgery when performed on appropriately selected patients", argues only for evidence-based non-surgical care *first*, and cites prehabilitation before arthroplasty ([Topp 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmrj.2009.06.003)) for those who do proceed. He also notes that people who improve with exercise often stay preoccupied with damage and inevitability ([Wallis 2019, qualitative](https://doi.org/10.1080/09638288.2017.1408710)) — a nice acknowledgement that changing outcomes and changing beliefs are separate problems.

## What to actually do

First-line per guidelines: self-management programmes, exercise, and physical activity consistent with national guidelines (ACR 2019, AAOS, AAFP). Real-world adherence is poor across five cited studies of clinician behaviour spanning 1993–2010.

Programming advice is deliberately unfussy: the *type* of exercise matters less than doing it regularly; there are no uniquely harmful movements and no right or wrong way to move. His concrete entry point is sit-to-stands from a chair, progressed by elevating the surface for range of motion, then adding a held weight, then removing the chair. Expect symptom fluctuation; tolerable symptoms during and after a session are fine to continue through, and a drastic spike means reduce load, intensity or volume and rebuild. Sleep, stress, mood and body fat are named as parallel targets ([Riddle 2013, cohort study of weight change and OA pain/function](https://doi.org/10.1002/acr.21692)).

**Evidence vs recommendation:** the closing claim that building self-efficacy "tends to result in better long-term outcomes than being entirely dependent on the healthcare system" is cited to a meta-analysis on whether exercises should be painful (Smith 2017) and a systematic review of kinesiophobia ([Luque-Suarez 2019](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-098673)). Those support that painful exercise is acceptable and that fear of movement predicts worse outcomes; they do not compare self-managed against clinician-delivered care. The recommendation is sound clinical practice, but it outruns the two papers under it.

**Overreach to flag:** the sentence carrying the 43%-asymptomatic figure also carries "about 20% of patients still experience knee pain after total knee replacement" and "people can experience significant improvement despite the joint structure not changing", all under one citation to Culvenor, which covers only the first. Those other two claims are well supported in the wider literature but are not sourced here.

**Commercial note:** the article closes by offering Barbell Medicine's own knee and back rehab templates and their paid Pain & Rehab consultations and coaching. To their credit, it first links a free third-party exercise inventory (oaoptimism.com).
