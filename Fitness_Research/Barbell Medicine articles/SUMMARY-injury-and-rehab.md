# Injury and rehabilitation

Twenty-four notes, spanning 2019 to 2025 and roughly 76,000 words of source material, covering the
decisions a lifter actually faces when something hurts: whether to see someone, whether to scan,
how far to cut the load, whether to have the operation, and how to get back afterwards. Most are
bylined by practising clinicians — **Derek Miles** (physical therapist; eleven of the twenty-four),
**Austin Baraki** and **Charlie Dickson** (four apiece), **Michael Amato** (two), **Charles Abrams**
(concussion) — and two carry no byline at all. This is the only clinical source in the library, so
nothing here has a second opinion, which raises rather than lowers the bar on saying how well
evidenced each claim is.

---

## The one distinction that governs everything below

**The asymptomatic-imaging evidence is strong and real. The rehab dose attached to it is almost
entirely uncited expert opinion.** Those two things arrive in the same voice, in the same article,
often in adjacent paragraphs, and they are not the same kind of claim.

The imaging side is built on prevalence and within-subject designs that genuinely support the
inference drawn from them. The dose side — sets, reps, tempo, pain thresholds, RPE caps, week
counts — is clinical practice pattern. Several articles admit this outright.
[The spondylolysis piece](train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md) says "treating this diagnosis
falls more in the realm of expert opinion than rigorous scientific evidence."
[Hip pain part II](training-hip-pain-part-ii.md) concedes there is **no research on heavy slow
resistance training for hip tendinopathy at all**. [The rehab-mistakes article](biggest-mistakes-re-making-lifting-rehab.md)
carries **zero citations across 3,639 words** and volunteers that "there is nothing special about
any of the sample starting programs provided here."

The corpus also splits by byline. The clinician essays quote sources against their own interest and
label their own inventions as inventions. The two unbylined pieces — [knee sleeves](knee-sleeves.md)
and [the DOMS article](t-straighten-arms-after-workout.md) — are the two that contain outright
citation errors: a 3% strength figure attached to the wrong study, and an arrhythmia claim linked to
a body-composition paper. Check the byline.

## Red flags, in full — the part that is neither optional nor contested

Every article puts these before its reassurance, which is the correct clinical order. Memorise
these; skim the rest.

**Cauda equina — the emergency.** New bowel or bladder problems (incontinence, or inability to
urinate or defecate) or altered sensation in the groin or saddle area. This is surgical. Flagged in
[the sciatica guide](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md),
[the sciatica exercises piece](simple-exercises-help-sciatica-pain.md) and
[the spondy article](train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md), each time in bold and before any
advice.

**Back or leg pain.** Also evaluate for: inability to bear weight after a fall or trauma;
unexplained weight loss, fever, recent illness or a cancer history; and radicular symptoms that are
**progressively worsening** (stable radicular symptoms need a longer timeline, not a scan).

**Shoulder.** Chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness, or pain radiating down the left arm.
That is a cardiac screen, not a shoulder screen. Also: severe pain not improving over a few weeks;
significant loss of strength or mobility; numbness, tingling or instability; onset from a fall,
impact or sudden force ([shoulder guide](barbell-medicine-guide-shoulder-pain.md)).

**Concussion**, from [the concussion guide](concussion-guide-symptoms-treatment-recovery.md) — the
longest list, and more serious in people **over 60 or on anticoagulants**: limb weakness or
paraesthesia; true vision loss or double vision (not blurring); abnormal or asymmetric pupils;
seizures (under 5% of concussions); loss of consciousness beyond **one minute**; persistent altered
mental status; skull fracture; **more than two vomiting episodes**; retrograde amnesia extending
**more than 30 minutes** before the injury; severe or worsening headache; escalating agitation; neck
pain or tenderness. Any of these means urgent care now. Loss of consciousness is *not* required for
the diagnosis itself — concussion is diagnosed on symptoms after a significant head impact, with
SCAT6 (2023) the named sideline tool.

**Ankle**, from [the ankle sprain article](manage-ankle-sprain.md). The Ottawa Ankle Rules screen
for **fracture, not severity**: four steps both immediately after injury and now; bony tenderness
along the distal 6 cm of the posterior fibula; the same on the posterior tibia or medial malleolar
tip; tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal; tenderness at the navicular. Miles is candid that
acutely everything hurts and correct palpation needs anatomy, so this is best applied by a
clinician. **Lay version: if you cannot bear weight, get evaluated.** He calls the rules validated
with predictive value but gives no sensitivity, specificity or study data. Isolated **medial /
deltoid** injury is uncommon and itself warrants fracture evaluation.

**Knee**, from [the meniscus article](second-opinion-meniscus-injury.md): a clear mechanism (slip,
fall, sudden twist) followed by swelling or inability to weight-bear; effusion obscuring the
kneecap border, or swelling "like an orange" along the medial joint line; persistent loss of range
of motion or true locking.

**Rhabdomyolysis**, the differential [the DOMS article](t-straighten-arms-after-workout.md) exists
to draw: red-to-brown urine, CK typically **>=5x the upper limit of normal**, risk of acute kidney
injury via myoglobin-driven tubular obstruction and iron-mediated damage, plus arrhythmia risk from
potassium, phosphate and calcium release. The catch: **fewer than 10% of rhabdo cases show the
classic triad** of myalgia, weakness and dark urine, and **over half report no muscle pain or
weakness at all**, so soreness severity screens badly in either direction. Against that,
**hyperCKemia** is real — military personnel and Division I football players have been documented
above 5x ULN with myoglobinuria and no symptoms or organ damage (Gemelli 2022) — so a frightening CK
in a trained athlete is not automatically rhabdomyolysis.

## Does this need a scan? Almost always no — and here the evidence is real

This is the best-evidenced material in the whole domain. Every study below is **cross-sectional
prevalence imaging in people without symptoms**, which supports one claim — a finding is weak
evidence that it caused *your* pain — and not the stronger one that findings never matter.

| Finding | Population and design | Prevalence without pain |
|---|---|---|
| Rotator cuff, labral tears, OA (Barreto 2019) | 123 people, **both shoulders scanned**, pain in one | near-equal on both sides; full-thickness supraspinatus tears and glenohumeral OA about **10 percentage points** more common on the painful side |
| Knee OA on MRI (Culvenor 2019, SR+MA) | asymptomatic uninjured adults over 40 | up to **43%** |
| Meniscus tear (Horga 2020) | 230 knees, mean age 44, 3.0 T MRI | **30%** |
| Meniscal change (Pappas 2016) | asymptomatic collegiate basketball players | **50% preseason, rising to 62% postseason** |
| Meniscal abnormality (Beals 2016, SR) | ~300 asymptomatic athletes | about one third |
| Spondylolysis | asymptomatic adolescents / adults | **3-7% / 11.5%** |
| SI joint degeneration (Eno 2015) | pain-free adults | **65.1%**, 30.5% of it substantial |

Two of these do more work than a bare prevalence figure. **Barreto** scanned both shoulders in the
same person, controlling for age, occupation, genetics and training history — a strong
within-subject design, and the best single study in the section. **Pappas** is longitudinal within a
season: the same asymptomatic athletes accumulated meniscal findings without developing symptoms,
which is a genuine dissociation between imaging change and pain, not a snapshot.

**The spine adds a temporal argument the others cannot.** Herniations are common in asymptomatic
people (Brinjikji 2015); most resorb spontaneously, and **larger herniations are more likely to
resorb than smaller ones** — sequestrated discs most often, bulges least (Chiu 2015; Zhong 2017).
Herniation size does not track symptom severity in sciatica patients (Karppinen 2001), and symptoms
resolve without the scan changing (Benson 2010, a 7-year follow-up of conservatively treated
*massive* prolapses). [The sciatica guide](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md)
builds this chain citation by citation across 15 verified references, and it is the strongest
argument in the domain.

**For spondy**, [Beutler's 45-year follow-up](train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md) found no
association with back-pain risk and no participant progressing to a problematic slip; Andrade's
systematic review of observational studies reached the same conclusion. One study found **time and
activity modification predicted successful return to sport, with no relationship between degree of
bony healing and outcome** — the reason imaging follow-up is not the reload trigger.

**For heel pain**, thickened plantar fascia on ultrasound occurs in people with no symptoms and
changes with running as ordinary adaptation, so [imaging cannot confirm the
diagnosis](guide-heel-pain.md) — history (morning first-steps pain) plus palpation outranks the
scan, and X-ray or CT is reserved for trauma.

**For the hip**, [part I](training-hip-pain-part-i.md) makes the same argument about range of motion
rather than imaging: of 120 pain-free adults, **47.5% had more external than internal rotation, 13%
more internal, 39.5% equal** (Kouyoumdjian 2012). Mean internal rotation was **26.9 degrees in male
and 34.7 degrees in female college freshman athletes, with a standard deviation near 10 degrees**
(Czuppon 2017) — so roughly a 40-degree spread covers the ordinary middle, and treating a mean as a
threshold mistakes a distribution for a cut-off. Range of motion is not even a fixed personal trait:
collegiate pitchers changed measurably across a season (Zeppieri 2015, a small uncontrolled pilot).
Miles states plainly that **there are no data on what "normal" is for a barbell athlete.**

**Where the imaging argument is overreached.** The 6,710-word [shoulder
guide](barbell-medicine-guide-shoulder-pain.md) rests on Barreto alone, and Barreto's own 10-point
gap cuts against the strong reading. [Hip pain part I](training-hip-pain-part-i.md) asserts that
imaging "commonly reveals abnormalities in all athletes" with **no citation at all** — the only
asymptomatic-imaging paper it cites is Eno, which is the SI joint, not FAI or cam morphology. And
the sciatica guide's *own* conclusion is properly hedged: scans "may not always be necessary"
because they rarely change non-surgical management, cost money and can create fear — with the
concession that in true radiculopathy "there's a good chance there will be something abnormal caught
on a scan."

## The four things they say before any diagnosis

**Modify the load, don't stop.** Rest is rejected in every single condition here —
[tendinopathy](barbell-medicine-guide-tendinopathy.md), [ankle sprain](manage-ankle-sprain.md),
[heel pain](guide-heel-pain.md), [tennis elbow](lateral-elbow-pain-lifters.md),
[sciatica](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md),
[spondy](train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md),
[post-op knee](post-operative-knee-rehabilitation.md),
[concussion](concussion-guide-symptoms-treatment-recovery.md),
[hip tendinopathy](training-hip-pain-part-ii.md). The reasoning is capacity: rest detrains the
tissue, so you return to the same demand with less tolerance than you started with. It is sound, and
it is mostly uncited. The *direct* evidence is narrow — Kerkhoffs 2002, a Cochrane review of
immobilisation versus functional treatment in acute lateral ankle ligament injury, and Maetz 2023, a
systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs in midportion Achilles tendinopathy comparing loading
protocols against passive modalities. Everywhere else it is inference from those two plus detraining
physiology.

**Pain is a poor readout of tissue damage.** The foundational claim, and its citation base is
thinner than the confidence suggests. In [recovering from
injury](recovering-injury-embrace-process.md) it rests on two editorials (Moseley and Butler 2015;
Lewis and O'Sullivan 2018); in the sciatica guide on two conceptual/definitional papers (Moseley
2007; Cohen 2018). The empirical support is really the imaging table above, not the pain-science
literature. Dickson does soften it: in acute injury pain correlates more strongly with tissue
damage, and he says so.

**Symptoms, not force capacity, set the ceiling.** Their one portable invention, and the most useful
idea in the domain: re-anchor RPE from "could I do another rep?" to "***should*** I?" It appears in
[the sciatica guide](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md),
[the rehab mistakes article](biggest-mistakes-re-making-lifting-rehab.md),
[the tendinopathy guide](barbell-medicine-guide-tendinopathy.md),
[the meniscus article](second-opinion-meniscus-injury.md) and
[the shoulder guide](barbell-medicine-guide-shoulder-pain.md) — uncited in all five. It explicitly
licenses *decreasing* load from the previous session, which is a real departure from how RPE is
normally used.

**The label rarely changes the plan.** Outside a short list (fracture, instability, frozen shoulder,
cervical radiculopathy, cardiac), [the shoulder guide](barbell-medicine-guide-shoulder-pain.md)
argues the diagnostic label does not change the rehab much. [Hip part
II](training-hip-pain-part-ii.md) does the same lumping move deliberately: greater trochanteric
bursitis, greater trochanteric pain syndrome and "snapping hip" all get the same approach. This is
a defensible clinical heuristic offered with no citation, and it is doing heavy work — it is the
premise that licenses skipping imaging.

## What the load cut actually looks like

Almost none of this is evidenced. All of it is worth knowing, because it is specific.

**"Tolerable" is defined**, identically in the shoulder and sciatica guides: mild-to-moderate
discomfort that does not interfere with the movement and does not spike to debilitating levels
during the session or in the following **24-48 hours**.

**The three-part test for "you have not cut enough"**, from [the rehab
mistakes](biggest-mistakes-re-making-lifting-rehab.md) article: pain rises set to set as weight
rises; pain worsens in the 24-48 hours after; **pain never returns to baseline before the next
session**. Any one of the three means pull back further.

**The worked reduction is deliberately shocking.** A lifter doing a hard set of 5 at **300 lb** on
the squat is moved to **12 reps at 95 lb with a 3-second tempo both directions**, plus a hard load
cap for the day. Baraki's point is explicit: taking 15 or 25 lb off the bar is not a load reduction.

**The pain thresholds**, all invented, all different:

- **3-4 out of 10 or below** during heavy slow resistance
  ([tendinopathy](barbell-medicine-guide-tendinopathy.md))
- **4/10 or below** during and for about 24 hours after; **two consecutive breaches means cut volume
  on the main lifts** ([tennis elbow](lateral-elbow-pain-lifters.md))
- symptoms rising **no more than 2 points out of 10** during a movement
  ([meniscus](second-opinion-meniscus-injury.md))
- **pain-out-of-10 plus RPE summing to 10 or less**
  ([spondy](train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md)) — pain at 4/10 caps effort at RPE 5-6; pain
  at 8/10 means easy movement only. It doubles as the gate back to max-effort work
- an **RPE 7 cap** in early spondy rehab, on the reasoning that an RPE 7 that accidentally becomes
  an 8 is far safer than a 9 that becomes a 10

**Heavy slow resistance** is the shared skeleton: **3-0-3 tempo** (three seconds down, no pause,
three seconds up), **RPE 8**, **2-3 times weekly**, placed at the **start** of the session so the
athlete is fresh and it warms the area, and held for **12-16 weeks** because tendons adapt slowly.
The tendinopathy sample runs weeks 1-4 at **4x10 RPE 8, 3-0-3 tempo**; weeks 4-8 at **3x10 RPE 8,
3-0-0**; weeks 8-16 at **3x10 RPE 8** at normal tempo, moving to the end of the session as accessory
work. [Hip tendinopathy](training-hip-pain-part-ii.md) uses a different ladder, all at RPE 8:
**weeks 1-2, 4x12-15; weeks 3-4, 4x10-12; weeks 5-6, 4x8-10; week 7 onward, 3x8.**

**Progression rules.** Load increases **no more than 10% per session**, or an absolute cap ("115 lb
today"). **Double progression** — hold the weight through an 8-12 rep range, add load only after
topping it cleanly. The **de-escalation order for tempo** is easy to get wrong: return the
concentric to normal speed first, keep the eccentric slow, normalise the eccentric last. Rep ranges
come down gradually (10-12, then 8-10, then 6-8, then 4-6), and explosive work enters only in the
final phase.

**Range of motion as a second progression variable** is the most distinctive practical idea, from
[the meniscus](second-opinion-meniscus-injury.md) and
[spondy](train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md) articles: pin squats give you two things to
progress — weight on the bar and pin height — and you progress **one variable per session**. In the
spondy version, RPE is anchored to "one pin lower than last session" so range, not load, is what
moves; full range is typically restored in **2-4 weeks**.

**Addition by subtraction.** The reflex is to add an exercise per symptom; Miles argues the
opposite — audit the whole programme and *cut* total exercises, sets or mileage early. Sleep and
work stress get equal billing with tempo work, on clinical reasoning rather than cited evidence.

**Vary the movements.** [The rehab mistakes article](biggest-mistakes-re-making-lifting-rehab.md)
treats novelty as therapeutic in itself ("the weirder the movement, the better") and gives the most
useful substitution list in the corpus: simple barbell variants (close-grip bench, front squat,
sumo, specialty bars); unilateral work (sissy squats, split squats, lateral lunges, step-ups,
single-leg press or extension, single-leg RDLs, suitcase deadlifts, machine rows, single-arm
dumbbell and landmine pressing); rotational work (rotating lunges, windmill presses) specifically
for back and hip pain; isolation work and conditioning as a way to feel like you trained hard while
the main lifts are deprioritised. The sample low-back day includes a **Jefferson curl starting as
light as 2.5-5 lb** — loaded spinal flexion as a rehab *entry point*, which is a position statement
rather than a finding.

## Condition-specific detail worth having

**Tendinopathy** ([guide](barbell-medicine-guide-tendinopathy.md), 4,701 words, **four citations**).
"Tendonitis" is the wrong word: a systematic review of inflammatory cells and markers in human
tendinopathy (Jomaa 2020) found the evidence for inflammation unclear. The causes named are all dose
variables — over-specificity, chronic overuse, acute overload from a programming change — and Miles
explicitly rules out poor flexibility and imperfect technique ("your quadriceps tendinopathy was not
the result of insufficiently stretchy quadriceps"). The direction of adaptation comes from Bohm 2015
and Lazarczuk 2022, two meta-analyses in **healthy** tendons: higher loads at slower tempos. The
extrapolation to painful tendons is the author's. Non-training contributors a gym reader will miss:
high cholesterol, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, gout, ankylosing spondylitis and
**fluoroquinolone antibiotics**. The honest puzzle he raises: if slow heavy loading is what tendons
want, powerlifters should be protected and they are not — his resolution is that dose and variety,
not tempo, are the operative variables.

**Hip tendinopathy** ([part II](training-hip-pain-part-ii.md)). Entry criteria: gradual onset, no
clear mechanism, pain at a muscle's origin or insertion. The practical core is **isolating the
affected tissue**, because a dozen muscles cross the hip and a lifter doing bilateral compound work
will unconsciously find a pattern that gets the bar up without loading the sore tendon — then
overload it anyway. Region by region: **proximal hamstring** — single-leg prone or seated curl,
3-0-3, slowing to 6-0-6 or 5-1-5 for people who habitually load too heavy; **gluteal** (lateral hip
at the greater trochanter, not the buttock mass) — abduction machine, curtsy lunge, lateral
step-down, partial-depth pistol squat; **quadriceps** — leg press with feet high on the platform, or
split squats with the shin against a bench, limiting forward knee travel temporarily ("no one cares
how much weight you split squat during rehab"); **hip flexor** — reverse Nordic curl, **4x6**
bodyweight through a tolerable range; **adductor** — the only region with cited evidence.
Yousefzadeh 2018 ran athletes with longstanding groin pain through the Hoelmich protocol (isometric
adductor and trunk work first, progressing to **5 sets of 10** strengthening exercises at
clinician-chosen load): **78% returned to sport at just over 14 weeks**, with over 20% gains in
isometric adductor and abductor strength and over 7% in eccentric strength. That study is an
**uncontrolled cohort** — 78% at 14 weeks in a condition with a long natural history cannot be
separated from time; the randomised evidence is Hoelmich 1999 (*Lancet*).

**Tennis elbow** ([lateral elbow pain](lateral-elbow-pain-lifters.md)). Prefer *epicondylalgia* —
"-algia" claims only pain, not a mechanism. The provoking pattern is a high volume of "power grip"
work: deadlifts, pull-ups, lat pulldowns, heavy carries, sometimes pressing, with triggers framed as
recent changes in intensity, volume or frequency. Modification, not cessation: **straps** on pulls
(explicitly not licence to add weight); **handle changes** such as mag grips instead of a straight
bar; low bar to high bar or a specialty bar on squats; a wider, narrower or reverse bench grip. The
prescription: wrist extension progressing eccentric-only, then extension to neutral, then full
range, at **2-4 sets of 8-10 at RPE 8, 3-0-3 tempo, 2-3 times weekly at the start of the session**;
accessories (hammer pronation/supination, wrist flexion, grip trainers, varied-grip loaded carries)
at roughly **2-3 sets of 10 at RPE 8, twice weekly**; keep the heavy slow work in for **at least a
month** after pain settles. **Isometrics are not part of this protocol**, and neither is a classic
eccentric-only programme. The recovery signal is a moving threshold, not a switch: pain at a 225 lb
deadlift becoming pain at 315 lb is real progress. Evidence is cited for exactly three things —
surgery, corticosteroid injection and PRP, none better than conservative care. Karanasios 2021
(SR+MA, **2,123 subjects across 30 trials**) found exercise beats passive treatment in lateral elbow
tendinopathy, with effects the authors themselves call **small** — a figure the article never
quotes.

**Plantar heel pain** ([guide](guide-heel-pain.md)). Call it plantar heel pain, not fasciitis.
Diagnosis is clinical. The driver is a **recent change in loading** — mileage, standing hours, a
shoe swap, a walking holiday — not the absolute amount. Contributors are listed flatly with no
design, sample size or effect size: BMI above 27, excessive running, a sedentary lifestyle, and jobs
with prolonged standing or walking. Limited ankle dorsiflexion is the classic culprit, and more
recent work has **not** found it a strong contributor (Landorf 2021). **Most cases resolve within
about a year untreated** — uncited, and it should discipline how you read every uncontrolled
treatment claim here. Stretching has **short-term** evidence only, with protocols typically using
**10-second holds for 10 repetitions**, and the article is explicit that this does not lengthen the
tissue. Strengthening: calf raises with a **rolled towel under the toes** to tension the fascia,
**2-3 sets of 15** double-leg progressing to single-leg, then external load, because bodyweight is
too little stimulus for a strong athlete. Sample programme, 2-3 times weekly: calf raises 3 sets at
2 reps in reserve; single-leg deadlift and curtsy lunge **3x8 capped near 25 lb**; stretching **4-5
sets of 10-second holds**. Footwear: no shoe is shown better than another, custom orthotics are not
necessary, over-the-counter is suggested over custom, and the preference for a rigid shoe during a
flare is explicitly labelled anecdotal. Magnetised insoles and topical wheatgrass cream are the
named examples of treatments that beat nothing. Shockwave has low-level support with conflicting
meta-analyses, so it is an add-on, not first-line. **Notable gap: heel spurs are never mentioned**,
though the fascia-thickness argument applies to them directly.

**Ankle sprain** ([article](manage-ankle-sprain.md)). **Lateral/inversion sprains are 85-90% of the
total** — ATFL, CFL and/or PTFL, the ATFL most often, described as the most common skeletal injury
in physically active populations; pain typically drops off quickly over the first two weeks.
**High/syndesmotic** sprains (extreme dorsiflexion plus external rotation; football, rugby, skiing)
take longest and may require a non-weight-bearing period. What the evidence supports: rest and
immobilisation lose to functional treatment; ice and compression are symptom relief only; NSAIDs
reduce pain without increased harms versus placebo **short-term (under 14 days)**, against a
*theoretical* concern that suppressing inflammation delays healing; kinesiotaping fails to improve
ankle function or performance; semi-rigid or lace-up bracing beats elastic bandaging. **The bracing
passage is the article's most rigorous moment** — Miles volunteers that most bracing trials compare
bracing to *immobilisation*, so what they show is that bracing beats rest. Three phases: restore
weight-bearing (crutches if needed, single-leg stance, curtsy lunge, single-leg cone touches, calf
raises, step-ups and step-downs; squats and deadlifts are out) — then reload the bar through
tolerable range via pin squats and block pulls, with dedicated calf work progressing bodyweight to
deficit to machine — then speed, landing and change of direction, where max effort is **RPE 9-10**
so volume stays low with near-full recovery: **3-10 max-effort jump reps, or 10-20 second AMRAP
sets**, plus reactive cutting drills. Nothing is cited for balance training reducing recurrence,
which is a real gap given how much that has been studied.

**Sciatica** ([guide](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md); [exercises
piece](simple-exercises-help-sciatica-pain.md)). "Sciatica" is not a diagnosis. Bogduk's split:
**radiculopathy** is objective loss of nerve function (reduced reflex, lost sensation, lost
strength); **radicular pain** is nerve-root pain with no functional loss. The distinction changes
the screening threshold, not the training. **The prognosis numbers carry no citation at all** and
are the most quotable part: non-specific low back pain resolves in **6-8 weeks**; among people with
new radicular pain, roughly **a third improve dramatically within a few weeks, a third improve
somewhat, and a third stay the same or worsen**; most improvement happens in the **first 3-4
months**, with milder relapse possible over the following year. The symptom-modification menu, all
bodyweight: trunk work (bird dogs, planks, sit-ups) **2-4 sets of 10-20 reps**, planks **2-4 x 10-30
seconds**; nerve glides **10 x 5-second holds**; repeated motions (cobra push-ups, Jefferson curls,
cat-cow) **2-4 x 10-20**; general movement — **three 20-minute walks a day**, plus **3 x 30 seconds
on 5-6 stretches**. They then undercut the category's own rationale and call these exercises
explicitly **"not a necessary component of recovery"** — "it's far more important that you move
rather than doing specific movements."

The modification grid from [the exercises piece](simple-exercises-help-sciatica-pain.md): barbell
squat becomes goblet squat; 5x5 becomes 3x12; RPE 6-8 becomes RPE 5-7; fast tempo becomes 3-1-0.
The two Week 1 programmes in [the main guide](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md)
both open with a symptom-modification movement, both close with a **25-minute walk at talking
pace**, and both use ascending RPE sets (12 at RPE 5, 12 at 6, 12 at 7) with slow tempos to keep
load light. **Denise, 58**, 15 months of low back pain with radiating left leg pain and L4-L5
impingement on MRI, home gym, 3 days a week: goblet good morning, goblet squat at 3.1.0 tempo,
single-leg RDL, single-leg hip thrust, split squat — with **feet-up dumbbell bench and seated press,
chosen because those variations remove the lumbar extension** that bothers people sensitive to
arching. **Troy, 21**, powerlifter, acute flare after a max deadlift, told by a doctor "never lift
again", 4 days a week: box squats to cap range, barbell good morning through a tolerable range, RDL
instead of floor pulls, belt squat and leg press to train legs without loading the spine, feet-up
bench, and a machine accessory day where he can train close to failure with little risk. **No
deadlift from the floor in week 1 — but plenty of hinging.** The stated design logic is that
symptom-modification work is the appetiser and dessert while traditional lifting is the meal, from
the start, and that leaving unaffected movements alone is itself a message that the body is not
fragile.

**Spondylolysis and spondylolisthesis** ([article](train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md)).
*Spondylolysis* is a fracture of the pars interarticularis, the bony bridge between a vertebra's
upper and lower joints; *spondylolisthesis* is one vertebra slipping forward on the one below. The
reload trigger is functional — full range of motion and symptom-free daily activities — not
radiographic. **Squats:** safety squat bar work including Hatfield squats to stay upright and reduce
forced spinal extension, goblet squats if that fails, hack squat or leg press as constrained
alternatives; **two sessions a week, 3-4 sets of 8-10 at an RPE 7 cap**. **Bench:** the arched
competition setup is the limiting factor, so feet-up or flat-backed benching starts early with
machine chest press as fallback, same **3-4 x 8-10 at RPE 7 or below**, extension reintroduced via
feet returning to the floor or elevated on plates and via dumbbell incline pressing. **Deadlift:**
conventional versus sumo does not matter, though sumo *may* help early by allowing a more upright
torso; the useful distinction is between a *training* variation (well tolerated, driving adaptation)
at **4x8 RPE 6-7** and an *exposure* variation (less tolerated, done lighter to rebuild tolerance)
at **4x8 RPE 5-6**, with a third session of machine hamstring curls 3x10 at RPE 9 and single-leg
RDLs 3x8 at RPE 7. Aerobic work continues (bike, walking, sled), rowing deferred to limit end-range
lumbar extension, target **at least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week**. Progression ladder:
**light and slow, then heavy and slow, then light and fast, then heavy and fast.** Returning lifters
commonly stall at **75-80%** of prior lifts on confidence rather than capacity — the fix is drop sets
emphasising bar speed, cutting **3-5%** and chasing rep quality. Note the explicit aside that
**lumbar extension is fine in normal training**; the constraint is a temporary rehab device, not a
lasting rule.

**Meniscus and everyday knee pain** ([article](second-opinion-meniscus-injury.md)). Expected
timeline for the deconditioned person who overdid it: **two to four weeks** to symptom resolution
with modification, offered without a citation. The diagnostic move for a new trainee is to look for
a recent jump in dose — restarting squats three times a week after five years, or a weekend laying
flooring after a decade of not kneeling. For pain severe enough to limit weight-bearing **without**
a clear mechanism: **weight-bearing as tolerated with crutches** — crutches to *support* the leg,
not to unload it, with weight progressively increased over days. For swelling that is actually
limiting: elevate with the heel propped so the back of the knee is off the ground and the leg is
above the heart, which also helps recover extension; an over-the-counter compression sleeve if the
person is on their feet at work. If swelling is not bothersome, no treatment is needed. **Absolute
rest is rejected throughout.** Substitutions: leg press, hack squat, goblet squat, lunge, leg
extension — with lunges temporarily keeping the shin vertical, then letting the knee travel over the
toes as symptoms allow. The true-beginner entry programme is deliberately unimpressive: 10 minutes
of biking, straight-leg raises, sit-to-stands from a chair, one single-leg exercise. Targets are the
public-health ones (**150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity, resistance training twice a week**)
at intensity **5-6 out of 10 or above**, with the 2-point symptom rule as the brake, and progress
tracked by load, reps, range of motion, or the same reps at a lower RPE. Post-operatively the two
stated priorities are **regaining full knee extension and controlling swelling**.

**Osteoarthritis** ([article](osteoarthritis-symptoms-causes-treatment.md), Baraki, 39 references).
Structured around Bunzli 2019, a qualitative study of patients scheduled for knee replacement, *all*
of whom believed their knee was "bone on bone" from wear and tear and avoided painful activity out
of fear. Three rebuttals. **(1) Imaging and symptoms come apart** — Culvenor's **43%**, plus roughly
**20% of patients still have knee pain after total knee replacement**, and pain can improve
substantially without joint structure changing. **(2) Routine loading does not cause OA; trauma
does** — ACL tears raise later risk (Paschos 2017), but Bricca 2019, a systematic review of RCTs on
exercise and articular cartilage, supports the null, and elite weightlifters had **thicker** knee
cartilage than controls, thicker the earlier they started (Grelak 2014 — small and cross-sectional,
so healthy-athlete selection could produce that without any adaptation occurring). **(3) Progression
is variable**; many people do not worsen over years, and inactivity is among the most prevalent risk
factors for functional decline in arthritis (Dunlop 2005). For treatment, progressive strength
training improves strength, pain intensity, physical function and self-efficacy even in advanced
arthritis (Kristensen 2012 systematic review; Jan 2008 RCT of high- versus low-resistance training;
Tanaka 2013 meta-analysis). Guidelines (ACR 2019, AAOS, AAFP) already put exercise first, and five
cited studies of clinician behaviour spanning 1993-2010 document that practice does not follow.
Oral analgesics, opioids, topicals, corticosteroid/PRP/hyaluronic acid injections and glucosamine
are grouped as offering "transient, minor, or no benefits over placebos". The concrete entry point
is **sit-to-stands from a chair**, progressed by lowering the surface for range, then adding a held
weight, then removing the chair; the *type* of exercise matters less than doing it regularly. Baraki
refuses the word "degeneration", calling radiographic OA a normal age-related change "analogous to
the development of grey hair or skin wrinkles" — a rhetorical move as much as a clinical one.

**Shoulder** ([guide](barbell-medicine-guide-shoulder-pain.md), 6,710 words, three inline sources
and no reference list). Barreto carries the imaging argument; two observational papers carry a
"general health affects local pain" claim (Hsu and Sheu 2016 on diabetes; Yang and Qu 2018, a
systematic review of hyperlipidaemia and rotator cuff disease), and the article extends it to excess
body fat, high blood pressure and inadequate sleep **with no citation at all**. The levers are
concrete: swap barbell overhead press for landmine press; use a **pin bench press with the pins set
just above the point where symptoms spike**; cut load and volume; slow the tempo (**3s eccentric, 1s
pause**) so the same rep scheme needs less weight; use shoulder isolation as a warm-up that doubles
as capacity work. Three cases from their paid service. **Jake, 25**, anterior pain diagnosed as
biceps tendinitis with no imaging, doing nothing but banded rotations for a month: pin bench above
the painful range plus 1-2 light full-range sets, landmine press, safety-bar squat, sidelying
external rotation and preacher curl as warm-ups; reps dropped 1-2 every two weeks, **load capped at
10 lb increases per week**, three roughly 6-week phases (~18 weeks) ending in new 3-rep maxes.
**Jessica, 32**, MRI-confirmed labral tear plus AC joint degeneration, symptoms only above roughly
**85% of 1RM** on bench and with overhead work: low pin bench, floor press, muscle snatch and muscle
clean replacing the full lifts (removing the fast drop under the bar), bottoms-up kettlebell press,
push-up plus and serratus punches, METCONs replaced with leg-driven assault-bike intervals — three
~4-week phases (~12 weeks) back to her previous bench max **with the labral tear untouched**, which
is the article's implicit point about imaging. **Anthony, 63**, retired dentist, posterior pain after
three days of cabinet installation and no training history: not modified lifting but a first-ever
lifting programme (band scaption, cable and sidelying external rotation, prone T and Y, serratus
wall slides, landmine press, wall push-ups, rows, pulldowns) over about two months. Passive
treatments — ice, massage, dry needling, injections, medication — are described as optional comfort
aids that can make training possible and **explicitly "not necessary"** for recovery: a stated
position, not a cited finding.

**DOMS** ([article](t-straighten-arms-after-workout.md)). Onset in the hours after training, **peak
at 24-72 hours**, then fading; requires loaded **eccentric** work; worst after novel exercises or a
volume jump. The Munich consensus (Mueller-Wohlfahrt 2013) grades it **1B** — functional rather than
structural. Nobody knows what causes it, and the article walks through five candidates and rejects
or qualifies each: muscle damage is partial at best (blood and urine markers do not correlate with
soreness severity or subsequent strength, and peak on a different timeline); lactate is dead
(concentric-only exercise produces *more* lactate and *no* DOMS); inflammation is plausible but many
studies biopsy to measure it **without a control group for the biopsy itself**; swelling does not
reliably track soreness; and the evolutionary neural story fails on its own terms, since a warning
arriving 48 hours late protects nothing. **Age shows no effect** (Sorenson 2018; Haynes 2023);
**sex shows no effect** (Dannecker 2013); **anxiety and depression correlate with more severe DOMS**
(Fabero-Garrido 2022). Soft-tissue work — compression, stretching, massage, heat, cold — has
variable and possibly negligible effects on pain ratings and no reliable relationship to perceived
fatigue or measured performance. Training itself reduces soreness acutely (exercise-induced
analgesia) and durably (the repeated bout effect). Protein has little to no effect once intake is
adequate at roughly **1.4-1.6 g/kg/day** — Barbell Medicine's house figure and the lowest in this
library, used here only as an adequacy floor, which is a defensible use of a low number. Their
closing heuristic — that never being sore means your load is too low — is coaching psychology, and
sits awkwardly next to their own point that soreness does not track performance.

**Concussion** ([guide](concussion-guide-symptoms-treatment-recovery.md)). Symptoms peak over
**7-10 days** and mostly resolve by **1-3 months**; beyond three months the label is persistent
post-concussion syndrome. **Over 30% still have lingering symptoms past one month** — which warrants
physician review, often MRI and neuropsychological testing — and roughly **15% of those who develop
PCS remain symptomatic beyond a year**. Management is **relative rest for 24-48 hours, not absolute
rest**: keep as much ordinary daily life as symptoms permit, cut screen time rather than substitute
it for the activity you stopped, start light walking inside the 24-48 hour window, minimise daytime
naps to protect night sleep, avoid alcohol and cannabis; paracetamol or NSAIDs are fine within
normal dosing. **Seven return-to-play stages, each a minimum of 24 hours:** (1) relative rest and
daily activity, with actual rest reserved for symptoms above 7/10; (2) steady-state aerobic work
under **55% of max heart rate**; (3) moderate aerobic work under **70% max HR** plus
symptom-limited light resistance training; (4) non-contact sport-specific drills, ideally alone to
avoid accidental impacts; (5) non-contact team practice with strength and conditioning back at
previous intensities; (6) full-contact practice; (7) unrestricted competition. Clearance is advised
before stage 4 if there is head-impact risk, and again before stage 6. **The stopping rule: symptoms
worsened for over an hour, or needing medication, means do not advance.** Re-injury risk
concentrates in the first 7-10 days — 1 in 15 college football players sustain a second concussion
in the same season, and three prior concussions triple future risk, both bare point estimates with
no population, interval or source. On CTE the article is careful: no clear consensus on the entity
itself, but repeated concussions are associated with cognitive impairment, psychiatric symptoms,
dementia risk and movement, gait and speech disorders. Hyperbaric oxygen (inconsistent, bias-prone,
thousands of dollars), neurofeedback and TMS (**$300-500 per session**, no consistent benefit),
blue-light lenses (never studied in concussion at all), creatine and electrolytes are all listed as
unproven.

**Running and knee OA** ([article](running-knee-osteoarthritis.md), 1,770 words, entirely
observational). Timmins 2017 found no association between running and OA diagnosis or imaging
findings, with runners at **50% lower odds of joint surgery**; Alentorn-Geli 2017 found hip and/or
knee OA prevalence of **3.66% in runners versus 10.23% in non-runners**. The split people skip:
within runners, **13.3% in competitive or elite runners versus 3.5% in recreational runners**. Lo
2018 (Osteoarthritis Initiative, age 50+) found self-identified runners with existing knee OA had no
increased odds of worsening radiographic findings or new knee pain and reported **improvement**
relative to non-runners, with BMI not differing between groups — but with **no intensity or volume
measurement at all** (subjects merely had to name running among their top three activities). The
general-health frame is worth carrying: physical inactivity accounts for **6-10% of the burden of
major non-communicable diseases** worldwide, and Pedisic 2020 (BJSM meta-analysis) found even
minimal doses — once a week, under 50 min/week, slower than 6 mph — associate with **27% lower
all-cause, 30% lower cardiovascular and 23% lower cancer mortality**. Practical advice for someone
with knee pain who wants to run: adjust pace, duration and frequency, add strength training, vary
terrain and surface, look at cadence and footwear, start with walk-jog intervals.

**Calves and the Achilles** ([case for training calves](case-training-calves.md), 768 words). The
plantarflexors generate higher ground reaction forces than the quadriceps across running speeds
(Schache 2014). From walking at 1.5 mph to running at 11.0 mph, **peak ankle power rises 23-fold
while peak soleus activity rises only 3.5-fold** (Lai 2015) — the soleus shifts toward near-isometric
force generation, transferring power through the Achilles. Healthy runners produced **200% bodyweight
plantarflexor torque**; runners with *unilateral* Achilles tendinopathy managed only **around 130%**,
and were deficient on **both** limbs (O'Neill 2019, cross-sectional). In healthy older adults,
plantarflexor strength predicted walking capacity and speed better than lower-limb lean mass or leg
extension strength and power (Stenroth 2015). Prescription: **two movements, one knee-extended and
one knee-flexed, 1-2 times weekly, 2-3 sets of 8 at RPE 7-8** — a seated barbell calf raise and a
single-leg deficit calf raise. Note this is a tendon-tolerance and function dose, not a growth dose,
and the article does not claim otherwise.

## Surgery, and coming back from it

**This is the strongest evidence in the domain.**

**Knee replacement.** [Osteoarthritis](osteoarthritis-symptoms-causes-treatment.md) carries Skou
2015 (NEJM RCT) and its 2018 two-year follow-up: among patients judged *eligible for* knee
replacement, **75% in the non-surgical arm declined the operation at 12 months, 68% at 2 years**.
Read that precisely — it is deferral and preference under good conservative care, not exercise
beating arthroplasty. In the trial, **surgery produced greater improvement with more serious adverse
events**, and Baraki says so, calls replacement "generally successful surgery when performed on
appropriately selected patients", argues only for evidence-based non-surgical care *first*, and
cites prehabilitation (Topp 2009) for those who proceed.

**Sciatica.** Peul 2007 (NEJM RCT, **283 patients** with disabling sciatica of 6-12 weeks) plus
Fernandez 2016 (SR+MA): **surgery buys faster short-term relief with similar long-term outcomes**.
The recommendation follows — conservative for tolerable pain, early surgery a defensible choice for
unrelenting debilitating pain.

**Meniscus.** Arthroscopic partial meniscectomy is typically no better than conservative care
long-term (Abram 2020 SR+MA; Rotini 2023, a systematic review of systematic reviews). Miles notes
that removing tissue does change load distribution (Willinger 2019, tibiofemoral contact pressure in
medial-meniscus-deficient knees) and explicitly frames that as **a mechanistic argument, not an
outcome argument**. [Post-op knee](post-operative-knee-rehabilitation.md) goes further: arthroscopy
for degenerative knee arthritis and meniscus tears is ineffective (Brignardello-Petersen 2017; Kim
2011), and the **AAOS position statement against arthroscopic debridement for knee OA has not
changed practice** — the procedure continues under "expert opinion".

**After surgery the message inverts: the failure mode is under-loading.** Return-to-sport figures
after ACL reconstruction: **81% return at any level, 65% to pre-injury level, only 55% to competitive
sport** in a cohort of over **7,500 athletes** (Ardern 2014); **83% in elite athletes** (Lai 2018),
so one in five never returns to the professional level; **23% reinjury in athletes under 25**
(Wiggins 2016, SR+MA). Only **13.9% of athletes met all return-to-sport criteria and 27.8% met the
strength criteria** (Toole 2017). Squat depth remains impaired **seven years** post-surgery (Sanford
2016). All observational — they establish that outcomes are frequently poor, not that better rehab
causes better ones, and Miles hedges accordingly ("the bigger problem is *likely* under-loading").
Grindem 2016 (Delaware-Oslo cohort) is the actionable one: **each additional month before return
lowers reinjury odds**. Vlok 2022, a scoping review of **117 exercises in 41 studies**, found the
exercise descriptors that determine strength gains largely **missing from published ACLR
programmes**.

**The staged model:** early post-op, then rehab, then return to participation (gated at **around 80%
quadriceps limb symmetry index**, adding sport-specific skill work), then return to practice (gated
at tolerating **around 80% of practice activity**, a deliberate intermediate step because practice
has "a more natural governor" than competition), then return to competition. Those thresholds are
practice standards, not validated cut-offs. **ACL physiological healing may take up to 9 months** and
joint kinematics are permanently altered afterwards, so Miles argues for a "new normal" framing
rather than a return to baseline. His benchmark line: *"If an athlete is 12 weeks after an
arthroscopic surgery and is still only performing bodyweight exercises, we are likely missing the
premise of progressive overload."* Work intended to build maximal strength that is rated **below RPE
5/10** is probably wasted. A rehab-specific twist on RPE: it may reflect not only effort against load
but whether the athlete can hold a clinician-imposed technical constraint, so the same movement can
be programmed with load as the target on one set, range of motion on another and symmetry on a
third, and **partial range is explicitly acceptable when load is the target**. And an athlete who
trains five days a week should have a five-day plan through rehab, because **2-3 clinic visits per
week does not cover the conditioning need** — even if some days are upper-body ergometer work, since
"a seated overhead press in a knee immobilizer is easy enough to perform."

**Rehab that never gets fast never gets the athlete back** ([power after
injury](train-power-after-injury.md)). The argument: standard rehab prescribes movements without
specifying **effort or intent**, so athletes go through the motions and arrive at clearance
underprepared for the velocities their sport imposes. **Effort is independent of load** — "flex your
quad" versus "flex your quad as hard as you can and hold it for 20 seconds" — so intensity can be
programmed from the first week after an acute injury. The progression: **isometric mid-thigh pull**
(high intensity, very early), then **spider stomps** (sprint mechanics without large ground reaction
force), then **A-skips, B-skips, jumping rope, oscillating split squats**, then **drop vertical
jumps, reactive ball drills and maximum accelerations** once the athlete needs to land, cut and
sprint. He concedes that the point at which both feet may safely leave the ground after
lower-extremity surgery **is still up for debate**. The contrarian bit: cueing athletes to "land
soft" trains a quality sport does not reward — in Guy-Cherry 2018, soft landings produced **1.3x
bodyweight** ground reaction force, self-selected normal landings **1.5x** and stiff landings
**1.8x** — so rehab should raise the floor of force the athlete can accept rather than lower the
ceiling. That is a training philosophy resting on an acute laboratory force-measurement study, and
it is a stronger claim than the citation supports. For lifters specifically he argues for *less*
volume of athletic power work, not none, and rejects chasing metrics like vertical jump height or
40 m time in favour of exposure to coordination, timing, rhythm and reaction.

**Measuring the deficit** ([when symmetry
matters](symmetry-matters-look-instances-where-indexes-matter.md), the most rigorous article in the
domain). Two claims cutting opposite ways: for untrained and generally healthy people, hunting
asymmetries is a waste of attention — asymmetry is normal and often sport-produced (youth baseball
pitchers show side-to-side shoulder and hip range differences; a sweep rower should not be
symmetrical). After specific injuries it predicts: hamstring limb symmetry index **below 85% gave
4x injury risk in 100 professional footballers** (Fousekis 2011, isokinetic, prospective) and
**2.4x in rugby union, rising to 3.4x below 80%** (Bourne 2015, custom Nordic rig with a load cell).
Base rates: reinjury **10.1% for hip flexor and quadriceps strains** (Eckard 2017), **up to 34% for
hamstrings** (Schmitt 2012), **23% after ACLR in younger athletes** (Wiggins 2016). Sanfilippo 2013
found that at return to sport the injured hamstring had a **20.4% smaller cross-sectional area but
only a 9.6% peak torque deficit**, resolved by six months — strength and size are not 1:1, and a
deficit persists at the moment athletes are typically cleared. Lepley 2015 (cited in the article as
"Kepley") found average side-to-side quadriceps deficits of **23% at six months** post-ACLR. Rambaud
2018 reports a **70% LSI** threshold before return to running; Myer 2008 proposes peak torque to
bodyweight of **1.6 (men) and 1.5 (women)** before sport-specific work.

**And the instrument most clinicians use cannot detect any of it.** Manual muscle testing grades
"slight", "moderate" and "strong" pressure, so its ceiling is *the clinician's own* force output. An
athlete kicking **150 lb on one leg and 120 lb on the other** has an **80% LSI** a clinician's hands
cannot resolve; at **200 versus 140 lb** — a **70% index** — it is hopeless, and both athletes grade
5/5. Yet **56% of physical therapists and 60% of sports-medicine physicians clear athletes on manual
muscle testing alone** (Greenberg 2018, 2019). Miles's counterweights against his own metric are the
best part of the article: Goodhart's law (hop-distance symmetry shows only weak association with
normal landing biomechanics — Wren 2018, and any skill-based test can be trained toward without the
underlying capacity changing), and the fact that **LSI inflates when the uninvolved limb also
detrains** (Wellsandt 2017), which is why he insists on carrying peak-torque-to-bodyweight alongside.
He states plainly that **no cited study shows that raising LSI lowers reinjury**, and labels his own
gym-floor substitute — 3x6 at a prescribed RPE on a knee extension or hamstring curl machine,
computed as a ratio — as having no peer-reviewed support.

His case study is the domain's best single data set, because it has isokinetic testing from four
months *before* the injury: a 220 lb 29-year-old who herniated an L3 disc with right-leg radicular
symptoms. Right knee extensor peak torque fell from **205.4 to 68 ft-lb**. Pre-injury LSI **87.4%**;
three weeks post-injury **28.6%**; at four months **140 ft-lb, LSI 54%, PT/BW up from 31% to 64%**;
at eighteen months **LSI 83.3% and PT/BW 82.3%** — still asymmetrical, and his answer to "does that
matter" is "possibly, for a sprinter or competitive powerlifter; absolutely not for the average
trainee." Management was continued training with constraints, not rest: **five days a week
throughout**, squats and deadlifts at sharply reduced absolute load with RPE caps, plus isolated
quadriceps work on a knee extension machine three times a week programmed **to failure**, on the
reasoning that at that level of capacity it took little load or volume to fatigue the muscle. That
is a rehab context recommending machine isolation to failure, which reads oddly against the
general-training material elsewhere in this source; his justification is specificity and load
tolerance, not growth, and he notes he would have used **1-2 reps in reserve** instead after a muscle
injury or ACLR.

## Equipment, passive treatment, and the negative findings

The evidenced treatment findings in this domain are overwhelmingly **negative**. Injections, PRP,
corticosteroid, dry needling, kinesiotaping, ice, cupping, scraping, laser, e-stim and shockwave
show at most small short-term effects across heel pain, elbow, ankle, tendinopathy and OA.
Karanasios 2021's **small** effect for exercise over passive care in the elbow is the closest thing
to a positive result anywhere in the set.

**Knee sleeves** ([article](knee-sleeves.md)) get the most careful equipment treatment, and it is
unbylined. Sleeves and wraps are different tools: **wraps** are woven elastic, wound tight, and
genuinely store and return elastic energy across the squat (Lake 2012, a measured mechanical effect,
which is why federations cap their dimensions — the IPF at **2 m by 8 cm**); a neoprene **sleeve**
does essentially none of this, so any benefit has to come from somewhere other than a spring. Best
case is roughly a **3% squat 1RM gain versus a loose placebo sleeve** in trained lifters, plus better
subjective comfort and stability; one study found **no improvement in mass lifted** at all; **sizing
down below the manufacturer's recommendation added nothing** (Machek 2020); none found harm. The
proposed mechanism — warmth, compression and improved joint position sense — is labelled by the
authors themselves as **"reasonable hypotheses"** needing more research, which is the right level.
The pain evidence is stronger than the performance evidence but sits in a different population:
knee osteoarthritis, where Cudejko 2018 (SR+MA of soft braces) is the strongest item in the
reference list, and where Bryk 2011 is an **immediate-effect** study rather than a training outcome
and Finestone 1993 is an RCT in military recruits with **patellofemoral** overuse pain, not
osteoarthritis. **Injury prevention is speculation and the article says so** — no knee-sleeve injury
data exists, so they reason by analogy from lifting belts, which have repeatedly failed to prevent
injury. Their buying recommendations (squat patterns and Olympic lifts yes, deadlifts no because
sleeves foul the bar path, **7 mm for powerlifting and 5 mm for dynamic work**) are experiential
preference; only "don't size down" has a study behind it. The closing line — that knee sleeves are
not necessary — is the right one.

**Core stability** ([article](core-stability-matter.md), 974 words) is a language objection, not an
exercise objection. There is no consensus definition, which makes it hard to measure and therefore
hard to link to injury risk or performance. The conventional **global** (rectus abdominis, external
oblique, thoracic lumbar iliocostalis) versus **local** (multifidus, transversus abdominis, internal
oblique) split, and the practice of "selectively activating" the local system with unloaded work to
avoid "overactivation", has no support per Wirth 2017. But Dickson presents the trials that go
against him — **Inani 2013** and **Moon 2013**, both small RCTs favouring lumbar stabilisation over
"traditional strengthening" in non-specific low back pain — and rebuts them methodologically rather
than dismissively: what those authors call traditional strengthening is very low-level movement, so
it is a weak-comparator problem. He calls lumbar stabilisation work "an excellent way to introduce
movement", useful for short-term symptom reduction and confidence in fearful patients. Darlow 2013
supplies the cost of the language: a woman repeatedly tested and told she was "ridiculously weak"
who terminated a pregnancy in part because she did not believe her body could carry it — and Dickson
labels this extreme himself, which is the right handling of one interview quote.

## Load management, and the one framework with real evidence under it

[Recovering from injury](recovering-injury-embrace-process.md) is where the quantitative backing
sits. **Eckard 2018**, a systematic review of **57 studies**, concluded the training-load/injury-risk
relationship is established, with **session RPE** (internal load) and the acute:chronic workload
ratio the best-evidenced measures. The practical translation is to rate each session **1 (rest) to
10 (hardest of your life)**, with the subjectivity defended as the point, since it absorbs the
intangibles — a defensible argument, not an evidenced one. Injury is defined per the **Timpka 2014**
athletics consensus statement, training load per the IOC "How much is too much?" consensus, and the
external (kg, sets, reps) versus internal (perceived response) split per **Impellizzeri 2019**.
Gabbett's loading "sweet spot" covers the return phase: after an injury there is deconditioning, so
resuming previous loads creates a workload spike. **Caveat:** the acute:chronic workload ratio was
already contested when this was written in 2019 and has been substantially criticised on
methodological grounds since — read the "strongest evidence" line as a 2019 snapshot, not current
standing. A useful clinical observation sits in the middle of it: **training fatigue can be misread
as pain and tissue injury**, and sleep, stress and accumulated fatigue amplify symptoms.
Expectations are treated as a modifiable variable (Thompson and Sunol's four-type taxonomy, with
*predicted* expectations the type associated with musculoskeletal outcomes), and the recommendation
— deliberately reset the goal to "return to baseline" so that symptomatic, reduced-capacity sessions
read as progress rather than failure — is advice, not a tested intervention.

## Youth: the problem is early specialisation, not lifting

[Youth training part I](resistance-training-youth-part-i.md) belongs in this domain because it is an
injury argument, not a programming one — the prescriptions are deferred to Part II. The old
objections are dead in the literature: the 2014 Lloyd international consensus statement and the 2008
AAP position statement put it the other way round, that childhood may be the opportune window for
building bone mass through weight-bearing activity. The population problem is **under**training: WHO
asks for **60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily for ages 5-17**, and PE lessons hit
that threshold only about **50% of the time in elementary school** (Hollis), **34.2%** in an earlier
analysis, **40.5% in secondary school**. **Today's 16-year-olds are about as active as today's
60-year-olds** (Varma 2017, accelerometer data).

Specialisation roughly doubles injury risk. Bell 2018, a systematic review with meta-analysis in
*Pediatrics*, found highly specialised athletes **1.81x more likely to be injured (95% CI
1.26-2.60)**; Rugg 2018 found **42.8% major injury in early-specialising NBA first-round picks
versus 25%**; O'Kane 2017 found that playing for more than one team **raised** injury risk **2.5x**
while multiple different activities **lowered** it **61%**. McGuine 2017 is the prospective study of
over 1,500 high school athletes that, with Bell, carries the claim. And specialisation does not buy
the outcome parents want: only **16.9%** of Division I athletes at one major university had
specialised by their freshman year — over half were multi-sport — NCAA high-school-to-college
conversion runs from **12.4% (male lacrosse) down to 2.9% (male wrestling)**, and the average
scholarship is **$10,400 a year** against parental spending that can reach **$1,000 a month from
age six**. Padaki 2017 is the number that should stop a reader: in youth athletes averaging just
under 14, **20.4% of parents hoped their child would play professionally** and 36.4% collegiately,
while **72% of the children had already sustained an overuse injury and 16.6% had already had
surgery**. Madigan 2018 found injury likelihood **more than doubled per one-standard-deviation
increase in perfectionistic concerns**; Milewski 2014 found adolescents sleeping under 8 hours were
**1.7x more likely to be injured**; Luke 2011 links overscheduling and practices within 48 hours of
competition to injury.

The sharpest clinical point in the piece: **humeral retroversion in youth throwers is treated as
adaptive and cam-type deformity in youth hockey players as pathological, and they arise by
essentially the same loading mechanism.** Whether repetitive load is stimulus or damage depends on
the adaptation it produces.

## What they sell

Commercial routing is near-universal, and worth stating plainly, because "skip the scan and
self-manage" points a reader toward a coaching subscription rather than a clinician. Most of these
24 articles close by directing readers to Barbell Medicine's **paid Pain and Rehab consultations**.
The **low back and knee rehab templates** are pitched in [the rehab-mistakes
article](biggest-mistakes-re-making-lifting-rehab.md), which works through exactly those two
conditions, and again in [the sciatica guide](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md),
[the sciatica exercises piece](simple-exercises-help-sciatica-pain.md),
[core stability](core-stability-matter.md) and
[osteoarthritis](osteoarthritis-symptoms-causes-treatment.md). [The spondy
article](train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md) sells The Bridge alongside their free Beginner
Prescription. [The shoulder guide's](barbell-medicine-guide-shoulder-pain.md) three case studies come
from the paid service and function as advertisements as well as illustrations. [The knee sleeve
article](knee-sleeves.md) recommends SBD and Rogue through links carrying referral tracking
parameters. [The calves piece](case-training-calves.md) routes to their own programmes and to the
rehab division. The advice itself is mainstream physiotherapy and is not bent to fit the product,
but the recommendation and the product coincide.

**Three sell nothing:** [power after injury](train-power-after-injury.md), [youth resistance
training](resistance-training-youth-part-i.md) — which also takes a swipe at the supplement industry
("single-digit effects" versus resistance training's "double-digit benefits") despite Barbell

## Weak points

**Absence of evidence stated as demonstrated absence.** The recurring error, and it appears in six
articles. *"No evidence that any modality speeds up the healing process"*
([ankle](manage-ankle-sprain.md)); *"there is simply no physiological justification for why any of
these modalities would expedite healing"* ([tendinopathy](barbell-medicine-guide-tendinopathy.md));
*"none of these have demonstrated a benefit beyond placebo"* ([heel](guide-heel-pain.md)); *"no
laser, suction, needling, taping, or any other passive intervention has been shown to outperform the
body's own healing process"* ([post-op knee](post-operative-knee-rehabilitation.md), uncited);
*"there is no evidence that rest speeds up healing or helps people return to their prior level of
function"* ([spondy](train-spondylolisthesis-spondylolysis.md), uncited); *"avoiding the movements
with absolute rest only serves to further decrease capacity, and does not facilitate recovery"*
([hip II](training-hip-pain-part-ii.md), uncited). In each case the defensible version — that the
modality has not been shown to affect tissue healing, which leaves symptom relief untouched — is
what the article's own reasoning actually supports. [The elbow
article](lateral-elbow-pain-lifters.md) does the same to braces, dismissing them flatly and without
citation when brace trials exist and are equivocal rather than absent.

**"Absolutely no scientific evidence" resting on one narrative review.** [Core
stability](core-stability-matter.md)'s strongest sentence rests on **Wirth 2017**, a narrative
critical analysis, which is also the sole source for the identical claim in **both** sciatica
articles. Absence of a definition is a measurement problem, not a disproof. The same article's read
of Owen 2020 is lossy — a network meta-analysis exists precisely to rank exercise modes against each
other, and Owen's did find differences — and its Steffens 2016 "**45%** reduction from exercise plus
education" is carried by the exercise half, so the bundled phrasing flatters education.

**Outright wrong citations.** [The DOMS article](t-straighten-arms-after-workout.md) links its
claim about potassium, phosphate and calcium release causing arrhythmias to **Gallagher 1997,
"Appendicular skeletal muscle mass: effects of age, gender, and ethnicity"** — a body-composition
paper that does not support it. It also links Dupuy 2018 to the same PMID as Mizuno 2016, so the
soft-tissue bullet effectively rests on one linked paper plus Lund 1998, and misspells Pasiakos and
Kerksick. [Hip part I](training-hip-pain-part-i.md) attributes a sentence to "Stovall 2010" that
links to the Cooperstein review, leaving the claim effectively unsourced — and cites **Kendall 2013,
titled *Steps toward the validation of the Trendelenburg test*, against the test**. [The elbow
article](lateral-elbow-pain-lifters.md)'s corticosteroid sentence links to an
exercise-versus-passive-treatment meta-analysis. [The knee sleeve article](knee-sleeves.md) attaches
its **3% 1RM figure to Sinclair et al.**, whose stated outcomes are kinetics, kinematics and muscle
forces, while the 1RM-versus-placebo design appears in **Machek's** title — and it puts Wassell's
JAMA **back-belt** trial inside a cluster cited for knee proprioception. [The sciatica
guide](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md)'s reference [9] (Zhong 2017) resolved
to a different, later meta-analysis in the automated record. [The symmetry
article](symmetry-matters-look-instances-where-indexes-matter.md) cites Lepley 2015 as "Kepley",
dates Robinson 2007 as 2008, and duplicates two entries in its own reference list.

**A citation used for the wrong question.** [The meniscus
article](second-opinion-meniscus-injury.md) answers "will lifting weights wear out the knees?" with a
**2020 JAMA Internal Medicine** cohort reporting roughly **10 additional years free of chronic
disease** for people meeting physical activity guidelines, not smoking, drinking only moderately and
with BMI under 25. That is a lifestyle-and-chronic-disease finding that says nothing about
cartilage or joint tissue; the honest answer was the asymptomatic-prevalence data already on the
page. The adjacent "menisci adapt to loading" has no source at all. The same article uses **Dye
1998** — an orthopaedic surgeon arthroscoping his own knees without intra-articular anaesthetic — to
support a general statement about meniscal innervation. Miles flags it as **n = 1** himself, to his
credit, but a single-subject neurosensory map cannot carry a population claim.

**Headlines that erase their own data.** [Running and knee
OA](running-knee-osteoarthritis.md) states that evidence running increases OA risk "does not exist"
and calls recreational running **"protective"** — while its own source found **13.3% prevalence in
competitive runners versus 3.5% in recreational**, a dose signal the headline deletes, from
cross-sectional data that cannot carry a causal word. Healthy-user and survivorship bias run in
exactly the wrong direction here: people whose knees hurt stop running, so "runners" is pre-filtered
for tolerating running, and the **50% lower odds of joint surgery** is a treatment decision, not a
disease state. [Training calves](case-training-calves.md) repeats it smaller: O'Neill's bilateral
plantarflexor deficit is cross-sectional, so "strengthen calves to protect the Achilles" outruns the
design. [Youth part I](resistance-training-youth-part-i.md) raises **survivorship bias explicitly**
and then cites two cohorts of survivors — NBA first-round picks and 102 professional pitchers — as
evidence about specialisation and injury; its Bush 2019 weightlifting figure is a retrospective
survey of 141 athletes with an odds ratio of **23.9 (95% CI 4.1-138)**, an interval that says almost
nothing about magnitude; and its claim that taking kids off the field and into the gym "can make
them better *athletes*" is not what its evidence shows.

**Overstated psychology.** [The sciatica
guide](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md) supports "beliefs are associated with
the pain experience" with a cross-sectional survey (Setchell 2017) and a clinical commentary on a
*model* (Bunzli 2017), then phrases it as "our feelings about our pain have a profound effect on how
much pain we are in"; its claim that accumulating training successes "helps us to hurt less" is a
mechanism story with no citation. [Recovering from injury](recovering-injury-embrace-process.md)
says pain *always* results from multiple factors and *never* one problem, on two editorials, and
uses causal language about self-efficacy resting on a review of correlational work. [Hip part
I](training-hip-pain-part-i.md)'s "glutes being weak, 'asleep', or forgetful are not based in
scientific plausibility" rests on two small EMG studies (Dwyer 2013 in hip osteoarthritis; Marshall
2011 in low back pain during standing) finding *increased* gluteal activation in people with pain —
which undercuts "pain switches the glutes off" but does not make weakness implausible. The
defensible version, available in the same article, is that manual muscle testing and functional
screens cannot detect what they claim to.

**Claims a clinical source should have sourced and did not.** [The rehab mistakes
article](biggest-mistakes-re-making-lifting-rehab.md) states bare that **"upwards of 90% of people
experiencing low back pain do not have a single, clear cause"** and that "the majority of folks will
improve faster with gradual exposure to movement" — both real, both guideline-aligned, both uncited
across 3,639 words — and dismisses ibuprofen, sleeves, warm-ups, technique tweaks, imaging and
injections together with no evidence that they fail. The sciatica prognosis (**6-8 weeks**; the
thirds) is uncited in **both** sciatica articles. [Heel pain](guide-heel-pain.md)'s "most cases get
better on their own over the course of a year" has no source, proportion or interval while doing
heavy work in the argument, and its risk factors (BMI above 27, sedentary lifestyle) arrive with no
design or effect size and are not independent of each other.

**Thin reference bases in long clinical pieces.** The 6,710-word [shoulder
guide](barbell-medicine-guide-shoulder-pain.md) has **three inline sources and no reference list**;
the 4,701-word [tendinopathy guide](barbell-medicine-guide-tendinopathy.md) has **four**; the
2,404-word [elbow article](lateral-elbow-pain-lifters.md) has **three, all in the final section**;
the 3,069-word [power article](train-power-after-injury.md) has **three**;
[post-op knee](post-operative-knee-rehabilitation.md) has **no bibliography at all**; [rehab
mistakes](biggest-mistakes-re-making-lifting-rehab.md) has **none**. The
[concussion guide](concussion-guide-symptoms-treatment-recovery.md) reports **no study design,
sample size, duration or effect size anywhere**, which in a clinical piece is a real weakness, and
states causally that "longer rest periods lead to worse symptom intensity and duration" from a
largely paediatric literature that supports *early activity helps* far more securely than *rest
harms*. Against all that, [the sciatica
guide](pain-progress-practical-approach-sciatica-recovery.md) builds a structurally identical
argument across 15 numbered references with a verified mapping, on roughly twenty times the
evidential base of the shoulder guide — which demonstrates that this source *can* do it when it
chooses.

**Two internal contradictions worth knowing.** [The sciatica exercises
piece](simple-exercises-help-sciatica-pain.md) closes with boilerplate claiming "some of the
exercises we've listed today are best done until failure, so it's crucial to program correctly" —
contradicting its own body, where everything is bodyweight and explicitly kept below a symptom
threshold — before pitching "the perfect hypertrophy program", which has nothing to do with
sciatica. And [the elbow article](lateral-elbow-pain-lifters.md) recommends **straps** as a
temporary offloading aid while dismissing **braces**, which offload similarly, in the same breath.

**Where they are unusually rigorous, and it should be recorded.** [Hip
II](training-hip-pain-part-ii.md) admits no evidence exists for its own protocol before prescribing
it. [The ankle article](manage-ankle-sprain.md) volunteers that most bracing trials compare bracing
to immobilisation, which undercuts its own recommendation. [The knee sleeve
article](knee-sleeves.md) labels its own proposed mechanism "reasonable hypotheses". [The meniscus
article](second-opinion-meniscus-injury.md) flags Dye 1998 as **n = 1** while still using it, and
frames the load-redistribution point as mechanistic rather than outcome evidence. [The symmetry
article](symmetry-matters-look-instances-where-indexes-matter.md) raises Goodhart's law against its
own metric and calls its gym-floor substitute unvalidated in as many words. [The
osteoarthritis](osteoarthritis-symptoms-causes-treatment.md) piece reports that surgery beat
exercise on improvement in the very trial it uses to argue for deferral, and notes that people who
improve with exercise often stay preoccupied with damage anyway (Wallis 2019) — changing outcomes
and changing beliefs are separate problems. And [the rehab mistakes
article](biggest-mistakes-re-making-lifting-rehab.md), for all that it cites nothing, contains the
most honest sentence in the domain: *"there is nothing special about any of the sample starting
programs provided here. Equally (or perhaps even more) effective programs could use different
exercise selection, rep ranges, volume, or frequency."*
