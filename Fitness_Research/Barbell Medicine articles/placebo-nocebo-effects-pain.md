# Placebo, Nocebo, and the Sentence That Makes a Back Hurt

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Placebo and Nocebo Effects in Pain" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/placebo-and-nocebo-effects-in-pain/)) · Nick Occhipinti · 2026-05-29 · 5,819 words
**Topic:** Pain science

## Bottom line
- **Two trials carry the argument.** Sihvonen 2018 (FIDELITY) found no significant difference in knee pain or function at 2 years between arthroscopic surgery for a degenerative meniscus tear and a **sham operation** — both groups improved [5]. Ashar 2024 gave 101 chronic low back pain patients a saline injection while **telling them it was a placebo**, and still found significant improvement at 1 month [20/6].
- The open-label result is the more important one: it kills the assumption that placebo effects require deception, which is what makes the article's clinical recommendations possible at all.
- **Placebo effects are confined to subjectively measured outcomes.** The article is explicit and correct that we should not expect placebo to move blood lipids or HbA1c — only pain, mood, function, well-being. This is the single most useful boundary condition in the piece.
- **Their strongest and least supported claim: "all treatments for pain are mediated by placebo effects."** No citation sits on that sentence. As stated it is close to unfalsifiable; the defensible version is that any treatment for a subjective symptom has a placebo component of unknown size.
- **Nocebo is treated as the bigger clinical risk** — stronger and longer-lasting than placebo, per Kunkel 2025 [9]. That study was run in **healthy individuals** with experimental pain, and the article extends it to patients without saying so.
- **The practical output is a four-item ethics checklist** for when a placebo-only treatment is acceptable, and a language rule for clinicians and coaches. Both are recommendations, not findings, and both are sensible.

## The evidence that placebo effects are real

**Sham surgery.** The article's flagship is FIDELITY [5]: patients with non-traumatic degenerative meniscus tears randomised to a real arthroscopic procedure or to a sham — anaesthetised, scoped, given a scar, told they had been treated. No significant between-group difference in pain or function at 2 years; both groups improved.

**One correction the note should carry:** the article repeatedly calls the real arm "meniscus repair surgery". It was **arthroscopic partial meniscectomy** — removal of torn tissue, not repair. Different operation, different rationale, and the distinction matters to anyone reading it against their own knee. The counts given (144 total, 70 vs 74) should be checked against the published trial before being quoted.

**Open-label placebo.** Ashar 2024 [20] — 101 patients with chronic low back pain, saline injection, explicitly told it was a placebo, significant improvement in pain at 1 month with functional neuroimaging. The article summarises this as "significant reductions in pain and mood", a wording slip; the finding is improvement in mood, not reduction of it.

**Nocebo.** No trial is offered for the flagship nocebo claim in the same way. The mechanism list — verbal suggestion, prior learning, social observation, mass psychogenic modelling, negative clinical communication — is cited to Colloca 2024 and Saunders 2024 [7,8], the latter a systematic review and meta-analysis of social learning specifically. The claim that **nocebo effects are stronger and more persistent than placebo** rests on Kunkel 2025 [9], whose title says plainly "in healthy individuals". That is a legitimate paradigm, but it is not the same evidential object as chronic pain in patients, and the article does not mark the gap.

Their mechanic analogy — brakes that "look like the brakes of an 80-year-old car", mapped onto a doctor saying "you have the spine of an 80-year-old" — is a good teaching device doing more persuasive work than any cited study.

## Three mechanisms, none settled

The article is honest that mechanisms are "in the theory and discovery stage" and offers three, likely operating together in different proportions:

1. **Classical conditioning** [10,11] — an unconscious learned association between stimulus and relief; the chiropractic "crack" becomes the bell. A related and useful point: **regression to the mean** [19] contaminates this learning. People seek treatment at their worst, pain drifts back toward its average regardless, and the treatment gets the credit — reinforcing the conditioned response for next time.
2. **Response expectancy** [12] — relief tracks how much the person believes it will work. The same source supports the moderator: **expectancy is weaker in pessimistic people and in those with higher anxiety**, so placebo effects are smallest in exactly the population most likely to be in persistent pain. Expectations are sourced socially [13], and social learning of *negative* outcomes is a strong nocebo route [8].
3. **Predictive coding / Bayesian brain** [14] — in chronic pain with central sensitisation, the brain's prediction defaults to pain even for light stimuli; treatments may work by violating that prediction and forcing new learning. The source, Kaptchuk 2020 in the *BMJ*, is an analysis piece, not an experiment.

**Note the register shift.** Mechanisms 1 and 2 have experimental support; mechanism 3 is a theoretical model presented with the same confidence. The article also asserts that expectancy and conditioning produce larger effects in *acute* pain while chronic pain needs the predictive-coding account — a claim about the literature the cited sources do not obviously establish.

## The physiology, and what makes the effect bigger

The table of biological correlates — placebo increasing endogenous opioids, endocannabinoid activity, dopamine and oxytocin; nocebo decreasing opioids and dopamine, suppressing endocannabinoids, and increasing **CCK**, a pain-amplifying peptide — is cited entirely to Knezevic 2025 [21], one narrative review. A reasonable summary of a real literature, but drawn from a single secondary source and presented as settled biology.

Effect size is then grouped into three families [15,21]:

**Intervention factors.** Being told a placebo costs more produces a larger effect [16]. Premium or exclusive branding raises expectation [17]. More elaborate, skilled-seeming, time-consuming treatments produce larger effects [17,21] — machines with lights and sounds, specialised postures and tools. Surgery carries some of the largest: the article cites a review of 17 studies and 535 patients given sham surgery for chronic low back pain, with **over half achieving clinically meaningful improvement** [17].

**A caution on that last number.** Reference [17] is Hartman 2009, *"Why do ineffective treatments seem helpful? A brief review"* (*Chiropractic & Osteopathy*) — a short narrative review, not itself a systematic review of sham surgery. The figure is secondhand, and this one reference carries three separate claims: pricing, treatment intensity, and the sham-surgery statistic. It is the weakest load-bearing citation in the piece.

**Person factors.** Two *competing* theories on severity, and the article is willing to say so without picking a winner: more severe symptoms give more room to improve, versus shorter and milder pain responding better because central sensitisation has had less time to develop. Beliefs and outlook also modulate the effect [12].

**Relationship factors.** Empathy, trust, feeling heard, unhurried history-taking — these enlarge placebo effects through expectancy [15,21]. The article notes the symmetry, that **a strong relationship magnifies nocebo too**, and extends the point to coaches and trainers, not just clinicians.

## The part worth keeping: the ethics checklist

If a treatment has a real physiological benefit, placebo is "the cherry on top". If it has no known benefit beyond placebo, they propose four conditions for pursuing it anyway — this is their recommendation, not a finding, and it is the most portable thing in the article:

1. Financial, time and effort cost is within reason for the person.
2. No adverse effects.
3. It is not displacing more effective interventions.
4. The patient is properly informed of the true effects.

The mirror case: expensive, risky, displacing better care, or **packaged with a harmful narrative** — their worked example is months of paid spinal manipulation instead of progressive exercise, sold with the story that your spine is out of alignment and needs a specialist to fix it. Their argument is that the short-term placebo relief is outweighed by the long-lasting nocebo of being told you are broken. That is a real and specific mechanism claim, and it is uncited.

**And the positive instruction:** build rapport, set realistic positive expectations, avoid negative language not supported by evidence, and stop framing normal movement as dangerous.

## Overreach, and what they sell

- **"All treatments for pain are mediated by placebo effects"** is asserted twice, once as a section heading, with no citation. It is the article's thesis and its least defended sentence.
- **Kunkel 2025 is generalised from healthy volunteers to patients** without a hedge.
- The **social learning** finding [8] is garbled: the article ends a paragraph with "These results were if the learning took place in a face-to-face interaction", which appears to be a mangled report of a moderator effect and is not readable as written.
- **The reference list duplicates itself** — [6] and [20] are the same Ashar 2024 paper, and [18] and [21] are the same Knezevic 2025 review. The marker mapping still verified, so claim-level attribution here is sound, but the numbering is sloppier than the citation density suggests. Two further references [10,13] are **StatPearls chapters** — tertiary teaching material, not research.
- **Commercial interest:** the article ends with three calls to action pointing at Barbell Medicine coaching and training programmes. There is a self-serving structure to the argument worth naming plainly — it explains why passive treatments people pay other providers for work largely through expectation, and then routes the reader to a paid product of its own. The underlying pain-science position is mainstream, and the article's own checklist would apply to its programmes as readily as to anyone else's; but the reader should see the shape of it.
