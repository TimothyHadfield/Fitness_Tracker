# Ten Chin-Up Substitutes, and Why the Choice Barely Matters

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "10 Chin-Up Alternatives That Are Very Effective" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/chin-up-alternatives/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-10-09 · 4,326 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- Ten substitutes, selected on one criterion: **they train the same muscle groups and use the lats as a primary mover**. One vertical pull (lat pulldown), one pullover, and eight rows.
- The article's actual thesis is anti-optimisation. Grip and range-of-motion variations are **not predicted to change regional growth**, and variation choice "should mainly reflect the individual's preferences." That is a striking thing to say inside a 4,300-word exercise listicle.
- **Only two claims in the whole article carry a citation**, and neither is an outcome claim — one is a practitioner review of the lat pulldown, the other a biomechanics study of rowing variations. Everything else, including all the muscle-attribution lists, is unsourced.
- The most useful practical content is difficulty scaling: the **inverted row and ring row are continuously adjustable by body angle**, which makes them the only entries on the list that work at any strength level without changing equipment.
- Their genuine recommendation is to **pair a vertical pull with a horizontal pull**, not to replace one with the other.

## What the list is and how they chose it

Chin-ups are framed as a pull-up variation with a supinated grip, generally easier because the biceps sit in a better position, working broadly the same musculature. The named prime mover is the latissimus dorsi, with teres major, rhomboids, trapezius, biceps brachii, brachioradialis, all three deltoid heads and rectus abdominis contributing.

The selection rule is stated up front and is more disciplined than most lists of this kind: exercises that train the same muscle groups **and** use the lats as primary movers. That admits a lot of rows, which is why eight of the ten are rows.

Notably, they open by naming the two options that are *not* on the list but are the right answer for the most common reason people want alternatives. If the goal is a first chin-up or more chin-up reps, **band-assisted chins or an assisted chin machine (Gravitron)** are what they recommend, because those train the actual movement pattern. The alternatives list is for a different problem — the movement is unavailable, unsuitable, or unwanted.

The ten:

1. **Lat pulldown** — the only true vertical pull on the list. Prime mover the lats, with infraspinatus, teres major and minor, trapezius, posterior deltoid, biceps brachii, brachialis and brachioradialis as synergists.
2. **Inverted row** — bar in a rack or Smith machine. Difficulty set by body angle: ~70 degrees to the floor is nearly upright and easy; closer to horizontal means lifting more of your bodyweight.
3. **Ring row** — the same movement on rings or straps, marginally harder for the instability, and portable. Progressions given: raise the rings or bend the knees to make it easier; lower the rings, elevate the feet, or go one-armed to make it harder.
4. **One-arm dumbbell row** — with the caveat that dumbbell weight is a hard ceiling for stronger lifters.
5. **Lever row** — a one-armed barbell row gripping near the collar, their answer to that ceiling and to home gyms without heavy dumbbells. Practical note: use smaller plates to preserve range of motion.
6. **T-bar row** — landmine or plate-anchored, narrower neutral grip; offered specifically for people who do not tolerate heavy barbell rows.
7. **Pendlay/barbell row** — the difference given is range of motion, the Pendlay starting and finishing on the floor each rep. They flag it as the powerlifter's preference for its similarity to the clean and deadlift.
8. **Dumbbell pullover** — the outlier, since pectoralis major and serratus anterior join the lats as primary movers. A bridge variation is the harder version.
9. **Chest-supported row** — incline bench, removing the torso-stabilisation demand.
10. **Seated cable row** — with an explicit instruction not to lean back or drive with the legs.

Wrist straps are recommended twice, for T-bar and chest-supported rows, to stop grip fatigue capping the working set.

## What the two citations actually support

This is where the article needs reading carefully, because the citation density is very low and neither reference does the work a reader might assume.

**Reference 1 ([Ronai 2019](https://doi.org/10.1249/FIT.0000000000000469))** is attached to a single sentence: that the lat pulldown is a vertical pulling movement training the back, shoulders and arms. It is a practitioner column in *ACSM's Health & Fitness Journal* — a technique and coaching review, not an experiment. It supports the descriptive claim it is attached to and nothing more.

**Reference 2 ([Fenwick, Brown & McGill 2009](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e3181942019))** sits on the sentence naming lats, infraspinatus, teres major and minor and trapezius as prime movers in the barbell row, with posterior deltoid, biceps and brachialis as synergists. **That is a mismatch worth flagging.** Fenwick compared rowing exercises for *trunk* muscle activation and lumbar spine motion, load and stiffness — it is a spine-loading study, and its interest is in what the low back is doing during different row variants, not in ranking prime movers of the upper back. Using it to underwrite a prime-mover list stretches it.

Every other anatomical attribution in the article — nine exercises' worth of prime movers and synergists — carries no citation at all. These are textbook-level statements and mostly uncontroversial, but the reader should know they are asserted rather than sourced.

## The claim that undercuts the genre

Buried in the lat pulldown section: "Generally speaking, it's difficult to predict that a different grip or ROM would produce a different result in regional muscle growth, though individual results will most certainly vary. The choice of a specific variation should mainly reflect the individual's preferences."

That is the article's real position, and it is the opposite of the implied promise of a ranked alternatives list. The same logic recurs at the end — pick exercises you enjoy and will keep doing for a long time — and it is consistent with the rest of the source's output. It is stated as a general principle without evidence, but it is stated honestly as a prediction problem rather than as a finding.

The one place they do rank things is dosing, not selection: for anyone who can do more than **10–12 consecutive chin-ups**, they recommend loading the movement (weighted vest, dumbbell between the legs, dip belt) rather than adding reps. That is a strength-progression recommendation, uncited, and reasonable.

Their concluding recommendation is complementary rather than substitutive: pair a vertical pull (chin-up or lat pulldown) with a horizontal pull (inverted row, lever row), because the two patterns are not interchangeable. This is consistent with what [their GPP article](general-physical-preparedness-training-lifters.md) argues from a different angle — that lat pulldowns beat weighted chins for upper-back hypertrophy on a volume-per-fatigue basis, while chin-ups mainly improve chin-ups.

## What this note does not contain

There is no evidence here on hypertrophy outcomes, strength transfer, or which row grows more back. The article does not claim there is. Treat it as a well-organised technique reference with an explicit stance that selection within the category is a preference decision — and treat the muscle lists as textbook assertion.

**Things they sell:** the closing section is a sales page. It links their training-template catalogue by goal (hypertrophy, strength, rehab, endurance, bodybuilding, powerbuilding) and their online coaching and consultation service, with the bridging line that their templates include alternative exercises so you can train "without hating the process." The article's advice is not distorted by this, but the final ~150 words are marketing rather than content, and the piece reads as search-optimised commercial content built around a thin evidence base.
