# "Lower Chest" Exercises: A Regional Growth Claim With No Regional Growth Data

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "10 Best Lower Chest Workout Exercises for Massive Pecs" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-lower-chest-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine (no named author) · 2023-12-07 · 5,988 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **Not one study in this article's reference list measured regional hypertrophy of the pectoralis major.** The only regional-growth papers cited anywhere in the list — Ema 2013 and Earp 2015 — are about **inhomogeneous growth of the quadriceps femoris**. The entire "lower chest" premise rests on EMG plus an analogy to a different muscle in a different limb.
- The ranking is therefore **activation-based, not outcome-based**, and the article says so itself in its fourth paragraph: "it's not clear whether changing muscular excitation levels … alters the amount of muscle growth or strength … electrical differences are not really predictive of actual outcomes." The supporting citation is Vigotsky's paper titled *"Greater electromyographic responses do not imply greater motor unit recruitment and 'hypertrophic potential' cannot be inferred."* An article headlined "10 Best Lower Chest Workout Exercises" contains a direct refutation of its own selection method.
- It also concedes the anatomy: "It's difficult to isolate the lower chest specifically, as the abdominal and sternocostal heads of the pectoralis major are still part of the same, larger muscle," and later, "not everyone will need to train the lower chest directly."
- **Four of the ten exercises have no supporting study of any kind in the list** — chest dip, cable crossover, medicine ball push-up and "jackhammer pushdown". The jackhammer pushdown, claimed to give "one of the best possible angles to isolate your lower chest", is uncited entirely.
- The health-benefit section is where it goes furthest wrong. The reference list contains **no bone-density study**, no resistance-training-and-cancer study and no diabetes study, yet the article claims chest flyes improve bone density and that lifting reduces cancer and type II diabetes risk. The conclusion tells readers to "watch your strength, muscles, bone density, and heart health improve" — and the sign-off boasts that "our methods are backed by the most up-to-date research in sports medicine, so you'll never have to worry about snake oil salesmen here."

## What is recommended, and on what basis

Ten exercises with prescriptions: decline dumbbell bench press (2–4 × 8–12, RPE 7–8), dumbbell chest fly (2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9), chest dip (2–4 × 8–12, RPE 7–8), cable crossover (2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9), push-up, incline push-up, decline push-up and medicine ball push-up (all 2–4 sets to within 1–2 reps of failure), seated machine fly and jackhammer pushdown (2–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9). Weekly volume guidance is 10–15 sets for the chest, adjusted by response. The loading model is RPE-based with explicit permission to reduce load on bad days — this is Barbell Medicine's house autoregulation approach and it is the most defensible part of the programming advice, though it is a coaching method rather than a finding.

The stated mechanism for targeting the lower chest is three-part: change the pressing angle (decline) to raise lower-pec excitation; increase attentional focus on the region; and train close to failure. All three are supported in the list only by **EMG studies** — Saeterbakken 2017 on bench press variations, Snyder & Fry 2012 on verbal instruction and muscle activity, Stastny 2017's systematic review of bench press surface EMG. Rodríguez-Ridao 2020 (five bench inclinations, EMG of pec major, anterior deltoid and triceps) and Solstad 2020 (barbell bench press vs dumbbell flyes, activation) supply the exercise-to-exercise comparisons. Gentil 2017's review of single- versus multi-joint exercises is the one paper cited that examines long-term adaptations rather than acute activation, and it supports the general case for isolation work, not a lower-pec case.

**So, explicitly, exercise by exercise:**

| Exercise | Evidence basis in the reference list |
| --- | --- |
| Decline dumbbell bench press | Bench-angle **EMG** only |
| Dumbbell chest fly | **EMG** (Solstad) + a general single- vs multi-joint review |
| Chest dip | **None specific.** The claim of "unique stimulation" from vertical pressing is not backed by any dip study |
| Cable crossover | **None** |
| Push-up | A closed-kinetic-chain training study in softball players (definitional use) |
| Incline push-up | **None** |
| Decline push-up | A body-mass-supported measurement study — **which does not measure the decline variant** (see below) |
| Medicine ball push-up | **None** |
| Seated machine fly | **None.** The force-curve argument is reasoning, not a citation |
| Jackhammer pushdown | **None** |

None of these is a hypertrophy outcome comparison. There is no trial in the list comparing decline to flat pressing for chest growth, no trial comparing any two of the ten, and no measurement of pectoral growth at all.

## The regional hypertrophy question specifically

This is the point the article should have had to answer, and the answer is: no cited study measured regional pectoral hypertrophy. The list does contain genuine regional-growth evidence — Ema 2013 ("Inhomogeneous architectural changes of the quadriceps femoris induced by resistance training") and Earp 2015 ("Inhomogeneous Quadriceps Femoris Hypertrophy in Response to Strength and Power Training"). Both are real findings that muscles can grow non-uniformly. Both are about the quadriceps.

That is a legitimate reason to think regional hypertrophy is *possible* in principle. It is not evidence that a decline press grows the lower pectoralis major more than a flat press, and the article does not distinguish the two claims. Combined with its own admission that the region cannot be isolated because it is part of the same muscle, and its own statement that EMG does not predict growth, the honest version of this article's thesis is: *nobody has measured this, so pick what you like and train it hard.* That is roughly what the "Tips" section ends up saying — "exercise selection is mostly up to personal preference and available equipment" — but only after 4,000 words of ranking.

## Mis-citations, and where the numbers don't hold

Several claims have no matching study anywhere in the list. Because the numbered mapping could not be used, these are stated at list level — i.e. the *kind* of study needed simply is not present:

- **Bone density.** "The chest fly machine is said to help boost body mass and bone density." There is no bone-density study, no DXA bone-mineral outcome and no osteoporosis paper in the thirty references.
- **Cancer and type II diabetes.** "Lifting weights is linked with reduced risk of many health-related issues such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and type II diabetes." No epidemiological study of resistance training and either outcome appears in the list. The nearest thing is Kodama 2009, a meta-analysis of **cardiorespiratory fitness** and mortality — aerobic capacity, not lifting.
- **Range of motion and failure proximity.** "Lifting weights through a relatively large range of motion using a load that gets the lifter to or within a few repetitions of muscular failure" is the article's stated hypertrophy recipe. No ROM trial and no proximity-to-failure trial is in the list; the volume claims are properly supported (Schoenfeld 2019, Figueiredo 2018), the ROM and failure claims are not.
- **Push-up percentages.** Suprak 2011 is quoted accurately — a regular push-up supports ~64% of body mass, a kneeling push-up ~49% — but it is used to justify why the **decline** push-up is harder. The numbers given compare standard to *kneeling*, not standard to decline. The measurement is real; it does not support the sentence it sits under.
- **Biressi 2007**, a developmental-biology paper on cellular heterogeneity in vertebrate skeletal muscle development, appears in this list attached to the muscle protein synthesis/breakdown discussion — and appears again in the same role in the companion "Best Chest and Shoulder Workouts" article. It is a recurring irrelevant citation across this batch of listicles.

The metabolism section contains a self-contradiction worth recording. It states that skeletal muscle is ~40% of adult body weight and "accounts for 80% of a person's resting metabolic rate" — a figure that is far too high (muscle is roughly a fifth to a quarter of RMR). Two paragraphs later it gives the correct and much more useful numbers: **~13 kcal/kg/day for muscle versus ~4.5 kcal/kg/day for fat, so gaining 10 kg of muscle — which takes years — raises daily expenditure by about 85 kcal.** Those two claims cannot both be true, and the second is the right one. To their credit, the practical conclusion drawn is the deflationary one: training the chest will not meaningfully change your metabolic rate.

The Yang 2019 push-up study (JAMA Network Open, active adult men) is reported as "men who can do 40 push-ups have fewer cardiovascular diseases compared to men who can't do even 10". That is an association between fitness capacity and later events in a specific occupational cohort. It is not evidence that performing push-ups reduces cardiovascular risk, and the conclusion's promise that readers will "watch your … heart health improve" treats it as if it were.

**Technique errors** suggest the exercise descriptions were not written or checked by a coach. The dumbbell chest fly instructions tell you to "sit on the edge of the decline bench" (copied from the preceding decline press section) and to reverse the movement "when the dumbbells touch your chest" — in a fly they do not. The medicine ball push-up's pro tip is "make sure you do an equal number of reps with each hand on the ball", which does not describe the exercise as instructed. This is the same pattern as the companion chest-and-shoulder article: no named author, and errors a clinician would not make.

**Commercial note:** the article links to Barbell Medicine's hypertrophy programme roundup, training templates and custom coaching. That is ordinary for a blog that sells programming, and the recommendations are not obviously distorted toward the products — but the closing "you'll never have to worry about snake oil salesmen here" is a strong claim to make in an article whose bone-density, cancer and diabetes assertions have no supporting reference.

**Where this sits against the rest of the library.** It is consistent with the pattern already established here: exercise rankings across every source in this collection rest on EMG and moment-arm reasoning rather than measured growth. What is unusual is that this article states the objection itself, cites the paper that makes it, and then publishes the ranking anyway.
