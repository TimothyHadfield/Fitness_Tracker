# Red Meat: The Absolute Risk Is Small, the Saturated Fat Question Is the Real One

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Science of Red Meat and Health" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/the-science-of-red-meat-and-health/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2019-11-20 · 9,306 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Every study linking red meat to colorectal cancer is observational** — prospective cohorts and case-control studies. There are no randomised trials, and there realistically cannot be, given cancer latency. Every effect size below inherits that limitation.
- **The relative risks are real but the absolute risk is small.** IARC puts colorectal cancer risk up about **17% per 100 g/day of red meat** and **18% per 50 g/day of processed meat**. Against a US lifetime risk of ~4.5% — about 39 new cases per 100,000 — that increment is roughly **7 extra cases per 100,000**, taking it to 46.
- **Average intake in North America and Europe (~70 g/day, 490 g/week) already sits inside the guidelines** it is supposedly violating. That single fact reconciles most of the apparent contradiction between the 2019 NutriRECs "keep eating meat" recommendation and the WCRF/NHS limits.
- **The stronger argument is about saturated fat, not meat.** Feigenbaum separates the **lipid hypothesis** (apoB-containing lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic disease — supported by cohorts, Mendelian randomisation, GWAS, familial hypercholesterolaemia and 30+ RCTs) from the **diet-heart hypothesis** (cutting dietary saturated fat lowers those lipoproteins and therefore events — supported, but conditional on baseline intake and on *what replaces it*).
- **What you replace saturated fat with determines whether replacing it helps at all.** Swap for sugar and refined carbohydrate and the benefit disappears — which is how you get null studies and a permanent controversy.
- **Their practical position:** if you are already at ≤10% of calories from saturated fat, ≥25–30 g fibre, adequate protein and a healthy body composition, further trimming red meat is "time wasted on useless detail." If you are above those thresholds — or eating deliberately more meat on a popular high-meat diet — it is not.
- **No product is sold in this article**, unusually for the site. The only self-promotion is links to their own nutrition-science series and podcast.

## Decision 1: Should you cut red meat because of cancer?

**What kind of evidence exists.** The article spends a full section on study design before touching the meat data, which is the right order. Cohort studies follow a defined group forward — the NYU Women's Health Study enrolled **14,727 women aged 34–65** at mammography clinics and later looked at whether red meat intake correlated with colorectal cancer ([Kato 1997](https://doi.org/10.1080/01635589709514588)). Case-control studies work backwards from disease — a UK group matched **579 colorectal cancer cases to 1,996 controls** on age, sex and recruitment date ([Spencer 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10552-010-9569-7)). Cohorts have the advantage that exposure was recorded before disease, which blunts recall bias and reverse causation; case-control designs suit long-latency disease. Neither establishes causation, because both are open to unmeasured confounding, and complex diseases have many confounders available.

**What the evidence says.** WHO commissioned 22 researchers as IARC, who reviewed **800 studies** across more than 12 cancer types. Associations were found for colorectal, pancreatic and prostate cancer; the evidence was judged insufficient for oesophagus, breast, stomach, liver, thyroid, testis, kidney, bladder, ovary, endometrium, brain and blood cancers. Colorectal is the strongest case, and pooled across **11 meta-analyses of 213 studies** the estimate is a **20–30% increase in colorectal cancer risk** with higher red and processed meat intake ([Aykan 2015](https://doi.org/10.4081/oncol.2015.288)). Mechanistic support comes from four compounds implicated in animal and human work: heme iron, heterocyclic amines, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and N-nitroso compounds.

**The consistency figure is the article's own most deflationary number, and it does not dwell on it.** Of the underlying studies, **7 of 14 cohorts and 7 of 15 case-control studies** found positive associations for *unprocessed* red meat and colorectal cancer — roughly half. For *processed* meat the picture is much stronger: 12 of 18 cohorts and 6 of 9 case-control studies. That gap between processed and unprocessed is arguably the most decision-relevant finding in the section, and it is reported in a single sentence.

**Converging supportive evidence.** In EPIC, a review of **386,355 participants** found adherence to the WCRF/AICR cancer-prevention recommendations associated with lower risk of most cancers ([Romaguera 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22592101/)), and a second analysis of the same cohort found the highest-adherence group had lower all-cause, cancer, circulatory and respiratory mortality ([Vergnaud 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23553166/)). Notably, even the NutriRECs paper that provoked the article found diets low in red and processed meat associated with a **10% reduction in cancer incidence and 11% in cancer mortality** — it simply judged that increment not worth the burden of changing what people eat.

**The absolute-risk conversion is the useful part.** Roughly 4.5% lifetime colorectal cancer risk in the US, about 39 new cases per 100,000; a population raising red meat by 100 g/day (or processed by 50 g/day) would expect about 46. Feigenbaum's reading: not a sensible priority at population level given that average intake already falls inside the guidelines — but "it doesn't seem particularly wise to suggest that individuals should consume as much red meat or processed meat as they want to." That distinction between population-level prioritisation and individual advice is well drawn.

## Decision 2: Should you believe the 2019 "keep eating meat" recommendation?

The NutriRECs consortium concluded adults should continue current unprocessed and processed meat consumption — both **weak recommendations on low-certainty evidence** ([Johnston 2019](https://doi.org/10.7326/M19-1621)). The article's diagnosis is methodological rather than tribal.

GRADE was built in the early 2000s largely for pharmaceutical evidence. It initialises certainty as *high* when the evidence base is mostly randomised and *low* when it is mostly observational, then adjusts for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias ([Guyatt 2008](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.39489.470347.AD)). Applied unmodified to nutrition, that starting rule guarantees a low-certainty verdict on any diet–chronic disease question, because the latency of cancer and cardiovascular disease makes long randomised feeding trials impractical and often unethical. Feigenbaum's alternative is to weigh **multiple converging lines of evidence** — consistency across populations plus mechanism — rather than defaulting to the design tier.

His best supporting example: **trans fats were banned in the United States in 2019 without a randomised controlled trial**, on the strength of epidemiology showing about a **23% increase in ASCVD risk per 2% of calories from trans fat** ([Iqbal 2014](https://doi.org/10.12669/pjms.301.4525)). If GRADE's design rule were the whole story, that ban could not have been justified.

The reconciliation he lands on is arithmetic rather than rhetorical. Average US and European red meat intake (~70 g/day, 490 g/week) is already inside the WCRF's 350–500 g/week and matches the NHS's 490 g/week ceiling. Average US saturated fat intake (~11% of calories) is close to the 2015–2020 Dietary Guidelines' <10%. **So a recommendation to "continue current consumption" is, for the average person, not far from a recommendation to follow the guidelines** — and telling people already inside the range to cut further achieves little. The failure of the NutriRECs paper, in his reading, is not the analysis but the absence of any statement of what people should do instead, and the media coverage that turned "no change needed" into "eat more."

## Decision 3: Should you cut saturated fat for your heart?

This is where the article is strongest, because it insists on separating two claims that are routinely merged.

**The lipid hypothesis — very well supported.** ApoB-containing lipoproteins (VLDL, IDL, LDL, chylomicron remnants) are causal in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. The evidence base spans animal models, genetic hypercholesterolaemia, prospective epidemiology, **Mendelian randomisation**, genome-wide association studies and randomised trials ([Ference 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28444290/); [Ference 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30165986/)). The scale, as the article summarises it: **200+ studies, >2 million participants, >20 million person-years and 150,000+ cardiovascular events** showing a dose-dependent, log-linear relationship between cumulative LDL exposure and risk; plus **30+ RCTs with 200,000+ participants and 30,000 events** showing event reduction proportional to the absolute LDL reduction achieved — roughly **20% fewer ASCVD events per 1 mmol/L (38 mg/dL) of LDL lowering**. Mendelian randomisation is the design doing the causal work here, and it is the reason the lipid hypothesis is on far firmer ground than the diet hypothesis built on top of it.

Two clarifications the article makes well: lipoproteins are not cholesterol, they are the particles carrying it, and **non-HDL cholesterol is the best cardiovascular correlate available from a standard lipid panel** without particle or apoB measurement.

**Risk reduction depends on baseline risk, not on the lipid number.** Their worked example: a 45-year-old non-smoking woman with LDL-C 155 and HDL-C 40 has roughly a **1%** ten-year event risk, so a statin's 20–30% relative reduction buys her 0.2–0.3 percentage points — trivial. A 60-year-old male smoker with identical lipids sits at about **11%**, and the same statin takes him to 7–8% — a **number needed to treat of 25–33**. Same LDL, different decision. The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists meta-analysis of **26 trials and 170,000 patients** ([Baigent 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21067804/)) shows benefit at any LDL level, which the article deliberately declines to turn into "statins in the water." On non-cardiovascular claims for statins, an umbrella review found no convincing evidence ([He 2018](https://annals.org/aim/article-abstract/2706169/)).

**The diet-heart hypothesis — supported, but conditional.** Saturated fat raises apoB-containing lipoproteins, shown repeatedly in metabolic ward and controlled feeding studies. The question is what happens on substitution, and the substitution estimates come from **prospective cohort modelling**, not trials — a design label the article omits at exactly the point it matters most. From [Li 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2015.07.055):

- replace **5% of energy** from saturated fat with **PUFA** → about **25%** lower coronary risk
- replace 5% with **MUFA** → about **15%** lower
- replace **10%** with **complex carbohydrate** → about **9%** lower
- replace with **sugar or refined carbohydrate** → **no significant change**

That last line is the whole controversy. As the article puts it, if you wanted to design a study showing saturated fat does not matter, you would compare two groups both eating under 10% of calories from it — say 8% versus 5% — or you would let refined carbohydrate do the replacing. Kris-Etherton's summary, quoted in the article: epidemiology that ignores what replaces saturated fat finds no association, so "the appropriate conclusion is that SFA and refined carbohydrates are equally deleterious on CVD risk" ([Kris-Etherton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjnph-2018-000009)).

The randomised evidence: the Cochrane review of **15 RCTs** found that reducing saturated fat from high intakes to <10% of calories cut cardiovascular events by **17%**, with the size of the event reduction tracking the size of the total cholesterol reduction. Mortality effects were unclear; there was **no evidence of harm** from the reduction with respect to cancer incidence, cancer mortality or blood pressure ([Hooper 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD011737)). And where intake is already low, replacing it with PUFA does little ([Abdelhamid 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012345.pub2)). The benefit is a function of where you start.

**Practical calibration.** A 10-ounce ribeye carries about **28 g of saturated fat** — roughly 10% of a 2,500-calorie day on its own. Average US intake is ~11% of calories; only about 5% of adults are under 7%, and 30–45% are under 10%. And **red meat is not in the top five US sources of saturated fat** — cheese, pizza, grain-based desserts, dairy desserts and chicken dishes are — with the caveat that saturated fat from some dairy sources appears neutral or protective ([O'Sullivan 2013](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2013.301492)).

**Body weight and lipids are messier than expected.** 5–10% weight loss produces roughly **−20% triglycerides, −15% LDL-C and +8–10% HDL-C** ([Nordmann 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16476868/)), and good adherence to more or less any LDL-lowering dietary pattern gives **10 to >15%** LDL reduction ([Chiavaroli 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29807048/)). Set against guideline targets of **30–49%** reduction for intermediate-risk and **>50%** for high-risk primary prevention ([Grundy 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30423391/)), that is an honest admission that diet alone frequently cannot reach the target — and the basis for their argument against leaving treatable residual risk on the table out of statin aversion. Counterweights they include rather than hide: in CARDIA, dyslipidaemia was the *first* cardiovascular risk factor to appear over 20 years of weight gain in young adults, before obesity, hypertension or diabetes ([Paynter 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25911605/)); while in Framingham, 1,666 subjects whose BMI rose over 10 years showed *increased* HDL and *decreased* triglycerides ([Ingelsson 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204219/)). Adiposity contributes to lipid risk; it does not determine it.

## Decision 4: What they actually recommend

Their four criteria, presented as recommendation rather than finding:

1. **Calories** set to reach healthy body fat and muscle mass while supporting activity. Vegetarian and vegan patterns are fine on preference — with the observation that vegans and vegetarians eat roughly **600 and 263 fewer calories per day** than omnivores ([Clarys 2014](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu6031318)), which is doing more of the work in the health outcomes than the absence of meat.
2. **Protein 1.6–3.1 g/kg/day** unless medically contraindicated; lower-middle of that range when gaining or maintaining, middle-upper when losing weight or at risk of anabolic resistance. **Source does not matter at these doses** — plant and animal sources appear equivalent — and only becomes relevant on a protein-restricted diet.
3. **Fibre at least 25–30 g/day** from vegetables, fruit and complex carbohydrate ([Reynolds 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31809-9)), with the suggestion that fibre may mitigate some of the effect of a high-saturated-fat diet ([Wallström 2012](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0031637), a prospective cohort).
4. **Fat mostly unsaturated, saturated fat ≈10% of calories or less**, replaced with PUFA, MUFA or complex carbohydrate by preference. Red meat: **12–18 oz cooked per week is reasonable, "although we feel less strongly about this if the other criteria above are being met."** Processed red meat: under **50 g/day**.

The closing line — if you are already doing everything else, further reduction is "a TWOUD, time wasted on useless detail" — is a rhetorical flourish that lands softer than the analysis preceding it.

## Where the argument overreaches, and what to watch

- **The substitution figures are cohort estimates, not trial results.** An article this careful about design labels the cancer literature meticulously and then presents Li 2015's 25%/15%/9% numbers as "the data suggest," without noting that they come from substitution modelling within a prospective cohort. They are the least-certain numbers in the piece and are presented among the most confident.
- **The processed-meat ceiling is oddly lenient.** Capping processed meat at "less than 50 g/day" is about 350 g/week — well above the WCRF's "little, if any." Fifty grams per day is the *exposure unit at which the 18% risk increase was measured*, not a demonstrated safe ceiling; adopting it as a limit reads as an error of framing.
- **The inconsistency finding is under-weighted.** Half the cohort and case-control studies on unprocessed red meat and colorectal cancer were null. Under the GRADE criteria the article has just explained, that is the "inconsistency" downgrade in action — and it applies to the association he is defending, not only to the paper he is criticising.
- **"Red meat isn't in the top five sources of saturated fat" is a population-average argument** used in proximity to an individual recommendation. It is true of the population and irrelevant to a person eating a 10-oz ribeye most days.
- **Vintage.** This is a 2019 article written in the immediate aftermath of the NutriRECs controversy, and the guidelines it cites (2015–2020 Dietary Guidelines) have since been superseded. The design reasoning is durable; the specific guideline numbers should be checked against current versions.
- **No commercial conflict in this one.** Barbell Medicine sells coaching, templates and supplements, but this article recommends none of them. Given that the same site's exercise listicles carry three product CTAs apiece, that is worth recording.
