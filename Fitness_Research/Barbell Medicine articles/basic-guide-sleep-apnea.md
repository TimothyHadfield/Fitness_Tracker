# Screen for Sleep Apnea Before You Blame Low Testosterone

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "A Basic Guide to Sleep Apnea" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/a-basic-guide-to-sleep-apnea/)) · Austin Baraki · 2022-05-13 · 2,104 words
**Topic:** Health & medicine

## Bottom line
- **The lifting-specific payoff is one paragraph:** men who feel fatigued, are stalling in the gym, and have low measured testosterone are frequently untreated sleep apnea cases. Sleep apnea produces nearly all the same symptoms *and* can itself lower testosterone. Baraki notes that **clinical guidelines recommend against starting testosterone replacement therapy in someone with untreated sleep apnea**, because TRT can worsen it.
- **The screening tool is free and takes a minute.** STOP-BANG: snoring, tiredness, observed apnoea, blood pressure, BMI >35, age >50, neck >40 cm, male sex. One point each; **≥3 means higher risk and warrants formal testing**.
- **Screening ≠ diagnosis.** Confirmation requires in-lab polysomnography (the gold standard, with EEG, ECG, oximetry, respiratory effort, leg movement and muscle tone) or a home sleep test measuring oxygen saturation and breathing effort. Consumer sleep trackers can hint but cannot diagnose.
- **Prevalence is high and diagnosis is low:** an estimated **15–30% of men and 10–15% of women in North America**, and over 900 million people worldwide.
- **The first-line treatments are unglamorous:** weight loss, treating nasal congestion, cutting alcohol and sedatives. A **10% or greater body-weight reduction** is given as the threshold for substantial improvement in apnoea, blood pressure and other markers.

## The mechanism, in their terms
Obstructive sleep apnoea is repeated airway blockage (apnoea) or narrowing (hypopnoea) by the soft tissues surrounding the airway — back of tongue, soft palate, pharyngeal muscles, the region behind the nose, upper neck musculature. Episodes last **10 seconds or longer** and can recur from a handful of times a night to hundreds of times an hour.

Two consequences follow. Oxygen delivery falls, and — the part usually left out of popular accounts — **carbon dioxide accumulates** because the obstruction also blocks full exhalation. Baraki attributes morning headaches specifically to the CO₂ retention rather than to the hypoxia. The arousals that end each episode fragment sleep architecture, so the person cycles back to lighter stages repeatedly and spends less time in deep sleep. The result is a full night in bed that is not restorative.

**One terminological slip.** The article says apnoea commonly occurs during "a period of deep sleep known as Rapid Eye Movement (REM)". REM is not deep sleep in standard sleep-staging — N3 slow-wave sleep is. The underlying point is right and is the interesting one: **REM atonia paralyses the airway musculature too**, which is exactly why obstruction clusters there.

## Risk factors worth knowing
Anatomical: neck circumference **>17 in (43 cm) in men, >16 in (40.5 cm) in women**; deviated septum or other nasal airflow obstruction; recessed jaw, enlarged tongue or tonsils. Metabolic: **BMI above 35** confers significantly increased risk, though the article is explicit that people below it still get the condition. Demographic: incidence peaks in middle age; men are at higher risk than women until menopause. Also family history (you inherit facial anatomy), and alcohol or sedating medications, which both relax the airway tissues and blunt the arousal response that would otherwise restore breathing.

High blood pressure is listed as both a risk marker and a consequence — untreated apnoea causes hypertension — which is why **difficult-to-treat high blood pressure** appears on their list of reasons to get evaluated.

## Why it matters beyond feeling tired
Quality-of-life effects: unrefreshing sleep, nocturia, daytime fatigue, memory problems, erectile dysfunction and reduced libido, depression, and a higher rate of traffic accidents. Moderate-to-severe untreated apnoea increases risk of atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease and stroke, and worsens glycaemic control.

Baraki is careful in the way the brief for this source would predict: he repeatedly says these symptoms have many other causes and should not be self-attributed to apnoea without evaluation. He also gives the TRT workup they actually run — waist circumference, alcohol and opioid use, sleep quantity and quality, apnoea risk — **before** considering testosterone. That is the practical contribution of the article and it is a genuinely counter-cultural position in the lifting audience it is written for.

## Treatment, briefly
Conservative first: weight loss (with physical activity as support), nasal congestion and allergy treatment, minimising alcohol and sedatives. Medical: positive airway pressure devices (CPAP, APAP, BiPAP), mandibular advancement oral appliances, and surgery — septal work, tonsil/adenoid or soft-palate removal, or an implanted hypoglossal nerve stimulator — usually reserved for severe cases where PAP is not tolerated. He declines to make specific recommendations, deferring to the reader's own physician, which is the appropriate limit for a public explainer.

**Where advice outruns evidence.** Nothing here is overstated, but the whole article is stated from clinical authority. Baraki does not claim otherwise, and none of the numbers are contentious — the prevalence estimates, the STOP-BANG cut-point and the 10% weight-loss threshold all match standard sleep-medicine teaching. A reader who wants to check any of them will have to go elsewhere to do it.
