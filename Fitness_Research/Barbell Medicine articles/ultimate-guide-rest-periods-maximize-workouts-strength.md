# Rest 2–5 Minutes for Strength, 2–4 for Size

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "The Ultimate Guide to Rest Periods: Maximize Your Workouts for Strength and Hypertrophy" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/rest-periods-during-training/)) · Nick Occhipinti · 2025-10-10 · 6,314 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **Their recommendation: 2–5 minutes for strength, 2–4 minutes for hypertrophy**, with the top of each range for heavy compounds and the bottom for isolation work. Both numbers are stated before the evidence review, and neither range's *upper* bound is tested by any study they cite.
- The organising principle is worth more than the numbers: **rest exists to let you train hard enough to get the adaptation you want**, and it fails in both directions — too short costs force production and reps, too long costs total volume because sessions run out of clock.
- The mechanism section lands on **PCr depletion plus acidosis** as the main peripheral fatigue drivers. PCr can fall ~80% in a single effort under 45 seconds and takes about **8 minutes** to fully restore ([McMahon & Jenkins 2002](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200232120-00002)) — which they note undercuts the idea that rest is about "topping up ATP", since ATP barely moves.
- The strongest single trial they cite is [Schoenfeld 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001272): 23 men, 8 weeks, **3 minutes beat 1 minute** on biceps, triceps and quad growth, squat 1RM (+15% vs +7.5%) and bench 1RM (+12.7% vs +4%).
- **This clashes with three notes already in this library** — see the section below. In particular, the 2024 network meta-analysis that House of Hypertrophy built a whole video around is absent from this 2025 article, which leans on a 2017 review of six studies instead.

## Why we rest, in their framing
Fatigue is split into central (upstream of the neuromuscular junction — reduced drive to the muscle, mediated by serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, and group III/IV afferent inhibition) and peripheral (at or below the junction — the signal arrives but force output falls). They cite [Zając 2015](https://doi.org/10.1515/hukin-2015-0118) for the central side and are candid that the data there is thin, so the article concentrates on peripheral fatigue.

The bioenergetics are handled carefully. ATP falls very little during resistance training because it is regenerated as fast as it is used, and returns to baseline within 3–5 minutes anyway. PCr is the interesting molecule: it plummets during a hard set and is slow to come back. Glycogenolysis contributes lactate and hydrogen ions, but that pathway peaks around **45 seconds of output**, so it matters for high-rep, drop-set and tempo work and much less for a triple at RPE 8. They then concede the honest point — [Wan 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/emm.2017.194) shows fatigue is multi-causal and hard to partition — before pivoting to the practical claim that fatigue simply scales with load, sets, muscle mass involved and proximity to failure.

## The strength evidence
- **[Jukic 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01344-2)** — 27 studies, 416 mostly-male adults with ≥1 year of training. Cluster sets and rest redistribution produced higher bar velocities, less velocity loss, less lactate and lower RPE than traditional sets. The article is careful that this meta-analysis measured *acute* fatigue markers only: it compared no strength or size outcomes at all.
- **[Grgic 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0788-x)** — the anchor review. 23 studies, 491 subjects, ≥4 weeks, half trained and half untrained. In the trained studies longer rest tended to maximise strength; in untrained subjects rest interval barely mattered.
- **[Robinson 1995](https://doi.org/10.1519/00124278-199511000-00002)** — 33 trained men, 30s vs 90s vs 180s, 5 weeks. Squat 1RM up **7% / 6% / 2%** for 180s / 90s / 30s. The 90-second group got nearly the full benefit at half the time cost, which the article flags but does not carry into its recommendation.
- **[de Salles 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2009.08.002)** — 36 men, 1 vs 3 vs 5 minutes, 16 weeks. Leg press 1RM +22% / +34% / +42%; bench +7% / +13% / +11%. Crucially **volume was not equated**: the 5-minute group did ~40% more volume than the 1-minute group and ~15% more than the 3-minute group, so this is largely a volume result, and the article says so.
- **[Ahtiainen 2005](https://doi.org/10.1519/15604.1)** — 6-month crossover, 2 vs 5 minutes, no difference in strength or size.
- **[Santana 2023](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbme/a/Y9vYkwhHhbzKcKNSG9Ft85s/)** — longer rest consistently produced greater training volumes than <2 minutes. **[González-Hernández 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/14763141.2020.1766102)** — on 3 heavy sets of 5, resting 3 or 5 minutes preserved bar speed on sets 2 and 3 relative to 1 minute, with **no difference between 3 and 5**.

Their synthesis: >2–3 minutes is clearly better than less for trained lifters; whether 3→5 minutes adds anything is unresolved and may cost volume. The one place they extend beyond 5 minutes is meet peaking, where volume is low enough that time is not a constraint — a recommendation, not an evidence claim, and framed as such.

## The hypertrophy evidence
The metabolite hypothesis (shorter rest → more metabolic stress → more growth) gets dismissed on its merits: metabolites accompany every contraction, so they cannot be cleanly separated from mechanical tension, and the article concludes tension does the work with metabolites playing an indirect role "if any at all."

- **[McKendry 2016](https://doi.org/10.1113/EP085647)** — 16 men, 4 sets leg press + 4 sets leg extension at 75% 1RM to failure. Myofibrillar protein synthesis rose **152%** with 5 minutes rest vs **76%** with 1 minute.
- **[Ahtiainen 2005](https://doi.org/10.1519/15604.1)** again, this time for hormones — no differences in lactate, growth hormone, testosterone or cortisol between 2 and 5 minutes over 6 months, and no difference in outcomes either.
- **[Schoenfeld 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001272)** — the real-world trial, described above. The 3-minute group also improved muscular endurance (50% 1RM to failure) by 10% more than the 1-minute group.
- **[Grgic 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2017.1340524)** — 6 studies, mostly untrained men, ~8 weeks; almost all results favoured longer rest. Short conditions were 20–60s, long conditions up to 4 minutes.

They then add three modifiers the evidence does not directly support: that isolation work may do better at 2–3 minutes, that rest requirements are "likely trainable", and that novices need longer rest for bioenergetic reasons. That last one is the weakest link in the article — it is sourced to a review of neural adaptations in **sprint running** ([Ross 2001](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200131060-00002)) and a study of anaerobic and aerobic enzyme activities in **children versus adults** ([Kaczor 2005](https://doi.org/10.1203/01.PDR.0000150799.77094.DE)). Neither compares novice and advanced lifters, and neither measures rest requirements.

## Supersets
The best-sourced section, resting on a 2025 meta-analysis ([Zhang 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02176-8)) rather than a single acute study. Three types, three verdicts: **agonist–agonist** (same muscle back to back) causes more damage and cuts both volume and force production, so it is acceptable for hypertrophy *only if* total volume is maintained and poor for strength; **agonist–antagonist** (bench then row) preserves volume and force because each muscle rests while the other works, making it the best time-saver; **alternate-peripheral** (squat then overhead press) holds up when sets stay at RPE 6–8 but taxes cardiorespiratory capacity enough that it is unsuited to max-strength work.

## Where this clashes with the library
Three existing notes bear on this article, and the disagreements are specific.

1. **The 2024 meta-analysis is missing.** The House of Hypertrophy note *[Rest Between Sets: What the First Meta-Analysis Actually Shows](../House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/rest-periods-new-study-changes-things.md)* covers the first network meta-analysis on inter-set rest and hypertrophy, which found >60s beats ≤60s but with credible intervals wide enough that **every rest band overlapped**, floated ~90 seconds as possibly sufficient, and showed genuine uncertainty about any benefit beyond intermediate durations. A 2025 Barbell Medicine article citing the 2017 six-study review as its hypertrophy evidence, without engaging that analysis, presents its 2–4 minute floor as firmer than the current literature supports.
2. **Ahtiainen 2005 is used without a known confound.** The House of Hypertrophy note flags that in that 2 vs 5 minute study the 2-minute group performed roughly **one extra set per exercise on average**. Barbell Medicine cites it twice — for strength and for hormones — with no mention of it.
3. **The isolation-work floor contradicts Jeff Nippard directly.** The note *[How Long to Rest Between Sets](../Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/rest-between-sets.md)* prescribes **1–2 minutes for isolation** exercises and says going beyond 2 minutes there just slows the session. Barbell Medicine says never below 2 minutes for anything. Both cite the same underlying literature; neither has a study testing the isolation case, so this is two coaching judgments, not two readings of evidence.
4. **They omit rest reduction as a progression variable.** The note *[Shrinking Rest Intervals Week by Week](../House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/decrease-rest-between-sets-overtime-more-hypertrophy.md)* covers two trials in which tapering rest from 2 minutes to 30 seconds produced the same strength and size outcomes as adding load. Barbell Medicine's claim that rest needs are "likely trainable" is the same idea, asserted without those studies or any others.

## Evidence versus recommendation, and what they sell
The evidence supports: longer rest beats very short rest in trained lifters, mostly through volume; 3 minutes beats 1 minute; 3 versus 5 minutes is a wash. The recommendation adds: specific 2–5 and 2–4 minute windows, an exercise-type gradient, and a novice/advanced distinction — none of which has a trial behind it. The article is honest about the volume confound in de Salles and about Jukic measuring no growth outcomes, which is more care than most training content shows. Reference 1 ([Baird 2012](https://doi.org/10.1155/2012/960363), creatine kinase and muscle damage) appears in the list but is never cited in the text. Commercially this is one of their lighter articles: it points to their own podcast episode #275 and to four of their own blog posts, with no template or coaching pitch.
