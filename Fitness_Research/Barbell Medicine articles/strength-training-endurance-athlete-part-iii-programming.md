# Endurance Athletes Lift Too Light: The Case for Load, Rest and Nordics

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Strength Training for the Endurance Athlete Part III: Programming" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/part-3-programming-resistance-training-for-the-endurance-athlete/)) · Derek Miles · 2019-08-19 · 5,445 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **The diagnosis: endurance athletes' lifting programmes prescribe sets and reps but not intensity or rest, so nothing progresses.** The worked comparison is stark — a typical magazine programme with **300 reps across 24 sets and no intensity prescribed at all**, against the author's **85 reps across 14 sets** with intensity on three of four exercises.
- The dosing offered is **4–5 exercises, 3–5 sets, 4–10 reps, >70% 1RM, 2–3 minutes rest**. Two-to-three minutes rest caps an hour's session at roughly 15–20 working sets, which is itself the argument against the high-volume circuit format.
- **Nordic hamstring curls are the one exercise the article says has strong injury-prevention evidence, and it is right.** [van Dyk 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30808663/) — meta-analysis of **8,459 athletes** — found including Nordics halves the rate of hamstring injuries. [Petersen 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21825112/), a cluster-RCT, gives a **number needed to treat of 3** to prevent a *recurrent* hamstring strain. [Goode 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25227125/) adds the caveat that the effect depends on compliance.
- **Autoregulation replaces 1RM testing.** RPE and reps-in-reserve are offered because most coaches will not max-test a runner and because a beginner's limiting factor is skill, not strength. Programme variation comes from changing the *dose* of the same lifts, not the exercises.
- **The clinical contribution is the contraindication section:** RED-S and the Female Athlete Triad. An athlete with fewer than 9 menses in 12 months, a stress fracture history, or a BMI below 17.5 should be medically evaluated before any additional training stress is added; on the De Souza risk-stratification, **>5 points means resistance training is contraindicated until energy balance is restored.**
- Unusually for this source, the article sells nothing. It advocates barbells — with a self-aware note about who is writing — and links no product.

## The core argument

Endurance athletes are handed strength programmes built on the wrong variable. Sets and reps are specified; intensity and rest almost never are. Without those two the athlete has no way of knowing how hard to work or whether they are progressing, and the default becomes a high-rep circuit that functions as more conditioning.

The intensity floor given is **>70% 1RM**, and the rest floor **2–3 minutes**, sourced to two dose-response reviews, [Lesinski 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26851290/) and [Borde 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26420238/). **Flag: neither review is in endurance athletes.** Lesinski is a meta-analysis of resistance training dose-response in *youth athletes*; Borde is in *healthy older adults*. The recommendations are a reasonable extrapolation and the article presents them as established guidelines for this population without naming the gap.

The rest-period argument has a nice second-order consequence the article draws out: if you rest 2–3 minutes, you cannot run a one-exercise-per-muscle-group circuit inside an hour, so the programme is forced toward a few multi-joint lifts loaded hard. Since no endurance sport is a single-muscle sport, that is the right shape anyway.

**Flag — a misattributed citation.** The claim that "the meta-analysis from Kiely *et al* recommends 12–25 sets per session" is linked directly to [Kiely 2018, *Periodization Theory: Confronting an Inconvenient Truth*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29189930/). That paper is a critical essay arguing that periodization models rest on weak foundations. It is not a meta-analysis and it does not issue set-count dosing. This is the article's only clear citation error, and because this article's marker mapping is verified, the attribution is unambiguous.

## Autoregulation instead of maxing out

The practical problem: prescribing 70% 1RM requires knowing 1RM, and most coaches will not max-test a runner — reasonably, since a novice's squat is limited by skill before it is limited by strength. Two proxies are offered.

**RPE**, a 0–10 scale, with >6/10 as the lower limit of useful loading. The stated advantages are real: a subjective rating absorbs sleep, life stress and yesterday's track session automatically, so a hard interval day and a well-rested day produce different loads without any explicit planning. The learning curve is acknowledged — early ratings vary because the stimulus is novel and the athlete does not yet know what hard is — with the good coaching rule that **what an athlete rates 8/10 is their 8/10, and the coach should accept it**, with the definitional constraint that a set rated 8 should not be a failed set.

**Flag:** the RPE section is sourced to [Haddad 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29163016/), which validates *session*-RPE — Foster's whole-session training-load monitoring method — not set-level RPE as a proxy for %1RM. These are different constructs and the article treats them as one. The set-level tool it actually describes is [Zourdos 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/), the RIR-based RPE scale, which is cited correctly a paragraph later.

**Reps in reserve** is offered as the alternative framing: work until you estimate you are 2 reps from failure. The author's note is that this often shows athletes they can move more weight than they believed — which is the actual intervention here, since the problem being solved is under-loading.

The pay-off is *equifinality*: vary the programme by changing dose, not exercises. Same three lifts, different prescriptions — 5×5 @ RPE 8, or 4–5 sets to 2 RIR, or fixed weight for as many reps as it takes to reach RPE 8. Within a single exercise, mixing RPE 7 and RPE 8 sets forces the athlete to work out what the difference feels like, which is how they learn to progress load at all.

## The 6 × 400 m analogy

The best piece of coaching writing in the article. A 50-second 400 m runner doing 6 × 400 at 80% runs 62–63 second repeats on 3 minutes rest — a workout every runner accepts as hard. Cut the rest to 30 seconds and it stops being a sprint session and becomes a 1.5-mile run. **Endurance athletes already understand that rest determines what the session is; they just don't apply it to the weight room.** The analogous squat session is 6 × 4 at RPE 8 with 3 minutes rest — about 80 lb for a 100 lb 1RM — and if the athlete is ready to go again in under a minute, either the load is too light or their technique is poor enough to be depressing the estimated max.

This feeds the article's line on "non-responders": failure to progress in load is far more likely improper dosing than individual non-response.

## The sample programme

Two sessions per week, A/B:

**Day A** — Back squat 1×6 @ RPE 6, 1×6 @ 7, 2×6 @ 8 · single-leg deadlift 3×7 @ 7 · calf raises 3×10 @ 8 · Nordic hamstring 4×6 · Romanian deadlift 3×6 @ 7
**Day B** — Deadlift 1×5 @ 6, 1×5 @ 7, 2×5 @ 8 · split squat 3×7 @ 7 · calf raises 3×10 @ 8 · Nordic hamstring 4×6 · lateral lunge 3×6 @ 7

Two design notes are worth keeping. First, the single-leg exercise is included **"not because single-leg exercises have any specific advantage over double-leg, but because the sport of running is a series of single leg exercises"** — a specificity argument, stated without the usual balance-and-stability mythology, and with the honest downside that load increments on unilateral work are coarse (15 lb to 20 lb is a 33% jump). Second, the last slot is deliberately open for an individual concern — Copenhagen adductor work for an athlete with a history of adductor strains, citing [Harøy 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29891614/), a cluster-randomised trial in male footballers.

Power work is treated as optional and downstream of a strength base ([Behm 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.00423/full) — a review in *youth*, again extrapolated). The closing instruction is that athletes need not chase DOMS, compromise their endurance training, or specialise in powerlifting.

## Nordics: the one strongly-evidenced exercise

The article opens this section by saying there are very few exercises we can confidently say reduce injury risk, and that Nordics are one of them. That framing is correct and rare. The supporting numbers are the strongest in the piece: **halved hamstring injury rates across 8,459 athletes**, an **NNT of 3** for preventing recurrence in those with a prior strain, protocols of at least six weeks, and a compliance-dependent effect that reverses when the programme stops.

The prescription attached to it is a genuine clinical judgement rather than a general rule: include Nordics specifically when an athlete is starting a training cycle, coming off injury, or about to increase speed work — because high-speed running volume raises hamstring injury risk ([Duhig 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27288515/)) and because inflection points matter, with most NFL hamstring injuries clustering at the start of the season ([Elliott 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21335347/)).

## Contraindications — the reason this source exists

The article stops and says that an athlete showing signs of the Female Athlete Triad or RED-S **should not** have additional training stress added and needs medical evaluation. The triad criteria given are low energy availability, low bone mineral density and menstrual disturbance; the screening flags are fewer than 9 menses in the previous 12 months, a stress fracture history, or BMI <17.5 kg/m². The De Souza risk stratification is reproduced: 0–1 points full clearance, 2–5 provisional or limited, **>5 points restricted from training and competition, with resistance training contraindicated until energy balance is restored.** RED-S is named as the broader syndrome covering male athletes too.

This is the clinical content that distinguishes Barbell Medicine from the rest of this library. It is also a case where "do not add load" is the recommendation, which is not what a strength-training company's incentives point toward.

## Where it sits against the rest of the library

The >70% 1RM floor sits against the low-load hypertrophy literature held elsewhere here — [Schoenfeld's 2017 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/) finding similar growth from low and high loads taken to failure. There is no real contradiction: the goal here is strength and injury reduction, not hypertrophy, and load specificity is better established for strength than for size. But the article states the intensity requirement as a general one without that qualification, and the endurance outcomes that actually matter — running economy, injury rate — have not been shown to require >70% specifically.
