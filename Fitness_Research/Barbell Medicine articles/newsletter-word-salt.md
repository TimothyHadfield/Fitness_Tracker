# Sodium: The 3–6 g/day Sweet Spot, and Why Hard Trainees May Need More Salt

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "From the Newsletter: A Word on Salt" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/4196-2/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2017-09-14 · 712 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Salt is not sodium.** The article's conversion is that 1 g of salt contains 400 mg sodium — so the US average intake of **~3,400 mg sodium/day** corresponds to roughly **7–10 g of salt**.
- Official targets are lower than that: **WHO recommends 2,000–2,400 mg sodium/day**; the **AHA suggests under 2,000 mg**.
- The **Institute of Medicine's 2013 review** found the evidence for going below 2,300 mg/day "insufficient and inconsistent" for cardiovascular or all-cause mortality benefit in the general US population, with some studies suggesting harm from too little.
- The position taken: a **sweet spot around 3–6 g/day**, the sodium–event association confined to hypertensive people, the elderly and very high consumers, and **a paradoxically higher event rate below 3 g/day**.
- **Athletes are a separate case.** Sodium losses from vigorous exercise can exceed **17 g/day**, and sports drinks dilute rather than replace — he cites two high-school football players who died of hyponatremia after drinking large volumes of Gatorade.
- The ACSM figure the article endorses as a starting point: **500–700 mg sodium per litre of fluid, pre- and post-workout**.

## Where the argument is strong
The core move is legitimate: it does not dispute that sodium raises blood pressure, only that the *population-wide* target is supported by hard outcome data — and the IOM is a mainstream body reaching that conclusion. The J-shaped pattern is a live disagreement in cardiovascular epidemiology, not a fringe reading.

The claim about trainees carries the most usable content. Feigenbaum argues that people who train seriously and eat mostly minimally processed food start from a lower sodium baseline, lose more through sweat, and often become **asymptomatically hyponatraemic** after training in a way that may impair later performance. His preferred replacement is food — bouillon, or salting meals — not pills or sports drinks.

## Where it outruns its evidence
**It cites nothing**: no reference list, no links, and two block quotations attributed only to "the IOM review" and "an editorial comment on the review". Every number — 400 mg sodium per gram of salt, 17 g/day sweat losses, the two football deaths — is unverifiable from the article. Two claims deserve caution:

- **"Sea salt has about 10% as much sodium as table salt"** is almost certainly wrong as stated. By mass both are predominantly sodium chloride; crystal size and density change sodium per *teaspoon*, not per gram. He hedges with "many different formulations", but the headline figure misleads.
- **"Most who train seriously will become hyponatremic after training"** is a prevalence claim with no citation, and asymptomatic post-exercise sodium shifts are often dilutional and transient. The performance consequence is hedged as "may"; the prevalence claim is not.

## What it is careful about
The exclusions are explicit and come *before* the recommendation: **salt-sensitive hypertensives, congestive heart failure, and other cardiovascular disease** are told the argument does not apply to them. That is what separates this from the usual "salt panic is a myth" genre.

**Evidence versus recommendation:** the evidence claim is that low-sodium targets lack outcome support. The recommendation — that a healthy, non-hypertensive person eating minimally processed food should *add* salt to most meals and to pre/post-workout nutrition — extrapolates from an absence of evidence for restriction, which is not evidence for supplementation. He labels it "my main takeaway", which is the right framing. No product is sold here; the Barbell Medicine supplement line is not mentioned.
