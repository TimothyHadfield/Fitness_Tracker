# Sodium: The 3–6 g/day Sweet Spot, and Why Hard Trainees May Need More Salt

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "From the Newsletter: A Word on Salt" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/4196-2/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2017-09-14 · 712 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Salt is not sodium.** The article's conversion is that 1 g of salt contains 400 mg sodium — so the US average intake of **~3,400 mg sodium/day** corresponds to roughly **7–10 g of salt**.
- Official targets are lower than that: **WHO recommends 2,000–2,400 mg sodium/day**; the **AHA suggests under 2,000 mg**.
- The **Institute of Medicine's 2013 review** concluded the evidence for going below 2,300 mg/day was "insufficient and inconsistent" for cardiovascular or all-cause mortality benefit in the general US population — and some reviewed studies suggested harm from too little.
- The position taken: a **sweet spot around 3–6 g/day**, with a strong sodium–blood-pressure–event association confined to hypertensive people, the elderly, and very high consumers, and **a paradoxically higher event rate below 3 g/day**.
- **Athletes are a separate case.** Sodium losses from vigorous exercise can exceed **17 g/day**, and trying to replace that with sports drinks dilutes rather than replaces — the article cites two high-school football players who died of hyponatremia after drinking large volumes of Gatorade.
- The ACSM figure the article endorses as a starting point: **500–700 mg sodium per litre of fluid, pre- and post-workout**.

## Where the argument is strong
The core move is legitimate and unusually well-sourced for a newsletter: it does not dispute that sodium raises blood pressure, it disputes that the *population-wide* target is supported by hard outcome data. The IOM review it quotes is a real, mainstream body reaching exactly that conclusion, and the J-shaped pattern it describes — risk rising at both very low and very high intakes — is a genuine and still-live disagreement in cardiovascular epidemiology rather than a fringe reading.

The practical claim about trainees is the part with the most usable content. Feigenbaum's argument is that people who train seriously and eat mostly minimally processed food start from a lower sodium baseline, then lose more through sweat, and that many become **asymptomatically hyponatraemic** after training in a way that may impair subsequent performance. His preferred replacement method is food, not pills or sports drinks — bouillon, or simply salting meals — for anyone training once a day.

## Where it outruns its evidence
This is a newsletter piece and **it cites nothing**: no reference list, no links, and two block quotations attributed only to "the IOM review" and "an editorial comment on the review." Every number in it — 400 mg sodium per gram of salt, 17 g/day sweat losses, the two football deaths — is unverifiable from the article itself. Two specific claims deserve caution:

- **"Sea salt has about 10% as much sodium as table salt"** is almost certainly wrong as stated. By mass, sea salt and table salt are both predominantly sodium chloride; the difference is crystal size and density, which changes sodium per *teaspoon*, not per gram. The article hedges with "many different formulations," but the headline figure is misleading.
- **"Most who train seriously will become hyponatremic after training"** is a strong prevalence claim with no citation, and asymptomatic post-exercise sodium shifts are often dilutional and transient. The performance consequence is offered as "may," which is appropriately hedged; the prevalence claim is not.

## What it is careful about
It carves out the exclusions explicitly: **salt-sensitive hypertensives, people with congestive heart failure, and other cardiovascular disease** are told the argument does not apply to them. That exclusion is the difference between this and the usual "salt panic is a myth" content, and it is stated before the recommendation rather than buried after it.

**Evidence versus recommendation:** the evidence claim is that low-sodium targets lack outcome support. The recommendation — that a healthy, non-hypertensive person eating minimally processed food should *add* table salt to most meals and to pre/post-workout nutrition — is clinical judgement extrapolated from an absence of evidence for restriction, which is not the same as evidence for supplementation. The article marks it as "my main takeaway," which is the right framing.

No product is sold in this piece; Barbell Medicine's supplement line is not mentioned.
