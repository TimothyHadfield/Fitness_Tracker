# Training at Home: Pick the Variation That Gets You Near Failure, Not the Equipment

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "How-to Exercise at Home" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/how-to-exercise-at-home/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2020-03-24 · 3,072 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **The organising rule is exercise selection, not equipment.** With no weights, difficulty is manipulated by changing range of motion, tempo, adding a pause, going unilateral, adding a plyometric concentric, or loading a backpack. The target is a variation that reaches the prescribed effort in the prescribed rep range — nothing else about the exercise matters.
- **The prescription:** three exercise slots at **8–12, 12–15 and 15–25 reps**, each taken to **RPE 8 (2–3 reps in reserve)**. Four-week block, **3 strength days and 3 conditioning days**, strength on non-consecutive days. Progress by adding **1–5% in reps or load per week**, holding exercise, ROM, tempo, rest and target RPE constant.
- **Honest about its own ceiling:** the article states plainly that this programme is **not designed to maximise 1RM strength, hypertrophy or athletic development**, and that an experienced lifter will not improve their squat 1RM without a barbell. That framing is rare in home-workout content.
- **The health case, with numbers:** physical inactivity is the fourth leading global risk factor for death (WHO 2009). About **20% of US adults report** meeting activity guidelines; **under 10% actually do** when measured by accelerometer (Zenko 2019). An extra **2,000 steps/day** associates with ~10% lower cardiovascular event risk and ~25% lower dysglycaemia risk (Yates 2014, Ponsonby 2011) — **but both of those cohorts were people already at metabolic risk.**
- **Strength training 2–3×/week associates with 23% lower all-cause mortality versus aerobic exercise alone** (Dankel 2016). Observational, dose-dependent, correctly labelled as an association by the article.
- **This is a free abridged version of a product they sell.** The paid At-Home Training Template is linked four times, and the free version explicitly omits the RPE instruction and exercise-difficulty charts that make the method usable.

## The evidence in the introduction, checked

**Inactivity prevalence.** Zenko 2019 measured adherence to the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines by accelerometer and found under 10% of adults meeting them, against the ~20% who self-report. The gap between reported and measured activity is the useful finding, and the citation fits it exactly.

**The step-count claims.** Both need a population caveat the article does not give. **Yates 2014** is a cohort analysis nested in the **NAVIGATOR** trial — participants with **impaired glucose tolerance**, not the general population. **Ponsonby 2011** is the AusDiab study, on objectively measured activity and **incident dysglycaemia**. So "walking 2,000 more steps per day lowers cardiovascular risk 10% and blood sugar problems 25%" is drawn from two studies of people already on a metabolic disease trajectory. The direction is almost certainly right and the effect may well be larger in higher-risk people, but **the figures are being generalised beyond the populations that produced them**, and both are observational.

**The 23% mortality figure.** Dankel 2016 is a prospective cohort in a national US sample, reporting a dose-dependent association between muscle-strengthening activity and all-cause mortality. The article says "associated with," which is correct. **No trial has randomised people to resistance training and measured mortality** — this is one of the strongest observational signals in exercise epidemiology, and it remains observational.

**Detraining.** McMaster 2013 supports the claim that strength is largely retained over short layoffs (<3 weeks). Worth noting the population: a **systematic review in elite rugby union, rugby league and American football players**. Applying its retention rates to "an experienced individual" is a reasonable but unmarked extrapolation.

**Individual variation.** Ahtiainen 2016 documented the wide spread in strength and mass responses to identical resistance training across men and women of different ages. Used correctly, to justify the expectation that progression rates will differ.

## The programme, and what makes it work

The genuinely transferable idea is the **exercise-selection algorithm**. Instead of prescribing movements, the article prescribes an *effort target within a rep range* and lists the levers for reaching it:

- add external load (a backpack with books or water bottles)
- go unilateral (bodyweight squat → split squat → single-leg squat)
- add a pause at a mechanically disadvantageous position
- extend the range of motion
- slow the tempo
- add a plyometric concentric

The worked example runs the ladder explicitly: bodyweight squats too easy at 12 reps → load a pack → if unavailable, split squat → single-leg squat → single-leg squat with a two-count pause. Regressions get the same treatment — squat to a chair, push-up off a countertop.

The **warm-up protocol** is a small, useful piece of structure: multiple sets of the same exercise at gradually rising difficulty, 2–3 minutes rest, working up to the target RPE set. Two worked versions are given, one loaded and one bodyweight-only. Overshooting or needing an extra set is explicitly declared fine.

**Conditioning** is deliberately unprescriptive: both HIIT and LISS, modality by preference and access, "the type of conditioning an individual performs is not really important from a health or non-specific performance standpoint." The talk-test RPE scale is the operational tool — RPE 6 full sentences, RPE 7 short sentences, RPE 8 cannot speak comfortably, RPE 9 near max, RPE 10 all-out. **Adherence is stated as more important than the exact prescription**, twice.

## Where it is loose

**An internal inconsistency.** The exercise-selection section says to get **"within about 5 repetitions of muscular failure"**; the programme section says the slots should reach **"within 2-3 reps of failure (e.g. RPE 8)"**. Those are different instructions for the same sets and the article does not reconcile them.

**The hypertrophy model is asserted.** "There are three major determinants for muscular hypertrophy: 1) motor unit recruitment, 2) range of motion, and 3) volume of exercise." No citation, and it is a house simplification — motor unit recruitment is an acute measure rather than a growth outcome, and the standard framing elsewhere runs through mechanical tension with recruitment as one input. **Flagged:** stated as settled fact, cited to nothing.

**RPE accuracy is asserted too.** "Our experience and the current research suggest that most individuals can effectively choose their exercises, repetitions, and/or loads to match a targeted level of exertion within a few weeks." No research is named for this, and RPE accuracy in novices is the shakiest link in an entirely RPE-driven programme. This is **"we recommend," presented with the confidence of "the evidence shows."**

**A note on completeness.** The article says "We recommend using the following chart to help determine your RPE" and "we can provide some general guidance in exercise selection... in the pictures below," but the charts and the exercise-difficulty images are the substance being referred to. Anyone working from the text alone is missing the operating manual.

## The commercial shape, stated plainly

This is a **free, cut-down version of the paid At-Home Training Template**, which is linked four separate times. The article says so directly: the full version has two 8-week templates, a menu of exercise options, and "a more thorough discussion of RPE and a step-by-step guide on how to apply it." **The free article prescribes an RPE-driven method and then points to the paid product for the RPE instruction.**

It closes with a funnel to their other paid templates — Beginner, Strength I, Powerbuilding I, Hypertrophy I, General Strength and Conditioning I — plus a sponsored-content link to a home-gym article on a property-listings blog.

None of this makes the advice wrong. The exercise-selection logic is sound and freely usable, the honesty about the programme's ceiling is real, and the public-health framing is not a sales pitch. But the article is a product page with a good article attached, and it is dated **24 March 2020** — the first week of widespread lockdowns, which is why it exists.
