# To Be A Beast: Feigenbaum's 2012 Macro Template, and Which Parts He Later Abandoned

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "To Be A Beast" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/584-2/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2012-07-29 (updated 2015-01-26) · 8,665 words
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- This is Barbell Medicine's most-read early post and it is **entirely uncited by the author's own deliberate choice** — he states up front that a full citation list would have been long enough to stop him writing it. Everything below is clinical and coaching opinion offered as such, from a decade before the company's evidence-first house style.
- The core argument is a good one and has aged well: **nutrition should be programmed and adjusted the way training is** — measure your current intake before changing it, then move it in small increments (10–30 g carbohydrate, 4–10 g fat) rather than 500 kcal jumps, using weight, waist and training performance as the feedback signals.
- The **specific numbers have not aged well**. The muscle-gain template for a 200 lb male is 16.9 kcal/lb (3,380 kcal) at 220 g protein / 400 g carbohydrate / 100 g fat — that protein figure is ~2.4 g/kg, materially above the 1.6 g/kg Barbell Medicine now recommends, and above what the same article argues is prudent.
- **The best section is the "metabolic damage" one**, and it is the only place the clinician shows through: he sets out a proper differential — adaptive reduction in expenditure, reduced training novelty, post-competition binge eating, disordered eating, hypothyroidism, medication effects, and plain non-compliance — and argues the diagnosis of choice is almost always the last one. That framing is right and still useful. The numbers he attaches to it (BMR falls ~30 kcal per kg of body weight lost) come with no source.
- **Superseded, and worth marking**: the recommendation to cap protein on ammonia-toxicity grounds; GOMAD as standard beginner advice; expecting 20 lb of bodyweight in two months; the aside about wheat and gluten threatening "gut health, optimal metabolic function, and inflammatory processes"; and the claim that women and older people are inherently more carbohydrate-sensitive.

## Decision 1: should you count macros at all?

His answer is yes for anyone past rank novice, and the argument by analogy is the strongest thing in the article. A coach does not teach the squat and walk away; they set loading, sets and reps, then modify systematically against recorded data. Refusing to do the same with food, he argues, is an inconsistency people accept only because tracking food feels tedious.

He concedes the counter-case honestly: eyeballing portions works for some people in the short term, and "eat a bit less" is itself a form of measurement, just an imprecise one. His position is that a point arrives — gaining weight without excessive fat gain, or losing fat while holding strength — where the imprecision becomes the limiting factor.

**The first instruction is the useful one:** track your intake for a week *without changing it*, to establish where your maintenance actually sits and how you respond there. Most of the article's later adjustment logic depends on having that baseline, and most people skip it.

**Flagged as overreach:** he argues that macronutrients matter *more* than calories, on the grounds that macros determine calories while calories do not determine macros, and that protein and fibre carry a higher thermic effect. The mechanism is real; the magnitude is not. Differences in the thermic effect of food between isocaloric diets amount to a few percent of daily expenditure, which does not support "macros are more important than calories" for weight change. He is explicit that this is his bias, which is more than most would say.

## Decision 2: where do you start, numerically?

The starting multipliers, stated as a rough guide and nothing more:

| Sex | Goal | kcal/lb | Protein g/lb | Carb g/lb | Fat g/lb |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Male | Recomposition | 12.75 | 1.15 | 1.25 | 0.35 |
| Male | Fat loss | 11.43 | 1.25 | 1.0 | 0.27 |
| Male | Muscle gain | 16.9 | 1.1 | 2.0 | 0.5 |
| Female | Recomposition | 12.62 | 1.1 | 1.2 | 0.38 |
| Female | Fat loss | 11.35 | 1.15 | 0.9 | 0.35 |
| Female | Muscle gain | 14.9 | 1.0 | 1.6 | 0.5 |

Worked out, a 200 lb male gets 2,550 kcal for recomposition, 2,286 for fat loss and 3,380 for muscle gain; a 150 lb female gets 1,893 / 1,709 / 2,235. He caveats the table twice — it ignores dietary history, age, height, training status and preference, and he says he does not use the multipliers on their own in practice.

Two things to notice. First, protein at 1.1–1.25 g/lb is **2.4–2.8 g/kg**, well above the 1.6 g/kg that Barbell Medicine's current protein articles recommend and above the point where the dose-response for hypertrophy flattens. Second, the same article then argues *against* higher protein intakes on safety grounds. The template and the reasoning do not agree with each other.

Supporting habits: 4–5 meals a day, 3 to 5 hours apart; **25–35% of daily carbohydrate in the meals immediately before and after training**, with those meals kept low in fat to avoid slowing gastric transit. Meal frequency and timing are described as secondary parameters, which is the right ranking, though the specific windows are asserted rather than argued.

## Decision 3: which case are you?

**Case 1 — the underweight novice, unmeasured.** Johnny, 23, 5'10", 160 lb, starting a novice linear progression. Recommendation is **GOMAD** (a gallon of whole milk daily: 128 g protein, 192 g carbohydrate, 128 g fat) on top of three meals of a palm-sized protein portion, a fist of starch, vegetables or fruit and a serving of fat — roughly 278 g protein, 400 g carbohydrate, 190 g fat, **over 4,400 kcal/day**. Duration 6–8 weeks, then reassess and start measuring. If weight stalls, add a couple of tablespoons of nut butter to a shake (200–600 kcal).

He is direct that fat gain will happen and that this is acceptable during a short novice phase, and that a rank novice may need to add 20 lb in two months while the squat rises 130+ lb. **This is the most dated advice in the article.** The 20 lb figure is asserted with no evidence, the strength claim is a linear-progression expectation rather than a measured result, and GOMAD as a default for a healthy 160 lb adult has been broadly abandoned, including at Barbell Medicine. He does add a useful escape clause: start measuring earlier if the person is unhappy with their composition or if weight is not moving as predicted.

**Case 2 — the novice, measured.** Johnny is now 180 lb at ~20% body fat, squatting 255×5×3, and worried about fat gain. Intake moves from 280/400/190 (4,200–4,800 kcal) to **220/500/100, just under 3,800 kcal** — protein and fat down, carbohydrate up, calories down. Adjustment rules: lost more than a pound, add 20 g carbohydrate and 10 g fat; gained two pounds, hold and reassess next week; keep protein at 25–35% of calories and bump by 25 g when it drifts toward the bottom of that band.

Two positions he argues at length here. He is a **carbohydrate advocate for lifters** — from personal experience of two low-carb Paleo years and from client outcomes — and he prefers starting carbohydrate high specifically so there is room to cut it later. And he endorses **IIFYM with two caveats**: a fibre minimum and roughly 80% of intake from single-ingredient foods, justified on habit formation, food-reward and micronutrient grounds rather than on any metabolic claim. That is a reasonable framing of a contested position.

**The gluten aside is the weakest paragraph in the article.** He grants a "potential (in some) for wheat and gluten-containing products to present a significant threat to gut health, optimal metabolic function, and inflammatory processes," recommends restricting starch sources for people who feel sensitive, then undercuts himself by observing that most self-diagnosed gluten allergies are really large-volume gluten allergies. This is 2012 nutrition-blog material with no support, and it is not a position Barbell Medicine holds now.

**The protein passage that has since reversed.** He separates the right question ("how much is optimal?") from the wrong one ("how much do I need to avoid deficiency?"), correctly notes that positive nitrogen balance tells you nothing about whether muscle is being accrued, and correctly identifies tracer studies as the standard of evidence. He then concludes that **because ammonia is a waste product of protein metabolism and chronically high ammonia can damage tissue, it is "inadvisable and unnecessary" to recommend larger protein intakes.** He is explicit that whether high dietary protein actually raises ammonia to a harmful level in healthy people is unknown. Recommending against something on the strength of an unquantified theoretical mechanism is the clearest case in this article of a conclusion running past its evidence — and it contradicts his own muscle-gain template on the same page.

**Case 3 — the "fluffy" novice or intermediate.** Jim, 34, 6'0", 240 lb, 35% body fat. Start at **13 kcal/lb (3,100 kcal)** at 275 g protein / 300 g carbohydrate / 85 g fat, with **30–40% of daily carbohydrate around training** and under 20 g fat in those meals, on the assumption that a man at that body fat is not especially insulin sensitive.

Conditioning, one of two options: **HIIT** — 5-minute warm-up, 20 s on / 140 s off × 7 rounds, 10-minute cool-down, on a prowler or rower rather than running, twice a week and capped at three times during a strength phase; or **fasted LISS**, a 30-minute walk after coffee before breakfast, 5–6 days a week. On the second he is refreshingly candid: he knows the fasted-versus-fed distinction does not do what tradition claims, and recommends it anyway on adherence grounds — getting out of bed for a walk predicts complying with the diet. That is a coaching argument, honestly labelled.

Monitoring: weekly full-body photos in consistent lighting, waist measured at the umbilicus, weight, and training progression. Expect 2–4 lb lost in the first two weeks; if not, hold one more week, then remove 15 g carbohydrate and 5 g fat across all days. When the novice progression stalls (8–12 weeks in), reset the squat 10% (295 → 265×5×3), make the second weekly squat session 80% of the first, and introduce **carbohydrate cycling**: high days 250/325/75, moderate 265/250/85, low 275/175/90, with the high days on the heaviest or highest-volume sessions and ideally two or three days apart.

He predicts Jim at 210–220 lb in the teens for body fat with a mid-to-upper-300s squat for reps at six months. This is a coaching projection presented as an expectation, not a finding.

## Decision 4: is your metabolism broken?

This is the section to read if you read only one, because the reasoning is clinical rather than anecdotal.

His definition of the folk concept: a reduction in basal metabolic rate secondary to restricted intake or training adaptation, sufficient to halt weight loss or cause gain without extra calories. His position: the evidence supports only a reduction in BMR proportional to the mass lost, **on the order of 30 kcal per kilogram of body weight lost** — a specific figure with no source attached.

The mechanism discussion is a genuine detour into uncoupling proteins: he explains that increasing them makes a person *less* calorically efficient (which is how DNP and salicylate toxicity work, with hyperthermia, renal injury and respiratory depression), and that the proposed mechanism for a diet- or endurance-induced slowdown runs the other way, via fewer uncoupling proteins. He does not overclaim it — "the mechanisms at play aren't perfectly worked out."

**The line that carries the section:** there is no evidence people stop losing weight because their intake is too low, and he notes drily that world hunger remains a thing. He then works the standard figure-competitor case — 16 weeks of prep, last 10 lb won't move, calories very low, cardio extreme, rapid regain afterwards "without eating more" — and shows every element is predicted by ordinary physiology plus behaviour: adaptive reduction in expenditure, improved movement efficiency as training stops being novel, and post-show intake well above pre-prep levels.

The clinical honesty is in what he adds around it. He explicitly names disordered eating and psychological problems as things figure prep can mask, encourage or reward. He names hypothyroidism and medication effects as real causes requiring workup. And he is candid that under-reporting and non-compliance are common enough that he sometimes has to cut a client's macros against his own judgement, because he cannot prove the food log is wrong.

**He also names the commercial incentive**, in one sharp footnote: imagine being told by someone you trust that you can lose weight while eating *more*, and consider how attractive that is. He says coaches use "metabolic damage" as a marketing device. That is an accusation, but it is fairly reasoned and he supplies the mechanism by which the claim sells.

**Flagged:** stating that metabolic damage has "never appeared in the literature as a case report" and has no proposed plausible mechanism is stronger than the state of the evidence, particularly given that adaptive thermogenesis beyond that predicted by mass loss is a live research question. The conclusion — that excess intake is a far more likely explanation than an undocumented pathology — is well supported. The absolute version of it is not.

## What this is and is not

It is a **coaching template with a rationale**, written by a physician-in-training who was then a competitive raw powerlifter, before Barbell Medicine existed as a company with a research posture. Its enduring contributions are the method (measure, adjust in small steps, use multiple feedback signals, be suspicious of your own food log) and the metabolic-damage differential. Its numbers, its protein reasoning, its gluten aside and its GOMAD advice are all superseded, in several cases by Barbell Medicine's own later material, to which the article itself links.

**Commercial content:** an Amazon link to *Starting Strength*, links to Barbell Medicine's forum and to two of their own articles on protein and conditioning. There is no product being sold here — this predates the supplement line — so the usual disclosure is a light one.

## References

**None. This article cites nothing at all, by the author's explicit choice.** He states in the opening that "given the format of this article, I won't be providing peer-reviewed literature because the citation list would be extremely long," and says references would be necessary in a future book. The only named source in the entire piece is an unreferenced attribution to Dr Jeff Volek for the figure that training raises daily expenditure by roughly 100 kcal immediately. Every quantitative claim — the calorie and macronutrient multipliers, the ~30 kcal/kg reduction in BMR per kilogram lost, the GOMAD macros, the 20 lb in two months, the thermic-effect argument, the ammonia-toxicity reasoning — is unsourced. Treat the whole article as expert opinion, and cross-check anything you intend to act on against the current Barbell Medicine protein and nutrition material, which is referenced.
