# What Counts as a Stall, and Why Three Weeks Is the Minimum

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Training Expectations: Understanding Stalls" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/training-expectations-understanding-stalls/)) · Leah Lutz · 2020-08-28 · 2,377 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **A stall requires a trend, not a session.** Their threshold is **at least three weeks**, sometimes longer, of a metric failing to move as planned. One or two bad workouts fall inside normal variation.
- They define a **"regression" arbitrarily and say so**: a marked drop in performance, roughly **greater than 15%**, over a sufficiently long window. The number is stipulated for usability, not derived from anything.
- **Bar weight alone is an inadequate progress metric.** Two identical log entries can represent different performances; RPE is offered as the qualitative dimension that recovers the difference.
- **Progress is not owed to you at any rate.** Adding 5 lb / 2.5 kg over three weeks can be outstanding progress in context, and forcing progression when it is unrealistic risks frustration, burnout and injury.
- The **five-question stall audit** and the **seven tracking practices** are the article's usable content, and both are coaching heuristics rather than findings.
- **Zero studies are cited.** This is experienced-coach reasoning presented as such — no evidence claims are made, so none outrun their support.

## Defining the thing before reacting to it
The framing move is to separate the *feeling* of stalling from the *fact* of one. Lutz's argument is that training outcomes are emotionally loaded — especially where training or competition is part of someone's identity — and that this produces two errors: judging the whole from a fragment, and reacting to normal variation as though it were signal.

Hence the definitional work up front. A stall is a metric (working weights, rep maxes, endurance performance) not progressing as planned **over a period long enough to show a trend** — three weeks minimum. A regression is a marked drop, arbitrarily set near 15%. Neither definition is empirical; both exist to stop a lifter rewriting a program after one bad Tuesday. The honest caveat is stated plainly: **no universal numbers for expected progress exist**, because realistic three-week improvement depends on training history, program goals, recovery and outside stressors.

## Why bar weight is not enough
Quantitative metrics get the attention because they are easy to observe, not because they are complete. Their worked example: "Week 1: 225 × 4" and "Week 2: 225 × 4" look identical in a log, but if week 2 had faster bar speed, less effort and less time between reps, it was an improvement — one you will have no memory of months later.

**RPE** is their fix, and the default expectation they set is that a lifter enters a session **planning to add load unless there is evidence not to**, with RPE the main source of that evidence. The clearest application is the inverse case: 315 × 1 at RPE 9 followed by 315 × 1 at RPE 8 is progress; 135 × 1 at RPE 8 followed by 145 × 1 at RPE 10 is not, because week two was simply a max attempt against a submaximal week one. Watching RPE 8 reps drift toward RPE 10 is their early-warning signal.

## The five-question audit
Before concluding you have stalled:

1. **Review the goal of the block.** Strength-biased and hypertrophy-biased blocks have different expected outcomes and metrics; a peaking block differs again. Powerbuilding aims at several outcomes at once, and nobody expresses all of them simultaneously.
2. **Expand the window** to three or four weeks or more, which smooths inconsequential variation and — they say so explicitly — moderates the emotional reading.
3. **Compare like with like.** A 1 rep @ RPE 10 is not comparable to 5 reps @ RPE 8 except through an estimated 1RM, which carries its own error.
4. **Account for movement changes** — injury, technique, squat depth, bench pause length — all of which alter what the bar weight means.
5. **Assess life context.** Competing goals, family and work load, or ongoing rehab all change what the data should be expected to show.

## The seven tracking practices
Condensed, since several restate the audit: keep a log at all; **use comparable data**, invoking SAID — a paused bench trains the paused bench, so a novel rack pull will not behave as expected; **note form corrections that reset the standard** (above-parallel to full-depth squats, a loose pause tightened — you changed the test, so rebuild from the new baseline); **track lift variants and rep ranges** without recording every irrelevant condition; **record effort honestly**; **note confidence and mindset** as real progress (315 becoming an everyday weight on a bad day is an outcome, even without a PR); and **allow for injury-modified programming**, since two or three weeks of altered training plus a rebuild reads as a stall if you only look at load.
