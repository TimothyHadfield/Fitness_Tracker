# What Counts as a Stall, and Why Three Weeks Is the Minimum

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Training Expectations: Understanding Stalls" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/training-expectations-understanding-stalls/)) · Leah Lutz · 2020-08-28 · 2,377 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **A stall requires a trend, not a session.** Their threshold is **at least three weeks**, sometimes longer, of a metric failing to move as planned. One or two bad workouts fall inside normal variation.
- They define a **"regression" arbitrarily and say so**: a marked drop in performance, roughly **greater than 15%**, over a sufficiently long window. The number is stipulated for usability, not derived from anything.
- **Bar weight alone is an inadequate progress metric.** Two identical log entries can represent different performances; RPE is offered as the qualitative dimension that recovers the difference.
- **Progress is not owed to you at any rate.** Adding 5 lb / 2.5 kg over three weeks can be outstanding progress in context, and forcing progression when it is unrealistic risks frustration, burnout and injury.
- The **five-question stall audit** and the **seven tracking practices** are the article's usable content, and both are coaching heuristics rather than findings.
- **Zero studies are cited.** This is experienced-coach reasoning presented as such — no evidence claims are made, so none outrun their support.

## Defining the thing before reacting to it
The framing move is to separate the *feeling* of stalling from the *fact* of one. Lutz's argument is that training outcomes are emotionally loaded — especially where training or competition is part of someone's identity — and that this produces two errors: judging the whole from a fragment, and reacting to normal variation as though it were signal.

Hence the definitional work up front. A stall is a metric (working weights, rep maxes, endurance performance) not progressing as planned **over a period long enough to show a trend**, which in strength training means three weeks minimum. A regression is a marked drop, arbitrarily set near 15%. Neither definition is empirical; both exist to stop a lifter rewriting a program after one bad Tuesday.

The honest caveat is stated plainly: **no universal numbers for expected progress exist**, because realistic three-week improvement depends on training history, program goals, recovery and outside stressors. What they offer instead are principles for interpreting your own data.

## Why bar weight is not enough
The central technical point is that quantitative metrics get the attention because they are easy to observe, not because they are complete. Their worked example: "Week 1: 225 × 4" and "Week 2: 225 × 4" look identical in a log, but if week 2 had faster bar speed, less effort and less time between reps, it was an improvement — and one you will have no memory of months later.

**RPE** is their fix. It records the difficulty of a performance, which lets improvement show up when the load does not. The default expectation they set is that a lifter enters a session **planning to add load unless there is evidence not to** — RPE being the main source of that evidence.

The clearest application is the inverse case: 315 × 1 at RPE 9 followed by 315 × 1 at RPE 8 a week later is progress; 135 × 1 at RPE 8 followed by 145 × 1 at RPE 10 is not, because the second week was simply a max attempt against a submaximal first week. Watching RPE 8 reps drift toward RPE 10 is their early-warning signal.

## The five-question audit
Before concluding you have stalled:

1. **Review the goal of the block.** A strength-biased block and a hypertrophy-biased block have different expected outcomes and metrics; a peaking block differs from what precedes it. Powerbuilding aims at several outcomes at once, and no individual expresses all of them simultaneously.
2. **Expand the window** to three or four weeks or more, which smooths inconsequential variation and, they note explicitly, moderates the emotional reading.
3. **Compare like with like.** A 1 rep @ RPE 10 is not comparable to 5 reps @ RPE 8 except through an estimated 1RM, which carries its own error.
4. **Account for movement changes** — injury, technique changes, squat depth, bench pause length — all of which alter what the bar weight means.
5. **Assess life context.** Competing goals (a marathon with a partner), family and work load, or ongoing rehab all change what the training data should be expected to show.

## The seven tracking practices
Condensed, since several restate the audit: keep a log at all (without one there is no basis for any decision); **use comparable data**, invoking SAID — a paused bench trains the paused bench, so a first-attempt touch-and-go or a novel rack pull will not behave as expected; **note form corrections that reset the standard** (above-parallel to full-depth squats, a loose pause tightened to a real one — you changed the test, so rebuild from the new baseline); **track lift variants and rep ranges** without descending into recording every irrelevant condition; **record effort honestly**; **note confidence and mindset** as a real form of progress (315 becoming an everyday weight on a bad day is an outcome, even without a PR); and **allow for injury-modified programming**, since two or three weeks of altered training plus a rebuild reads as a stall if you only look at load.
