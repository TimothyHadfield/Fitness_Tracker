# Five Cable Chest Exercises — Ranked on Activation Data, Not Growth Data

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "5 Best Cable Chest Workout Exercises That Are Effective" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-cable-chest-exercises/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2023-12-07 · 3,076 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **The ranking is not backed by hypertrophy outcome data.** Not one of the four unique papers behind this article measured muscle growth from a cable exercise. Three of the six numbered references are the same EMG-and-kinematics study, and the fourth is a conference abstract. The "best five" is a coaching opinion with activation citations attached.
- The five exercises are the **cable chest press** (mislabelled "cable chest flye" in its own heading), **flat cable fly**, **low-to-high cable fly**, **cable crossover** and **low-to-high cable crossover**. Prescriptions are the useful part: presses at 3–5 × 6–10 at RPE 7–8; every fly and crossover at 2–4 × 10–15 at RPE 8–9.
- Their honest lines are worth keeping. Seated versus standing cable press: **"the results will mostly be similar, so a trainee could technically choose based on personal preferences."** Feeling a chest fly in the delts is normal, not a technique fault.
- The two genuinely evidence-backed general claims are **range of motion** (Pallarés 2021 meta-analysis, ref 5) and the fact that **cable and machine setups shift where load sits in the range** (Signorile 2017). Neither is specific to the five exercises listed.
- **They sell what the article recommends.** Two sections pitch Barbell Medicine personal coaching and their pre-made training programmes. Strip that and the technique content stands on its own.
- Editorial quality is poor for this source: the exercise-one heading contradicts its own body text, and the closing recap lists a "High-to-Low Cable Crossover" that the article never covered.

## What the citations actually support

The marker-to-reference mapping is **verified**, so each number below really does sit on the sentence quoted. That makes it possible to check the fit — and the fit is often bad.

**Ref 1 (Muyor 2023), on seated vs standing cable press.** Cited to support "the results will mostly be similar." The paper is a surface-EMG comparison of the **horizontal barbell bench press versus a seated chest press machine across several grips** — it does not test cable presses, does not compare seated to standing, and reports activation rather than results. The conclusion drawn (choose by preference) is a reasonable coaching default; the citation does not establish it.

**Ref 2 (Signorile 2017), on "for some, this may produce greater muscle hypertrophy than free weights."** This is the load-bearing claim of the article and the citation cannot carry it. Signorile compared **cable-based to selectorized (pin-loaded) machine** training on muscle activation and joint kinematics — not to free weights — and measured EMG, not growth. **An activation difference is not a hypertrophy result.** Flag this one.

**Ref 5 (Pallarés 2021), on longer muscle lengths.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of range of motion in resistance training. It supports the broader claim that fuller ranges tend to produce more hypertrophy than partial ranges. The article's specific gloss — that a cable fly's force curve is what delivers this — is not what the meta-analysis tested.

**Refs 3 and 4 (Signorile 2017 again; Richardson 2016), on cables giving a larger range of motion "which may help in building more size."** Richardson is an **ACSM conference abstract** comparing plate-loaded to cable machines, not a peer-reviewed paper; Signorile again compares cable to selectorized. Neither compares cables to free weights, and neither measured size. The "may help" hedge is doing all the work.

**Ref 6 (Signorile 2017, third appearance), on deltoid involvement.** The most defensible use of the paper — it is an activation study being cited for an activation claim.

## The prescriptions, which are the usable content

Barbell Medicine classify the cable chest press as a **primary** movement and everything else as **tertiary**, which is their own programming taxonomy rather than an evidence claim. The pattern is consistent and sensible: the press gets heavier loads and more distance from failure (RPE 7–8, 2–3 reps in reserve), the isolation work gets lighter loads closer to failure (RPE 8–9, 1–2 in reserve). This mirrors the standard rationale that fatigue cost per unit of stimulus is lower on single-joint work, so it tolerates being pushed harder.

Their case for cables, stripped of citations, is two practical points rather than a physiological one:

1. **Angle freedom.** Pulley height plus body position lets you press and fly at many angles a barbell cannot reach. Explicitly, high-to-low loads the lower chest and low-to-high the upper — described as a line-of-pull argument, not an outcome finding.
2. **Isolation is easier to achieve.** Free weights have more degrees of freedom, so stabilising them takes effort that a fixed cable path removes. Framed as helpful for novices learning a range of motion and for advanced lifters rehearsing a pattern.

Both are mechanical reasoning, offered as such. Neither is presented as proven, and that restraint is appropriate.

## Where the recommendation is advice, not evidence

The whole article is best read as **"we recommend X"** rather than **"the evidence shows X."** There is no comparative trial anywhere in it — no cable-versus-dumbbell fly hypertrophy study, no head-to-head of the five exercises. The claim that cable machines are "quite efficient" at working the chest is plausible and almost certainly true, but it is asserted.

**Flagged overreach:** "For some, this may produce greater muscle hypertrophy than free weights" is stated on the strength of an EMG study that did not include free weights and did not measure hypertrophy. That is the single sentence in the article a careful reader should discard.

**Commercial note, stated plainly:** the article contains a section titled "Train With Barbell Medicine" pitching their coaching service, and a second pitching their off-the-shelf training programmes. This is a product-adjacent article; nothing in the technique content depends on buying anything, and the marketing sits in identifiable blocks rather than being woven through the advice.
