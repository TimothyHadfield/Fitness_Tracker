# The Same Prescription at Six and at Ninety: Barbell Medicine's Strength Training Guide

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Strength Training Guide: How to Get Stronger" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/strength-training-guide/)) · Jordan Feigenbaum · 2026-05-20 · 3,789 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- **One thesis, stated repeatedly:** the *mechanisms* of strength adaptation do not change with age or sex — only the magnitude and the variability do. Everything else in the article follows from it.
- **Four adaptations run in parallel from session one:** neural/skill, hypertrophy, tendon stiffness, bone density. Not phases, not a sequence.
- **Four inputs:** load (~60% 1RM for untrained, ~80% for trained), volume (10–20 hard sets per muscle group per week), frequency (2–4 sessions, mostly a distribution device), progression (matched to the lifter, not to the calendar).
- **Novice/intermediate/advanced are redefined as detection windows** — how long it takes to distinguish real progress from noise (session, weeks, months) — not biological tiers. This is the most useful reframe in the piece.
- **They explicitly deny that squat, bench and deadlift are special.** "They aren't uniquely beneficial movements"; they are there for historical and practical reasons. No exercise ranking is offered at all, and none of the claims here rest on EMG activation data.
- **The article is a store catalogue.** A goal × training-history table routes the reader to roughly fifteen paid templates, plus coaching, consultations and a free lead-magnet action plan. The programming reasoning is sound; the article's structure exists to sell the template.

## The four adaptations, and what backs each
**Skill and neural drive.** Most early strength gain is skill, not tissue — cited to Damas 2016, which showed myofibrillar protein synthesis only tracks hypertrophy after muscle damage attenuates. That paper is about the *timing of hypertrophy signalling*; it is a defensible pointer to "early change isn't tissue" but not direct evidence for neural drive.

**Hypertrophy.** Muscle size as one of the strongest correlates of strength, cited to Folland & Williams 2007 — a review, and the right kind of source for a correlate claim.

**Connective tissue.** Tendons stiffen under repeated deforming load, older tendons need heavier loads, light loading does nothing. Cited to a GeroScience paper on muscle and tendon contributions to rate of force development in older males — cross-sectional, so "very light loading does not produce these changes" is stronger than the citation supports.

**Bone — the best-evidenced section.** LIFTMOR (Watson 2018) is described in unusual detail and correctly: **101 postmenopausal women** with osteopenia or osteoporosis, **8 months**, supervised, **5×5 at 80–85% 1RM** in deadlift, squat and overhead press plus jumping chin-ups with drop landings. Results against low-intensity controls: lumbar spine BMD **+2.9% vs −1.2%**, knee extensor strength **+37% vs +5%**, back extensor strength **+36% vs +11%**, plus functional test improvements. **One minor adverse event across 2,600+ supervised sessions.** The safety figure is the load-bearing one and they use it four separate times. Worth remembering the qualifier they keep: *supervised*.

## The four inputs
**Load.** Rhea 2003, a meta-analysis of 140 studies, for ~60% 1RM in untrained and ~80% in trained adults; Borde 2015 (dose-response meta-analysis in healthy older adults) for a 70–79% 1RM productive range. RPE 6–8 is offered as the practical selector, with the reps-in-reserve translation spelled out.

**Volume.** They claim a roughly logarithmic dose-response and put most progressing lifters at 10–20 hard sets per muscle group per week. **The citation attached to "weekly volume amplifies the effect of both load and effort" is Pelland 2024, a methods paper on controlling and reporting proximity to failure — not a volume dose-response study.** It cannot support the sentence, and "the evidence here is strong" is the article's own emphasis, not the cited paper's finding. The 10–20 set figure carries no citation at all.

**Frequency.** Two to four sessions; weekly total matters more than distribution, cited to Rhea 2003 — a dose-response meta-analysis on load and volume, which speaks to frequency only indirectly.

**Progression.** Their strongest programming argument. Because adaptations run on different timescales with wide between- and within-person variance, **scheduled load increases that ignore whether adaptation has occurred are a bad bet**. Progression can be more weight at the same reps/RPE, more reps at the same weight/RPE, or the same work at lower RPE and higher bar speed — all three count, cited to Plotkin 2022, which compared load versus repetition progression and found comparable muscular adaptations. That citation genuinely supports the claim.

## Recovery inputs
- **Sleep:** habitual under-six-hours degrades lift performance and recovery (Craven 2022, meta-analytical review of *acute* sleep loss — the article's habitual-sleep framing goes slightly beyond it). They are honest that no sleep duration has been shown to maximise hypertrophy.
- **Protein:** 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day for hypertrophy and strength, ~0.4 g/kg per meal, 1.2–1.6 g/kg for general health; source irrelevant at adequate doses (Morton 2018 meta-analysis and meta-regression). Per-meal dose matters more in older adults, and "anabolic resistance" shrinks when dose and training load are both adequate (Holwerda 2018, an RCT that found *no* added benefit from pre-sleep protein in active older men — a null result used to argue that adequate baseline protein is what matters).
- **Energy balance:** deficits of ~10–20% below maintenance usually still permit strength gain (Murphy & Koehler 2022 meta-analysis, whose actual finding is that energy deficiency impairs **lean mass** gains but not strength — the article's framing is consistent with it, though the lean-mass cost is glossed over).

## Age, sex, and the claim that little changes
- **Children:** Behringer 2010 meta-analysis of RCTs in healthy youth, **13–30% strength gains over 8–20 weeks**, dominated by neural adaptation. AAP 2020 clinical report (Stricker) for no increased growth-plate injury under supervision and injury rates below common youth sports. Their line that "lifting stunts growth is not supported by any prospective controlled study" is correct as stated and is a claim about absence of evidence.
- **20–50:** Latella 2024, longitudinal growth modelling of powerlifters across federations, for smooth strength trajectories with no abrupt age inflection. This is competitive-athlete registry data, so it is a selected population; the article extends it to "a 45-year-old and a 25-year-old with the same training history will see comparable relative gains," which is an inference beyond the model.
- **Over 65:** heavier loads work better (Steib 2010 meta-analysis), and Fiatarone 1990 — **ten nursing-home residents, mean age 90, 8 weeks**, +174% quadriceps strength, +9% mid-thigh muscle area, +48% walking speed. They call it "the strongest test of this principle." It is a landmark trial and it is also **n=10 over 8 weeks**; the percentage gains are large partly because the baseline was so low. The article does not say so.
- **Power:** declines faster than strength and predicts falls and disability better. Their practical answer is not plyometrics but lifting the concentric phase as fast as control allows, cited to a meta-analysis of power versus strength training in older adults.
- **Women:** same prescription. Roberts 2020 meta-analysis for equal lower-body strength and hypertrophy gains and faster *relative* upper-body strength gains in women. Post-menopause, they argue heavy lifting becomes more important, not less, leaning again on LIFTMOR.

## Stalls and long-term expectations
A stall of four to six weeks is treated as **information about the prescription**, not about age, genetics or hormones — look at total training load first (lifts, intensity, volume, proximity to failure, frequency, rate of progression), then at sleep, protein, deficit, stress and illness. This is clinical reasoning, presented as such, with no citation and none really needed.

Long-run numbers, all from Latella 2024: roughly **7.5–12.5% strength gain in year one, ~20% by year ten**, front-loaded. Health outcomes: **~21% lower all-cause mortality** in people who do any resistance training versus none (Saeidifard 2019 meta-analysis), and **10–17% lower risk** of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, cancer and diabetes from muscle-strengthening activity across 16 cohorts with median 25-year follow-up, largest benefit at **30–60 minutes per week** (Momma 2022). Both are observational; the article does not label them as such, and "21% less likely to die" reads as causal in context.
