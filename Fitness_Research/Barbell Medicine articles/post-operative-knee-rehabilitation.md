# Post-Operative Knee Rehab: The Problem Is Under-Loading, Not Over-Loading

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Post-Operative Knee Rehabilitation" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/post-operative-knee-rehabilitation/)) · Derek Miles · 2020-09-18 · 4,207 words
**Topic:** Injury & rehabilitation

## Bottom line
- **Return-to-sport numbers after ACL reconstruction are worse than most people assume.** Elite athletes return to competitive sport only **83%** of the time (Lai 2018) — one in five never returns to the professional level. In a cohort of over 7,500 athletes, **81% returned to sport at any level, 65% to their pre-injury level, and only 55% to competitive sport** (Ardern 2014). Reinjury rate in athletes under 25 is **23%** (Wiggins 2016).
- **Rehab commonly fails on dose, not on caution.** Only **13.9% of athletes met all return-to-sport criteria** in one study, and only **27.8% met the strength criteria** (Toole 2017). Squat depth has been shown to remain impaired **seven years** post-surgery (Sanford 2016). Miles's diagnosis is systematic under-loading.
- **Manual muscle testing cannot detect the deficits that matter.** His worked example: 150 lb versus 120 lb of knee extension force is an 80% limb symmetry index that a clinician's hands cannot resolve; at 200 versus 140 lb the athlete has a 70% LSI and the clinician cannot even generate enough force to test it. Dynamometry is the gold standard; a unilateral leg press or knee extension at a set RPE is the practical substitute (Jewiss 2017).
- **There is no moment of being "healed."** ACL physiological healing may take **up to 9 months**, and joint kinematics are permanently altered afterwards. He argues for a "new normal" framing rather than a return to baseline.
- **Some knee surgery should not have happened.** Arthroscopy for degenerative knee arthritis and meniscus tears is ineffective (Brignardello-Petersen 2017, Kim 2011) and the AAOS has a position statement against arthroscopic debridement for osteoarthritis, yet the procedure continues under "expert opinion."
- **The staged model** is early post-op → rehab → return to participation (gated at ~80% quadriceps LSI) → return to practice (gated at tolerating ~80% of practice activity) → return to competition. Those thresholds are presented as practice standards, not as validated cut-offs.

## The preoperative argument

Miles frames the whole article through ACL reconstruction because it has the largest evidence base, then generalises to other procedures including total knee replacement. Two points do real work before surgery even happens.

First, the surgical indication itself is sometimes wrong. He is direct that the evidence against arthroscopy for degenerative meniscal tears and knee OA has not changed practice, and cites the AAOS position statement as an institutional acknowledgement that is routinely overridden.

Second, expectations. He presents the return-to-sport statistics explicitly to counter the framing of surgery as a fix: "it is more likely that the work an athlete puts in before and after surgery has the greatest impact on outcomes." Note what those numbers are and are not. Lai, Ardern and Wiggins are observational cohort and review data on what happens, not trials of rehab quality; they establish that outcomes are frequently poor, not that better rehab causes better outcomes. Miles does not claim otherwise — he lists surgeon skill, rehab quality, social support and personal factors as joint contributors, which is appropriately hedged.

He also raises psychological readiness and athletic identity as managed variables from the outset (Everheart 2015), and returns to it as a return-to-competition criterion at the end. This is a strength of the article; the psychological dimension is usually an afterthought in lifting-adjacent rehab writing.

## Early post-operative phase

Three domains: psychology, biomechanics and conditioning — with the criticism that most plans are almost entirely biomechanical.

The concrete claims are about what *not* to do. Swelling, range of motion and weight-bearing status come first, and then: "no laser, suction, needling, taping, or any other passive intervention has been shown to outperform the body's own healing process." He also pushes back on the reflex to suppress inflammation, swelling and bruising, on the grounds that inflammation is a necessary healing pathway rather than a complication. This is the pain-science-aligned Barbell Medicine position, stated more absolutely than any evidence offered here supports — no citation accompanies either claim.

He is similarly blunt about fabricated findings: "neither a 'weak core' nor a 'rotated hip' caused an athlete to get injured," and warns against rehab that invents problems the athlete did not arrive with. Again, asserted rather than evidenced in this piece, though it is consistent with the wider literature on the weak predictive value of movement screens.

The practical point that transfers well: rehab timelines cannot be assigned discrete durations because the surgery dictates them. A six-week non-weight-bearing microfracture protocol shifts everything — but "a seated overhead press in a knee immobilizer is easy enough to perform." Similarly, an athlete who trains five days a week should have a five-day plan through rehab, even if some of those days are upper-body ergometer conditioning, because 2–3 clinic visits per week does not cover the conditioning need.

## The rehab phase: progressive overload, autoregulated

The central claim: "If an athlete is 12 weeks after an arthroscopic surgery and is still only performing bodyweight exercises, we are likely missing the premise of progressive overload."

Intensity is set by RPE or reps-in-reserve, but with a rehab-specific twist worth noting. In this context RPE may reflect not only effort against load but whether the athlete can hold a clinician-imposed technical constraint — squat depth, even weight distribution — so the same movement can be programmed with different targets on different sets: load on one, range of motion on another, symmetry on a third. Partial range is explicitly acceptable when load is the target.

Alongside that, he keeps a separate track for absolute strength in the surgical limb, where RPE means what it usually means. His heuristic there: work intended to build maximal strength that is rated below **RPE 5/10** is probably wasted.

The claim that early loading of the graft is safe is credited to Escamilla 2012. Worth reading carefully — Escamilla's work is biomechanical analysis of ACL loading during closed- and open-chain exercises, so it supports "these exercises do not impose dangerous strain" rather than "athletes loaded early have better outcomes." The outcome case is made instead by the failure statistics (Toole, Sanford), which is inference rather than direct evidence. It is a reasonable inference and he presents it as likely rather than proven ("The bigger problem is *likely* under-loading athletes").

Barbell Medicine's own preference — barbell squats and deadlifts starting from an empty bar, early — is stated as house practice, not as an evidence-backed superiority claim. That is the honest framing.

## Return to participation, practice and competition

Participation begins around a **quadriceps LSI of 80%**, adding sport-specific skill work — ball handling, batting practice — while strength and conditioning continue. Change of direction, deceleration and jump/landing work start here; controlled plyometric, agility and landing drills are cited as reducing reinjury risk (Emery 2015).

Practice is a deliberate intermediate step before competition, on the reasoning that practice has "a more natural governor" than competition, where the objective is to win. The suggested gate is participation in at least **80% of practice activity**.

For competition, the strongest evidence cited is that **delayed return to sport reduces reinjury risk after ACL reconstruction** (Grindem 2016) — the well-known finding that each additional month before return lowers reinjury odds. He pairs it with a sensible non-clinical point: most readers are recreational, so there is little to gain by rushing.

The final section addresses lifters directly: cutting and landing drills matter less, everything else holds. A knee surgery "is not the end of your lifting career," early movement is not to be feared, and the rehab period is an opportunity for single-leg and accessory work that may earn a permanent place in the programme. His framing — that there should be little difference between "rehab" and "training", since both address deficits limiting current goals — is the article's most portable idea.

**Overreach to flag:** the blanket statement that no passive intervention outperforms the body's own healing process is uncited and stated as settled; the defensible version is that passive modalities have not demonstrated an effect on tissue healing, which leaves symptom relief untouched.

**Commercial note:** the article twice directs readers to Barbell Medicine's paid Pain and Rehabilitation consultation service — once in the introduction and once in the closing paragraph. The clinical content stands on its own, but the call to action is to a product they sell.
