# Strength vs Hypertrophy Training: Same Lifts, Different Constraints — and an Effort Prescription That Breaks With the Library

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Strength vs Hypertrophy Training - Similarities & Differences" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/strength-training-vs-hypertrophy-training/)) · Barbell Medicine · 2024-08-08 · 5,083 words
**Topic:** Programming

## Bottom line
- Their framing is the useful one: **strength training is constrained and hypertrophy training is not.** A powerlifter must train the squat, bench and deadlift at competition-like loads and speeds because adaptation is specific. Someone chasing size has no such constraint, so they can use more exercises, more rep schemes, more loads and more isolation work — which is why hypertrophy volume ends up higher.
- **Load:** heavy (>60% 1RM) beats light for strength; hypertrophy works across a much wider band. This agrees with the rest of the library, and rests on the same [Schoenfeld 2017 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000002200) that the House of Hypertrophy rep-range material uses.
- **Effort: this is where they part company with everyone else here.** They prescribe **RPE 6–8 on compound lifts even in a hypertrophy programme**, reserving RPE 8–10 for isolation work. That is 2–4 reps in reserve on the main lifts, which is further from failure than Jeff Nippard's hypertrophy standard and much further than the industry norm.
- **Rep ranges:** 3–20 for compounds, 6–20+ for isolation. Narrower at the top than the 5–35 band this library has established, and lower at the bottom than House of Hypertrophy's advice to stay above 5 reps for pure hypertrophy.
- The strength-side evidence is unusually well matched to the claim: the "stay away from failure for strength" recommendation is backed by two **velocity-loss training trials**, not by mechanism or activation data.
- Roughly the last 40% of the article is a catalogue of programmes they sell — eight templates plus coaching. Say so up front; the argument in the first half stands on its own.

## The core argument

Both goals are pursued by loading muscles and both produce both adaptations. What separates them is not physiology but **constraint**.

Strength, as they define it, is force production measured in a specific context — a 1RM squat, a sprint, a multi-rep effort. Because adaptation is specific (the SAID principle, invoked by name), a strength programme is boxed in: it has to use the competition movements, the competition loads and something like the competition rep schemes, and movement variety drops accordingly. Transfer to anything else scales with similarity.

A hypertrophy programme has no test to prepare for. Any loading that produces mechanical tension in the target muscle counts, so exercise selection, load and rep scheme are all free variables. Their conclusion follows cleanly: hypertrophy programmes carry **more volume**, not because volume is a hypertrophy-specific requirement, but because the format permits more of it — more reps per set, more sets, more exercises, more isolation work that is cheap in fatigue terms.

They then note that outcomes for *both* goals are dose-dependent on volume, which makes the volume difference a consequence of the constraints rather than a principle.

**This is a good frame** and it is not the frame most articles on this question use. It is also mostly uncited — the constraint argument is reasoning, not a finding.

## What the evidence actually supports

The mapping verified clean, so individual sentences can be checked.

**Load for strength — well supported.** Loads above 60% 1RM beat loads below it for strength gains, from Schoenfeld's 2017 low- vs high-load meta-analysis. That meta-analysis is doing double duty here: it also supports their claim that hypertrophy tolerates a much wider intensity band, which is the correct reading of it.

**Singles above 85% 1RM — thin.** The claim that single-rep work above 85% 1RM improves single-rep performance rests on [Androulakis-Korakakis 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports6030086), a **pilot study** of reduced-volume "daily max" training in a small group of competitive powerlifters. It is a reasonable specificity argument in its own right; the citation does not carry much weight. (The same pilot study is used for a similar claim in their chest hypertrophy article.)

**Staying away from failure for strength — the best-supported claim in the article.** Two Pareja-Blanco velocity-loss trials, one in professional soccer players and one in resistance-trained men, both roughly 8 weeks, comparing groups that stopped sets at a smaller velocity loss (further from failure) against groups that pushed to a larger velocity loss (closer to failure). The lower-velocity-loss groups matched or beat the higher-loss groups for strength and sprint performance while accumulating less fatigue. **These are training outcome studies with a defined, measurable proxy for proximity to failure** — a much better class of evidence than the activation studies underpinning the exercise-selection articles elsewhere in this source.

**Rest periods — supported, and it corrects a common belief.** They state that hypertrophy does not require short rest periods, and that rest longer than 2–3 minutes produces better hypertrophy than short rest. The citation is [Schoenfeld 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001272), which randomised resistance-trained men to 1-minute versus 3-minute inter-set rest over 8 weeks and found the longer-rest group better on both strength and hypertrophy.

**Protein — supported.** The 1.6 g/kg/day recommendation comes from [Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608), a systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of protein supplementation in resistance training, which is where the ~1.6 g/kg plateau estimate originates, alongside NHANES intake data.

**Health claims — stated more strongly than observational data allow.** The 23% reduction in all-cause mortality for people who resistance train 2–3 times per week, the linear volume-to-health-outcome relationship, and the strength-mortality association are all introduced as "backed by science too." These come from prose-linked observational work (Dankel, Figueiredo, Volaklis), not from trials. **Flagged:** association presented as though it settles causation.

## The three clashes with material already in this library

The task of reconciling this article with the rep-range, load and effort material elsewhere here comes down to three points, and only one of them is agreement.

**1. Load — no clash.** BM's position (>60% 1RM for strength; a wide band for hypertrophy) and the [House of Hypertrophy rep-range note](../House%20of%20Hypertrophy%20videos/best-rep-range-build-muscle.md) and [Nippard's rep-ranges note](../Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/rep-ranges-and-training-intensity.md) all derive from the same Schoenfeld 2017 meta-analysis and reach the same conclusion. Three independent sources, one evidence base, consistent readings.

**2. Rep ranges — partial clash, in both directions.** BM prescribes **3–20 reps for compounds and 6–20+ for isolation**. The library's established band is roughly **5–35** (House of Hypertrophy) or **3–35 with about 75% of volume in 6–15** (Nippard). BM's window is narrower at the top — they never endorse the 20–35 rep territory that the load literature says works when sets go near failure — and looser at the bottom, since House of Hypertrophy explicitly recommends staying above 5 reps for pure hypertrophy because the sub-5 evidence is confounded by unequal set counts. Neither difference is argued; BM simply states the ranges.

**3. Effort — a real clash, and the most consequential one.** BM's hypertrophy prescription is **RPE 6–8 on compound lifts, RPE 8–10 on isolation lifts**. Set against the library:

- **Nippard** ([proximity to failure](../Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/how-hard-to-train-proximity-to-failure.md)) targets **1–3 RIR / RPE 7–9** for most hypertrophy work with a good share at ~1 RIR, and no more than 1–2 RIR on lighter sets. BM's compound-lift prescription sits below that floor.
- **Menno Henselmans** ([how close to failure](../Menno%20Henselmans%20videos/close-failure-should-train.md)) argues the opposite way, that **4–6 RIR can match 0 RIR when total reps are equated** and that failure has close to no independent stimulatory effect. On that view BM's RPE 6–8 is defensible.

So BM lands between the two, but with a **split prescription nobody else in this library uses**: low effort on compounds, high effort on isolation. The implicit logic is a fatigue argument — compounds are expensive in fatigue and cheap in per-muscle stimulus, isolation is the reverse — and it is consistent with the "cheap volume from isolation work" reasoning in their chest and shoulder articles. **But it is asserted, not argued, and no citation supports the split.** Note also that Menno's position depends on equating total reps: if you take compound sets to RPE 6–8 without adding sets to compensate, his own argument does not rescue the prescription.

Worth recording that BM's own article series is internally inconsistent on this too — their chest-and-triceps article prescribes RPE 6–8 across the board, while their shoulder article prescribes RPE 8–10 for the same class of isolation movements.

## Two positions worth keeping

**Progressive loading, not progressive overload.** They reject the usual reading of progressive overload — that training must get harder to force adaptation — and invert it. As fitness improves, load must rise **to keep the training stress the same**. You get stronger first and are then able to lift more; the added load maintains the stimulus rather than creating it. This is a framing claim, uncited, and it is a cleaner way to think about the same practice. It also directly implies the RPE-anchored autoregulation they sell.

**Hyperplasia is unsettled, not disproven.** They describe hyperplasia as an active debate rather than a myth, while noting the more generally accepted position that fibre number is fixed by about age one. That is more honest than the flat "fibre number is fixed" statement in their own chest hypertrophy article, which cites the same four papers.

## Commercial content

This should be stated plainly because of how much of the article it occupies. From "How to Get Started With Strength Training" onward, the piece is a product catalogue: **Strength I, Powerlifting II, Strength III, Low-Fatigue Strength, Hypertrophy I, Hypertrophy II, Beginner Template** (all paid), plus Powerbuilding I and III mentioned in passing, the free **Beginner Prescription**, and a closing pitch for personal coaching. The Low-Fatigue Strength template section doubles as the article's clearest statement of the stay-away-from-failure argument — the evidence discussion and the sales copy are the same paragraphs.

None of that makes the training argument wrong. It does mean the recommendation to keep effort moderate and let a structured template manage the dose arrives attached to the templates.
