# Chest and Shoulder Workouts: Five Exercises Chosen on EMG, Sold With a Programme Link

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Best Chest and Shoulder Workouts (With Samples)" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-chest-and-shoulder-workout/)) · Barbell Medicine (no named author) · 2023-12-29 · 5,995 words
**Topic:** Exercise selection & technique

## Bottom line
- **The five-exercise ranking is backed by activation data and 1RM comparisons, not by hypertrophy outcome data.** Every exercise-specific study in the reference list is an EMG study, an EMG-plus-1RM comparison, or a correlational sports-performance study. **No study in the list measured muscle growth from any of the five recommended exercises**, or compared any two of them for growth.
- The strongest example: the article states that "research suggests that the incline press allows for much more significant hypertrophy as opposed to the traditional bench press." **The reference list contains no incline-versus-flat hypertrophy trial.** The only bench-angle study listed is Lauver 2015, "Influence of bench angle on upper extremity muscular *activation*" — an EMG study. The claim is EMG dressed as growth.
- The article then contradicts itself. Twelve paragraphs later, under safety precautions: "the more horizontal movements cause more chest excitation … **Whether or not this changes hypertrophy outcomes between, say, an incline and a flat bench for the chest, is unclear.**" That second statement is the accurate one.
- The frequency section is the best-evidenced part and cuts *against* the usual advice: [Grgic 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x), a meta-analysis of 22 studies, found a frequency dose-response for strength that **disappeared once volume was equated**, and [Thomas & Burns 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27182422/) found 1×/week and 3×/week produced the same hypertrophy and strength at matched weekly volume. Their recommendation of two sessions a week is presented as a starting point, not a finding.
- **This is commercial content, and heavily so.** Three identical "Find your Hypertrophy Program" call-to-action blocks are embedded in the body with campaign tracking parameters, plus links to training programmes, custom coaching, online nutrition coaching and their protein recommendations. It carries no named author, unlike the clinician-written essays elsewhere in this source, and it contains technique and anatomy errors a clinician would not make.

## What is actually recommended

Five exercises with set and rep prescriptions: flat bench press (3–4 × 4–6, RPE 7–8, "primary"), incline bench press (2–4 × 6–10, RPE 7–8, "secondary"), overhead press (2–4 × 6–10, RPE 7–8, "secondary"), cable fly (3–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9, "tertiary") and one-arm cable lateral raise (3–4 × 10–15, RPE 8–9, "tertiary").

Two sample sessions follow, one built around horizontal pressing strength and one around vertical, each pairing a heavy primary at RPE 7 with lighter accessory work. Note that **the sample workouts do not match the prescriptions above them** — the summary list says 3–4 sets of bench press, the workouts say 2–3 sets at RPE 7. Small, but it is the kind of inconsistency that suggests the list and the workouts were written separately.

The underlying logic is defensible and is the article's genuinely useful idea: the shoulder is a synergist in most pressing, so it gets trained incidentally, **but pressing does not meaningfully stimulate the lateral and posterior deltoid**, which therefore need direct work if hypertrophy is the goal. They are careful to add that this does not hold for strength — if the test is a bench press, lateral raises and rear-delt flyes are unlikely to help as much as more bench variation or direct triceps work. That is a clean statement of specificity, and it is one of the few places where the article distinguishes the two goals properly.

## The evidence base, exercise by exercise

Because the numbered mapping could not be used (see References), the honest way to assess this is at the level of the reference list as a whole — and the list is unambiguous about what kind of evidence it contains.

**Bench press.** The claims made for it are that it works pectoralis major, anterior deltoid and triceps, and that it is "associated with improved club head speed in golf players and higher punching power in boxing". Both of those come from **cross-sectional correlational studies** (López-Laval 2020 in professional boxers, Sorbie 2021 in golfers) — bench press strength correlates with punch and club-head performance in people who already have both. Nothing about training the bench improving either.

**Incline bench press.** Two claims: it trains the clavicular head, and it produces "much more significant hypertrophy" than flat pressing. The first is anatomical and uncontroversial. The second, as noted, has no supporting growth study anywhere in the list. Separately, the barbell-versus-dumbbell point is well sourced: Saeterbakken 2011 compared three chest presses with different stability demands and found **dumbbell 1RM roughly 17% lower than barbell**, which the article correctly attributes to the extra degrees of freedom — and then correctly adds that both "likely produce similar results in terms of muscle growth". That is the right inference and it undercuts their own incline claim.

**Overhead press.** The claim that barbell, machine, dumbbell and kettlebell variants "load the musculature similarly" rests on Dicus 2018, "Stability of Resistance Training Implement alters EMG Activity during the Overhead Press" — **activation data**, and a paper whose own title says the implement *does* alter EMG. Preference is offered as the tie-breaker, which is fine, but the evidence cited does not establish equivalence of outcome.

**Cable fly.** The claim that a standing cable fly "can put greater strain on the involved muscle groups, allowing you to train for hypertrophy with much lower loads" is supported in the list only by Schoenfeld's 2010 mechanisms-of-hypertrophy review — a general mechanism paper, not a study of cable flyes. No fly-versus-press growth comparison exists in the list.

**One-arm cable lateral raise.** Supported by Coratella 2020, an **EMG analysis of lateral raise variations in competitive bodybuilders**, and Escamilla 2009, a review of shoulder muscle activity in rehabilitation exercises. Both are activation papers. The sentence making the rotator cuff claim is also broken mid-thought in the published text ("therefore making shoulder [8]").

So: five exercises, zero growth comparisons. This is the same pattern the library has found across every exercise-ranking article in it, and this one is unusual only in that the article itself admits the problem in a later section.

## Frequency, volume and the parts that are well argued

The frequency discussion is the article at its best, and it argues *down* from the usual claims. Increasing frequency usually increases volume, which confounds the two. Grgic's meta-analysis of 22 studies initially found a graded frequency–strength relationship that vanished on volume equating, and the volume-equated studies within it individually showed no frequency effect. Thomas & Burns trained chest press and hack squat once versus three times weekly at matched volume and found equal hypertrophy and strength. For higher-volume programmes (>10–12 sets per muscle group per session) the evidence is thin — Brigatto 2019 and Lasevicius 2019 — and does not indicate much benefit either. Their conclusion is properly hedged ("it remains to be seen") and the two-sessions-a-week suggestion is flagged as a starting point. **Note a link error:** the inline link introducing "another study using machines" points at the Grgic paper, not at Thomas & Burns.

Progressive loading gets a sensible treatment: they explicitly reject the common reading of progressive overload as "training must get harder", preferring increases in weight and/or reps matched to fitness, with rep schemes, set counts and exercise selection revisited every four to eight weeks.

The nutrition section is bolted on but competently sourced: ISSN-derived intake of ~25–35 kcal/kg/day; protein RDA of 0.8–1.0 g/kg challenged for trainees, dieters and older adults; **Morton's 2018 meta-analysis of 49 studies and 1,863 subjects giving ~1.6 g/kg/day as the point of optimised resistance-training outcomes**; ~1.4 g/kg for endurance athletes; 1.3–1.7 g/kg from the 2019 IAAF consensus statement; carbohydrate 3–5 g/kg as a starting point with a recommendation against very-low-carbohydrate diets for trained lifters pursuing hypertrophy or strength; fat by type rather than amount, favouring unsaturated sources. One typo to watch: the fat recommendation reads "0.5 to 1.0 grams of **protein** per kilogram".

## Errors and overreach

The technique instructions are the weakest material. The bench press cues say to keep "your seat, shoulders, and head … flat on the bench" and "keep your feet slightly off the floor" — floating the feet on a barbell bench press is a niche variation, not a general instruction, and it sits oddly against the same paragraph's advice to retract the shoulder blades. The anatomy section defines flexion as "moving the humerus backward" and extension as "moving the humerus backward" — the same words for both. The safety sections are numbered 1, 2, 3, 5, 4. The heading "Movement selection should be arbitrary" appears to mean *idiosyncratic*, i.e. preference-driven.

The volume-and-recovery reasoning is garbled: "if the training dose is higher than necessary, the trainee may experience too much muscle protein breakdown to actually boost muscle size" is not how the MPS/MPB balance is usually described, and one of the two references attached to that stretch of text is Biressi 2007 on cellular heterogeneity in **vertebrate skeletal muscle development** — a developmental biology paper with no bearing on training dose. Similarly, "overexertion … may lead to tears, which can prevent growth" overstates the risk from ordinary training volume.

**Where the evidence and the recommendation part company:** the specific set/rep/RPE prescriptions, the two sample sessions, the exercise ordering advice, and the twice-weekly frequency are all clinical and coaching judgement. Only the frequency claim, the barbell-versus-dumbbell load difference, and the protein numbers are backed by the cited literature at the strength implied.

**Commercial disclosure:** three in-body calls to action route to Barbell Medicine's hypertrophy programme funnel with campaign tracking (`pilot=hypertrophy_routing_pilot_90d`), and the conclusion pitches their training programmes, custom coaching, nutrition coaching, forum, podcast and social channels. The advice given is not distorted toward the products in any obvious way, but the article's structure is a sales funnel with a literature review in it.
