# The SI Joint Barely Moves, and Nobody Can Feel It Move

**Source:** Barbell Medicine, "Does SI Joint Movement Cause Hip and Low Back Pain?" ([read](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/does-si-joint-movement-cause-hip-and-low-back-pain/)) · Derek Miles · 2023-03-15 · 2699 words
**Topic:** Pain science

## Bottom line
- Measured sacroiliac (SI) joint motion is tiny. A systematic review pooling 3D studies reported roughly **8° about the Z-axis, 4° Y, 2.2° X** ([Goode 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19119382/)), and two radiostereometric studies of patients with long-standing pelvic girdle pain found **under 1°** during single-leg stance and the active straight-leg raise ([Kibsgård 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24602677/), [2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/)).
- Clinicians cannot reliably detect it. Motion palpation of the SI joint gave **48% agreement and kappa −0.06** — worse than coin-flipping ([Robinson 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16843031/)); palpating the PSIS, the biggest landmark available, gave **kappa 0.27** ([Cooperstein 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27069265/)).
- Manipulation does not reposition the joint. Roentgen stereophotogrammetric analysis, sensitive to **0.2 mm**, found **<1 mm translation and <1°** of change after a manual technique ([Tullberg 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9615363/)).
- SI joint degeneration is **65% prevalent in pain-free adults**, ~30% "substantial" ([Eno 2015](https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.N.01101)) — the same asymptomatic-findings problem as spinal imaging ([Brinjikji 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25430861/)).
- **The evidence supports:** motion is small, detection is unreliable, manipulation does not realign. **The author recommends:** dropping "rotated"/"out of alignment" language entirely on nocebo grounds — a reasonable clinical inference, but a step beyond what the cited papers test.
- No product is recommended anywhere in this article. Barbell Medicine sells templates, seminars and supplements; none appear here.

## How much the joint actually moves
The article's core move is to put a number on the motion and then compare it to the resolution of a human hand. Goode's systematic review is the anchor: pooling three-dimensional measurement studies, movement is limited to "minute amounts of rotation and translation", from which its authors concluded that palpation-based diagnosis of SI pathology has limited clinical utility. Nagamoto 2015 is cited alongside it for the general point that measured motion sits below any plausible detection threshold through several layers of soft tissue — that study used 3D CT in patients with degenerative lumbar disorders, so it is a measurement paper, not a pain paper.

The two Kibsgård papers matter most for the specific claim clinicians make. Both used radiostereometry (implanted tantalum markers, the reference standard for skeletal micromotion) in patients who actually had long-lasting pelvic girdle or SI pain — the exact population manual therapy targets — during the exact manoeuvres used to "test" the joint. They found under a degree. This is stronger evidence than a cadaver or healthy-volunteer study would be, and the article underuses it.

## What the reliability numbers mean
Kappa corrects agreement for chance. **Zero means clinicians agree no better than guessing; negative means worse than guessing.** Robinson's −0.06 for SI motion palpation is therefore not "poor reliability", it is the absence of a signal. Cooperstein's 0.27 for the PSIS is conventionally "fair" and nowhere near the ~0.6–0.8 usually demanded of a test that drives treatment. Stovall and Kumar's review found the same across multiple lumbopelvic landmarks, and Nolet's 2021 systematic review generalised it: soft-tissue palpation "inconsistent", bony landmarks and joint mobility "poor".

Preece 2008 supplies the anatomical reason. Working on cadaveric pelvises, it found **side-to-side landmark differences of up to 11° and 16 mm in hip bone position** in the same specimen. If a normal pelvis is asymmetric by more than the joint's entire measured range, a felt asymmetry cannot be read as a displaced joint. The article is careful that a cadaver study cannot speak to pain causation.

Worth noting: Robinson's paper tested motion palpation *and* pain provocation tests, and the article quotes only the palpation figure. Laslett's 2008 paper, quoted here for its demolition of "dysfunction" testing, in fact argues that provocation-test *clusters* have diagnostic value against anaesthetic block. The article acknowledges his dysfunction-versus-pain distinction but does not follow it through.

## Words, nocebo and the pareidolia argument
The second half shifts from biomechanics to communication. If tests are unreliable and the joint barely moves, what are clinicians perceiving? The author's answer is pareidolia — trained expectation generating a signal in noise — illustrated with Merckelbach's 2001 study in which ~30% of undergraduates reported hearing "White Christmas" in a recording that contained no song. That is an analogy, not evidence about palpation.

The nocebo case rests on Setchell's cross-sectional survey of what people believe causes their own back pain, Testa and Rossettini on contextual factors, Bossen's finding that rewording MRI reports improved patient response, and Bishop and Cook on clinician equipoise and belief shaping outcomes. The recommendation — frame prognosis positively, avoid mechanical-failure narratives, treat yourself as facilitator not mechanic — is clinical judgement built on that base, not a tested intervention for SI language specifically.

## Where the claim outruns the evidence
"The joints themselves essentially do not move" and "there was not any movement in the first place" overstate cited work that measured real, non-zero motion — the finding is that motion is too small to palpate, not that it is absent. More importantly, **"clinicians cannot reliably detect SI motion" is a different claim from "the SI joint is not a source of pain"**, and the take-home points blur them; the article's own Laslett material concedes that posterior pelvic pain is real and that the missing piece is a gold standard for localising it, not proof of innocence.
