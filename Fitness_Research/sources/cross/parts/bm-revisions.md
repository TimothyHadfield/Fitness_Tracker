# Revisions Barbell Medicine forces on the existing verdicts

A working list for assembly, not prose. Each item quotes the line in `WHAT-TO-BELIEVE.md` as it
currently stands, states what Barbell Medicine claim and on what evidence, and gives the revised
verdict. Verified against the notes, not taken from the source summary.

Two global changes before the specifics: **every "four sources" and "all four sources" in the file
becomes five**, and the source table needs a fifth row —
[Barbell Medicine](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/SUMMARY.md), 155 notes, mostly practising
physicians and physiotherapists, the only clinical source in the library.

---

## 1. Daily protein — the number holds, the "converge" claim does not

**Existing table row:** `| Daily protein | 1.6–1.8 g/kg. All four sources converge. | High |`
**Existing body:** "All four sources converge on the same daily number by different routes, which is
the strongest signal in this domain."

**What BM claim.** Four different figures across nineteen articles, spanning better than two-fold,
none of them acknowledging the others:

- **1.3–3.1 g/kg** ([protein guide](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md),
  2021) — 1.3–2.2 for maintaining or gaining, **2.3–3.1** for people lean *and* dieting. The bottom
  is Morton 2018 (49 studies, 1,863 adults, breakpoint ~1.6), cited correctly. The top rests on
  **Helms 2014 alone**, a review titled *A Case for Higher Intakes* whose endpoint is minimising
  lean-mass loss and whose underlying trials compare higher protein against *inadequate* protein.
  The article's own Key Points state the range as 1.6–3.1 — it does not agree with itself.
- **1.6 g/kg** ([healthy diet](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/eat-healthy-diet.md),
  [training while losing weight](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/train-while-losing-weight.md)) —
  traced correctly to Morton's ~1.62 breakpoint. Their best-supported number.
- **1.4–1.6 g/kg** ([protein on Ozempic](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/protein-ozempic-much-need.md),
  [protein and weight loss](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/protein-weight-loss-maximizing-fat-loss-preserving.md),
  [working out but gaining weight](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/reasons-re-working-out-but-gaining-weight.md)).
- **1.6 g/kg of *target* body weight**
  ([creatine on Ozempic](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/creatine-ozempic-prevent-muscle-loss.md)) — a
  smaller denominator, so a lower gram figure in the clothes of the third number.

**Revised verdict.** **1.6–1.8 g/kg, unchanged. Confidence: high on the number, and the fifth source
does not touch it** — its own best-cited figure sits inside the band. But delete "all four sources
converge" and replace with something honest: *four of five sources converge; the fifth gives four
numbers from one publisher, cross-references none of them, and its lowest is below the band.* That
is a fact about publishing, not about protein. The convergence signal is now **weaker as rhetoric
and unchanged as evidence** — Morton 2018 is doing the work in every source that gets it right,
which is one meta-analysis wearing four hats.

## 2. Protein in a deficit — the floor is now attacked from below, and it holds

**Existing table row:** `| Protein in a deficit | The upper half of the library's cutting range has
no trial behind it. | Moderate |`
**Existing body verdict:** "1.6–1.8 g/kg suffices in a moderate deficit; confidence moderate-high.
At extreme deficits or stage leanness, unstudied."

**What BM claim, and on what.** Both directions at once, from one publisher.

- *Upward:* **2.3–3.1 g/kg** for lean people actively dieting, on Helms 2014 alone. This is the
  **same confound the existing verdict already names** — higher versus inadequate, never 1.6 versus
  2.4 — arriving from a fifth source and confirming it.
- *Downward:* **1.4–1.6 g/kg**, the lowest figure in the library. Its only stated anchor is the
  **2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines' general-population band of 1.2–1.6**, not a deficit trial. In the
  8,500-word weight-loss guide, **Morton 2018 and Helms 2014 are both printed in the reference list
  and never attached to a sentence** — part of **19 of 49 references never cited at all** — and no
  resistance-training trial is attached to any sentence in the piece. In the "gaining weight"
  article Morton 2018 is absent from the list entirely.

**Revised verdict.** **1.6–1.8 g/kg in a moderate deficit. Confidence: moderate-high, unchanged.**
The floor does not move: a general-population dietary guideline does not outrank the meta-analysis
the same publisher cites elsewhere and leaves unattached here. The ceiling verdict is
**strengthened** — a second independent source has now built its high-protein tier on the
adequate-versus-inadequate literature without noticing. Add to the body: *the same publisher
recommends 1.4–1.6 and 2.3–3.1 for overlapping populations in articles that do not cite each other.*
Their triage order is worth keeping and is unaffected: **training first, calories second, total
protein third, distribution a distant fourth** — the move that matters is 0.6 → 1.2, not 1.4 → 1.6.

## 3. Effort and RIR — no revision to the verdict, a new dissent to record

**Existing table row:** `| Must you train to failure? | No. 1–3 RIR matches failure at normal
volumes; below ~2 weekly sets, failure wins. | High |`

**What BM prescribe.** **RPE 6–8 on compound lifts (2–4 RIR) even in a hypertrophy programme, and
RPE 8–10 on isolation** ([strength vs hypertrophy](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/strength-vs-hypertrophy-training-similarities-differences.md);
[strength vs bodybuilding](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/strength-training-vs-bodybuilding-similarities-differences.md);
[intermediate powerlifting](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/intermediate-powerlifting-programs-increase-strength-size.md)).
That is **below the library's 1–3 RIR floor on the main lifts**, and the compound/isolation split is
used by nobody else here.

**On what evidence: none for the split.** No citation supports it in any of the three articles. Their
best-supported adjacent strand is **two Pareja-Blanco 2017 velocity-loss trials** — one in
professional soccer players, one in resistance-trained men, both ~8 weeks — where lower velocity loss
matched or beat higher loss with less fatigue. Two papers from one lab, measuring **strength and
sprint performance, not hypertrophy**, and **velocity loss is a proxy for proximity to failure, not
the same construct**. They also contradict themselves: the chest-and-triceps article prescribes RPE
6–8 across the board where the shoulder article prescribes 8–10 for the same class of isolation
movement, and [how many chest exercises](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/many-chest-exercises-per-workout.md)
argues for **~4–5 reps from failure** while calling the Physical Activity Guidelines' "within 1 rep
of failure" instruction made up.

**Revised verdict.** **Unchanged: 1–3 RIR. Confidence: high.** An uncited split prescription
contradicted by its own publisher does not move an adjudicated band. Record the dissent in the body
with the reconciliation attached: **RPE 6–8 on compounds is only defensible on Menno's terms — equate
total reps by adding sets — and Barbell Medicine never require that.** Their implicit logic is
fatigue budgeting (compounds expensive per unit of per-muscle stimulus, isolation cheap), which is a
coherent programming preference and not an efficacy claim.

## 4. RIR accuracy — verdict holds, but the sentence "all sources agree" needs a footnote

**Existing table row:** `| Can people judge RIR? | Reasonably when trying to, badly in habitual
practice. All sources agree. | High |`

**What BM claim.** [What is RPE in lifting](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/rpe-lifting.md) is **4,831
words carrying exactly one citation and no reference list**. That citation is **Helms 2017,
*Self-Rated* Accuracy of RPE-Based Load Prescription in Powerlifters**, attached to the sentence
"trainees can implement RPE to a reasonable degree of accuracy within the span of a few weeks."
**Self-rated accuracy measures confidence, not accuracy** — wrong design for the claim, and it is the
only design the article offers. Its answer to the subjectivity objection is otherwise modest and
sound: not "lifters are accurate" but "you don't need to be", because the useful load band is narrow
(their own rule of thumb, **±5% load ≈ ±1 RPE**).

**The correction is inside their own corpus.**
[Novice, intermediate, advanced](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training.md)
cites **Halperin 2022 correctly** — lifters at every experience level under-predict reps to failure by
**2–5 reps** — and adds **Malleron 2025**, a head-to-head on the Smith-machine back squat finding **no
significant trained-versus-untrained difference in RIR accuracy**. The two articles are never
reconciled.

**Revised verdict.** **Unchanged: reasonably when trying to, badly in habitual practice. Confidence:
high.** Amend "All sources agree" to *All five sources agree on the finding; the fifth's dedicated
article on the subject reaches it through a mis-designed citation and carries its own correction
elsewhere, unreconciled.* One substantive addition: **Malleron 2025 is new to the library and cuts
against the intuition that experience fixes this** — it strengthens the existing conclusion that
calibration must be periodic and deliberate rather than accumulated. Note also that
**Barbosa-Netto 2021 is not Barbell Medicine's citation** — it is the library's counter-evidence
brought to their article; do not attribute it to them.

## 5. Exercise claims backed by measured growth — thirteen becomes sixteen

**Existing table row:** `| Which exercise claims are backed by growth data? | Thirteen. Every other
ranking in this library is EMG, moment arms or acute measures. | High |`

Barbell Medicine's 40 exercise articles contain **three growth-positive claims and a set of
equivalence results**. Three of their studies **confirm rows already in the table** (Maeo 2021 seated
hamstring curl; Hernández-Belmonte 2023 machines ≈ free weights; Plotkin 2023 hip thrust ≈ squat) and
three are **new**. Merged and deduplicated, the list is **sixteen**:

Rows 1–13 stand as written, with two amendments and three additions.

**Amendments to existing rows:**

- *"Overhead triceps extensions beat pushdowns — Maeo 2023"* → the same paper is
  **Maeo 2022** in [BM's triceps article](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/best-triceps-exercises-cable.md)
  (*Eur J Sport Sci* 2022;23:1240-1250 — online 2022, issue 2023). One paper, two years across the
  library. Pick one and say which; log it in the defects section.
- *"Free weights ≈ machines near failure"* → add the caveat Barbell Medicine state correctly and the
  existing row omits: **strength transfer is specific to the mode trained** — barbell training
  improves the barbell lift more, machine training the machine lift more. The hypertrophy null is
  unaffected.

**New rows:**

| Claim | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Push-ups match the bench press for growth | Kikuchi 2017: **18 men with ≥1 year of training**, 9 vs 9, 3 sets to failure twice weekly, bench at 40% 1RM against kneeling push-ups — **~3 mm of thickness at pecs and triceps in both, no significant difference**; Kotarsky 2018, 4 weeks, similar 1RM and chest thickness ([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/best-chest-exercises-muscle-strength.md)) |
| Cable and barbell preacher curls grow the biceps equally | Nunes 2020: opposite resistance profiles, **hypertrophy equal**, cable stronger only when tested at long muscle lengths ([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/best-chest-biceps-workout.md)) |
| Bench angle does not change pec hypertrophy | Chaves 2020: 8 weeks, untrained young men, flat / incline / both — **no meaningful difference in pec major hypertrophy or pressing strength** ([note](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/bench-press-technique-benefits-muscles-worked.md)). **The weakest entry in the table** — see item 6 |

Kikuchi is worth flagging as unusual: **trained subjects, direct thickness measurement, a clean
null**, and it belongs in "what actually deserves high confidence" more than several entries already
there. **Excluded:** Wei 2023 (bodyweight vs barbell squats) measured **strength only**.

**Revised verdict.** `| Which exercise claims are backed by growth data? | Sixteen. Every other
ranking in this library is EMG, moment arms or acute measures. | High |`

## 6. Incline versus flat bench — Nippard and Barbell Medicine read Chaves 2020 oppositely

**Existing body verdict:** "House of Hypertrophy wins the study critique decisively; Chaves cannot
carry the claim… Do incline; the usual reason for it is wrong. Moderate confidence."

**What BM claim.** They cite **Chaves 2020 as a null** — flat, incline and both produced no
meaningful difference in pec hypertrophy — and say directly that the belief incline emphasises the
upper pecs is not supported. **Nippard cites the same study as confirming incline "with longitudinal
data."** House of Hypertrophy dismantles it on method (pec thickness rising **54–62% across eight
training sessions**; only **30 of 47 completers** appearing in the hypertrophy data).

**Revised verdict.** **Strengthened, and the reason changes. Confidence: moderate-high.** Two of
three sources reading the study now read it as a null or worse, and the third is the only one
claiming a positive result from it. Add to the body: *Nippard's incline default rests on a study its
other two readers treat as showing no difference, and one of them shows why the study cannot be
trusted in either direction.* The surviving argument for incline work is unchanged and is
arithmetic, not physiology: the upper pec starts at ~15 cm² against 25–27 cm². Also add to the
defects section — **one study, three incompatible readings across three sources** is a sharper
instance of the same problem as the Wolf 30-versus-25 sample-size discrepancy.

## 7. Range of motion — convergence, and the table understates its own body text

**Existing table row:** `| Lengthened partials vs full ROM | Equal in trained lifters. Not worse —
the nulls are nulls, not reversals. | Moderate |`
**Existing body verdict:** "moderate-to-high confidence."

**What BM claim.** [Inglis 2022](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/range-motion-considerations.md)
splits "how deep" into strength, hypertrophy, pain and health, and lands on: long-length partials
**"may produce similar results compared to full ROM"**, and given the thin evidence, **advises
against using them for a significant portion of training**. That is *equal, not better*, reached in
**2022 — two years before the trained-subject nulls** — and reached for the right reason rather than
by luck. Two details worth importing: **Kubo 2019's depth advantage was an adductor and glute
advantage, quadriceps statistically indistinguishable (4.9 ± 2.6% vs 4.6 ± 3.1%)**; and Goto 2019's
**48.7% vs 28.2%** partial-ROM result is passed along uncritically despite a mechanism —
intramuscular hypoxia — that does not belong in a muscle-length argument.

**Revised verdict.** **Equal in trained lifters, and raise the table row to `Moderate-high` to match
the body text it already contradicts.** Independent convergence from a source with no stake in the
partials debate, arriving before the evidence did, is real corroboration. Record it in the body as
**the clearest instance in the library of a source ageing well on a contested question** — the same
sentence that makes Nippard's 2023 position look worst makes this one look best. The Kubo
decomposition should also be added to the deeper-squats row in the exercise table, which currently
implies a quad advantage the study did not find.

## 8. Overtraining — a verdict the table does not yet have, and Nippard loses it

**Existing status:** no table row, no section. Nippard's overtraining video is not adjudicated
anywhere in the file.

**What BM claim.**
[Are you really overtrained](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/overtrained-evidence-shows.md): **no
controlled resistance-training study has ever produced overtraining syndrome.**

**On what evidence — and it is good.** **Grandou 2020**, a systematic review of **22 overload
studies**, found **10 reported zero performance decline** and identified **no reliable biomarker**.
The failed attempts carry the argument: **Zourdos 2016** — three athletes squatting a true 1RM daily
for **30 consecutive days**, all improved (473→500 lb, 275→303 lb, 484→530 lb); **Raastad 2001** —
daily submaximal leg work for two weeks producing **+6% 1RM**; **Coleman 2024** — 9 supervised weeks
at ~**90 working sets per week** to failure, nobody meeting criteria. Only **Fry 1994** (11 men, 10
sets at 1RM daily for 14 days) dropped 1RM by ~12 kg, with recovery in **2–8 weeks** — short of the
months the syndrome requires — and **Fry 1998** found **testosterone rose while cortisol fell**, the
reverse of the T:C model's prediction. On monitoring, **Saw 2016 (56 studies)** found self-report beat
hormones, resting heart rate and HRV.

**The direct clash with Nippard, on four points:** the 2–3 week definition (which is functional
overreaching on the consensus definition), supercompensation itself, whether volume or intensity
drives it (Nippard: volume; BM read Raastad and Fry as intensity), and HRV monitoring. **Both cite
Fry 1994 for opposite conclusions** — Nippard as proof overtraining is real, BM as the strongest
attempt on record that still failed the criteria — and **both cite Saw 2016**, which Nippard uses for
a self-report survey before recommending HRV as "the more objective marker."

**New verdict for the table.** `| Can a lifter actually overtrain? | No controlled resistance-training
study has produced overtraining syndrome. What is reachable is functional overreaching, and it
follows near-maximal intensity rather than volume. | Moderate-high |` **Barbell Medicine win this
one** — they have the systematic review, the failed-induction attempts and the correct reading of the
one positive study, against a video that cites the same paper for the opposite conclusion. Confidence
is moderate-high rather than high because it is a negative claim resting partly on the difficulty of
inducing a syndrome in volunteers, which is not the same as its non-existence in athletes with
years of accumulated load.

**Consequential:** the HRV recommendation goes with it.
[Understanding HRV](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/understanding-heart-rate-variability-strength-training.md)
reports **both head-to-head resistance-training trials null** (21 older women over 7 weeks; 22 young
men racing to 20 sessions) and **Chen 2011**, where 1RM returned to baseline at **~30 hours while HRV
took ~60** — so HRV would have pulled an athlete from a session they were ready for. **Under 5% of
the parameters wearables report have been validated** (Doherty 2024); Apple Watch and WHOOP
underestimate HRV. **Verdict: do not use HRV to skip sessions. High confidence.** Their own
recommendation — a 5–7 day rolling average used only to *add* a session — is coaching judgement
stacked on a null result, and they label it experimental.

## 9. Rest periods — a second verdict the table does not have

**Existing status:** no table row. Rest appears only as a *confound* inside the rep-band section
(Mangine's 3 minutes against 1; Schoenfeld 2014's 3 minutes against 90 seconds).

**What BM claim.**
[The ultimate guide to rest periods](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/ultimate-guide-rest-periods-maximize-workouts-strength.md):
**2–5 minutes for strength, 2–4 for hypertrophy** — both stated *before* the evidence review, and
**neither upper bound tested by anything they cite**. Their evidence is real but narrow: Schoenfeld
2016 (23 trained men, 8 weeks, 3 min beat 1 min — squat 1RM **+15% vs +7.5%**, bench **+12.7% vs
+4%**); de Salles 2010 (36 men, 16 weeks, leg press +22/+34/+42% at 1/3/5 min, **volume not
equated**, which they say); González-Hernández 2023 finding **no difference between 3 and 5 minutes**.
Their hypertrophy case rests on **Grgic 2017, six studies in mostly untrained men**, and they never
engage the **2024 network meta-analysis** this library covers, in which **>60 s beat ≤60 s with every
rest band's credible intervals overlapping and ~90 seconds possibly sufficient**. Their
novices-need-longer-rest claim is sourced to a sprint-running review and a study of enzymes in
children. Their never-below-2-minutes floor for isolation work contradicts **Nippard's 1–2 minutes**.

**New verdict for the table.** `| How long to rest? | More than 60 seconds; past that the bands
overlap. ~2 minutes is a safe default and 3+ is defensible on compounds. | Moderate |` **Neither side
wins outright.** Barbell Medicine have the better single trial (Schoenfeld 2016, trained, 8 weeks)
and the worse literature review; Nippard's shorter isolation rest is not contradicted by anything
they cite. Record this as **the clearest case in the corpus of a recommendation stated before its own
evidence** — the numbers appear in the article's opening and survive a review that does not test
them.

## 10. Creatine — a third inflated headline, and the ranking is now four-way

**Existing table row:** `| Creatine's effect on muscle | Real, small. Both headline figures in this
library are inflated in different directions. | Moderate |`
**Existing body:** "Do not quote 'one-third faster'."

**What BM claim.**
[Creatine: benefits, side effects](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/creatine-benefits-side-effects-safe-consumption.md)
cites **Burke 2023 — the right meta-analysis — and reports it as "a couple of pounds or so in a
year."** Burke pooled ten studies using **site-specific ultrasound and MRI** and found **0.10–0.16 cm
of thickness at four sites, pooled effect size 0.11**, the authors calling the practical significance
possibly minimal. Feigenbaum reports **neither the effect size nor that verdict**, and **converts a
regional thickness result into a whole-body mass unit the paper never measured**. Everything else in
the article is restrained — "modest" effects, the ISSN stand cited for dosing only rather than its
"most effective ergogenic supplement available" line, agnostic on endurance, unconvinced on
cognition. **They sell creatine.**

**Revised verdict.** **Real, small, unchanged. Confidence: moderate.** But the table row now
undercounts: `| Creatine's effect on muscle | Real, small. All three headline figures in this library
inflate the same paper, in different units and different directions. | Moderate |` Add the fourth
rung to the body: **ISSN > House of Hypertrophy > Barbell Medicine > Burke's own reading** — and note
that Barbell Medicine are the *least* enthusiastic of the three secondary sources and still commit the
unit error. Extend the existing instruction: **do not quote "one-third faster", and do not quote "a
couple of pounds a year" either.** Both are conversions of the same 0.11 effect size that neither
source reports. What survives: **3–5 g/day of monohydrate, performance effect secure, ~1–2 lb of
actual contractile tissue a year.** Their honest contribution is
[creatine on Ozempic](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/creatine-ozempic-prevent-muscle-loss.md), which
argues against their own shelf — no trial has tested creatine during GLP-1 therapy, and the effect is
**mediated entirely by training**.

## Also carried over, no verdict change

- **Regional hypertrophy.** BM argue correctly, and against most of the library, that incline bench is
  not shown to preferentially build the upper chest — then several of their own listicles assume the
  opposite. Reinforces the existing "everything else in this domain is unmeasured" position; no line
  changes.
- **Concurrent training.** They agree with House of Hypertrophy on headline and mechanism, and are
  better than the received view on modality (two reviews pointing opposite ways; no basis to choose).
  They differ on same-session ordering. Not currently adjudicated in the file; low stakes.
- **Detraining.** [How fast do you lose muscle and strength](Barbell%20Medicine%20articles/fast-lose-muscle-strength.md)
  is new material with no rival in the library — Wall 2014's two weeks in a leg cast costing **8.4% of
  cross-sectional area and 22.9% of strength**, a noise floor set first at ~2–5%, and the organising
  point that the *reason* you stopped predicts the damage better than the days do. Nothing to
  adjudicate against; worth a line in the exercise or volume section.

## Defects to add to the library-defects list

- **Maeo's overhead triceps extension paper appears as 2022 in one source and 2023 in another.** One
  study, two years.
- **Chaves 2020 is read three incompatible ways by three sources** — confirmation of incline
  (Nippard), a null (Barbell Medicine), and methodologically unusable (House of Hypertrophy).
- **The lengthened-partials confidence level is inconsistent inside `WHAT-TO-BELIEVE.md` itself** —
  the summary table says Moderate, the section says moderate-to-high.
- **Barbell Medicine's RPE article and their novice/intermediate/advanced article contradict each
  other on RIR accuracy**, one citing a self-rating study and the other citing Halperin correctly.
  The corpus contains its own correction and never makes it.
- **Their liver-enzyme article gives three escalation thresholds** (>5× ULN, >10×, <3×). Cite it only
  with the caveat.
