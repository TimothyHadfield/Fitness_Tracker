## Nutrition

### Four protein numbers from one publisher, and none of them cite the others

Barbell Medicine give at least four daily protein targets across these nineteen articles, spanning better than a two-fold range, with no article acknowledging that any of the others exist.

**1.3–3.1 g/kg/day** is the 2021 house guide ([protein guide](barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md)): 1.3–2.2 g/kg for maintaining or gaining, 2.3–3.1 g/kg for people who are lean *and* dieting, training 5+ days a week, or anabolically resistant. The bottom is well founded — Morton 2018, 49 studies, 1,863 adults, breakpoint ~1.6 g/kg. The top rests on **one source**, Helms 2014, a review titled *A Case for Higher Intakes*, whose endpoint is minimising lean-mass loss rather than hypertrophy and whose underlying trials compare higher protein against *inadequate* protein. They show too little is bad; they do not show 3.1 beats 1.8, and the article does not say so.

**1.6 g/kg** appears in [how to eat a healthy diet](eat-healthy-diet.md) and as the floor in [training while losing weight](train-while-losing-weight.md), both traced correctly to Morton's ~1.62 breakpoint. This is their best-supported number. The healthy-diet article also gives 3.1 g/kg for physique athletes in a deficit with **no citation at all**.

**1.4–1.6 g/kg** is the current figure in [protein on Ozempic](protein-ozempic-much-need.md), the 8,500-word [protein and weight loss](protein-weight-loss-maximizing-fat-loss-preserving.md) guide and [working out but gaining weight](reasons-re-working-out-but-gaining-weight.md). **It is the lowest number in the whole library** — below the adjudicated 1.6–1.8 g/kg, below the ISSN's ≥1.6–2.4, below Henselmans, and below the 1.4–2.2 g/kg quoted earlier in the same article. Its anchor is the **2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines' general-population band of 1.2–1.6**, not a deficit trial. In the weight-loss guide the two references arguing for higher intakes, Morton 2018 and Helms 2014, are printed but **never attached to a sentence**; 19 of its 49 references are never cited at all.

**1.6 g/kg of target body weight** turns up in [creatine on Ozempic](creatine-ozempic-prevent-muscle-loss.md) — a smaller denominator again for anyone with obesity, so a lower gram figure wearing the clothes of the second number. The 2012 archive swung the other way: [To Be A Beast](to-be-a-beast.md) sets a muscle-gain template at 1.1–1.25 g/lb (**2.4–2.8 g/kg**), then argues on the same page against high protein on ammonia-toxicity grounds while conceding nobody knows whether dietary protein raises ammonia harmfully at all.

### What a reader should actually do

Take **1.6–1.8 g/kg of current body weight**, deficit or not, and stop optimising. That is the library's adjudicated band and it sits inside Barbell Medicine's own best-sourced figure. Below it you are trusting a general-population guideline over the meta-analysis they cite themselves; above 2.0 g/kg you are buying insurance, not effect — nobody has run 1.6 against 2.4 in a moderate deficit. Their GLP-1 triage order survives all of this: **training first, calories second, total protein third, distribution a distant fourth**. If a drug has taken your appetite and you are at 0.6 g/kg, the move that matters is 0.6 → 1.2, not 1.4 → 1.6.

### Per-meal distribution: right in 2014, wrong in the same article

[Seven rules to optimize protein intake](rules-optimize-protein-intake.md) killed the absorption ceiling early and correctly: tracer work shows essentially complete absorption, ~110–120% of intake once enterocytes secrete their own protein into the lumen, and rate is not extent. [Protein slows digestion? Nope](protein-slows-digestion-nope.md) does the same for gastric emptying, though it too cites nothing.

The same 2014 article then builds a system that has not held: a 3–4 g leucine per-meal threshold, a **3–5 hour refractory period**, "3–5 meals tops", and the claim that protein eaten inside that window "would contribute nothing to MPS and then, by definition, be stored away" as glycogen or fat. That is **unsourced** — the article cites nothing whatsoever — and treats an acute MPS measurement as a full accounting of protein fate.

The later bands soften without abandoning the idea: **~20–45 g** per meal in the protein guide, **25–40 g** in the weight-loss guide (uncited, quietly raising the ISSN's lower bound from 20 g). Stating a band still implies a ceiling, and the verdict here is that there is none.

### Creatine: the right meta-analysis, the wrong unit

[Creatine: benefits, side effects](creatine-benefits-side-effects-safe-consumption.md) cites Burke 2023, the direct-measurement meta-analysis, and reports it as "a couple of pounds or so in a year." Burke pooled ten studies using site-specific ultrasound and MRI and found **0.10–0.16 cm of muscle thickness at four sites, pooled effect size 0.11**, the authors calling the practical significance possibly minimal. Feigenbaum reports neither the effect size nor that verdict, and converts a regional thickness result into a whole-body mass unit the paper never measured.

Everything else is restrained — "modest" effects, the ISSN 2017 stand cited only for dosing rather than its "most effective ergogenic supplement available" line, agnostic on endurance, unconvinced on cognition: markedly less enthusiastic than the ISSN. [Creatine on Ozempic](creatine-ozempic-prevent-muscle-loss.md) argues against its own shelf — no trial has tested creatine during GLP-1 therapy, and the effect is **mediated entirely by training** (~1–2 kg lean mass and 5–10% strength over 8–12 weeks with lifting, near nothing without). **They sell creatine.** The contamination section (~20% of supplements contaminated, 30–50% mislabelled) is properly cited and then links their own line, naming PeriRx as what to buy.

### Collagen: the best appraisal in the corpus, against a category they profit from

[Collagen protein](collagen-protein-work.md): PDCAAS **0**, because collagen has no tryptophan and the score is set by the limiting amino acid, against 1.00 for whey; 30 g collagen failed to stimulate muscle protein synthesis where 30 g whey succeeded; and Benito-Ruiz 2009, n=250, gave collagen a **4.7 mm** advantage on a 100 mm pain scale against a minimal clinically important difference of **13.7 mm**.

[Collagen and tendinopathy](collagen-protein-tendinopathy.md) has the best evidence appraisal in these notes. Praet 2019: **n=20**, double-blind crossover, 2 × 3 months, 2.5 g hydrolysed collagen peptides twice daily on top of 2 × 90 daily eccentric calf raises. Raw VISA-A moved 58.5 → 60.8 on collagen and 55.9 → 62.8 on placebo against an MCID of 6.5 — only placebo cleared it. Three observations do the work: the **crossover-order artefact** (the placebo-first sequence did better overall, the opposite of a real collagen effect), **unblinding** (60% and 55% correctly identified collagen, only 40% and 44% identified placebo), and the **maltodextrin control**, which cannot separate "collagen" from "any extra dietary protein". One correction: he calls the linear mixed model a rescue manoeuvre, and mixed models are standard for repeated measures in a crossover. The qualification is that **whey is the comparator they favour and they sell whey** — in the 2025 article "whey protein isolate" links to their own product page.

### Red meat: meticulous on cancer design, casual on cohort modelling

[The science of red meat](science-red-meat-health.md) labels the cancer evidence exactly: all observational; IARC's ~17% per 100 g/day red meat and ~18% per 50 g/day processed meat are relative risks; against a US lifetime colorectal risk of ~4.5% (39 cases per 100,000) that is roughly **7 extra cases per 100,000**. It even reports the deflationary consistency figure — only **7 of 14 cohorts and 7 of 15 case-control studies** found a positive association for *unprocessed* red meat.

The saturated-fat section then presents Li 2015's substitution estimates — 5% of energy from saturated fat replaced by PUFA → ~25% lower coronary risk, by sugar or refined carbohydrate → **no significant change** — as "the data suggest", without noting these are **substitution models inside a prospective cohort**, not trials: the least certain numbers in the piece, delivered with the most confidence, right after the most careful design labelling in the corpus. Capping processed meat at "under 50 g/day" compounds it — that is the *exposure unit at which the risk was measured*, not a safe ceiling. No product is sold here.

### Where they argue against their own side

[Why weight regain is not a failure of willpower](weight-regain-not-failure-willpower.md) is built as a falsifiable claim: BMI heritability of **40–70% of between-person variance** from 540 Danish adoptees and 93 identical twin pairs reared apart, with a careful paragraph explaining that heritability describes differences between people rather than a verdict on your own body. Sumithran 2011: 50 people, ~13.5 kg lost, self-rated hunger *higher than pre-diet* a year later. Fothergill's Biggest Loser cohort ~700 kcal/day below baseline at six years — from **14 contestants**, which the article never says. The strongest item is Wright 2023: over 12,000 siblings, where the "smarter is leaner" correlation survives between strangers and **shrinks to zero between siblings raised in the same family**.

[The modern food environment](modern-food-environment-makes-overeating-default.md) rests on Hall 2019 — **20 adults, inpatient crossover, two weeks per arm, menus matched for calories offered plus protein, carbohydrate, fat, sugar, salt and fibre, ad libitum**; people ate ~500 kcal/day more on the ultra-processed arm. Enormous effect, tiny trial. Then the section that earns the article its place: they report the BMJ umbrella review's ~50% higher cardiovascular mortality and ~55% higher obesity odds across nearly ten million people and **immediately say the review itself grades that low to very-low certainty**; they call NOVA a blunt instrument (whey isolate and an Oreo land in the same bin); and they give seed oils the opposite of the popular verdict, citing the biomarker analysis linking omega-6 to **lower** cardiovascular risk. [Does your metabolism change](metabolism-change-weight-loss.md) supplies the number underneath: expenditure falls ~**30 kcal/day per kg lost** while intake rises ~**100 kcal/day per kg lost**.

### Salt and fibre: the uncited tail

The [salt newsletter](newsletter-word-salt.md) is 712 words with **zero references** and two factual failures. **"Sea salt has about 10% as much sodium as table salt" is wrong by roughly tenfold** — both are predominantly sodium chloride by mass, and crystal size changes sodium per *teaspoon*, not per gram. "Most who train seriously will become hyponatremic after training" is an unhedged prevalence claim with nothing behind it. And advising a healthy person to *add* salt extrapolates from an absence of evidence for restriction, which is not evidence for supplementation. The [fibre guide](dietary-fiber-guide-viscosity-fermentability-gut-health.md) is the cleanest reframing in the set — drop soluble/insoluble for **viscosity and fermentability** — and the least sourced, four links across 2,229 words.

### The commercial layer, plainly

WheyRx recurs throughout, sometimes in the sentence carrying the recommendation: linked mid-paragraph in the [protein guide](barbell-medicine-guide-protein.md); named at the close of Rule 6 in the [2014 rules](rules-optimize-protein-intake.md), straight after whey is asserted superior to every other source; named twice in [protein on Ozempic](protein-ozempic-much-need.md); named in the [weight-loss guide](protein-weight-loss-maximizing-fat-loss-preserving.md) as meeting supplement criteria that same article had just set; and linked twice in [how to eat a healthy diet](eat-healthy-diet.md), whose most rigorous section reports ~20% of supplements contaminated before recommending two of their own.

It cuts both ways. Recommendation 4 of the protein guide states intakes to 4.4 g/kg are safe but improve nothing. The weight-loss guide spends 8,500 words concluding the flagship satiety mechanism for its own product does not hold up. Both collagen articles argue against a category they could stock. And their supplement criteria are checkable against any brand, including theirs.
