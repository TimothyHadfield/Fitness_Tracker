## Programming: rest, progression, cardio and time

### How long to rest between sets

Four videos across five years; the direction is stable, the precision keeps loosening. The [2020 deep dive](short-vs-long-rest-intervals-muscle-hypertrophy.md) sets the frame: on compounds, 2.5–3 minutes beat 1 minute or less in four of five training studies — Longo (28 untrained, 10 weeks), Schoenfeld 2016 (13 men, ≥6 months' experience, 8 weeks), Buresh (12 men, 10 weeks), Fink 2017 (14 untrained, 8 weeks). The fifth, Villanueva (22 men, mean age 68), favoured 1 minute, and he disqualifies it in one move: volume load was equated, so the short-rest group necessarily trained closer to failure. That is a proximity-to-failure result, not a rest result. The [2022 guide](optimal-rest-time-muscle-strength-12-studies.md) reorganises the same evidence on a better axis — rest tracks **how much muscle mass the exercise recruits**, not size versus strength — and kills the hormonal rationale: McKendry 2016 found protein synthesis higher at 5 minutes than 1, while acute testosterone was higher at 1 minute.

The [2024 meta-analysis](rest-periods-new-study-changes-things.md), the first on rest and hypertrophy, he uses to soften the number rather than harden it. Longer rest (>60 s) beats shorter (≤60 s) for arm and thigh growth, but split four ways the intermediate band has both the largest effect and the widest credible interval. The paper floats ~90 seconds as possibly enough; he says explicitly not to treat that as a conclusion, and names three unsuitable studies it pooled. **Current state: rest as long as you feel you need — longer on compounds, shorter on isolation.** He volunteers that nothing compares 2.5–3 minutes to 2 minutes, and that the isolation evidence is contradictory (Fink 2016 favours 30 s, Ozaki 2018 finds 3 min / 90 s / drop sets equivalent, Fink 2018 favours drop sets — two of those gave the short-rest arm extra sets).

The escape hatch matters more than the number: Longo's third arm ran **4.5 sets at 1 minute** and matched 3 sets at 3 minutes in equal session time. Short rest lowers yield per set; it does not forbid growth. That licenses [shrinking rest as a progression variable](decrease-rest-between-sets-overtime-more-hypertrophy.md) — two Souza-Junior studies (~11 per group, trained men, 8 weeks) found tapering 2 min to 30 s matched adding load, though he notes samples too small to trust either way.

### How to progress

Two trials converge. The [2022 RCT](best-progressive-overload-method.md) randomised trained lifters to load or rep progression: similar quad, gastrocnemius and soleus growth, a 2 kg edge to load on squat 1RM. The [2024 within-subject trial](best-progressive-overload-way.md) — **39 untrained people, unilateral leg extension, 4 sets, 2–3×/week for 10 weeks**, one leg adding load inside 9–12 reps, the other holding session-one load and drifting from 9 to 15 reps — found growth and strength similar, with genetics and nutrition designed out.

The reframe is the durable part: progressive overload is **not training harder than last time, it is what keeps each session as hard as usual** — evidence that adaptation happened, not its cause. Since 6–35 reps grow similarly near failure, rep progression runs out around 35 reps. His claim that adding sets or sessions "is not progressive overload" is a definitional move, not a finding, and his own cited set-progression study cuts against it. On maximal strength he rightly declines the null: both trials tested at eight-plus reps, and 2–4 reps beats 8–12 for 1RM.

Block organisation adds nothing for size. The 2022 Mosegaard meta-analysis found no hypertrophy difference [periodized versus not](periodization-size-vs-strength-science-says.md), and none between linear and undulating; its 1RM advantage carries a confound he raises himself — periodized arms did more heavy low-rep work — leaving only **undulating beating linear for 1RM in trained subjects**. [A strength block first](building-strength-first-enhances-muscle-growth-later.md) (Carvalho, 26 trained men, 3 weeks at 1–3 reps then 5 at 8–12) beat 8 weeks of 8–12 for quad growth, and he still declines to recommend it, rejecting the authors' "heavier load = more tension" mechanism because recruitment scales with effort, not absolute load. **That video's description carries no links** — the studies are named in prose only, so all are unverified.

### Does cardio cost you muscle

Not at studied volumes. [The interference effect has shrunk](combine-cardio-weights-should.md): updated meta-analyses find no compromise to hypertrophy or strength, including a trained-only subgroup, superseding Wilson 2012 — and Hickson 1980, the origin story, ran eleven sessions a week. He bounds it by the actual protocols (cardio 2–4×/week for 20–45 min; lifting 2–4×/week) and keeps three caveats: extreme volumes probably do interfere, "trained" is not "highly trained", and **power adaptations genuinely are compromised**. The recommendation: separate sessions, since a 2021 meta-analysis in trained individuals found same-session cardio lowered strength gains; if same session, lift first.

The reverse gets a harder no. [Lifting cannot replace cardio](lifting-replace-cardio-true-false.md): in 16 well-trained powerlifters and strongmen over 8 weeks, HIIT lifting at 60% 1RM raised VO2max less than duration-matched HIIT cycling. The inversion is easy to miss — **capillary and mitochondrial adaptations are local to the muscles trained**, so running does nothing for your upper body. [Cardio increases growth](cardio-increase-muscle-growth-here.md) is the weakest: the cycling → capillary → hypertrophy chain rests on **one 14-person untrained single-leg study** plus correlational and older-adult data, with fibre counts from biopsies he elsewhere calls unreliable. He volunteers the limitations; the title does not. The 21% versus 40% mortality contrast in both cardio notes is observational and hedged less than the training data.

### Training when time is short

**Antagonist supersets** are best supported ([11 studies](increase-performance-while-saving-time-11-studies.md)): minimal rest between the paired exercises reliably raises acute reps, 2+ minutes does not, but both halve session time (Robbins 2009: ~20 min versus ~10, 1RM gains not different). Long-term evidence is nearly absent, and Fink's 23 untrained band-training subjects numerically favoured the *traditional* group — which he presents in full. **Drop sets** now have the best data ([within-subject trial](they-trained-one-side-drop-sets-vs.md)): 29 trained lifters averaging 7.6 years, 10 weeks, one arm each way — thickness numerically favoured normal sets but sat inside the pre-declared smallest effect of interest, in **60% less time**; two meta-analyses of five studies each agree, at 30–70% savings. He discloses the semi-supervision and Tonal funding himself. **The 3/7 method** ([one study](3-7-method-build-more-muscle.md): 43 untrained men, 8 weeks) beat 8×6 for biceps thickness and 1RM in ~5–6 min per session against 19–20. **Pre-exhaustion** ([41 trained individuals, 8 weeks](training-style-intense-but-work.md)) cut duration ~36% but produced slightly *worse* growth at higher perceived exertion — and rests entirely on an unpublished preprint, the video's only source.

### Inter-set stretching

Three notes track a documented reversal over 30 months. September 2020 ([cautiously positive](stretching-between-sets-help-build-more-muscle.md)): Evangelista, 29 untrained men, 8 weeks, a 30-second pain-free stretch inside 90 seconds of rest — **only vastus lateralis reached significance**, not elbow flexors, triceps or rectus femoris. October 2021 ([two new studies](stretch-between-sets-more-hypertrophy.md)): Nakamura (16 untrained men, within-subject, flywheel squat, 5 weeks) found nothing at any of twelve regions; Wadhi (26 trained men, loaded cable chest stretch, 8 weeks) nothing at either pec region. March 2023 ([a fourth](stretching-between-sets-transform-muscle-strength.md)): 22 trained subjects, 8 weeks, 45-second stretches at 70–90% discomfort — nothing except lower long-head triceps, which he refuses to claim after reading the individual data points behind the group means.

Reconciled plainly: **three of five published studies find nothing, one finds a small single-region effect, the original finds one significant site of four, and none finds harm.** Strength is never compromised even though stretching groups lift lighter. The studies are too heterogeneous to pool — trained versus untrained, loaded versus unloaded, 30 versus 45 versus 60 s, pain-free versus 70–90% discomfort — so his verdict is right: *not validated*, not *refuted*. This is weak evidence in every direction. [Flexing between sets](flexing-between-sets-enhance-hypertrophy.md) resolves more cleanly: Schoenfeld 2020, 27 trained men, 8 weeks — nothing anywhere except mid-thigh, and only because squats and leg presses train rectus femoris poorly, and only after a post-hoc removal of two outliers he reports openly.

### When to back off

[Overtraining is hard to reach](overtraining-easier-think.md) is his most disciplined video: of 22 studies that deliberately tried to induce it, **10 produced no sustained performance decrease at all**, and 18 trained individuals doing **40+ weekly quad sets for two weeks** finished with leg press strength improved. What worked was 60 one-rep-max squat attempts a week for two weeks, taking 2–8 weeks to recover from — while testosterone stayed unchanged, which is why the field defines overtraining by recovery time, not markers. The real risk is **too much too soon**: unaccustomed maximal eccentric work caused months-long strength loss in six untrained men and one case of rhabdomyolysis in a single session. Two 12-week trials progressing trained lifters to **38–52 weekly quad sets** produced the best outcomes with no injury dropouts — evidence for specialisation phases, not high volume everywhere.

Breaks are safe, probably not magic. [Ogasawara](secret-long-term-muscle-strength-gains-maybe.md): 14 untrained men, 24 weeks, 6-on/3-off matched continuous training on **25% less work**, with continuous gains decelerating (~15% then ~5%) while the break group held ~15–20%. The resensitisation mechanism leads him to a **10-day break recommendation no trial has tested**, which he says repeatedly. The direct test since ([39 trained individuals, 9 weeks, week 5 off](train-less-more-muscle-new-training-breaks.md)) matched every lower-body growth measure on **40 fewer sets**, though strength was slightly worse. Take breaks when you want one, not on a calendar; deloads remain untested. [Psychological stress](minimize-max-muscle-strength.md) roughly doubled recovery time after a hard session (~96 h versus 48 h, 31 subjects) and cost 1RM over 12 weeks (81 men, 54 women) — **that video cites sources on screen only, with no reference list anywhere, so all of it is unverified**. [Morning versus evening](morning-vs-evening-training-muscle-hypertrophy.md) goes the other way: Grgic's 2019 meta-analysis found no hypertrophy difference and no strength difference once tests are averaged across times of day.

### Where to trust him, and where not

He is most rigorous when a result would flatter him, and the recurring move is spotting **which variable a study wrongly equated**: Villanueva equated volume load and so tested proximity to failure; the Prestes rest-pause control applied no progressive overload. His volume-load arithmetic — 3 sets to failure at 60% 1RM accumulate far more volume load than 3 sets at 80% while growing the same muscle — is the cleanest debunk in the domain, aimed at a paper other creators cite in his favour. He also dismantles the central-fatigue mechanism that would have *supported* his own long-rest conclusion, and in [the CNS fatigue video](most-misunderstood-muscle-building-area.md) flips a common belief on tight evidence: **lighter loads produce more CNS fatigue than heavier**, driven by contraction duration.

Where he overruns, the pattern is mechanism-forward videos with thin outcome data — the cardio-capillary chain, the untested 10-day break, the vertical-jump fatigue metric he recommends while admitting no study has tested it, and the 1–2 minute rest figures for women and older lifters extrapolated from acute data. Numbers travel further than their caveats. Several claims also rest on sources you cannot check: the strength-block-first and stress videos name studies with no links, the 2024 rest video relies on a creator-maintained Google Sites page rather than a description list, and the pre-exhaustion and drop-set videos rest on unpublished preprints.
