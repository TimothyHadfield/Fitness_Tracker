## How much, how often, and how hard

### What he actually concludes about weekly sets

There are two answers and he knows it: the **analysis** says more is more, the **recommendation** says 10–20.

[Schoenfeld/Ogborn/Krieger 2017](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md) pooled 15 studies for roughly **0.37% more growth per added weekly set** — but mostly untrained subjects and almost no arm above 9–12 sets, so he narrows it to "9–12 beats less, in beginners." [The 2024 meta-regression](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md) (Pelland et al., **35 studies, 1,000+ subjects**) then found a square-root curve with **no plateau and no inverted U**, climbing to **43 weekly sets per muscle**, later sets worth about **one third** of the first. [The companion per-session analysis](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md) found the same shape per session, still rising past 20, with the researchers' uncertainty threshold at **11 sets per session and 31 per week**. And [Enes 2024](2-2x-more-muscle-new-study-surprising.md) — 31 trained men, 12 weeks, progressing from 22 to **42 or 52 weekly quad sets** — beat a fixed 22 on cross-sectional area and squat 1RM.

Against that he recommends **10–20 weekly sets per muscle**, "if I had to guess," after training quality is fixed. He is candid that this is his prior, not the paper's output — [the curve does not bend at 20](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md) — but a skimming viewer hears "the data says 10–20" when it does not. Two mismatches sit underneath: the meta-regression's winning counting scheme was **fractional** (indirect sets count 0.5), so 10–20 fractional sets is more work than 10–20 counted traditionally, which he does not say; and his 2021 position of **12–18** with long rests and **30–45** with short ones was hedged as "probably not practical or sustainable" — [a dose only defensible under conditions he advises against](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md).

His reason for overriding the curve is the strongest thing in this domain: **a fair number of the pooled studies used incomplete programs**, training one or a handful of muscle groups, so systemic fatigue and recovery demand do not resemble a real program. He says he "probably should have done a better job at emphasizing" this earlier. It lands hardest on the headline results — Enes trained **quads only**, and [that is the very study he cites as finding benefit at high volume with long rests](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md). His calibrated position: **confident moderate beats low even in a complete program, not confident very high beats moderate.** Durations averaging **~10 weeks** carry the rest.

### Rest interval as the hidden variable — and the position he retired

His sharpest original contribution — and the arc ends with him abandoning it.

In [the 2021 ultimate guide](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md) he counted **exactly six trials** in trained lifters testing above 9–12 weekly sets, splitting three-three. **Brigatto** (27 men, 16/24/32 sets, 8 wk, **60 s rest**), **Radaelli** (48 men, up to 45 triceps sets, **6 months**, **90–120 s**) and **Schoenfeld 2019** (34 men, ~4.4 yr, up to 45 quad sets, 8 wk, **90 s**) favoured the highest volume. **Heaselgrave** (49 men, 9/18/27 biceps sets, 6 wk, **3 min**), **Ostrowski** (35 men, 7/14/28 triceps sets, 10 wk, **3 min**) and **Aube** (35 men, 12/18/24 quad sets, 8 wk, **2 min**) found a plateau at 12–18. The correlation is perfect: every high-volume win used short rest, every plateau long rest. The mechanism is **Longo** — 3 leg-press sets with 3 min beat 3 with 1 min, but **4–5 sets with 1 min matched 3 with 3 min** — so the six trials become one finding in two currencies. He used it to attack the [2022 Baz-Valle review for pooling short- and long-rest studies](many-sets-per-muscle-examine-science.md), and to insist any per-session ceiling carry its rest interval: **15–16 sets was productive with short rests**, against a mechanistic **10–12** with long rests drawn only from acute protein synthesis. [In the splits video he pushed that to 6–10](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md), labelled it speculation, and supplied the counterexample — Bartolomei's bro-split group did **20 quadriceps sets in one session** with 2 min rest and still won.

Then the 2025 per-session meta-analysis ran rest interval as a moderator and the relationship **held across both shorter and longer rests**. [He calls this out and retires his own position](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md), having already declined to bank the 2024 moderator that pointed his way [because the authors called it exploratory and accepting it would force him to conclude that very high volumes with one-minute rests are optimal](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md). Correct in both directions — and the honest summary is that the hypothesis was always a **post-hoc regrouping of six trials**, never a tested comparison, and the one analysis that tested it did not support it.

### Where the Barbalho retraction bears

The "less is more" leg of this dispute was largely the Barbalho papers: twin trials in trained men and untrained women, every muscle trained **once weekly** at 5, 10, 15 or 20 sets — so weekly sets equalled per-session sets — concluding **5–10 sufficed and 15–20 were slightly worse**. [He states plainly that both are retracted, probably because the data were not legitimate](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md), citing Vigotsky and Stronger by Science, and **removes them from an argument they would have supported** — he was at that moment building the case for a per-session ceiling. The clearest instance of the channel's core virtue.

The "5 beat 20" position therefore loses its primary evidence. What remains against high volume is the three long-rest plateau trials (**8–10 weeks**, n=35–49), two **German Volume Training** trials (n=19 and 12) in which [5 sets beat or tied 10 but **neither group showed significant ultrasound hypertrophy**](german-volume-training-actually-build-muscle-fast.md) — detection failure more than ranking — plus two unpublished nulls he volunteers. The strongest surviving objection was **swelling** — that studies measuring 48–72 h post-session read residual fluid as growth. [Alvarez tested it directly](many-sets-muscle-growth.md) (13 trained men, ~5.1 yr, crossover, 7 vs 14 vs 21 sets, measured to 72 h): **no differences between set numbers, swelling resolved by 24 hours**. He reports **no effect sizes, confidence intervals or power discussion** for that null: the hedge does more work than the evidence.

### Frequency: the variable that keeps coming back empty

With weekly sets equated, frequency does almost nothing. The Pelland analysis found [**no consistent or large effect** — 1×→2× a slight increase compatible with nothing, nothing beyond 2×, and the "high frequency only wins at recoverable volumes" defence failing in both bins](often-should-train-muscle-new-study-epic.md); he is **"genuinely surprised"**. Below ~12 weekly sets the picture is clean ([Schoenfeld 2019, 13 studies, 11 at ≤12 sets](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md)) and bro splits are not inferior; above 12, five studies give four answers. The mechanism collapses too: [the MPS argument runs on *mixed* protein synthesis, which does not track myofibrillar MPS](high-frequency-training-more-frequent-muscle-protein.md), whose damage-free readings stay elevated 48–72 h — arguing for *lower* frequency, which he also refuses. [Overtraining is likewise hard to induce: 10 of 22 studies that tried produced nothing qualifying](scientifically-dismantling-mike-mentzer-claims.md), and [24-hour spacing tested fine](long-should-wait-train-muscle-again.md). Frequency is a **container for volume**, [possibly an individual-response variable](best-training-frequency.md); **2×/week** is a hedge, not a finding.

### Effort is a separate question, and the answer is narrower

Failure is not required, and the claim is small: **3–1 RIR matches failure for hypertrophy.** [Four longitudinal studies say so](training-failure-must-hypertrophy.md) — Lacerda (n=10), Nóbrega (n=32) and Sampson (n=28), all untrained over 12–14 weeks, plus **Santanielo** (14 men, ~5.1 yr, within-subject, 10 wk), the only trained entry. The best test since is [an 18-subject within-subject trial averaging **7.7 years' experience**](hard-should-workout-new-study-epic.md): failure vs 1–2 RIR, 8 weeks, 10–17 weekly quad sets, similar growth, with **verified RIR accuracy** and individualised set counts.

The bounds matter more than the headline. The null holds at **6–19 weekly sets**; at **~2 weekly sets** Giessing found failure better, and [the minimum-dose video reverses his usual position on that ground](build-muscle-while-barely-training-using-science.md). Beyond 3 RIR is **unresolved, not permitted**: [four studies, three directions, one confounded by unequal rest (Karsten: 2 vs 1 min), one by concurrent sprint training (Carroll)](leaving-more-3-reps-reserve-vs-training.md). Where higher RIR survives, **extra sets compensate** — Lasevicius needed 2–3 extra sets for 5 RIR to match failure, and 14 RIR could not be rescued at all. He also retracted his own claim that velocity data licensed >3 RIR, binning a 6,000-word script. And [the 2023 meta-regression that appeared to favour failure is dismantled from the inside](must-train-failure-build-muscle.md): the benefit shrank with heavier loads, higher frequency, more weekly sets and longer durations, the null inside the interval each time — and studies using a **strict** failure definition showed *less* benefit than those using vague "volitional failure" — the wrong way round if failure is the driver.

### How well can anyone actually judge RIR?

Better under instruction than assumed, badly in habitual practice. [Halperin's pooled 12 studies found an average **1-rep underestimation** — but individuals out by **10–13 reps**](become-laser-accurate-leaving-reps-reserve.md). Accuracy improves with heavier loads (past ~12 reps underestimation grows), with estimates made *during* rather than before a set, and on upper body; training years barely helped. Against that, [Hackett 2017 had 81 adults predict 5–6 more reps when they could do 10](should-train-failure-muscle-growth.md), while [the 2025 varied-RIR trial measured a worst average error of **−1.5 reps**, improving to −1 on retest](train-easier-size-strength-new-study-interesting.md). The reconciliation: people judge proximity reasonably when *trying to*, and poorly when just training — which makes occasional failure sets **calibration, not stimulus**, and means "3 RIR" silently degrades into junk volume for anyone who never checks.

Two points close it. **Volume load is the wrong currency** — [matched volume load produced very unequal growth in Lasevicius](volume-load-not-drive-muscle-hypertrophy.md) — so count **hard sets of 5–35 reps within ~3 of failure**. And effort cannot be bought structurally: [rest-pause, drop sets and clusters only reach parity](normal-sets-vs-rest-pause-vs-drop.md), [buying time rather than stimulus](science-drop-sets-vs-normal-sets-hypertrophy.md).

### Rigour and reach

He is rigorous three ways: discarding retracted data that helps him, retiring his own rest-interval and >3 RIR claims, and volunteering unpublished nulls against his own findings. Where he outruns: the 30–45 set figure was never usable; the swelling null rests on n=13 with no effect sizes; the individual-differences case is built almost entirely on **untrained** subjects and its transfer asserted rather than shown. Two claims rest on videos with **no retrievable reference list** — [the 27-study HIT video](1-set-failure-hit-vs-high-volume.md), and [the minimum-dose video, including its correction that fewer than five weekly sets yield **53%** of the growth of more than ten](build-muscle-while-barely-training-using-science.md), a figure conflicting with the uncorrected 64%/84% numbers held elsewhere in this library and unverifiable from the source.
