## How much, how often, and how hard

### What he actually concludes about weekly sets

There are two answers and he knows it. The **analysis** says more is more. The **recommendation** says 10–20.

The ladder runs like this. [Schoenfeld/Ogborn/Krieger 2017](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md) pooled 15 studies for roughly **0.37% more growth per added weekly set**, 9+ sets beating fewer — but mostly untrained subjects and almost no arm above 9–12 sets, so he narrows it to "9–12 beats less, in beginners." [The 2024 meta-regression](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md) (Pelland et al., **35 studies, 1,000+ subjects**) then found a square-root curve with **no plateau and no inverted U**, climbing to **43 weekly sets per muscle**, later sets worth about **one third** of the first. [The companion per-session analysis](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md) found the same shape per session, still rising past 20, with the researchers' uncertainty threshold at **11 sets per session and 31 per week**. And [Enes 2024](2-2x-more-muscle-new-study-surprising.md) — 31 trained men, 12 weeks, progressing from 22 to **42 or 52 weekly quad sets** — beat a fixed 22 on cross-sectional area and back squat 1RM.

Against that he recommends **10–20 weekly sets per muscle**, "if I had to guess," after training quality is fixed. He is candid that this is his prior, not the paper's output — [the curve does not bend at 20](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md) — but a viewer skimming hears "the data says 10–20" when it does not. Two mismatches sit underneath. The meta-regression's winning counting scheme was **fractional** (indirect sets count 0.5), so 10–20 fractional sets is more work than 10–20 counted traditionally, and he does not say so when giving the number. And his 2021 position was **12–18** with long rests and **30–45** with short ones, then hedged as "probably not practical or sustainable" — [a dose only defensible under conditions he advises against](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md).

His reason for overriding the curve is the strongest thing in this domain and easy to miss: **a fair number of the pooled studies used incomplete programs**, training one or a handful of muscle groups, so systemic fatigue and recovery demand do not resemble a real program. He says explicitly he "probably should have done a better job at emphasizing" this in earlier videos. It lands hardest on the headline results — Enes trained **quads only**, and [that is the very study he cites as finding benefit at high volume with long rests](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md). His calibrated position: **confident moderate beats low even in a complete program, not confident very high beats moderate.** Durations averaging **~10 weeks** and varying failure definitions carry the rest of the doubt.

### Rest interval as the hidden variable — and the position he retired

This is his sharpest original contribution, and the arc is worth recording in full because it ends with him abandoning it.

In [the 2021 ultimate guide](ultimate-guide-many-sets-optimize-muscle-hypertrophy.md) he counted **exactly six trials** in trained lifters testing above 9–12 weekly sets, splitting three-three. **Brigatto** (27 men, ~3.5 yr, 16/24/32 sets, 8 wk, **60 s rest**), **Radaelli** (48 men, up to 45 triceps sets, **6 months**, **90–120 s**) and **Schoenfeld 2019** (34 men, ~4.4 yr, up to 45 quad sets, 8 wk, **90 s**) favoured the highest volume. **Heaselgrave** (49 men, 9/18/27 biceps sets, 6 wk, **3 min**), **Ostrowski** (35 men, 7/14/28 triceps sets, 10 wk, **3 min**) and **Aube** (35 men, ≥3 yr, 12/18/24 quad sets, 8 wk, **2 min**) found a plateau at 12–18. The correlation is perfect: every high-volume win used short rest, every plateau long rest. The mechanism is **Longo** — 3 leg-press sets with 3 min beat 3 with 1 min, but **4–5 sets with 1 min matched 3 sets with 3 min** — so the six trials become the same finding in two currencies. He used it to attack the [2022 Baz-Valle review for pooling short- and long-rest studies](many-sets-per-muscle-examine-science.md), and to insist any per-session ceiling be quoted with its rest interval attached: **15–16 sets per session was productive with short rests**, against a mechanistic **10–12** with long rests drawn from rat protein synthesis (Ogasawara) and human myofibrillar MPS (Damas). [In the splits video he pushed the long-rest ceiling to 6–10 sets](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md), labelled it speculation, and supplied the counterexample that breaks it — Bartolomei's bro-split group did **20 quadriceps sets in one session** with 2 min rest and still won.

Then the 2025 per-session meta-analysis ran rest interval as a moderator and the relationship **held across both shorter and longer rests**. [He calls this out and retires his own position](many-sets-per-workout-new-study-epic.md). He had already declined to bank the 2024 moderator that pointed his way, [because the authors called it exploratory and accepting it would force him to conclude that very high volumes with one-minute rests are optimal overall](more-sets-more-growth-new-study-surprising.md). Correct handling in both directions — and the honest summary is that the hypothesis was always a **post-hoc regrouping of six trials**, never a tested comparison, and the one analysis that tested it did not support it.

### Where the Barbalho retraction bears

The "less is more" leg of this dispute was largely the Barbalho papers: identically designed trials in trained men and untrained women, every muscle trained **once weekly** at 5, 10, 15 or 20 sets — so weekly sets equalled per-session sets — concluding **5–10 sufficed and 15–20 were slightly worse**. [He states plainly that both are retracted, probably because the data were not legitimate](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md), citing Vigotsky's improbable-data analysis and Stronger by Science, and **removes them from an argument they would have supported** — he was at that moment building the case for a per-session ceiling. That is the clearest instance of the channel's core virtue.

The "5 beat 20" position therefore loses its primary evidence. What remains against high volume is the three long-rest plateau trials (**8–10 weeks**, n=35–49), two **German Volume Training** trials in which [5 sets beat or tied 10](german-volume-training-actually-build-muscle-fast.md) — 19 and 12 subjects, and **neither found significant ultrasound hypertrophy in either group**, closer to a detection failure than a ranking — plus two unpublished studies he volunteers as null at very high volumes. The strongest surviving objection was **swelling**: that studies measuring 48–72 h post-session read residual fluid as growth. [Alvarez et al. tested it directly](many-sets-muscle-growth.md) — 13 trained men, ~5.1 yr, crossover, 7 vs 14 vs 21 sets, thickness and echo intensity at 24/48/72 h — and found **no differences between set numbers, swelling resolved by 24 hours**. He reports **no effect sizes, confidence intervals or power discussion** for that null, the clearest reporting weakness in the set: the hedge does more work than the evidence.

### Frequency: the variable that keeps coming back empty

With weekly sets equated, frequency does almost nothing. The same Pelland analysis found [**no consistent or large effect** — 1×→2× a slight increase whose credible intervals are compatible with nothing, no benefit beyond 2×, and the "high frequency only wins at recoverable volumes" defence failing in both bins](often-should-train-muscle-new-study-epic.md). He says outright he is **"genuinely surprised"**. Below ~12 weekly sets the picture is clean — [Schoenfeld 2019, 13 studies, no frequency effect, 11 of them at ≤12 weekly sets](comparing-training-splits-muscle-growth-20-studies.md) — so bro splits are not inferior there; above 12, five studies give four answers, Zaroni and Bartolomei reaching **opposite conclusions on vastus lateralis** in near-identical subjects. The mechanistic case collapses too: [the MPS argument runs on *mixed* protein synthesis, which does not track myofibrillar MPS](high-frequency-training-more-frequent-muscle-protein.md), whose cleanest damage-free readings stay elevated 48–72 h — arguing for *lower* frequency, a conclusion he also refuses. Frequency is a **container for volume**; **at least 2×/week** is a hedge, not a finding.

### Effort is a separate question, and the answer is narrower

Failure is not required, and the claim is deliberately small: **3–1 RIR matches failure for hypertrophy.** [Four longitudinal studies say so](training-failure-must-hypertrophy.md) — Lacerda (10 untrained, 14 wk), Nóbrega (32 untrained, 12 wk), Sampson (28 untrained, 12 wk) and **Santanielo** (14 men, ~5.1 yr, within-subject, 10 wk), the only trained entry. The best test since is [an 18-subject within-subject trial averaging **7.7 years' experience**, half competitive powerlifters or bodybuilders](hard-should-workout-new-study-epic.md): failure versus 1–2 RIR, 8 weeks, 10–17 weekly quad sets, similar growth, with verified RIR accuracy, individualised set counts and nutrition targeted at 1% monthly gain.

The bounds matter more than the headline. The null holds at roughly **6–19 weekly sets**; at **~2 weekly sets** Giessing found failure better, and [the minimum-dose video reverses his usual position on that ground](build-muscle-while-barely-training-using-science.md). Beyond 3 RIR is **unresolved, not permitted**: [four studies, three directions, one confounded by unequal rest (Karsten: 2 vs 1 min), one by concurrent sprint training (Carroll)](leaving-more-3-reps-reserve-vs-training.md). Where higher RIR survives, **extra sets compensate** — Lasevicius needed 2–3 extra sets for 5 RIR to match failure, and 14 RIR could not be rescued at all. He also retracted his own earlier claim that velocity-based data licensed >3 RIR, binning a 6,000-word script. And [the 2023 meta-regression that appeared to favour failure is dismantled from the inside](must-train-failure-build-muscle.md): the benefit shrank with heavier loads, higher frequency, more weekly sets and longer durations, the null inside the interval each time — and studies using a **strict** failure definition showed *less* benefit than those using vague "volitional failure," the wrong way round if failure is the driver.

### How well can anyone actually judge RIR?

Better than assumed under instruction, badly in habitual practice. [Halperin's pooled 12 studies found an average **1-rep underestimation** — but individuals out by **10–13 reps**](become-laser-accurate-leaving-reps-reserve.md). Accuracy improves with heavier loads (past ~12 reps underestimation grows), with estimates made *during* rather than before a set, in later sets, and on upper body over lower. Years of training barely helped; his inference that experience *with failure specifically* is what matters is labelled as his own. Against that, [Hackett 2017 had 81 adults predict 5–6 more reps when they could do 10](should-train-failure-muscle-growth.md), and [the 2025 varied-RIR trial measured a worst average error of **−1.5 reps**, improving to about −1 on retest](train-easier-size-strength-new-study-interesting.md). The reconciliation is clean: people judge proximity reasonably when *trying to*, and poorly when just training — which makes occasional failure sets **calibration, not stimulus**, and means the "3 RIR" prescription silently degrades into junk volume for anyone who never checks.

Two structural points close it. **Volume load is the wrong metric** — [four lines break it, decisively Lasevicius, where matched volume load produced very unequal growth](volume-load-not-drive-muscle-hypertrophy.md) — so the currency is **hard sets of 5–35 reps within ~3 of failure**. And no set structure buys more than normal sets: [rest-pause, drop sets and clusters reach parity](normal-sets-vs-rest-pause-vs-drop.md), their value being **time** — [one drop set matched three normal sets at ~2 minutes versus ~7](science-drop-sets-vs-normal-sets-hypertrophy.md).

### Rigour and reach

He is rigorous three ways: discarding retracted data that helps him, retiring his own rest-interval and >3 RIR claims when the evidence turned, and volunteering unpublished nulls against his own exciting findings. Where he outruns: the 30–45 set figure was never usable; the swelling null rests on n=13 with no effect sizes; the individual-differences case is built almost entirely on **untrained** subjects and asserted rather than shown to transfer. Two claims rest on videos with **no retrievable reference list** — [the 27-study HIT video, whose description carries only history links](1-set-failure-hit-vs-high-volume.md), and [the minimum-dose video, including its central correction that fewer than five weekly sets yield **53%** of the growth of more than ten](build-muscle-while-barely-training-using-science.md). That corrected figure conflicts with the uncorrected 64%/84% numbers held elsewhere in this library, and cannot be verified from the source.
