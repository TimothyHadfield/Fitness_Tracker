## Load and rep ranges

### The band, and where it moved

The channel's most repeated number moved twice. The 2020 version: [The hypertrophy rep range is roughly 5 to 35](best-rep-range-build-muscle.md) reads Schoenfeld's 2017 meta-analysis as spanning 30–85% of 1RM — "roughly 5 to 35 reps" — provided sets reach failure. It held two years.

In April 2022 he raised the floor on camera. [Are sets of 5 reps enough](5-reps-maximize-hypertrophy.md) walks back his own position on Vann et al.: 15 trained men (average squat 167 kg), within-subject, 3×/week for 6 weeks, one leg at 5 reps and 82.5→95% 1RM near failure, the other at 10 reps and a fixed 60% with ~10 reps in reserve. The light leg grew more vastus lateralis CSA; the heavy leg averaged **no gain at all** — while doing *more* weekly sets (18 vs 15) and training *closer* to failure. New advice: **8–35 reps**.

Five months later it came back down and stopped. [4 vs 8 vs 12 reps](4-vs-8-vs-12-reps-strength.md) states the position he has held since: **6–35 reps are similarly effective per set; 3–5 reps work but are less efficient per set and need extra sets; 1–2 reps may not build muscle at all.** [The whole rep-range literature in one place](many-reps-muscle-growth-40-studies.md) (2024, five meta-analyses) defends 6–35 against the 5–30 others quote, arguing it better describes the reps actually performed.

**He landed on 6–35.** [Can sets of 3–5 reps maximise growth](3-5-reps-truly-maximize-growth.md) (August 2025) covers a bioRxiv preprint — n=14, 2+ years trained, within-subject, 3–5 vs 20–25 reps to failure, 3 sets at 2 min rest, 9 weeks — finding similar vastus lateralis growth (7.4% and 8.5% at two regions). He will not lower the minimum on two supporting studies, says he *hopes* it comes down, and tells anyone already progressing below 6 reps to change nothing.

### Why he rejects the low-rep studies

This is the sharpest argument in the domain, and it pins the floor. [Can reps below 5 maximise growth](reps-below-5-maximize-muscle-growth.md) tallies six studies comparing 2–5 reps against moderate or high reps; five found parity. Four of those five gave the low-rep group more sets, longer rest, or both — usually because researchers equated *volume load*. Mangine (33 men, 2+ years trained), 3 min rest against 1 min; Schoenfeld 2014 (17 trained), **7 sets of 3 against 3 sets of 10** at 3 min against 90 s; Campos (27 untrained), 4 sets/3 min against 2 sets/1 min; Chestnut & Docherty (19 untrained), 6 sets/3 min against 3 sets/2 min.

Two clean comparisons remain, and they contradict. Weiss 2000 — 44 untrained men, 4 sets of squat, 6 min rest throughout, 7 weeks — found 3–5 reps as good as 13–15 and 23–25. Schoenfeld 2016 — 19 trained men, 3 sets, 2 min rest, 8 weeks — found 2–4 reps grew *less* vastus lateralis and elbow flexor than 8–12.

The inference, put plainly in [Can sets of 3–5 reps maximise growth](3-5-reps-truly-maximize-growth.md), is that those confounds run *toward* the low-rep group, so they cut against the low-rep case rather than for it: handed extra sets and longer rest, ≤5 reps should have *beaten* higher reps if it were equally potent per set, and it only tied. He also dismantles the volume-load equating behind the confound — 6–35 reps grow similarly at matched sets despite very different volume loads, so equating it introduced a confound rather than removing one — the same move he runs in [Adding load vs adding reps](adding-more-load-repetitions-better-hypertrophy-strength.md) against a pooled n=14-vs-10 re-analysis.

Below the floor it is firmer: in [Maxing out](maxing-out-effective-building-muscle-one-rep.md), Dankel (n=5, 21 consecutive days) and Mattocks (38 untrained, 8 weeks) both had 1RM-only training match full training for strength with near-zero thickness change — singles build the skill, not the tissue. The ceiling is softer — Lasevicius (untrained, 12 weeks) found 40/60/80% 1RM similar and all better than 20% (60+ reps, half the growth), yet a curl trial averaging **100 reps on set one** matched 80%, and [How light is too light](light-too-light-maximizing-muscle-growth.md) leaves sub-30% at two studies for and two against, refusing to resolve it. **No reference list** there.

### Fibre type, and whether rep range varies by muscle

It does not. [The optimal rep range for each muscle](optimal-rep-range-each-muscle-true.md) and [Training by fibre type](should-train-muscles-according-fiber-type.md) both centre on Schoenfeld 2020: 26 untrained men, within-subject, one leg 4×20–30 and the other 4×6–10 to momentary failure on calf raises, 2×/week for 10 weeks. Soleus and both gastrocnemius heads grew equally — and the soleus is where the hypothesis predicts most strongly. Grgic's five-study meta-analysis adds the fibre level: type I and type II growth comparable across loads, confidence intervals wide and flagged. Sixteen histochemical surveys show most muscles sit near 50/50 anyway, and the rep-count self-test fails (Terzis 2008, r < 0.2 against biopsies). His closing line that fibre type "is not a consideration" in programming outruns one 8-week untrained calf study: it shows *load prescription* need not track fibre type, nothing more.

The regional version is thinner: [Heavy and light loads and regional growth](heavy-light-weights-produce-different-regional-muscle.md) rests on Hisaeda 1996, **11 untrained women**, whose groups differed in reps, sets, rest *and* tempo at once — he breaks his own fibre-type explanation on the data and still recommends load variety. **No reference list.**

### Combining and alternating

Permitted, not prescribed. [Alternating rep ranges](alternating-rep-ranges-improves-muscle-growth-18.md) is titled "18 studies" and rests on three with MRI or ultrasound: Schoenfeld (19 trained men, 8 weeks, constant 8–12 vs 2–4/8–12/20–30 weekly), De Souza (26 untrained, 6 weeks), Fink (21 untrained, within-arm, 8 weeks). All null. He goes past "not significant" to Krieger's individual-data reanalysis, then calls the residual triceps signal noise. **No retrievable reference list; studies listed unverified.** The contrary result — Kraemer 2003, 27 untrained women, nine months — is by **skinfold calipers**, discounted on measurement, duration aside. [The benefits of combining rep ranges](benefits-combining-rep-ranges.md) is where framing outruns evidence: he kills the fibre-type rationale, concedes the hypertrophy data show no difference, then argues from neural recruitment, capillarisation and pain tolerance. The defensible part is concurrent development of *other* attributes — 1RM and bone density from heavy work, local endurance and mitochondrial content from light (Lim 2019).

And the average hides people. [The best rep range isn't what you think](best-rep-range-muscle-growth-isn-t.md) — 24 untrained women, mean age 58, 24 weeks, 12 at 8–12 reps and 12 at 27–31 — found comparable means over individuals who diverged wildly (one −5% then +10%, another +13% then +3%). He rejects the authors' plateau-delay reading with a worked two-subject example: rotation is insurance for the uncertain, not a plateau-breaker. The design cannot separate response from measurement error, which he concedes in 2024 via the Robinson critique.

## Nutrition, evidence and how he reads a study

### Protein: the number, and the scatter behind it

[The protein myth](protein-myth-much-really-need.md) argues against its own headline. Three meta-analyses put the plateau at 1.5–1.6 g/kg/day; nitrogen balance and IAAO land at 1.5–2.0. Crediting Eric Trexler, he strips the fitted line off the 2018 dataset: above 1.25 g/kg, protein explains **1% of the variance** in fat-free mass change (r² = 0.011). So he gives a spectrum instead of a number — 1.2–1.59 possibly enough, 1.6–2.0 almost certainly, above 2.0 more than enough. Experience does not raise it (Lemon 1992: similar gains at 1.35 and 2.62 g/kg; Bandegan 2017: 1.7 g/kg in eight near-maximal bodybuilders). Only the cutting advice outruns its evidence, as he says: Mettler and Longland both used **40% deficits** against 1.0–1.2 g/kg control arms.

[The protein limit for a meal](protein-limit-meal-they-were-wrong.md) is looser. The 20 g ceiling came from trials training one muscle with one or two exercises; Trommelen's 100 g dose produced greater, longer-lasting MPS with small oxidation. But he then argues MPS is a poor proxy for growth, undercutting his own title: the three growth trials show only that three servings slightly beat two.

### Pre-sleep protein: the confound is the whole story

[Pre-sleep protein disappears once total intake is matched](protein-before-bed-really-work.md) is the cleanest teardown on the channel. The 2021 Reis review everyone cites **did not control total daily protein**: its key trial gave the pre-sleep group 27.5 g extra against a non-caloric placebo, leaving them at **1.9 vs 1.3 g/kg/day**. Nothing there separates "protein before bed" from "more protein". Three trials that did match intake — n=26 trained (54 g casein, 8 weeks), n=13 recreationally active (35 g, 10 weeks), n=42 untrained (whey, 6 weeks) — found no significant differences, with directions flipping between measures — small-sample noise, as he says. Casein is not special either: same area under the amino acid curve as whey. His own flag is right — three underpowered trials give absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

### Creatine: a trivial effect size, relabelled

[The science behind creatine](science-behind-creatine-much-more-muscle-strength.md) is where a conclusion most clearly outruns its measurement. Five meta-analyses put the lean-mass benefit at 1–1.4 kg over 7–14 weeks, but lean mass includes water. The informative analysis pools 10 studies measuring directly by ultrasound or MRI: creatine adds **0.10–0.16 cm of thickness**, **effect size 0.11** — which those authors called trivial. His objection is legitimate, since those thresholds came from strength and power research; his replacement is not. He takes thresholds from Greg Nuckols' **Stronger by Science blog post**, under which 0.11 reads small-to-medium, then benchmarks against a literature average to reach "**about one-third faster muscle gain**." That is a relabelling against a non-peer-reviewed threshold set; the measurement is still 0.10–0.16 cm. He shows his working, and elsewhere reports the spread honestly — ES 0.23 in young adults against 0.06 in older ones, and a hair-loss scare traced to one unreplicated n=20 study measuring **serum** rather than **scalp** DHT.

### EMG, and the position he later softened

[Four reasons EMG can't rank exercises](ryan-humiston-has-made-mistake-emg.md) is his hardest methodological line: EMG measures neuromuscular excitation, not activation; it is unreliable even at that (constant electrical stimulation at three joint angles gave different amplitudes); it reads higher with heavier loads that grow no more muscle; and blind to passive tension. Verdict: EMG exercise rankings have "a high probability of just being plain wrong." That position did not hold. By his 2024 chest guide he argues EMG **is** informative for subtle comparisons — grip widths, bench angles, biomechanically similar presses — while staying unreliable across exercises differing in muscle length, and his upper-chest content leans on EMG throughout. The two are reconcilable, but the change is real — a live inconsistency across the channel, which he flags himself.

### Sampling variance is his master key

[3 sets is not better than 1 set](3-sets-not-better-1-set.md) is his most methodologically careful video. Pinto 2025 (15 untrained men, unilateral pec deck, 2 vs 6 weekly sets, 12 weeks) found similar growth — which he argues is *compatible with* three sets being better, via Krieger's simulation: sampling a population with known true effects, only one of five ten-per-group studies resembles the truth; pooling recovers it. The same restraint runs the round-ups: [2022](best-muscle-growth-strength-research-2022.md) flags a stretch result as an unpublished conference abstract, [2024](most-important-muscle-building-studies-2024.md) retires lengthened partials as superior on two trials (one near 300 subjects), and [Popular myths](popular-muscle-strength-building-myths.md) falsifies "strength causes size" with the 1RM-only studies.

[Defending science-based lifting](defending-science-based-lifting.md) turns the lens on the field; its concessions make it worth keeping: **85% of the 20 most-cited strength and conditioning meta-analyses contained at least one statistical error**, and he found a d_z effect-size error himself in a 2022 protein meta-analysis, which James Steele is now redoing. Weak spots: the "research has delivered" list is drawn entirely from settled, comfortable findings, and he rates recent meta-analyses "exceptional" without showing his work. **That note gives surnames and bare links only.**

Where the instinct fails: [This holds back your potential](holds-back-muscle-strength-potential.md) and [Turkesterone works](turkesterone-works-but-not-reasons-think.md) lean on placebo-steroid studies with **n=6 and n=11**, decades old, one with no comparison group, neither measuring hypertrophy — and returns to close [You cannot gain much more muscle](cannot-gain-much-more-muscle-16-studies.md), an acute strength result arguing about long-term growth. [Jeremy Ethier's new study](need-talk-about-jeremy-ethier-new-study.md) surfaces growth values "upwards of 100%", says he cannot locate the measurement error, and moves on. **Neither of those has a reference list.**
