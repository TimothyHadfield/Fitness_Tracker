## Load and rep ranges

### The band, and where it moved

Nothing on the channel is repeated more often than the equivalent rep range, and the number moved twice.

The first version is 2020. [The hypertrophy rep range is roughly 5 to 35](best-rep-range-build-muscle.md) reads Schoenfeld's 2017 meta-analysis as spanning 30–85% of 1RM — "roughly 5 to 35 reps" — provided sets reach failure. That held for two years, restated in [Maxing out](maxing-out-effective-building-muscle-one-rep.md) and [How light is too light](light-too-light-maximizing-muscle-growth.md).

In April 2022 he raised the floor on camera. [Are sets of 5 reps enough](5-reps-maximize-hypertrophy.md) walks back his own position on Vann et al.: 15 trained men (average squat 167 kg), within-subject, unilateral leg press and extension, 3×/week for 6 weeks. One leg did 5 reps at 82.5→95% 1RM near failure; the other 10 reps at a fixed 60% with roughly 10 reps in reserve. The light leg grew more vastus lateralis CSA; the heavy leg averaged **no gain at all** — while doing *more* weekly sets (18 vs 15) and training *closer* to failure. New advice: **8–35 reps**.

Five months later the floor came back down and stopped there. [4 vs 8 vs 12 reps](4-vs-8-vs-12-reps-strength.md) states the position he has held since: **6–35 reps are similarly effective per set; 3–5 reps work but are less efficient per set and need extra sets; 1–2 reps may not build muscle at all.** [The whole rep-range literature in one place](many-reps-muscle-growth-40-studies.md) (2024, five meta-analyses) defends 6–35 against the 5–30 others quote, arguing it better describes the reps actually performed in the trials.

**He landed on 6–35.** [Can sets of 3–5 reps maximise growth](3-5-reps-truly-maximize-growth.md) (August 2025) covers a bioRxiv preprint — n=14, 2+ years trained, within-subject, 3–5 vs 20–25 reps to failure, both 3 sets at 2 min rest, 9 weeks — finding similar vastus lateralis growth (7.4% and 8.5% at two regions) and, unexplained, no fibre CSA change on biopsy in either leg. He refuses to lower the minimum on two supporting studies, says he *hopes* the floor comes down, and adds that anyone progressing below 6 reps should change nothing.

### Why he rejects the low-rep studies

This is the sharpest argument in the domain and it is what pins the floor.

[Can reps below 5 maximise growth](reps-below-5-maximize-muscle-growth.md) tallies six studies comparing 2–5 reps against moderate or high reps; five found parity. Four of those five gave the low-rep group more sets, longer rest, or both — usually because researchers equated *volume load*. Mangine (33 men, 2+ years trained): 3 min rest against 1 min. Schoenfeld 2014 (17 trained men): **7 sets of 3 against 3 sets of 10**, 3 min against 90 s. Campos (27 untrained): 4 sets at 3 min against 2 at 1 min. Chestnut & Docherty (19 untrained): 6 sets at 3 min against 3 at 2 min.

Two clean comparisons remain and they contradict each other. Weiss 2000 — 44 untrained men, 4 sets of squat, 6 min rest throughout, 7 weeks — found 3–5 reps as good as 13–15 and 23–25. Schoenfeld 2016 — 19 trained men, 3 sets, 2 min rest, 8 weeks — found 2–4 reps grew *less* vastus lateralis and elbow flexor than 8–12.

The inference, stated plainly in [Can sets of 3–5 reps maximise growth](3-5-reps-truly-maximize-growth.md), is that those confounds run *toward* the low-rep group, so they cut against the low-rep case rather than for it: handed extra sets and longer rest, ≤5 reps should have *beaten* higher reps if it were equally potent per set, and it only tied. He also dismantles the volume-load equating that created the confound — 6–35 reps grow similarly at matched sets despite wildly different volume loads, so volume load is not what tracks hypertrophy, and equating it introduced a confound rather than removing one. The same move powers [Adding load vs adding reps](adding-more-load-repetitions-better-hypertrophy-strength.md), where he attacks a pooled re-analysis (n=14 vs n=10, unmatched frequency and duration) favouring load progression.

The very bottom is firmer. [Maxing out](maxing-out-effective-building-muscle-one-rep.md) pairs Dankel (n=5, one arm each way, 21 consecutive days) with Mattocks (38 untrained, 8 weeks): 1RM-only training matched full training for strength and produced virtually zero thickness change, in places slightly negative. Singles build the skill, not the tissue.

The top end is softer and he says so. Lasevicius (untrained, 12 weeks) found 40/60/80% 1RM similar and all better than 20% (60+ reps, half the growth); a curl trial averaging **100 reps on the first set** still matched 80%. [How light is too light](light-too-light-maximizing-muscle-growth.md) leaves sub-30% at two studies for and two against, with rest confounds on two, and declines to resolve it — its central-fatigue explanation rests on one isometric study never linked to any growth trial. **No retrievable reference list.**

### Fibre type, and whether rep range varies by muscle

It does not, on the cleanest test available. [The optimal rep range for each muscle](optimal-rep-range-each-muscle-true.md) and [Training by fibre type](should-train-muscles-according-fiber-type.md) both centre on Schoenfeld 2020: 26 untrained men, within-subject, one leg 4×20–30 and the other 4×6–10 to momentary failure on calf raises, 2×/week for 10 weeks. Soleus and both gastrocnemius heads grew equally — and the soleus is where the hypothesis makes its strongest prediction. Grgic's meta-analysis of five studies adds the fibre level: type I and type II growth comparable across loads, confidence intervals wide, and he says so. Sixteen histochemical surveys show most muscles sit near 50/50 anyway, and the popular self-test fails (Douris 2006, r = −0.48 indirect; Terzis 2008, r < 0.2 against biopsies).

Flag: the closing line that fibre type "is not a consideration" for programme design outruns one 8-week untrained calf study. It shows *load prescription* need not track fibre type; it says nothing about recovery or fatigue cost. The regional version is thinner still — [Heavy and light loads and regional growth](heavy-light-weights-produce-different-regional-muscle.md) rests on Hisaeda 1996, **11 untrained women**, whose groups differed in reps, sets, rest *and* tempo at once. He proposes a fibre-type mechanism, breaks it on two contradictions inside the same dataset, and still recommends load variety. **No retrievable reference list.**

### Combining and alternating

Permitted, not prescribed. [Alternating rep ranges](alternating-rep-ranges-improves-muscle-growth-18.md) is titled "18 studies" and rests on three with MRI or ultrasound: Schoenfeld (19 trained men, 8 weeks, constant 8–12 vs 2–4/8–12/20–30 weekly), De Souza (26 untrained, 6 weeks), Fink (21 untrained, within-arm, 8 weeks). All null. He follows Krieger's individual-data reanalysis rather than stopping at "not significant", then calls the residual triceps advantage sampling noise. **No retrievable reference list; studies listed unverified.** The one contrary result — Kraemer 2003, 27 untrained women, nine months, varied favoured on fat-free mass — is by **skinfold calipers**, which he discounts while keeping the duration open.

[The benefits of combining rep ranges](benefits-combining-rep-ranges.md) is where the framing outruns the evidence: he kills the fibre-type rationale immediately, concedes the direct hypertrophy data show no difference, then argues mechanistically from neural recruitment, capillarisation and pain tolerance. The defensible part is concurrent development of *other* attributes — 1RM and bone density from heavy work, local endurance and mitochondrial protein content from light (Lim 2019).

Underneath the average sit real people. [The best rep range isn't what you think](best-rep-range-muscle-growth-isn-t.md) — 24 untrained women, mean age 58, 24 weeks, each doing 12 weeks at 8–12 and 12 at 27–31 — found comparable means over divergent individuals (one −5% then +10%, another +13% then +3%). He rejects the authors' plateau-delay reading with a worked two-subject example and inverts their advice: rotating is insurance for the uncertain, not a plateau-breaker. He misses the design objection — no repeated exposures, no control arm — but catches up in 2024, accepting the Robinson critique that such designs cannot detect individual response at all.

## Nutrition, evidence and how he reads a study

### Protein: the number, and the scatter behind it

[The protein myth](protein-myth-much-really-need.md) is his best nutrition work because the argument runs against his own headline. Three meta-analyses put the plateau at 1.5–1.6 g/kg/day; nitrogen balance and IAAO land at 1.5–2.0. Then, crediting Eric Trexler, he strips the fitted line off the 2018 dataset: above 1.25 g/kg, protein intake explains **1% of the variance** in fat-free mass change (r² = 0.011). So he refuses a single number and gives a spectrum — 1.2–1.59 possibly enough, 1.6–2.0 almost certainly, above 2.0 more than enough. Training experience does not raise the requirement (Lemon 1992: gains similar at 1.35 and 2.62 g/kg; Bandegan 2017: 1.7 g/kg in eight near-maximal bodybuilders; Tarnopolsky 1988: 1.2). Only the cutting advice outruns its evidence, and he says so in the same breath: Mettler and Longland both used **40% deficits** against 1.0–1.2 g/kg control arms.

[The protein limit for a meal](protein-limit-meal-they-were-wrong.md) is looser. The 20 g ceiling came from trials training one muscle with one or two exercises; train the whole body and 40 g beats 20 g, and Trommelen's 100 g dose produced greater, longer-lasting MPS with small oxidation. He then argues MPS is a poor proxy for growth — undercutting his own title, since the three growth trials (12 weeks untrained; 10 rowers, 8 weeks; 24 rugby players, 6 weeks) show only that three servings slightly beat two and nothing beats three.

### Pre-sleep protein: the confound is the whole story

[Pre-sleep protein disappears once total intake is matched](protein-before-bed-really-work.md) is the cleanest teardown on the channel. The 2021 Reis review everyone cites **did not control total daily protein**: its key trial gave the pre-sleep group 27.5 g of extra protein against a non-caloric placebo, leaving them at **1.9 vs 1.3 g/kg/day**. Nothing there separates "protein before bed" from "more protein". Three trials that did match daily intake — n=26 trained (54 g casein, 8 weeks), n=13 recreationally active (35 g, 10 weeks), n=42 untrained (whey + vitamin D, 6 weeks) — found no significant differences, with the numerical directions flipping between growth and strength measures, which he correctly reads as what small samples look like. Casein has no special claim either: whey and casein differ in curve shape but not area under it, and myofibrillar synthesis did not differ. His own flag is the right one — three underpowered trials give absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

### Creatine: a trivial effect size, relabelled

[The science behind creatine](science-behind-creatine-much-more-muscle-strength.md) is where a conclusion most clearly outruns its measurement. Five meta-analyses put the lean-mass benefit at 1–1.4 kg over 7–14 weeks, but lean mass includes water and connective tissue. The informative analysis pools 10 studies with direct ultrasound/MRI measurement: creatine adds **0.10–0.16 cm of thickness** across triceps, elbow flexors, quads and hamstrings, **effect size 0.11** — which those authors called trivial.

His objection is legitimate: those thresholds were derived from strength and power research. His replacement is not equally solid — thresholds from Greg Nuckols' **Stronger by Science blog post**, under which 0.11 becomes small-to-medium, then benchmarked against a literature average to yield the headline "**about one-third faster muscle gain**." That is a relabelling of an existing number against a non-peer-reviewed threshold set, not a new measurement; the underlying figure is still 0.10–0.16 cm. He explains exactly what he did, and elsewhere shows the spread honestly (young adults ES 0.23, older 0.06, with an explicit refusal to conclude older adults benefit little; 20–30% non-responders; the hair-loss scare traced to one unreplicated study in 20 rugby players measuring **serum** rather than **scalp** DHT).

### EMG, and the position he later softened

[Four reasons EMG can't rank exercises](ryan-humiston-has-made-mistake-emg.md) is his hardest methodological line: EMG measures neuromuscular excitation, not activation; it is unreliable even at that (constant electrical stimulation at three joint angles produced different amplitudes); it reads higher with heavier loads that grow no more muscle; and it is blind to passive tension. Verdict: EMG conclusions about exercise ranking have "a high probability of just being plain wrong."

That position did not hold. By his 2024 chest guide he argues EMG **is** informative for subtle comparisons — grip widths, bench angles, biomechanically similar presses — while remaining unreliable across exercises differing in muscle length, and his upper-chest content leans on EMG counts throughout. The two positions are reconcilable, but the softening is real and it is a live inconsistency across the channel. He flags it himself.

### Sampling variance is his master key

[3 sets is not better than 1 set](3-sets-not-better-1-set.md) is the most methodologically careful video in the set. Pinto 2025 (15 untrained men, unilateral pec deck, 2 vs 6 weekly sets, 12 weeks) found similar growth — and he argues this is *compatible with* three sets being better, via Krieger's simulation: build a population of 3 million where high volume truly adds 3 mm and low volume 1 mm, sample five studies of ten per group, and only one resembles the truth; pool them and you recover it. Conflicting studies are what small samples do.

[Defending science-based lifting](defending-science-based-lifting.md) applies the same lens to the field, and its concessions make it worth keeping: **85% of the 20 most-cited strength and conditioning meta-analyses contained at least one statistical error**, and he found a d_z effect-size error himself in a 2022 protein meta-analysis, contacted the authors, and James Steele is redoing it. Weak spots: his "research has delivered" list is drawn entirely from settled, comfortable findings, and he rates recent meta-analyses "exceptional" without showing his work. That note gives surnames and bare links only — **no full citations retrievable**.

Where the instinct fails him: [This holds back your potential](holds-back-muscle-strength-potential.md) and [Turkesterone works](turkesterone-works-but-not-reasons-think.md) both lean on placebo-steroid studies with **n=6 and n=11**, 25 and 50 years old, one with no comparison group, neither measuring hypertrophy — and the pair reappears as the closing beat of [You cannot gain much more muscle](cannot-gain-much-more-muscle-16-studies.md), where an acute strength result argues about long-term growth. [Jeremy Ethier's new study](need-talk-about-jeremy-ethier-new-study.md) surfaces individual growth values "upwards of 100%", says he has no idea where the measurement error is, and moves on without letting it dent his confidence. Neither of those two notes has a retrievable reference list.
