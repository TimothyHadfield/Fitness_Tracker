# Nutrient Timing (2008): The Original Anabolic-Window Document

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2008 Oct 3 · [PMID 18834505](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18834505/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-5-17)
**Authors:** Kerksick C, Harvey T, Stout J, Campbell B, Wilborn C, Kreider R, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 2.0 (<http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0>)
**Topic:** Nutrient timing

## The position, in one paragraph

This is the document that put the "anabolic window" into mainstream lifting culture, and it should be read as a historical artefact rather than as current guidance — the ISSN itself replaced it in 2017 ([PMID 28919842](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919842/)), and the revision reverses or drops most of the protein-timing claims here. The 2008 stand is organised around three windows — pre-exercise, during exercise, post-exercise — and asserts that *when* you eat carbohydrate and protein materially alters training adaptation. It prescribes a 30-minute post-exercise deadline, a 3:1 or 4:1 carbohydrate-to-protein ratio, and states that pre-exercise amino acid ingestion "may result in the best response of all." Read it for the carbohydrate work, which has held up, and read the 2017 note for what happened to the rest.

## Bottom line

- **Carbohydrate loading:** ~65% of calories daily, ~70% in the **5–7 days** before competition. One day of high-glycaemic carbohydrate at **10 g/kg/day** nearly doubled muscle glycogen (95 → **180 mmol/kg wet wt**) and it stayed there for three days ([Bussau 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12111292/)).
- **Pre-exercise meal: 1–2 g CHO/kg and 0.15–0.25 g PRO/kg, 3–4 h before competition.**
- Carbohydrate within an hour of starting gives **equivocal** performance results — one study worse, three better, five neutral in Hawley and Burke's tally ([Hawley 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9155497/)) — but reliably raises glycogen use and carbohydrate oxidation.
- **During exercise: 30–60 g CHO/h**, delivered as 1–2 cups of a **6–8% solution every 10–15 min** past 60 min of work.
- **Mixing carbohydrate types** lifts oxidation from **1.0 g/min to 1.2–1.75 g/min**, and glucose+fructose improved time-trial performance **8%** ([Currell 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18202575/)).
- Adding protein to carbohydrate at **3–4:1** during endurance work extended time to exhaustion from **19.7 min (CHO) to 26.9 min (CHO+PRO)** ([Ivy 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14669937/)) and cut muscle damage markers **83%** ([Saunders 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15235331/)).
- **The 30-minute rule:** delaying carbohydrate by two hours cut glycogen resynthesis **~50%** ([Ivy 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9694422/)). Prescription: **0.6–1.0 g CHO/kg/h** in the first 30 min then every 2 h for **4–6 h**, or **1.2 g/kg/h every 15–30 min**.
- **Post-exercise protein: 6–20 g EAA plus 30–40 g high-glycaemic carbohydrate within three hours**, practically 1.2–1.5 g/kg carbohydrate with 0.3–0.5 g/kg protein at a 3:1 or 4:1 ratio.
- The stand claims **pre-exercise** amino acid + carbohydrate produces the best protein-synthesis response of any timing ([Tipton 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11440894/)). **This did not replicate** and is abandoned in the 2017 revision.
- **Creatine at 0.1 g/kg/day** added to post-workout carbohydrate and protein is claimed to improve strength and body composition further. Dropped entirely from the 2017 scope.

## Carbohydrate before exercise

Glycogen is the limiting fuel and stores last **90 min to 3 h** at 65–85% VO₂max. As they fall, intensity and work output fall with them ([Coyle 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3897180/)), and tissue breakdown and immunosuppression follow ([Gleeson 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14971437/)).

Traditional loading used a 3–6 day depletion phase, but the stand reports that **1–3 days of high carbohydrate with reduced activity** achieves maximal storage. A six-day high-carbohydrate diet (600 g/day) versus low (100 g) produced pre-exercise glycogen of **104.5 versus 72.2 mmol/kg wet wt** and better blood glucose maintenance through a 45-min ride at 82% VO₂peak ([Kavouras 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15129930/)).

A single high-carbohydrate meal 4 h before 105 min of cycling raised muscle glycogen **42%** ([Coyle 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3897180/)). Glycaemic index of the pre-exercise meal appears not to matter for performance ([Febbraio 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8889742/), [Febbraio 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11053335/), [Earnest 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15320674/)).

## Carbohydrate during exercise

The clearest performance data in the document. An 8% solution given before and every 15 min pushed time to volitional exhaustion up **30%** — an extra **47 min** of cycling ([McConell 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10484580/)). In a 70-km time trial, carbohydrate mattered most when starting glycogen was low; with high glycogen the drink added little, but with low glycogen the non-carbohydrate condition lost power and pace over the final 14% ([Widrick 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8365999/)).

Feeding pattern matters too: **10.75 g every 30 min** beat **86 g every 60 min** for end-of-ride sprint capacity despite identical blood glucose and insulin ([Fielding 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4033404/)). And pre-exercise carbohydrate only helped when feeding continued during the ride ([Febbraio 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11090571/)).

Peak carbohydrate oxidation is about **1 g/min** on a single transporter. Mixing sources beats it: glucose+sucrose raised oxidation **21%** to 1.2 g/min ([Jentjens 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15877291/)), and maltodextrin+fructose **40%** to ~1.5 g/min ([Wallis 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15741841/)). The stand warns that large fructose doses cause GI upset.

## Protein and amino acids around training

**Pre-exercise.** The stand leans heavily on [Tipton 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11440894/), in which 35 g sucrose + 6 g EAA immediately *before* resistance exercise produced greater net protein balance than the same dose immediately after, attributed to amino acid delivery coinciding with exercise-elevated blood flow. The same group's later study with 20 g whey found both timings anabolic and **no difference between them** ([Tipton 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15570142/)).

**Longitudinal work.** [Coburn 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16686554/) reported **+30.3%** strength with pre-exercise whey + leucine versus **+22.4%** with energy-matched carbohydrate and **+3.6%** with training alone. [Willoughby 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16988909/) found 20 g protein before and after each session over 10 weeks produced greater mass, strength, IGF-1 and myofibrillar protein content than carbohydrate. [Cribb and Hayes 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17095924/) is the timing keystone: identical protein + creatine + carbohydrate taken immediately before and after workouts beat the same supplement taken morning and evening, over 10 weeks, for lean mass, 1RM, type II fibre CSA and intramuscular creatine and glycogen.

Note what Cribb and Hayes actually shows. Creatine was in both arms, so a timing effect on creatine uptake is not separable from a timing effect on protein — a limitation the 2008 stand does not raise but the 2017 revision does.

**During exercise.** Bird's series had subjects take 6% carbohydrate, 6% carbohydrate + 6 g EAA, or placebo through a 60-min session. Cortisol rose **105%** on placebo versus **11%** and **7%** on the two feeding conditions; urinary 3-methylhistidine fell **27%** with CHO+EAA while rising **56%** on placebo ([Bird 2006a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16631431/), [Bird 2006b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16472979/)). Over 12 weeks the combination reduced breakdown markers **26%** (placebo **+52%**) and increased type I, IIa and IIb fibre cross-sectional area ([Bird 2006c](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16456674/)). Feeding carbohydrate + hydrolysed casein at 0.15 g/kg before and every 15 min through a two-hour session raised fractional synthetic rate **49 ± 22%** and produced a **five-fold** improvement in protein balance ([Beelen 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18430966/)).

## The post-exercise window as 2008 stated it

For **glycogen**, the case is strong and mostly still stands: 1.5 g CHO/kg within 30 min beats a two-hour delay, and the delay costs about **50%** of the resynthesis rate, attributed to heightened insulin sensitivity ([Ivy 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9694422/)). Adding protein helped further — carbohydrate + protein + fat gave **88.8 mmol/kg wet wt** versus **75.5** (high CHO) and **70.0** (low CHO) ([Ivy 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12235033/), consistent with [Zawadzki 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1601794/) and [Berardi 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16775553/)).

For **protein**, the stand is more honest than its reputation suggests. It states outright that "the optimal time point for supplementation has not yet been demonstrated," and that similar responses were found immediately, 1 h, 2 h and 3 h after exercise. It also notes that studies using 6 g EAA, 12 g EAA, 17.5 g whey, 20 g casein, 20 g whey, 40 g mixed amino acids and 40 g EAA all produced similar increases in protein synthesis — i.e. dose-insensitivity across a nearly seven-fold range, which should have been a warning about measurement sensitivity.

The **30-minute, 3:1–4:1** prescription is therefore explicitly labelled "a often utilized practical approach," not a finding. It became dogma anyway.

The one strong timing result cited is [Levenhagen 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11350780/): 10 g protein + 8 g carbohydrate + 3 g fat immediately versus 3 h after 60 min of moderate exercise raised leg protein synthesis **three-fold** and whole-body synthesis **12%**. That is a small mixed feeding in an aerobic-exercise model, not a resistance-training hypertrophy result.

## Protein source and the creatine add-on

Whey digests fast and drives synthesis; casein digests slowly and suppresses breakdown; on area-under-the-curve casein may give the better net balance ([Boirie 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9405716/), [Dangin 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11158939/)).

The creatine section is the weakest part of the document by the stand's own account. [Tarnopolsky 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11740297/) found creatine+carbohydrate gained more body mass (**5.4%** vs **2.4%**) than protein+carbohydrate but no difference in fat-free mass, fibre area or strength. [Cribb 2007a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277594/) found no advantage of creatine over protein or carbohydrate carriers. Two studies did favour creatine addition ([Cribb 2007b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17986903/), [Kerksick 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17679046/)). The stand calls the findings "somewhat mixed" and then concludes in its bullet list that creatine addition "facilitates greater improvements in strength and body composition" — a firmer statement than the paragraph above it supports.

## Strength of the evidence

**Solid.** The carbohydrate sections. Glycogen loading, in-exercise carbohydrate for long efforts, multiple-transportable carbohydrate mixes, and rapid post-exercise glycogen restoration all rest on controlled crossover trials with biopsy or time-trial endpoints. These claims survived into 2017 essentially intact.

**Overreach.** Three specific places. First, the claim that pre-exercise amino acids give "the best response of all" rests on one acute study by one group, and the same group's follow-up found no difference. Second, the creatine bullet is stronger than the evidence paragraph beneath it. Third, and most consequentially, the 30-minute post-exercise protein deadline is presented as a practical convention in the body text but reads as a rule in the summary bullets, and that is how the world received it.

**Structural weakness.** Nearly the entire protein case is acute amino-acid kinetics — net balance and fractional synthetic rate over hours. Almost no longitudinal trial in the document isolates *timing* rather than *supplementation*; the comparisons are usually protein versus carbohydrate placebo, which tests whether protein helps, not when to take it. The single true timing comparison (Cribb and Hayes) is confounded by creatine.

**Volume of evidence.** 102 references, three of which are supplementary summary tables the text leans on for its pre-exercise and post-exercise conclusions. The stand declares **no competing interests**.

**Superseded.** Do not cite this document for current practice. The 2017 revision keeps the carbohydrate recommendations, reverses the pre-exercise superiority claim, deletes the 30-minute protein deadline, drops the fixed CHO:PRO ratio, removes creatine and fat from scope, and states plainly that protein timing is lower priority than hitting 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day.

## Disclosures and funding

**No separate disclosure record for this article came back from PubMed Central** — common for the older BMC-era papers in this corpus, where such statements sit in the article body rather than in a tagged section. The body carries a one-line declaration, "The authors declare that they have no competing interests," and no funding statement of any kind. No company, grant or role is named. That blanket line should not be read as an absence of ties: several of these authors declare substantial supplement-industry relationships on later ISSN stands in this library.

**Does any of it bear on the conclusions?** Nothing declared does. The thing worth watching in this document is not a commercial tie but who wrote the evidence. J.L. Ivy is a co-author, and the post-exercise glycogen case — the 30-minute rule, the ~50% loss from a two-hour delay, and the carbohydrate-plus-protein co-ingestion advantage — rests substantially on his own trials ([Ivy 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9694422/), [Ivy 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12235033/), [Ivy 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14669937/)). Lead author C. Kerksick is likewise an author of one of the two studies cited as favouring the creatine addition ([Kerksick 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17679046/)) in the section the stand itself calls "somewhat mixed". These are the relevant studies and the field was small, so this is not a defect in itself — but the 30-minute rule, which is the single claim this document put into the world, was written up by the man who ran the study it comes from, and no declaration would have surfaced that.
