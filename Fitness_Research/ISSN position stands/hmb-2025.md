# HMB: the ISSN's 2025 Stand

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2025 Dec · [PMID 39699070](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39699070/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2024.2434734)
**Authors:** Rathmacher JA, Pitchford LM, Stout JR, Townsend JR, Jäger R, Kreider RB, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 (<https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>)
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

Twelve numbered points, and the striking thing about them is how many carry the word *may*. HMB is safe for at least a year at **1.5–3 g/day** and for eight weeks at up to **6 g/day**; it works by raising muscle protein synthesis and — more distinctively — suppressing muscle protein breakdown, now demonstrated in humans; **38 mg/kg/day** combined with training "may potentially improve body composition"; it "may improve strength and power in untrained individuals, but its performance benefits in trained athletes are mixed"; it is probably useful against disuse atrophy and sarcopenia. The centre of gravity has moved decisively away from the young trained lifter and toward **older adults, bed rest, surgery and recovery**. For a healthy trained lifter, the honest reading of this document is: safe, mechanistically real, probably helps you recover from unusually damaging sessions, and unlikely to add meaningful muscle on top of a good training programme and adequate protein.

## Bottom line

- Dose: **38 mg/kg body mass/day** (≈**17 mg/lb**) — about **3 g/day** for an 80 kg lifter. Unchanged from 2013.
- **1.5 g/day** was enough to improve recovery of maximal voluntary contraction and range of motion after eccentric elbow-flexor damage in untrained men ([Tsuchiya 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32281915/)) — a genuinely lower effective dose than 2013 allowed.
- Safety: **1.5–3 g/day for at least one year** and **up to 6 g/day for 8 weeks** with no adverse clinical, haematological, renal or hepatic effects ([Nissen 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10917905/), [Rathmacher 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32857128/), [Baier 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19164608/)). No genotoxicity ([Pitchford 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30359700/)).
- Rodent NOAEL of **3.49–4.16 g/kg/day** translates to **~0.5 g/kg/day** in humans — over **30 g/day for a 60 kg person**, more than ten times the recommended dose ([Baxter 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16006030/)).
- **3 g of HMB produces a near-maximal stimulation of muscle protein synthesis** in human muscle, and cuts **muscle protein breakdown by ~57%** — insulin-independently ([Wilkinson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23551944/), [Wilkinson 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29097038/)).
- HMB activates mTORC1 **independently of leucine's Sestrin2-GATOR2 sensing pathway** — so it is not simply "leucine, but concentrated".
- **HMB-FA is no longer clearly better than HMB-Ca.** Two studies favour the free acid, one recent study found the calcium salt had higher plasma concentrations, faster peak and greater AUC ([Ribeiro 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38564019/)).
- Recovery: a meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (n = 324) found **3 g/day for longer than six weeks** significantly attenuated CK and LDH after damaging exercise ([Rahimi 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29676656/)).
- Strength and power in **trained** athletes: mixed, and only tending to appear in studies **longer than six weeks**.
- Best-supported use is **not** bodybuilding: preventing muscle loss during bed rest, after surgery, and in sarcopenic older adults.

## What it does, mechanistically

Only about **5% of leucine** is converted to HMB, so the 3 g dose is not obtainable from food. The 2025 stand's mechanistic case is far stronger than 2013's because the key human work now exists. [Wilkinson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23551944/) gave 3.42 g HMB-FA to healthy young men and measured myofibrillar protein synthesis directly: HMB stimulated synthesis comparably to an equal dose of leucine, **and additionally cut protein breakdown by 57%**, which leucine did not do — insulin-independently, since HMB is not an insulin secretagogue. [Wilkinson 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29097038/) replicated this with the calcium salt.

The stand is candid that this evidence base is **young, healthy men only**, that no changes in the atrophy proteins MuRF1 or MAFbx were seen in human muscle, and that the mechanism of breakdown suppression in humans "remains to be elucidated". The rest — ubiquitin-proteasome suppression, caspase inhibition, autophagy, AMPK/PGC-1α/Sirt1 — is preclinical.

## Body composition: read the table, not the summary

The stand's Table 1 lists roughly 30 placebo-controlled trials of HMB plus exercise. The summary says HMB "likely improves measures of body composition"; the table is far less flattering, with a large share of rows reading **NS** — including [Kreider 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10606212/), [Slater 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11599506/), [Ransone 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12580653/), [Lamboley 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17469236/), [Teixeira 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30588860/), [Tritto 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30859862/), [Fernández-Landa 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31936727/), [Rathmacher 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32857128/), [Osuka 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34081113/), [Cabre 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36407519/) and [Han 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35195339/). The two largest positive results — [Kraemer 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19346975/) (~+5 kg LBM, with 14 g arginine, 14 g glutamine and 6 g taurine alongside) and [Wilson 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24599749/) (+5.3 kg LBM, −3.7 kg fat mass) — are both flagged in footnotes as having attracted published editorial challenge.

The stand's own concession: the largest effects came from the studies that tightly controlled diet, and "these studies were questioned for the large effects reported."

The most important single citation here is [Jakubowski 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32456217/), a systematic review and meta-analysis in young adults which found HMB increased **total body mass** but that this **did not translate into greater fat-free mass, greater strength, or greater fat loss**. The 2025 stand spends a long paragraph attacking its methods — post-hoc study exclusions, inclusion of a non-placebo-controlled trial, omitted studies — without conceding any of its conclusion. Readers should note that this rebuttal comes from a document whose lead authors are commercially connected to HMB, and that Jakubowski's mean FFM difference was **0.29 kg (95% CI −0.01 to 0.60)** — which even taken at face value is a trivial effect.

## Strength and power

| Population | Finding |
|---|---|
| Untrained, ≤8 weeks | Consistent positive: dose-dependent strength gains at 1.5 and 3 g ([Nissen 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941534/)); increased isometric/isokinetic strength but **not whole-body 1RM** ([Gallagher 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11128859/)); +9.5% peak power and +41.3% leg press 1RM vs +0.4% / +25.3% placebo ([Asadi 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29207472/)) |
| Mixed trained/untrained | Upper-body strength up, reduced damage, independent of sex or training status ([Panton 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10978853/)) |
| Trained, ≤6 weeks | Largely null ([Kreider 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10606212/), [Slater 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11599506/), [Ransone 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12580653/)) |
| Trained, >6 weeks | Mixed: lower-body but not upper-body strength at 9 weeks ([Thomson 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19387396/)); positive in adolescent elite volleyball ([Portal 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21327797/)) and combat athletes ([Durkalec-Michalski 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28708126/)); **no effect** at 8 weeks with either form ([Teixeira 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30102677/)) |

## Where HMB looks best: disuse, surgery, ageing

This is the strongest material in the document and the reason HMB still has a serious literature at all.

**Bed rest.** Ten days of bed rest in 19 older adults: lean body mass fell **0.17 kg with HMB versus 2.05 kg with placebo** (p = 0.02); leg lean mass **0.08 kg versus 1.01 kg** ([Deutz 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23514626/)). After eight weeks of rehabilitation the HMB group trended above baseline while controls stayed below it. Muscle biopsies from the same participants showed higher mitochondrial content and fusion/fission markers with HMB ([Standley 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28705993/)).

**Hip fracture.** **81.3%** of older female patients on an HMB-containing supplement were ambulating by days 15 and 30 after surgery, versus **26.7%** of controls ([Ekinci 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26965178/)). Multi-ingredient, non-blinded, small — but the effect size is not subtle.

**Sarcopenia and ageing.** An umbrella review supports HMB for lean soft-tissue retention and muscle strength in older adults ([Phillips 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35818771/)) — notable because Phillips is HMB's most prominent critic in the young-athlete literature. Strength benefit appears **conditional on vitamin D status**: in the year-long Baier cohort, gains only materialised in those with 25-OH-D ≥ 30 ng/mL ([Fuller 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21807930/)). The 12-month HMB-Ca + vitamin D3 trial found benefit in **non-exercising** older adults but **no added benefit over exercise alone** in the exercising groups ([Rathmacher 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32857128/)).

That last result is the honest summary of HMB for a healthy trained person: it helps most when the training stimulus is absent or impossible.

## Combinations

**Creatine.** Nine studies, three with 2×2 factorial designs. [Jówko 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11448573/) found **additive, not synergistic**, effects on lean mass and strength. [Fernández-Landa 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31936727/) reported synergy on aerobic power in elite rowers. Three studies found nothing from either ingredient ([Mangine 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32460801/) among them). Adding HMB to creatine is not established as worthwhile.

**Probiotics.** *Weizmannia coagulans* GBI-30, 6086 raised peak HMB by 16% and total exposure by 19% ([Rathmacher 2021](https://doi.org/10.1096/fasebj.2021.35.S1.01949) — FASEB abstract, no PubMed record) and improved muscle-integrity outcomes in military training ([Gepner 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28408697/)).

**Whey protein.** The comparison that matters most for a lifter already eating enough protein: [Jakubowski 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30113522/) found HMB added to whey produced **no greater muscle or strength gain than leucine added to whey**. The stand notes it was not placebo-controlled, which is true, and it remains the most directly relevant comparison anyone has run.

## Strength of the evidence

**Solid.** Safety, across three decades, three species, genotoxicity batteries, GLP-compliant rodent studies and two year-long human trials. The acute human mechanism work ([Wilkinson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23551944/), [Wilkinson 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29097038/)) is rigorous and is the strongest single addition since 2013. The muscle-damage attenuation meta-analysis ([Rahimi 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29676656/)) is real. The disuse-atrophy and post-surgical data, though small, are internally consistent.

**Weak, or overstated relative to what is cited.**

- **Position statement point 3** asserts "There are **no** negative effects of HMB-Ca and HMB-FA on glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in humans." The body text does not support that flatly: it describes multiple rodent studies showing impaired glucose tolerance or insulin sensitivity, a human study showing reduced insulin secretion during an OGTT in young men, and closes by saying "further research is needed to determine the role of chronic HMB supplementation on insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism." A summary point should not be more certain than its own section.
- **Body composition.** "Likely improves measures of body composition" sits awkwardly above a table in which the majority of well-controlled recent trials report no significant effect, and above a meta-analysis the stand rebuts rather than absorbs.
- **Aerobic performance.** Promoted to its own position point, but built from small studies with no agreed mechanism — the stand itself says "the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of HMB are unknown."
- **Multi-ingredient confounding persists.** The elderly, hip-fracture and Kraemer results all involve HMB plus arginine, lysine, glutamine, taurine or vitamin D. The stand acknowledges that "it is currently unknown if the multi-ingredient combinations are superior to HMB alone due to a lack of comparative studies" — after using those studies as evidence for HMB.

**Conflicts of interest.** Our full-text extract carries **no competing-interests or funding statement**, which is a notable gap for this particular topic. What is verifiable from the reference list itself: the lead author (Rathmacher) and second author (Pitchford), together with Fuller, are authors on a large share of the pivotal cited work — the HMB-FA kinetics papers ([Fuller 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21134325/), [Fuller 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26373270/)), the acute and subchronic toxicity studies ([Fuller 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35122888/), [Fuller 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24576552/)), the genotoxicity assessment ([Pitchford 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30359700/)), the 12-month vitamin D trial ([Rathmacher 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32857128/)) and the probiotic bioavailability abstract. This is normal for a small field, but it means the panel evaluating HMB's evidence substantially *is* the group that generated it. For comparison, the 2013 stand disclosed openly that one author conducted research for **Metabolic Technologies Inc.**, the company that commercialised HMB.

## What changed since the 2013 stand

**Direction of travel: less confident about lifters, more confident about patients.** Both editions recommend 38 mg/kg/day. But 2013 argued that HMB builds muscle and strength in trained athletes and that the negative trials failed for methodological reasons. 2025 argues that HMB is safe, mechanistically real, useful for recovery and for muscle preservation in the old and immobilised — and that its effects on trained-athlete performance are "mixed".

**Doses that moved.**

| | 2013 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Standard dose | 3 g/day or 38 mg/kg/day, split 2–3× | **38 mg/kg/day** (unchanged) |
| Minimum effective | "3 g produces greater gains than 1.5 g" | **1.5 g/day** shown effective for recovery ([Tsuchiya 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32281915/)) |
| Pre-workout timing | **Prescribed**: HMB-Ca 60–120 min before, HMB-FA 30–60 min before | **Dropped**. "None have specifically evaluated the timing of HMB administration on outcomes" |
| Lead-in period | **2 weeks minimum** before a damaging bout | No lead-in requirement stated |
| Stated safe range | 6 g/day for 1 month; rodent NOAEL read as ~50 g/day human-equivalent | **1.5–3 g/day for ≥1 year**; 6 g/day for 8 weeks; NOAEL read more conservatively as ~0.5 g/kg/day |

**Claims dropped.**

- **HMB-FA superiority.** In 2013 this was near-doctrine: double the peak in a quarter of the time, 91–97% greater AUC, 25% greater clearance. 2025 reports [Ribeiro 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38564019/) finding the **opposite** — HMB-Ca with higher plasma concentrations, faster peak, greater AUC and better bioavailability — and concludes only that "recent evidence is mixed". The entire timing rationale that 2013 built on free-acid kinetics falls with it.
- **The flagship trained-lifter results.** 2013 leaned on an unpublished 12-week HMB-FA gel abstract. Its published form ([Wilson 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24599749/)) and the companion HMB+ATP paper drew formal published criticism ([Gentles & Phillips 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28680453/)); the authors responded ([Wilson 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29209404/)). 2025 states plainly that "due to the criticism surrounding them, they were **not used to inform** the overall interpretation of the literature and the ISSN's position statements." An ISSN stand disowning the evidence base of its own previous edition is unusual and, to its credit, transparent.
- **The unfalsifiable framing softens.** 2013 explained away essentially every null result as inadequate training stimulus, supervision or periodisation. 2025 still deploys that argument once (against [Teixeira 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30102677/)), but it now also prints a table of null results without excusing them, and drops "the exercise stimulus will likely need to centre on free weights and compound movements".

**Claims strengthened.**

- **Human mechanism.** In 2013 the mTOR/proteasome story was in vitro and rodent. 2025 has direct human tracer measurements of synthesis and breakdown at a 3 g dose, plus the specific finding that HMB reaches mTORC1 by a **different route than leucine** (Sestrin2-GATOR2-independent). This is the one place the 2025 stand is more confident and is entitled to be.
- **Safety.** Genotoxicity battery, an HMB-FA-specific 90-day toxicity study, and two year-long human trials that did not exist in 2013.
- **Disuse atrophy, surgery and sarcopenia.** Entirely new sections built on the bed-rest work ([Deutz 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23514626/), [Standley 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28705993/)), post-surgical ambulation ([Ekinci 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26965178/)), and an umbrella review ([Phillips 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35818771/)).
- **Vitamin D dependency.** New and practically useful: HMB's strength benefit in older adults appears to require adequate vitamin D ([Fuller 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21807930/)).

**New evidence that shifted the position.** Above all, [Jakubowski 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32456217/) — the meta-analysis finding no FFM, strength or fat-mass benefit in young adults — and [Jakubowski 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30113522/), finding HMB no better than leucine when both are added to whey. Neither existed in 2013. The 2025 stand argues with both rather than accepting them, but the hedged language throughout the rest of the document shows the argument landed. The counterweight the panel found was not in the weight room; it was in bed-rest wards and hip-fracture clinics.

**So: more or less confident?** On what a lifter buys it for — muscle and strength in a trained person — **clearly less**. 2013 said HMB "can enhance muscle hypertrophy and dynamic strength" and told you exactly when to take it; 2025 says benefits are "mixed", declines to recommend a timing, and withdraws its predecessor's headline studies. On safety, mechanism and clinical muscle preservation, **more** — and better evidenced. HMB's twelve-year fall is visible in the hedging: it survived as a geriatric and clinical agent while losing its case as a bodybuilding supplement.
