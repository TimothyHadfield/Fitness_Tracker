# HMB: the ISSN's 2013 Stand (superseded by the 2025 edition)

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2013 Feb 2 · [PMID 23374455](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23374455/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-10-6)
**Authors:** Wilson JM, Fitschen PJ, Campbell B, Wilson GJ, Zanchi N, Taylor L, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 2.0 (<http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0>)
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

HMB is a leucine metabolite that is claimed here to work by *speeding recovery* — blunting muscle damage and protein breakdown so you can train harder, more often. The recommended dose is **3 g/day, or 38 mg/kg body mass/day, split into 2–3 servings**, started **two weeks before** a hard training block, with an acute pre-workout dose **60–120 min before** training if using the calcium salt and **30–60 min before** if using the newer free-acid form. The stand's central and most contested move is to explain away the many null results in trained lifters: HMB, it argues, only works when the training stimulus is novel, supervised, high-intensity and damaging enough — so failed studies failed because the *training* was inadequate, not because HMB doesn't work. That reasoning, plus two extraordinary effect sizes it leans on, is what makes this document the high-water mark of HMB's reputation. It has been **superseded by the [2025 stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39699070/)**, which is markedly more restrained.

## Bottom line

- Dose: **3 g/day**, or **38 mg/kg BM/day** (≈17 mg/lb), divided into **2–3 servings**.
- Start **at least 2 weeks** before a damaging training block; the 2-week lead is when protein-breakdown markers actually move ([Nissen 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941534/)).
- **3 g beats 1.5 g** in untrained lifters; **6 g is not better than 3 g** ([Nissen 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941534/), [Gallagher 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11128859/)).
- You cannot get there from food. Only **~5% of leucine** becomes HMB, so 3 g of HMB implies **60 g of leucine — over 600 g of high-quality protein**.
- Timing: **HMB-Ca 60–120 min pre-exercise**; **HMB-FA 30–60 min** ([Wilson 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19193206/)).
- HMB-FA reaches **double the peak plasma concentration in one quarter of the time** (30 vs 120 min) with **91–97% greater AUC** and **25% greater plasma clearance** than an equivalent HMB-Ca dose ([Fuller 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21134325/)).
- Taking HMB-Ca **with glucose delays the peak by an hour** and lowers it (487 → 352 nmol/mL) ([Vukovich 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12031256/)).
- Safety: **6 g/day for a month** produced no change in cholesterol, haematology, glucose, liver or kidney markers ([Gallagher 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11128860/)); one year in elderly subjects, likewise ([Baier 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19164608/)).
- In **untrained** lifters HMB raises fat-free mass and strength in as little as **3 weeks**. In **trained** lifters, studies of **≤6 weeks are mostly null**; only longer ones show effects.

## Dosing and kinetics

| | HMB-Ca | HMB-FA |
|---|---|---|
| Time to peak plasma | ~120 min (1 g); ~60 min (3 g) | ~30 min |
| Peak concentration | 120 nmol/mL (1 g); 487 nmol/mL (3 g) | ~2× the HMB-Ca peak |
| AUC over 180 min | reference | **+91–97%** |
| Plasma half-life | ~2.5 h | ~3 h |
| Plasma clearance (tissue uptake proxy) | reference | **+25%** |
| Pre-exercise timing | 60–120 min | 30–60 min |

Sources: [Vukovich 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12031256/) for HMB-Ca kinetics and the glucose effect; [Fuller 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21134325/) for the HMB-Ca vs HMB-FA comparison. Note that at the time of writing, **HMB-FA was commercially unavailable** — almost the entire efficacy literature was on the calcium salt, while the stand's most enthusiastic recommendations lean on free-acid kinetics.

## Muscle damage and recovery — the mechanism the stand bets on

The original dose-response study blunted CK, LDH, blood and urinary urea nitrogen and 3-methylhistidine by **20–60%** across three weeks of monitored high-intensity training, dose-dependently ([Nissen 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941534/)). Trained runners showed lower CK and LDH after a **20 km run** ([Knitter 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11007567/)). In a mixed cohort of 41 trained and untrained lifters, HMB-Ca blunted the CK rise **regardless of training status** ([Panton 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10978853/)). Pre-exercise HMB-FA blunted CK and protein breakdown and improved perceived recovery in trained men ([Wilson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23286834/)).

Against these: 28 days in resistance-trained athletes instructed **not to change their training** produced no change in damage markers or strength ([Kreider 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10606212/)). The stand reads this as confirmation of the mechanism — no overload, no damage, nothing for HMB to rescue — rather than as a negative result.

## Body composition and strength

**Untrained.** Effects appear in as little as 3 weeks ([Nissen 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941534/)). The showpiece is [Kraemer 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19346975/): 12 weeks of periodised, monitored training with controlled diets, HMB-Ca **plus 14 g glutamine and 14 g arginine**, producing **+9 kg lean body mass versus +3.5 kg placebo** and **+46 kg squat versus +29 kg**. Those are drug-like numbers from a multi-ingredient supplement, and the stand presents them without qualification.

**Trained.** The pattern the authors draw out is duration-dependent:

| Study | Length | Population | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Kreider 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10606212/) | 4 wk | resistance-trained, unmonitored | null |
| Kreider (J Exerc Physiol Online, no PubMed record) | 4 wk | college footballers | null |
| [Slater 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11599506/) | 6 wk | water polo, rowers | null |
| [Panton 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10978853/) | 4 wk | mixed, >80% 1RM loads | BF −1.1% vs −0.5%; bench +7.5 vs +5.2 kg; LBM +1.4 vs +0.9 kg |
| [Thomson 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19387396/) | 9 wk | trained men, unsupervised, BIA | small but significant FFM and strength gains |
| [Nissen 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941534/) | 7 wk | strong lifters (≥135 kg bench) | +4.5 kg bench, +3.2 kg squat over placebo |
| Dunsmore/Wilson 12-wk HMB-FA gel | 12 wk | resistance-trained | cited as positive — **conference abstract only, no PubMed or DOI record** |

**Energy restriction.** Three days of caloric restriction in female judo athletes: body fat fell more with HMB, LBM fell less (−0.5% vs −1.6%), peak power fell 5% vs 11% (Hung et al. 2010, *J Exerc Sci Fitness* — DOI [10.1016/S1728-869X(10)60007-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1728-869X(10)60007-X), no PubMed record). This is a **three-day study**.

**Older adults.** 12 weeks of HMB + arginine + lysine improved lean mass, leg and grip strength and timed-up-and-go ([Flakoll 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15105032/)); a year of the same cocktail raised lean mass and protein turnover ([Baier 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19164608/)). With exercise added, [Vukovich 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11435528/) in 70-year-olds found only a **trend** (p = 0.08) on skinfold-estimated lean mass and **no strength difference** — which the stand attributes to an inadequate training protocol (2 sets, twice a week).

**Aerobic.** Two weeks of 3 g/day in trained cyclists increased time to VO2peak by **8%** and VO2 at 2 mM lactate by **9.1%** ([Vukovich & Dreifort 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11726262/)) — one study, eight subjects.

## Mechanisms

Leucine's anti-proteolytic effect requires **10–20× (5–10 mM)** the concentration needed to maximally stimulate protein synthesis, which is the argument for a metabolite doing the work ([Zanchi 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18637185/)). HMB is then proposed to act on both sides of the balance: **synthesis** via mTOR/S6K/4EBP1 and possibly the GH/IGF-1 axis ([Gerlinger-Romero 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21237681/)); **breakdown** via suppression of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway ([Smith 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15665304/)) and of caspase-mediated myonuclear apoptosis ([Eley 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18840762/), [Hao 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21697520/)); plus satellite cell proliferation and differentiation in culture ([Kornasio 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19211028/)). Nearly all of this is in vitro or rodent.

## Strength of the evidence

**Reasonably solid.** The pharmacokinetics — dose-response of plasma HMB, the glucose delay, the HMB-Ca vs HMB-FA comparison — are direct human measurements and have held up. The safety record is genuine: rodent NOAEL of **3.49–4.16 g/kg/day** ([Baxter 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16006030/)), 6 g/day for a month in humans, a year in the elderly, all clean. The muscle-damage attenuation in *untrained* people is replicated.

**Thin.** Almost everything about *trained* lifters. Of the studies in trained populations, most are ≤6 weeks and null; the positive ones are either short and modest ([Panton 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10978853/)), measured by bioelectrical impedance ([Thomson 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19387396/)) — which the stand itself criticises as high-variability, in the same paragraph where it accepts the result — or from the original 1996 paper by the compound's own developers.

**Where the stand overreaches.**

- **Unfalsifiability.** The core interpretive move — null results reflect insufficient training stimulus, insufficient supervision, inadequate periodisation or poor compliance — is applied to every negative study and to none of the positive ones. It may well be true. But as written, no experiment could disconfirm HMB, and the reader should notice that.
- **Multi-ingredient results reported as HMB results.** The [Kraemer 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19346975/) **+9 kg lean mass in 12 weeks** and the Flakoll/Baier elderly findings all used HMB **plus arginine, lysine, glutamine and/or taurine**. No arm isolates HMB. The 9 kg figure is larger than most anabolic-steroid trials report and is presented without a word of scepticism.
- **A key positive trial is an unpublished abstract.** The 12-week HMB-FA gel study (Dunsmore, Lowery, Rathmacher, Baier, Wilson et al.) is cited as evidence for HMB-FA's superiority in trained lifters. Our extract shows **no PMID and no DOI** — it is a supplement abstract. The full publication that followed ([Wilson 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24599749/)) went on to be formally challenged in the literature, and the 2025 stand explicitly excludes it and its companion from informing the ISSN's conclusions.
- **A citation that does not match its claim.** The adolescent volleyball findings (7 weeks, 3 g/day, increased FFM, −6.6% fat mass, greater Wingate power) are attributed to reference [56], which in our copy of the reference list is Zadik, Nemet & Eliakim's review *Hormonal and metabolic effects of nutrition in athletes* ([PMID 19960886](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19960886/)) — not a volleyball trial. The 2025 stand cites the correct study ([Portal 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21327797/)). Whether this is an error in the original or in our extraction, the 2013 attribution is unreliable as it stands.
- **Single-study claims stated as findings.** The aerobic-performance section rests on one 8-subject cyclist study; the energy-restriction section on one 3-day judo study with tiny groups; the pre-workout timing recommendation on one acute study in 16 untrained men.

**Conflicts of interest — and this one matters.** Unlike the creatine stands, this document does carry a competing-interests statement, and it is directly relevant. **JRH (Hoffman) "currently conducts research for Metabolic Technologies Inc."** — the company that commercialised HMB. Several other authors declare industry grants, paid speaking, advisory roles at supplement companies (Abbott Nutrition, Biotest, Woodbolt, Nordic Naturals, VPX) or employment at a contract research organisation funded by the nutrition industry. The lead author (Wilson) declares industry grants and is also an author on several of the studies the stand relies on most heavily. That does not make the document dishonest — it discloses all of this openly — but a stand that resolves an equivocal literature in favour of the product, partly by discounting the negative trials, is exactly the situation where those disclosures should be read carefully.
