# Nutrition for Female Athletes: Cycle Phases, Contraceptives, Energy Availability and Iron

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2023 Dec · [PMID 37221858](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2023.2204066)
**Authors:** Sims ST, Kerksick CM, Smith-Ryan AE, Janse de Jonge XAK, Hirsch KR, Arent SM, et al
**Licence:** CC BY-NC 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/>
**Topic:** Populations

## The position, in one paragraph

Sports nutrition guidance was built almost entirely on male subjects and then generalised to women, and the ISSN says that generalisation is not safe. Estradiol and progesterone shift substrate use, protein turnover, fluid handling, iron absorption and appetite across the menstrual cycle; contraceptive hormones shift them differently again; menopause changes the baseline entirely. The central practical claim is that **energy availability is the dominant variable** — get that wrong and cycle-phase micro-optimisation is irrelevant — followed by protein at the **upper end** of standard ranges, carbohydrate accounting for phase-dependent glycogen storage, and iron screening. Crucially, the stand is candid that much of this is inference: on several topics it states outright that no studies exist.

## Bottom line

- **Energy availability ≥45 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day** is the target for normal physiological function and body mass maintenance; above that supports weight gain and hypertrophy ([Slater 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27430502/)).
- **30 kcal/kg FFM/day is the damage threshold**: LH pulsatility and metabolic hormones are suppressed after as little as **5 days** below it in healthy women ([Loucks 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519869/), [Loucks 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9756553/)).
- Daily protein **1.8–2.2 g/kg (0.8–1.0 g/lb)** for pre-, peri- and post-menopausal women alike — the mid-to-upper end of general guidelines, not the middle.
- Add roughly **+12% protein in the luteal phase** to offset progesterone-driven catabolism ([Kriengsinyos 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15308475/), [Lamont 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3574042/)).
- **0.32–0.38 g/kg protein** pre- and post-exercise; **~0.3 g/kg every 3 hours** across long recovery windows ([Mercer 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33207749/)).
- Post-menopausal: an **~6–10 g essential amino acid** bolus as close to the end of training as possible, to punch through anabolic resistance ([Bukhari 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25827594/)).
- Energy intake rises roughly **+300 kcal/day in the luteal phase** — that is physiology, not a lapse ([Barr 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7825535/)).
- Carbohydrate during exercise: start at **30–60 g/h**. The 90 g/h multi-transportable ceiling is unvalidated in women; at **60 g/h** oxidation was already maximal with no further gain at 90 ([Wallis 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17218895/)).
- Post-exercise carbohydrate **≥1.2 g/kg (0.55 g/lb)** promptly after prolonged work ([Burke 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16766497/)).
- **Iron deficiency is 5–7× more common** in female athletes than male ([DellaValle 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22089308/)); supplementation is likely more effective in the follicular phase when hepcidin is low ([Sim 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24225901/), [Pedlar 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29280410/)).
- **Combined oral contraceptives can mask low energy availability** by suppressing the very hypothalamic signals used to detect it ([Mountjoy 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29771168/)).
- Creatine dosing is the same as for men — **~20 g/day for 5 days, then 3–5 g/day** — despite women starting from **70–80% lower phosphocreatine stores** ([Smith-Ryan 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33800439/), [Brosnan 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17430086/)).

## Energy availability and RED-S: the part that actually matters

Energy availability (EA) is energy intake minus exercise energy expenditure, normalised to fat-free mass. Low EA is the underlying cause of relative energy deficiency in sport, with consequences spanning reproductive, skeletal, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, endocrine and neurological function ([Mountjoy 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29771168/), [Logue 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32245088/)).

| EA (kcal/kg FFM/day) | What happens |
|---|---|
| >45 | Surplus available for weight gain and hypertrophy |
| ~45 | Threshold for optimal function and mass maintenance |
| ≤30 | LH pulsatility and metabolic hormones suppressed within **5 days** |

Two mechanisms matter. The female hypothalamus is more sensitive to energy deficit: kisspeptin signalling to GnRH neurons downregulates at a lower threshold, partly because kisspeptin-AVPV/PeN neurons are almost exclusive to the female brain ([Hrabovszky 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20529119/)). And exercise-induced deficits provoke a defensive appetite response — higher acylated ghrelin, lower insulin — that men do not show ([Hagobian 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20016296/)). This is why "just add cardio to the deficit" works less well in women: a physiological brake, not a willpower failure.

**Daily average is not enough.** Within-day energy deficits over 24 hours track with worse health and body composition markers even when the daily EA total looks fine, and women with menstrual dysfunction show larger cortisol rises and greater drops in RMR and estradiol the longer they delay post-exercise feeding ([Fahrenholtz 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29205517/)).

The stand admits EA is hard to measure in the field — it needs accurate fat-free mass, energy intake and exercise expenditure, each with its own error, plus non-purposeful activity. Treat the thresholds as guides for structuring intake, not a number to compute precisely.

## Menstrual cycle phases: what changes, and how much to care

A normal cycle runs **21–40 days**: follicular half estrogen-rising, luteal half with both estrogen and progesterone elevated. Estrogen promotes lipolysis and spares glycogen; progesterone increases amino acid oxidation, suppresses muscle protein synthesis, raises resting metabolic rate and shunts glucose toward the endometrium via GLUT-1/GLUT-4 ([Zhang 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33101047/)). Practical consequences:

- **Carbohydrate loading works in the follicular phase and largely fails in the luteal.** Follicular loading at **8.4–9.0 g/kg/day** raised resting muscle glycogen **17–31%** ([Paul 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11915778/), [Tarnopolsky 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11408434/)). The same protocol mid-luteal produced no change ([McLay 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17507743/)) or a modest **13%** ([Walker 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10846030/)), against **18–47%** typical in men. **But the extra glycogen didn't improve time trial performance** in either follicular study — worth knowing before building a protocol around it.
- Loading is often impractical anyway: ≥8 g/kg/day for a 60 kg woman habitually eating <2000 kcal means **70–90% of total energy from carbohydrate** ([Tarnopolsky 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7615443/)).
- **GI symptoms cluster in the early-follicular and late-luteal phases** ([Bernstein 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24450290/)), on top of slower baseline gastric emptying ([Sadik 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12608462/)) and more exercise-induced gastroparesis ([Gonzalez 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32527595/)). If fuelling tolerance varies unpredictably, track the cycle before blaming the product.
- **Carbohydrate mouth rinse does not appear to work in women** — null in 60-minute running ([Chryssanthopoulos 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28678622/)) and in resistance-trained women at several doses ([Karayigit 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33810470/)), despite being an established male finding ([Beaven 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23724880/)).
- Caffeine accumulates more and clears more slowly in the luteal phase ([Lane 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1483492/)). Glycogen **resynthesis**, oddly, is slower in the follicular than luteal phase ([Nicklas 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2606593/)) — the opposite direction to storage capacity, and a single small study.

## Hormonal contraceptives

The most useful message here is a warning about generalisation: multiple estrogen doses, four generations of progestin with different androgenicity, monophasic versus multiphasic, oral versus intrauterine. Only oral steroids undergo hepatic first-pass metabolism, so an IUD's localised progestin behaves differently, with no observed change in body composition or mass. **Findings from one COC do not transfer to COCs in general.**

What is reported:

- Exogenous hormones affect exercise glucose metabolism **more strongly than endogenous ones**, downregulating glucose flux during exercise ([Suh 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12391078/)).
- Higher-hormone COCs can reduce glucose tolerance and increase insulin resistance ([Silva-Bermudez 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33048062/)); markers of inflammation ([Larsen 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32523546/)) and oxidative stress ([Cauci 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27747795/)) run higher in COC-using athletes.
- **The masking problem.** COCs suppress ovulation via hypothalamic inhibition, so the hypothalamic response to energy deficiency is blunted and LEA can go undetected ([Mountjoy 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29771168/)). A withdrawal bleed on the pill-free week is not evidence of energy sufficiency.
- Resistance training adaptations: a low-androgenic progestin impaired resting and post-exercise myofibrillar protein synthesis without affecting breakdown ([Hansen 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19883384/)), while a **30 μg** ethinyl estradiol third-generation OC trended toward more muscle mass and significantly greater type I fibre area than a **20 μg** dose or no OC — with **no strength difference** ([Dalgaard 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31312144/)).
- Beetroot juice showed **no benefit** to submaximal cycling VO2 or time trial in recreationally trained COC users, despite raised plasma nitrate and nitrite ([Wickham 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30653856/)).
- COC use is **protective against iron deficiency**, so the stand advises against unsupervised iron supplementation in OC users ([Sim 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28329745/)).

## Iron

Iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia are **5–7× more common** in female athletes ([DellaValle 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22089308/)), driven by menstrual losses, exercise-induced losses via hepcidin ([Peeling 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19346972/)) and intake. Hepcidin — the hormone that blocks absorption — tracks the cycle:

| Cycle phase | Hepcidin | Absorption |
|---|---|---|
| Early follicular (days 0–5) | Low | Favourable ([Sim 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24225901/)) |
| Late follicular (days 6–14) | Stays low | Favourable ([Laine 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26687536/)) |
| Post-ovulation / luteal | Rises with progesterone | Restricted ([Li 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26657863/)) |

So supplementation is plausibly more effective in the follicular phase — the stand explicitly flags this as needing more research ([Pedlar 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29280410/)). Post-menopausal women show slower recovery of basal hepcidin after high-intensity running, up to 24 h ([Alfaro-Magallanes 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33348847/)), which matters for supplement timing.

## Hydration, sodium and hyponatremia risk

Estrogen shifts the osmotic threshold for thirst and vasopressin release **earlier**, and this shift persists on oral contraceptives ([Stachenfeld 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9458917/)). Women carry less total body water absolutely (**~31 vs ~44 L**) and proportionally (**~49% vs ~58%**) ([Ritz 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18774628/), [Driscoll 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32392422/)), so an equivalent sweat loss is a larger fraction of the pool.

**Exercise-associated hyponatremia risk is higher in women** ([Almond 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15829535/), [Hew-Butler 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26102445/)) — lower body mass, over-drinking, longer race times, plus sex-hormone effects on sodium handling. Thirst itself varies across the cycle ([Vokes 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3354712/)). The lesson is the opposite of "drink more": don't outrun sodium losses with plain water in long events.

## Peri- and post-menopause

Most body composition change happens in the four to five years before the final period; afterward it is more age- than hormone-driven ([Gould 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35231009/)). Falling progesterone then estradiol bring anabolic resistance, insulin resistance, central adiposity, lower bone mineral density and lower energy expenditure. Post-menopausal women have a *higher* resting muscle protein synthesis rate that is outweighed by upregulated breakdown ([Smith 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22620287/), [Rosa-Caldwell 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31462271/)).

Recommendations: protein at the **1.8–2.0+ g/kg/day** upper end; a **6–10 g EAA** bolus with resistance exercise, which raised MPS from 0–2 h and held it to 4 h in older women ([Bukhari 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25827594/)); prompt post-exercise carbohydrate exploiting the non-insulin-dependent **30–40 minute** window ([Ivy 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3132449/)). Nitrate may be more useful here than in younger women — one double-blind study in **13** post-menopausal women found improved force development and time to fatigue ([Proctor 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35240317/)). Thirteen subjects; preliminary.

## Supplements, briefly

- **Creatine.** Women have **70–80% lower** endogenous phosphocreatine stores and lower dietary intake ([Brosnan 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17430086/)) but **~10% higher** resting intramuscular creatine ([Forsberg 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1716189/)). The stand speculates higher doses may help but keeps the standard **20 g/day × 5 days, then 3–5 g/day** ([Smith-Ryan 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33800439/)). Luteal-phase dosing is floated and explicitly labelled untested.
- **Beta-alanine.** Same dose as men: **4–6 g/day** in 1–2 doses over 4 weeks ([Harris 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16554972/), [Trexler 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26175657/)). Women build more carnosine on **6 g/day** but showed **no greater fatigue attenuation** ([Varanoske 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29246277/)).
- **Caffeine.** Effects during resistance exercise may be *smaller* in women at the same dose ([Mielgo-Ayuso 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31574901/)), and habituation blunts it ([Guest 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33388079/)). The stand calls the female evidence "mixed at best".
- **Nitrate.** Recreationally trained women show reduced O2 cost, but performance effects are inconsistent and COC users showed none ([Wickham 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30653856/)).

An audit of the six best-supported performance supplements found female representation shallow across the board ([Smith 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35267928/)) — the honest summary of this whole section.

## Strength of the evidence

**Strongest.** The energy availability thresholds are the best-supported claims in the document, resting on Loucks' controlled laboratory work showing LH pulsatility disruption at ≤30 kcal/kg FFM/day ([Loucks 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519869/)). The sex difference in substrate use — more fat, less carbohydrate and leucine oxidation in women — is replicated across many studies and has a mechanistic basis in CD36 abundance ([Miotto 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29513564/)). The iron deficiency prevalence gap is solid.

**Single small studies dressed as recommendations.** Several headline numbers rest on one trial each: the luteal-phase carbohydrate loading failure ([McLay 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17507743/)), the follicular/luteal glycogen resynthesis difference ([Nicklas 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2606593/), n small, 1989), the OC dose effect on fibre area ([Dalgaard 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31312144/)), the nitrate finding in **13** post-menopausal women ([Proctor 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35240317/)). The **+12% luteal protein** figure is an inference from lysine requirement and protein oxidation data, not from a trial that manipulated protein intake and measured an outcome.

**Genuinely absent, and the stand says so.** No studies address protein requirements across the menstrual cycle or on hormonal contraceptives — the stand states this plainly. No studies of glycogen resynthesis in peri- or post-menopausal women. No investigation of hormonal contraceptive patterns and creatine kinase. The effect of carbohydrate loading on COC users' muscle glycogen is unknown. Pre-sleep protein has not been elucidated in female athletes. Whether 90 g/h multi-transportable carbohydrate raises oxidation in women is untested. In each case the recommendation given is a male-derived default with a caveat attached — which is defensible, but readers should know that is what it is.

**Extrapolated from male data.** The during-exercise carbohydrate guidance, the beta-alanine dose, the creatine protocol, and the protein per-meal distribution are all male-derived and carried across essentially unchanged. The stand is unusually honest about this, repeatedly writing that guidelines "have largely been established from male data and generalized to women."

**A notable gap in the stand itself.** Given how much space RED-S gets, **bone health is barely covered**. Reduced bone mineral density appears only as a listed consequence of low energy availability and of menopause, and in the finding that high protein diets (**>2.2 g/kg/day**) did not harm bone or kidney function in trained women ([Antonio 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29434529/), [Antonio 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26500462/)). There is **no section on calcium or vitamin D intake, no bone stress injury guidance, and no screening advice** — a real omission in a female athlete stand, since bone stress injury is one of the most common ways RED-S actually presents.

**Conflicts and framing.** The author list overlaps heavily with ISSN position stand authorship on creatine, beta-alanine, caffeine and nutrient timing, and those stands are cited as authority within this one ([Buford 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17908288/), [Trexler 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26175657/), [Guest 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33388079/), [Kerksick 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919842/)). The document is partly self-referential — which doesn't invalidate it, but is worth knowing.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** Almost nothing. The funding line reads "the author(s) reported there is no funding associated with the work featured in this article," and the disclosure statement is a single line of publisher boilerplate: "No potential conflict of interest was reported by the author(s)." There are no per-author entries, no companies named, no roles described.

**Is any declared tie material?** There is no declared tie to assess, so the honest answer is that this section cannot do its job on this document — and that is itself worth recording. Several of this stand's authors appear on other ISSN stands from the same period, including the 2023 essential amino acids stand and the 2021 caffeine stand, where they declare research grants, advisory-board seats, consulting relationships and in some cases patents and equity. None of that appears here. A blanket "no potential conflict" from a large panel whose members disclose extensively elsewhere is a weak disclosure, and it should be read as a gap in the record rather than as evidence that no ties exist.

**The independence limitation is the one flagged above.** The stand cites earlier ISSN stands as authority on creatine, beta-alanine, caffeine and nutrient timing, and its author list overlaps with theirs. Those source documents carry detailed declarations of their own. If you want to know who has a commercial interest in the supplement recommendations reproduced here, the declarations worth reading are on the stands being cited, not on this one.
