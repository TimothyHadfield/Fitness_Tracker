# Caffeine and Performance: The 2010 Position (Superseded)

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2010 Jan 27 · [PMID 20205813](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20205813/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-7-5)
**Authors:** Goldstein ER, Ziegenfuss T, Kalman D, Kreider R, Campbell B, Wilborn C, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 2.0 — <http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0>
**Topic:** Supplements

> **Read this one for history, not for advice.** It was replaced by the [2021 stand](caffeine-2021.md), which reverses three of its central claims. Where the two disagree, follow 2021.

## The position, in one paragraph

Caffeine at **3–6 mg/kg** taken **60 min before exercise** improves endurance and sustained high-intensity performance; more than that adds nothing. It is best taken in **anhydrous form** (capsule or powder) rather than as coffee. It does **not** dehydrate you during exercise, whatever the folklore says — that is the claim this stand argues most forcefully and most successfully. Its weakest claim is that caffeine only works in **trained** athletes, stated with a confidence the underlying studies don't carry, and reversed eleven years later.

## Bottom line

- **3–6 mg/kg** (**1.4–2.7 mg/lb**) is the working range. **≥9 mg/kg adds no further benefit** and, in at least one study, may impair performance through excessive CNS stimulation ([Graham & Spriet 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775331/)).
- **60 min pre-exercise** is the standard protocol; **15–30 min** also works.
- **Anhydrous > coffee.** Capsules improved run time to exhaustion, while the same caffeine dose delivered as coffee did not ([Graham 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9729561/)).
- **Caffeine does not cause meaningful dehydration during exercise.** Seven or eight independent studies found no change in sweat rate, total water loss or fluid balance, even under heat stress.
- **Endurance is where the effect is largest.** 9 mg/kg raised cycling time to exhaustion from **~75 to ~96 min** ([Spriet 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1616022/)); running time went from **~49 to 71 min** at the same dose ([Graham & Spriet 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1778925/)).
- **Team-sport skill improves, not just capacity.** A 10% gain in rugby ball-passing accuracy at 6 mg/kg ([Stuart 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16286872/)), and 8.5%/7.6% more total sprint work across two halves ([Schneiker 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16540848/)).
- **Sleep-deprived cognition responds at low absolute doses.** In Navy SEAL trainees, **200 mg beat 100 mg** but **300 mg was no better than 200 mg**; 100 mg was indistinguishable from placebo ([Lieberman 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12424548/)).
- **Strength results are a mess** — 2.1–3.0 mg/kg improved bench 1RM by 2.1% in one study ([Beck 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16937961/)) while 6 mg/kg improved nothing in another ([Astorino 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17851681/)). Lower body consistently fails to respond.
- **Caffeine does not block glycogen resynthesis** — 8 mg/kg with carbohydrate raised it by **66%** over carbohydrate alone ([Pedersen 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18467543/)).
- The stand claims caffeine works in **trained athletes only** for high-intensity work. This is its weakest claim (see below).

## Dose–response: the plateau

Three studies drive the 3–6 mg/kg recommendation, and they are more contradictory than the summary admits.

| Study | Doses tested | Result |
|---|---|---|
| [Graham & Spriet 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775331/) | 3, 6, 9 mg/kg | Performance improved at **3 and 6 only**; 9 mg/kg failed despite the biggest metabolic response |
| [Pasman 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7657415/) | 5, 9, 13 mg/kg | **All three** improved endurance, ~**27%** on average, with no difference between them |
| [Lieberman 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12424548/) | 100, 200, 300 mg | 200 > 100; **300 = 200**; 100 = placebo |

The Graham & Spriet paradox is the interesting one: 3 mg/kg improved performance while raising neither epinephrine nor free fatty acids, and 9 mg/kg raised both and improved nothing. The 2010 stand notes this without following it to its conclusion. The 2021 revision does, and uses it to discard the substrate-availability mechanism entirely.

## Anhydrous caffeine versus coffee

This is the claim the ISSN later softened. The evidence in 2010:

- **[Graham 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9729561/)** gave trained runners **4.45 mg/kg** as capsules, regular coffee, decaf coffee, or decaf + capsules. Only the capsule condition significantly extended run time — an extra 2–3 km. The authors speculated that some other coffee compound blunts caffeine's action.
- **[de Paulis 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12065074/)** offered a mechanism: chlorogenic acid derivatives formed during roasting may interfere with caffeine's adenosine antagonism.
- **[McLellan & Bell 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15657474/)** showed the interference isn't practically important: a cup of regular coffee 30 min beforehand did **not** blunt the ergogenic effect of a subsequent **3–7 mg/kg** anhydrous dose, which averaged a **24%** improvement over placebo.
- Coffee alone still worked in some designs — **3 g of coffee (~150–200 mg caffeine)** produced a **4.2 s** faster 1500 m treadmill time than decaf ([Wiles 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1623356/)); 6 mg/kg from coffee lowered RPE during submaximal cycling but nothing else ([Demura 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18380106/)).

So the 2010 conclusion rests substantially on **one 1998 study with nine subjects**. The 2023 ISSN coffee stand re-examines the same paper and reaches a different verdict.

## Hydration: the claim this stand got right

The strongest section, and the one that has aged best. The dehydration folklore traces to a **1928 study with three subjects** (Eddy & Downs 1928 — the reference carries no PMID or DOI, so it is cited here by author and year only). Against it:

- **7.5 mg/kg** before loaded treadmill walking: no difference in total water loss or sweat rate ([Falk 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2383801/)).
- **8.7 mg/kg** in divided doses: urine volume rose **at rest** but fluid balance during 180 min of cycling was unchanged ([Wemple 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9059904/)).
- **Chronic 3 mg/kg for 6 days, then 0/3/6 mg/kg for 6 more:** no effect on sweat rate, fluid-electrolyte balance or thermoregulation in 59 men ([Roti 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16491580/)).
- **6 mg/kg** with or without water or a carbohydrate-electrolyte solution: no effect on sweat rate or heat dissipation ([Del Coso 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19092693/)).
- Rehydrating with ~**741 mg/day** of caffeine from Coca-Cola across three days of two-a-day practices: no change in heart rate, rectal temperature, plasma volume or sweat rate ([Fiala 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15467100/)).

The distinction the stand draws is the useful one: **diuresis at rest is real; diuresis during exercise is not.** Studies from 1970–1990 that measured urine at rest were being wrongly generalised to athletes ([Armstrong 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12187618/)).

## Trained versus untrained: the claim to distrust

The stand concludes it is *"exceedingly apparent that caffeine is not effective for non-trained individuals participating in high-intensity exercise."* The supporting studies:

| Population | Dose | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Untrained, 30-s Wingate | 5 mg/kg | No effect ([Collomp 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1752708/)) |
| Non-trained, 4× Wingate | 6 mg/kg | Power **declined** in final two bouts ([Greer 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9760347/)) |
| Untrained, 2× 60-s cycling | 6 mg/kg | Slower time to peak power in bout 2 ([Crowe 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17240784/)) |
| Non-trained, agility + Wingate | 6 mg/kg | No effect ([Lorino 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17194233/)) |
| Trained swimmers vs untrained | ~4.3 mg/kg | Faster only in the **trained** group ([Collomp 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1592065/)) |
| Conditioned athletes, Wingate | 5 mg/kg | Greater peak power ([Woolf 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18708685/)) |

The stand offers the alternative explanation itself, in the same paragraph — *"the variability in performance of untrained subjects may mask the effect"* — and then states the strong version anyway. That is the pattern to watch for in position stands: a mechanistic caveat acknowledged and then discarded in the conclusions. By 2021 the ISSN had reversed it.

**Two citation errors sit in this same section.** The text attributes the agility study to "Lorino et al. [47]", but marker 47 resolves to Crowe (the correct Lorino reference is 73). And it attributes the trained-vs-untrained swimmer study to "Collomp et al. [46]", but 46 is Collomp's 1991 Wingate paper; the swimmer study is reference 29. Both are in the source document, not in this note.

## Women

The stand devotes a section to women and admits the literature is *"scant."* What it has:

- **9 mg/kg** improved 2000 m rowing time by **1.3%** in trained oarswomen; **6 mg/kg** gave a non-significant 0.7% ([Anderson 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11099373/)). This is one of very few results anywhere favouring the high dose.
- **5 and 10 mg/kg** both reduced leg muscle pain during cycling, with **no difference between them** ([Motl 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16540851/)).
- **6 mg/kg** raised energy expenditure during treadmill walking by **seven calories per 30 minutes** — statistically real, practically negligible, and the stand says so ([Ahrens 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17313293/)). Notably, an initial 9 mg/kg arm was abandoned after **7 of 10** subjects had profuse sweating, tremor, dizziness and vomiting.
- The bench-press finding in resistance-trained women is cited to **Goldstein et al. 2009, an unpublished Master's thesis** (Florida Atlantic University) by this stand's own lead author. No PMID, no DOI, no peer review.

## Habituation

The 2010 position is essentially "we don't know, so ask the athlete." Users (≥300 mg/day) and non-users (≤50 mg/day) both improved, but the effect lasted about **three hours longer in non-users** ([Bell & McLellan 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12235019/)). Two other studies found no user/non-user difference in performance ([Dodd 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1893906/)). The practically useful observation is about side effects, not performance: 13 of 22 subjects reported jitters, elevated heart rate and tremor at 6 mg/kg, and those feelings were **worse in the low-habitual consumers** ([Astorino 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17851681/)).

Nothing in this stand supports cycling off caffeine. It does not mention the practice.

## Strength of the evidence

**Well supported.** The hydration conclusion. Half a dozen independent trials, varied doses and conditions, consistent direction, and a clear mechanistic distinction between rest and exercise. This is the part of the document that has held up completely.

**Well supported but narrow.** The endurance dose range. The effect sizes reported (up to +27% time to exhaustion) come from time-to-exhaustion protocols, which are far more variable and more flattering than the time-trial protocols the 2021 stand relies on. A 2–4% time-trial improvement and a 27% time-to-exhaustion improvement can describe the same underlying effect — the reader should not carry the bigger number forward.

**Overreached.** The "not effective in untrained individuals" conclusion, stated as *exceedingly apparent* on the basis of four null studies in populations with high performance variability, and contradicted by the ISSN's own 2021 revision.

**Thin.** The women's strength claim, which rests on an unpublished master's thesis authored by the position stand's own first author. The stand declares no competing interests, and by the disclosure norms of 2010 that is unremarkable — but citing your own unpublished thesis as evidence in a consensus document is a self-citation problem regardless.

**Superseded on mechanism.** This document devotes substantial space to fat oxidation and glycogen sparing as the ergogenic route. The 2021 stand states that hypothesis was "challenged and eventually dismissed." The 2010 text actually contains the evidence against itself — [Graham & Spriet's](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775331/) 3 mg/kg dose improved performance while changing no substrate marker — but doesn't act on it.

**Stale on doping.** The stand writes that "the International Olympic Committee mandates an allowable limit of 12 μg of caffeine per ml of urine," which had ceased to be true in 2004 when caffeine came off the prohibited list. It does mention WADA's monitoring programme two sentences later, so the document contradicts itself within one paragraph.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** Very little, in the manner of its era. The competing-interests statement reads in full: "The authors declare that they have no competing interests." No funding statement appears in the record. There are no per-author entries and no companies named.

**Is any declared tie material?** None is declared, so there is nothing to weigh. Whether that reflects the authors' actual position in 2010 or the disclosure norms of BMC journals at the time cannot be settled from the document itself. What can be said is that several of these authors declare extensive supplement-industry relationships on later ISSN stands in this library, including the 2013 HMB stand and the 2015 beta-alanine stand. A blanket one-line declaration should not be read as evidence that no ties existed.

**The independence limitation is the one already noted.** The claim that caffeine improves strength performance in women rests on an unpublished master's thesis authored by this stand's own first author. That is not a conflict of interest and no disclosure statement would catch it, but it is the point at which the document is least independent of the people who wrote it. The dose-response, hydration and coffee-versus-anhydrous conclusions draw on a wide independent literature and are unaffected.
