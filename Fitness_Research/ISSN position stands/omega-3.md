# Long-Chain Omega-3 (EPA and DHA): Dose, What It Does, and What It Doesn't

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2025 Dec · [PMID 39810703](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39810703/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2024.2441775)
**Authors:** Jäger R, Heileson JL, Abou Sawan S, Dickerson BL, Leonard M, Kreider RB, et al (12 authors)
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

EPA and DHA are conditionally essential — you convert almost none from plant-source ALA — and athletes are systematically short of them. The stand's ten position points claim benefits across endurance, strength, soreness, immunity, head-impact protection, sleep and the gut. Read the body of the document rather than the summary and the picture is narrower: the best-supported findings are that supplementation reliably raises the omega-3 index and produces measurable cardiovascular changes (lower submaximal heart rate, faster heart-rate recovery, better endothelial function), and that prophylactic dosing in collision-sport athletes lowers a blood marker of axonal injury. **It does not build muscle in young adults** — the stand says so explicitly. Performance effects on time trials, power and strength are inconsistent. Soreness effects are equivocal by the document's own admission, despite the position statement saying otherwise.

## Bottom line

- **Athletes are deficient.** In 404 NCAA Division I football players, **not one** had an omega-3 index above the 8% cardioprotective target; the mean was **4.4 ± 0.8%** ([Anzalone 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30645147/)).
- To move from typical values to an **O3i of 8%** takes roughly an extra **1.4 g/day of EPA+DHA** ([Schuchardt 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38879135/)). Standard fish oil is only ~30% omega-3 (180 mg EPA + 120 mg DHA per 1000 mg capsule) — so that's **4–5 standard capsules/day**, or a concentrate.
- **Flax, chia and walnuts won't do it.** Only **5–10% of dietary ALA converts to EPA** and **2–5% to DHA** ([Wijendran 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15189133/)). Use fish, fish oil, krill oil or algal oil.
- **No hypertrophy benefit in young adults.** Across 8 trials, **1** showed a statistically significant fat-free-mass difference, and that one used no structured training ([Noreen 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20932294/): +0.5 kg vs −0.1 kg, p = 0.03).
- **Why it doesn't:** 8 weeks of **5 g/day fish oil (3500 mg EPA + 900 mg DHA)** failed to raise muscle protein synthesis after 30 g whey, at rest or post-exercise ([McGlory 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27009278/)) — 30 g whey already saturates the response ([Witard 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24257722/)).
- **It may help if your protein is low.** Omega-3 status tracked with muscle mass **only in adults with low protein intake** ([De Branco 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36508774/)); the null resistance-training trial had participants averaging **≥1.2 g/kg/day protein** ([Heileson 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36822153/)).
- **Strength is the more promising signal.** 10 weeks of RET plus **4.5 g/day fish oil (2280 EPA + 1580 DHA)** produced higher absolute bench 1RM (**11.3 kg vs 6.3 kg**, p = 0.047) and higher relative bench (p = 0.011) and squat (p = 0.045) — with no FFM difference ([Heileson 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36822153/)).
- **Timeline:** muscle phospholipid incorporation takes a **minimum of 4 weeks** ([McGlory 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24726616/)), with strength effects reported to plateau at **3–6 months**. Nothing here works in a fortnight.
- **Cardiovascular effects are the most consistent finding**, and they appear at surprisingly low doses: **140 mg EPA + 560 mg DHA for 8 weeks** lowered exercise heart rate and improved heart-rate recovery ([Macartney 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25355484/)).
- **Head impacts:** pooled across three American-football trials, supplementation lowered end-of-season neurofilament light by **−2.23 ± 0.83 pg/mL** ([Heileson 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39429508/)).

## Dosing, by what you want

| Goal | Dose | Duration | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Correct deficiency (reach O3i 8%) | **~1.4 g/day EPA+DHA** above habitual intake | ongoing | High |
| Cardiovascular / exercise economy | **140–800 mg EPA + 560–2400 mg DHA** | 8 weeks | Moderate |
| Strength alongside resistance training | **~2.3 g EPA + 1.6 g DHA** (4.5 g/day fish oil) | ≥10 weeks, plateau 3–6 months | Low–moderate |
| Soreness after damaging exercise | **2–4 g/day EPA+DHA** | 4–7.5 weeks pre-loaded | Low, equivocal |
| Head-impact prophylaxis (collision sport) | **2 g/day DHA** minimum | full season (~26 weeks) | Low–moderate |
| Hypertrophy | — | — | **No support** |

Form barely matters for raising blood levels: triglycerides, re-esterified triglycerides, free fatty acids, ethyl esters and phospholipids all work, with phospholipids (krill) most bioavailable and ethyl esters least. Krill oil also carries astaxanthin and less contamination risk, but the one krill resistance-training trial used a low dose (393 mg EPA + 240 mg DHA) and was underpowered ([Georges 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29854443/)). Algal oil is the vegetarian option and is DHA-dominant.

## Endurance: cardiovascular yes, performance no

The mechanism is real — omega-3s incorporate into the sarcolemma within two weeks (versus months for adipose tissue) and into mitochondrial membranes, altering ADP sensitivity ([McGlory 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24726616/)). The measurable downstream effects are cardiovascular: reduced heart rate and myocardial oxygen demand at 55% VO₂peak on **800 mg EPA + 2400 mg DHA for 8 weeks** ([Peoples 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19034030/)); improved flow-mediated dilation and nitric oxide release, with ΔNO correlating to ΔVO₂max, on **660 mg EPA + 440 mg DHA twice daily for 3 weeks** ([Zebrowska 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25176010/)); improved running economy and VO₂peak on **2234 mg EPA + 916 mg DHA for 12 weeks** ([Tomczyk 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36161864/)).

But scan the outcome column of the stand's own summary table and **time-trial and time-to-exhaustion results are overwhelmingly "no significant difference"** — including in the Tomczyk trial that improved running economy. Trained soccer players on **1600 mg EPA + 1040 mg DHA for 10 weeks** showed nothing at all ([Raastad 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9089901/)). The stand's own key-findings box concedes that "direct impact on endurance performance remains inconsistent." The position statement then says supplementation "has been shown to enhance endurance capacity." Those two sentences are in the same document.

## Recovery: the claim the document argues against itself

The most informative recovery trial is by one of the stand's own authors. Seven weeks of **4 g/day of DHA alone, or EPA alone**, reduced 48 h soreness after downhill running and jumping lunges — but **the combined EPA/DHA arm did not beat placebo** ([Heileson 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38051142/)). If the effect were robust the combination should not be the arm that fails.

Elsewhere: **2145 mg EPA + 858 mg DHA for 4 weeks** reduced soreness at 24 h ([Kyriakidou 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33441158/)); **6 g/day for 7.5 weeks** reduced soreness up to 96 h and maintained vertical jump ([VanDusseldorp 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32727162/)); professional rugby players showed a moderate soreness benefit and better maintenance of explosive power ([Black 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29985775/)). Against that, five or more trials found nothing.

On objective markers the picture is worse. **Inflammation markers do not move** after damaging exercise — the stand's own key finding says omega-3 "does not decrease measures of inflammation following exercise-induced muscle damage", with nulls on IL-6, TNF-α and CRP. And the single study said to confirm reduced CK and LDH at 48 and 72 h (Rajabi *et al*, 2013 — no PubMed record, no DOI) is published in a journal outside the indexed literature.

## Immunity, brain and sleep

**Immunity:** a recurring finding is reduced TNF-α after ≥4 weeks of **2400 mg EPA + 1200 mg DHA** ([Delfan 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26026502/), [Andrade 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17923401/)). But cytokine changes are not illness rates. **Exactly one study** has looked at upper respiratory infection incidence in athletes — 16 weeks, no difference — and its experimental arm also contained vitamin D and whey protein, so it cannot isolate omega-3 at all ([Da Boit 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25664999/)). Nieman found no change in cytokines, CRP, CK or time trial in cyclists on 2000 EPA + 400 DHA ([Nieman 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19910654/)). The one clinically hard immune result is for **exercise-induced bronchoconstriction**: 3 weeks of 3200 mg EPA + 2200 mg DHA improved post-exercise pulmonary function in elite athletes ([Mickleborough 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12904324/)).

**Head impacts** are where this stand is most useful and most honest. Three American-football trials: **2000/4000/6000 mg/day DHA over 189 days** attenuated the rise in neurofilament light when collapsed across doses ([Oliver 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26765633/)); a multi-site **non-randomised** study using a different university's team as control found Nf-L stable in the supplemented team ([Heileson 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34579748/)); and a proper RCT found **no effect** ([Mullins 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35631280/)). The meta-analysis of all three favours supplementation. Nf-L is a biomarker, not a clinical outcome. For *treating* concussion, the one pilot RCT in 40 adolescents on 2 g/day DHA found **no statistically significant difference in recovery time** ([Miller 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35722886/)); the 5-days-earlier symptom resolution was not significant.

**Sleep and gut** are the two weakest sections. The sleep evidence in healthy adults amounts to one pilot study of 2.5 g/day for 60 days reducing daytime sleepiness in **deployed US soldiers** ([Dretsch 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24690964/)), plus children and adolescent studies, plus a null in chronic insomnia. The gut section contains no human athlete data at all — it establishes that ISAPP's 2017 definition *could* include omega-3 ([Gibson 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28611480/)), notes that GI symptoms affect up to **70%** of endurance athletes ([Peters 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10364027/)) and that splanchnic blood flow can drop **80%** during maximal exercise, then concedes that "studies in exercising athletes are needed."

## Strength of the evidence

**Solid:** the deficiency data; the pharmacokinetics (form, dose–response, 4-week incorporation window); the negative hypertrophy finding, which is well-reasoned and mechanistically explained; the cardiovascular measures, which are replicated across several independent groups.

**Overstated.** The final position statement outruns the document's own section summaries in at least four places:

| Position statement says | The body of the document says |
|---|---|
| "has been **shown to enhance** endurance capacity" | "direct impact on endurance performance remains inconsistent" |
| "**may decrease** subjective measures of muscle soreness" | "**equivocal** in decreasing subjective measures of muscle soreness" |
| "**is associated with improved** sleep quality" | "inconsistency of results indicates more research is needed" |
| "ω-3 PUFA **are classified as** prebiotics" | ISAPP's definition was updated to *potentially* include them; no athlete data exist |

That is a consensus document tightening its language between the evidence review and the headline claims, which is exactly the failure mode to watch for in position stands.

**Two citation problems.** Table 1 attributes a study of *25 well-trained football players* running a 2200 m time trial to reference [52], but [52] resolves to [Hill 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17490962/), a fish-oil-plus-aerobic-exercise trial in **overweight adults**. Separately, the body-composition section cites [52] as an aerobic-training study showing no influence on fat-free mass, when Hill 2007 reported improved body composition. And the CK/LDH recovery claim rests on [Rajabi 2013], a paper with no PubMed record and no DOI in the stand's own reference list.

**Self-citation is heavy.** Author J.L. Heileson is first or senior author on references [98], [103], [202] and [208] — the resistance-training trial, the EPA-vs-DHA soreness trial, one of the three Nf-L studies, *and* the meta-analysis that pools those Nf-L studies. Those four papers carry a disproportionate share of the strength, soreness and head-impact conclusions. This is not misconduct — the field is small and these are the relevant studies — but it means the "consensus" on those points is thinner than twelve author names imply.

**On funding and competing interests:** set out in the section below. It matters more here than for most stands: omega-3 is a large consumer supplement category, and the lead author holds abandoned patents on ω-3 PUFA.

**Cross-reference note:** the 2026 ISSN antioxidant stand ([PMID 41701327](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41701327/)) ranks omega-3 as a **top-tier** antioxidant at **1000–6000 mg/day EPA+DHA for 6–12 weeks**, citing the same VanDusseldorp recovery trial. That stand is more confident about omega-3's recovery effects than this stand — the dedicated document on the topic — actually is. Prefer this one on recovery.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** No funding. Lead author R.J. (Jäger) states he has no financial or business interest related to the manuscript, and in the same breath that he "is an inventor of abandoned patents on ω-3 PUFA (US20190060268, and WO2017031502)"; he also reports supplement research grants and contracts, paid industry consulting, honoraria and expert-witness work. S.A. (Abou Sawan) "is employed by Iovate Health Sciences International Inc. a company that sells dietary supplements." R.B.K. (Kreider) has "consulted with industry on product development including dietary supplements containing ω-3 PUFA." J.R.S. (Stout) has held grants and contracts on dietary supplements "including ω-3 PUFA" over 25 years. J.A. (Antonio) is CEO of the ISSN, which "has received on occasion grant support from companies that sell, market, or manufacture ω-3 PUFA." G.M.T. (Tinsley) owns a consultancy serving supplement manufacturers, stating none of his research funding has been ω-3-related. C.M.K., S.M.C., S.M.A., D.S.K. and R.C. report supplement-industry funding, consulting or advisory roles not specific to ω-3. D.M.C. is affiliated with the Pan Am Clinic Foundation, which receives support from ConMed Linvatec, Ossur, Zimmer Biomet and Arthrex — orthopaedic device firms with nothing in this subject. J.L.H., B.D., M.L., S.C.F., D.G.C. and T.B. declare nothing.

**Does any of it bear on the conclusions?** Four ties do, and together they form a pattern: the lead author holds ω-3 patents (abandoned, but held), a co-author works for a supplement seller, Kreider has consulted on ω-3 product development, and the society issuing the stand takes grant money from ω-3 manufacturers. That is squarely in-category, and it sits on a document whose headline position statement — as the section above shows — is worded more favourably than its own evidence review. Worth noting in the other direction: J.L. Heileson, whose four papers carry a disproportionate share of the strength, soreness and head-impact case, declares no competing interests at all. That concern is about how narrow the evidence base is, not about money.
