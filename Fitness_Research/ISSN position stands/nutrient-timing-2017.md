# Nutrient Timing: What Actually Matters, and What the Anabolic Window Became

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2017 · [PMID 28919842](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919842/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0189-4)
**Authors:** Kerksick CM, Arent S, Schoenfeld BJ, Stout JR, Campbell B, Wilborn CD, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 (<https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>)
**Topic:** Nutrient timing

## The position, in one paragraph

This is the 2017 revision of the ISSN's 2008 nutrient timing stand, and it is a substantially quieter document than its predecessor. Timing still matters, but almost entirely for carbohydrate, and mostly only when a second hard session is coming within hours. For protein the stand's own summary demotes timing outright: it is "of lower priority than the ingestion of optimal amounts of daily protein (**1.4–2.0 g/kg/day**)." The remaining protein-timing advice is a compliance argument rather than a metabolic deadline — eat protein around training because that is when you will actually eat it, and because not eating confers no benefit. The stand then expands into three areas the 2008 version never touched: how protein is spread across the day, protein before sleep, and how many meals you eat.

## Bottom line

- Daily carbohydrate **5–12 g/kg/day**, the **8–10 g/kg/day** end reserved for athletes training ≥70% VO₂max more than 12 h/week. Percentage-of-calories targets are explicitly abandoned as unworkable.
- Carbohydrate loading no longer needs a depletion phase: **8–10 g/kg/day for 1–3 days** while cutting training volume maximises muscle glycogen ([Bussau 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12111292/), [Fairchild 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12048325/)).
- During prolonged exercise, **230–350 mL of a 6–8% solution every 10–12 min**. **39 g/h works as well as 64 g/h** ([Newell 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25203688/)) — the higher dose buys nothing.
- Carbohydrate during exercise is reliably ergogenic only past **~90 min**; below **70 min** the literature is mixed ([Colombani 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23356905/), [Pochmuller 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27408608/)).
- Aggressive post-exercise carbohydrate — **0.6–1.0 g/kg within 30 min, repeated every 2 h for 4–6 h**, or **≥1.2 g/kg/h** — matters only when you must train again soon or cannot hit daily totals ([Jentjens 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12617691/), [van Loon 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10871568/)).
- Adding protein to carbohydrate speeds glycogen recovery **only when carbohydrate is below 1.2 g/kg/h** ([Jentjens 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11457801/), [Howarth 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19036894/)). Above that, protein adds nothing to glycogen.
- **20–40 g of high-quality protein (0.25–0.40 g/kg), containing 10–12 g EAA**, maximises MPS, which stays elevated **3–4 h** per dose. After whole-body training **40 g beat 20 g** ([MacNaughton 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27511985/)).
- Pre- versus post-workout protein makes no measurable difference over 10 weeks ([Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28070459/)); muscle stays sensitised to protein for **at least 24 h** after a session ([Morton 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26388782/)).
- Spread protein as **20–40 g every 3–4 h**. Four 20 g doses beat two 40 g doses and eight 10 g doses for both whole-body turnover ([Moore 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23067428/)) and myofibrillar synthesis ([Areta 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23459753/)).
- **30–40 g casein within 30 min of sleep** raises overnight MPS ([Res 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22330017/)) and, over 12 weeks, muscle and strength ([Snijders 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926415/)) — though that trial was not protein-matched.
- Meal frequency has **limited effect on body composition** once calories are set; the meta-analytic signal collapsed to a single confounding study ([Schoenfeld 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26024494/)).

## What changed since the 2008 stand

The 2008 stand ([PMID 18834505](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18834505/)) was built around three windows — pre, during, post — and its post-exercise advice was a deadline: consume carbohydrate and protein in a 3:1 or 4:1 ratio **within 30 minutes**. Nine years later almost none of that survives in the form it was written.

**How much the window widened.** In 2008 the operative number for protein was 30 minutes, with an outer bound of "within three hours after an exercise bout." In 2017 there is no post-exercise protein deadline at all. The stand instead reports that myofibrillar protein synthesis remains sensitised to amino acids for **at least 24 hours** after a resistance bout ([Burd 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21289204/), reviewed in [Morton 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26388782/)) and concludes only that "the pragmatic recommendation is for an athlete to feed as soon as possible after a workout" because "not eating does not offer any benefit." That is a shift from a metabolic window to a scheduling heuristic. The 30-minute figure survives in 2017 for **carbohydrate only**, and only under two named conditions: when rapid glycogen restoration is genuinely needed, and when daily carbohydrate is inadequate.

**Claims from 2008 that no longer stand:**

| 2008 claim | 2017 position |
|---|---|
| Pre-exercise amino acids "may result in the best response of all" ([Tipton 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11440894/)) | [Fujita 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18535123/) failed to replicate it; [Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28070459/) found pre and post identical over 10 weeks |
| CHO+PRO at **3:1 or 4:1 within 30 min** post-exercise | Protein only aids glycogen when carbohydrate is <1.2 g/kg/h; adding carbohydrate to adequate protein does not raise MPS ([Staples 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131864/)) |
| Insulin from co-ingested carbohydrate drives the benefit | Anti-proteolytic effect of insulin plateaus at **15–30 μIU/mL**, reached by **45 g of whey isolate alone** ([Power 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18679613/)) |
| Adding creatine to post-workout CHO+PRO improves strength and body composition | Creatine removed from scope entirely; named only as a possible future topic |
| Daily carbohydrate as **~65% of calories**, ~70% pre-competition | Percentage targets "have fallen out of favor"; **5–12 g/kg/day** replaces them |
| Fat timing within scope | "Research examining a specific timing question has yet to take shape" |
| 6–20 g EAA / ~20 g protein as the anabolic dose | **20–40 g (0.25–0.40 g/kg)**, with 40 g superior to 20 g after whole-body work |

**What drove the change.** Four things, all cited in the document. First, the direct replication failures — Fujita on pre-exercise superiority, and Schoenfeld's 10-week pre-versus-post trial, the first longitudinal test of the question. Second, [Aragon and Schoenfeld's 2013 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23360586/) and the accompanying [meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299050/), which found the apparent timing effect on strength and hypertrophy disappears once total daily protein is accounted for. Third, the 24-hour sensitivity work, which removed the physiological basis for a narrow window. Fourth, [Mitchell 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24586775/), showing acute post-exercise MPS does not correlate with hypertrophy over a training block — which undercuts the entire acute-MPS evidence base the 2008 stand rested on.

**What the 2017 stand added rather than revised:** protein distribution across the day, pre-sleep protein, meal frequency, and time-of-day calorie distribution. Two of the twelve authors (Schoenfeld, Arciero) are the primary investigators behind the distribution and protein-pacing evidence respectively, which is worth knowing when reading those sections.

## How wide is the window, really

The stand does not give a number. This is worth being precise about, because a **4–6 hour window spanning the session** is a figure that circulates widely and traces to Aragon and Schoenfeld's 2013 review, which the 2017 stand cites approvingly ([ref 125](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23360586/)) but never quantifies.

What the stand actually asserts, in its own words:

- A 20–40 g dose keeps MPS elevated for **3–4 hours**.
- Serum amino acids and MPS rise and stay elevated **3–5 hours** depending on bolus size.
- Muscle remains sensitised to protein for **at least 24 hours** post-session.
- Optimal spacing between protein doses is **3–4 hours**.
- Practical advice: eat "as soon as possible after a workout."

Those are not a single coherent window. The 24-hour sensitisation figure is much wider than 4–6 hours; the "as soon as possible" advice is narrower. The stand resolves the tension by declining to resolve it — it argues that where a strategy "may either help or have a neutral effect," employ it, because "differences in real-world athletic performances can be so small that even strategies that offer a modicum of benefit are still worth pursuing." That is a judgement call about asymmetric risk, not an evidence claim.

## Carbohydrate: the part where timing still earns its keep

Resistance training depletes glycogen — six sets of 12RM leg extensions cut vastus lateralis glycogen **39%** ([Robergs 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2055849/)) — but modestly compared with exhaustive endurance work, and the resistance-training timing literature is thin and mostly null. One study found benefit, and only in a *second* session on the same day. Pre- and during-exercise carbohydrate (1.0 g/kg pre, 0.5 g/kg every 10 min) cut glycogen loss **49%** without changing isokinetic performance ([Haff 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10997956/)).

For endurance the picture is firmer. **1–4 g/kg** in the hours before long, hard efforts raises muscle and liver glycogen ([Coyle 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3897180/)). The old fear of rebound hypoglycaemia from feeding close to the start ([Foster 1979](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/582616/)) is largely dead: a normal warm-up negates it, and moving intake *closer* to the start (15 min rather than 75 min) reduces symptoms ([Moseley 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12527977/)). Feeding 30 min out beat 120 min out for high-intensity capacity ([Galloway 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24281911/)).

Post-exercise, the founding observation still holds: glycogen restoration was **50% faster** when carbohydrate came within 30 min versus a 2-hour delay ([Ivy 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9694422/)). But outside genuine rapid-recovery situations, with daily carbohydrate matching demand, "the importance of timed carbohydrate ingestion is notably decreased." With **≥8 g/kg/day** glycogen restores fully within 24 h anyway ([Keizer 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3298088/)); **9–10 g/kg/day** sustains repeated hard days ([Nicholas 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9407252/)).

## Protein around training: the honest summary

Every longitudinal comparison the stand can cite comes out null or nearly so.

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| [Tipton 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16896166/) | 20 g whey immediately pre vs post, acute | MPS similarly raised by both |
| [Andersen 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15690307/) | 25 g protein blend pre+post vs maltodextrin, 14 wk | Fibre size and jump height improved — but this tests protein, not timing |
| [Hoffman 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19478342/) | 42 g collagen pre+post vs morning/evening, 10 wk | No timing effect. Poor protein source, DEXA, hypoenergetic subjects |
| [Candow 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16767436/) | 0.3 g/kg (~26 g) pre vs post, men 59–76, 12 wk | No timing effect; dose likely sub-threshold for anabolic-resistant muscle |
| [Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28070459/) | 25 g whey pre vs post, trained men, 10 wk | No difference in mass or strength |
| [Esmarck 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11507179/) | Immediate vs 2 h delayed, men ~74, 12 wk | Immediate better — but the delayed group gained **zero** cross-sectional area, which is implausible and has drawn criticism |

Esmarck is the single positive longitudinal timing study in the literature and the stand flags its problem directly. [Cribb and Hayes 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17095924/) is the other frequently cited positive, but it delivered creatine alongside the protein and carbohydrate, so it cannot isolate timing.

The stand's own caveat on Schoenfeld is worth repeating because it is the strongest defence of timing left: subjects trained 30 sessions over 10 weeks, while an NCAA athlete may accrue that volume in a fortnight. Nobody has tested peri-workout nutrition in genuinely high-volume athletes.

## Distributing protein across the day

This is where the stand makes its most confident *new* claim, and the underlying data are acute. Feeding a fixed 80 g of whey isolate over 12 h post-exercise as **4 × 20 g every 3 h** beat **2 × 40 g every 6 h** and **8 × 10 g every 90 min** for whole-body turnover ([Moore 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23067428/)) and for myofibrillar synthesis ([Areta 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23459753/)). The stand notes the caveats itself: 80 g/12 h is far below what a large athlete needs, whey alone is not a mixed meal, and gastric emptying differs in real food.

The mechanistic frame is the "muscle full" effect ([Millward 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19094281/), [Atherton 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20844073/)): MPS peaks ~90 min after ingestion and falls back to baseline within roughly 90 min more, even while blood amino acids stay high. If doses land too close together, later ones may be wasted. The stand is candid that "no clear consensus exists as to the acceptance of this theory" and that "the practical importance has yet to be demonstrated."

**Protein pacing** — 20–40 g every ~3 h, first meal within 60 min of waking, last within 3 h of sleep — is presented with a body of work by Arciero, an author of the stand ([Arciero 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27258301/), [Ives 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27789212/)).

## Pre-sleep protein

The most genuinely new section. **30–40 g of casein 30 min before sleep** raises overnight MPS in young and older men ([Res 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22330017/), [Groen 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21917635/)) and morning resting metabolic rate ([Madzima 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23768612/)), and does **not** blunt overnight lipolysis or fat oxidation in obese men ([Kinsey 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27472361/)).

Only two trials ran longer than four weeks, and they disagree revealingly. [Snijders 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926415/) found more muscle and strength over 12 weeks with 27.5 g pre-sleep casein — but the protein group ate **1.9 g/kg/day versus 1.3** in placebo, so this is a total-protein result wearing a timing costume. [Antonio 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28515842/), which *was* nitrogen-matched (54 g casein morning versus evening), found no difference. Pre-sleep protein "represents another opportunity to consume protein," not a distinct mechanism.

## Time of day and meal frequency

Weighting calories toward breakfast produced ~**2.5× more weight loss** and a **34% fall in triglycerides** over 12 weeks in overweight women on 1400 kcal ([Jakubowicz 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23512957/)). An earlier crossover found slightly more weight lost on morning-weighted intake but **more fat-free mass lost too** ([Keim 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9040548/)). All of this is in sedentary populations with no exercise intervention, which the stand says plainly.

On meal frequency it defers to the ISSN's own 2011 stand: the classic inverse correlations are epidemiological ([Fabry 1964](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14186149/), [Metzner 1977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/855813/)), controlled trials find nothing ([Cameron 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19943985/), [Alencar 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25862614/)), and the one meta-analysis suggesting benefit ([Schoenfeld 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26024494/)) was confounded by a single two-week outlier — which happened to be the only trial including exercise.

## Strength of the evidence

**Strongest.** Carbohydrate loading, in-exercise carbohydrate for efforts over ~90 min, and aggressive post-exercise carbohydrate for rapid glycogen restoration. These rest on decades of controlled crossover work with objective outcomes (biopsy glycogen, time trials), and the effect sizes are large.

**Weakest, relative to how it is usually presented.** Peri-workout protein timing. Every adequately designed longitudinal trial is null. The stand's defence of the practice is explicitly not an efficacy claim — it is that the strategy is free, harmless, and might help, so do it. That is defensible advice and honest reasoning, but readers should not mistake it for evidence that timing works.

**Acute-versus-chronic problem.** Most of the protein-distribution and pre-sleep evidence is acute MPS measurement, and the stand cites [Mitchell 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24586775/) showing acute MPS does not predict hypertrophy. It then proceeds to build recommendations on acute MPS anyway. That is the clearest internal tension in the document.

**Confounded positives.** Three of the studies most often used to support timing are confounded in ways the stand names: Cribb and Hayes included creatine, Snijders was not protein-matched, Esmarck's control group gained no muscle at all.

**Population limits, stated by the authors.** Small samples, mostly men, mostly non-elite. Female carbohydrate and fat metabolism differ ([Riddell 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14967866/), [Devries 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16690766/), [Carter 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11350771/)) and women may need substantially more total energy to supercompensate glycogen like men ([Wismann 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18500960/)).

**Two errors in the source.** The recommendation of **5–12 g/kg/day** carbohydrate is cited to refs 25–27 — Howarth, van Hall and van Loon — all of which are post-exercise glycogen-resynthesis papers, not daily intake guidance; the correct citation is almost certainly the adjacent ACSM stand ([Rodriguez 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19225360/)). Separately, the reference list duplicates two papers: Jentjens and Jeukendrup 2003 appears as both refs 62 and 64, and Schoenfeld's pre-versus-post trial appears as both refs 112 and 124.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** No funding statement appears in the record we have. The competing-interests declaration is long and industry-heavy. **CMK** (Kerksick, lead author) consults with and receives external funding from companies selling supplemental protein, is paid to present at conferences and to write for print and online media, and has served as an expert witness. **RBK** is a university-approved scientific advisor for **Nutrabolt**. **MJO** is on the advisory board of **Dymatize Nutrition**. **RW** is Chief Science Officer for **Post Active Nutrition**. **PJA** consults to and receives funding from supplement companies and is founder and CEO of **PRISE LLC**. **DSK** works for a contract research organisation running trials for supplement and pharmaceutical clients. **SMA**, **JRS**, **ASR**, **DSW**, **LT**, **CDW**, **TAV**, **MG** and **TNZ** declare supplement-industry grants, advisory roles or paid speaking. **JA** is CEO of the ISSN, "supported in part by grants from raw good suppliers and branded companies." **BJS** (Schoenfeld) and **AAA** (Aragon) report no conflicts.

**Is any of it material? No — and the direction is worth noticing.** This stand's central conclusion is deflationary. The anabolic window is far wider than the supplement industry spent twenty years implying; total daily protein matters more than its distribution; pre- and post-exercise feedings are largely interchangeable. That is a bad conclusion for the post-workout shake, and it was reached by a panel funded substantially by the people who sell them. The two authors who did most to build the deflationary case — Schoenfeld and Aragon, whose meta-analytic work underpins it — are the two who declare nothing. Declared ties pushing against the conclusion actually reached is reassuring, not concerning.

**Where to keep an eye open.** The protein-dose recommendations and the carbohydrate co-ingestion sections sit closest to the products the declaring authors are tied to. Those recommendations are conservative and match the independent literature, so nothing here needs discounting — but they are the parts of the document where a declared interest and a conclusion touch.
