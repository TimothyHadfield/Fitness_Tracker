# Essential Amino Acids (2023): What Free-Form EAAs Do, and Who They Are Actually For

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2023 Dec · [PMID 37800468](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37800468/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2023.2263409)
**Authors:** Ferrando AA, Wolfe RR, Hirsch KR, Church DD, Kviatkovsky SA, Roberts MD, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Protein & macronutrients

## The position, in one paragraph

Free-form essential amino acids — all nine, not leucine alone and not the three BCAAs — stimulate muscle protein synthesis harder per gram than intact protein, and they do it fast, because they need no digestion. The dose-response runs from about **1.5 g up to a plateau at 15–18 g** in a single serving. Combining EAAs with exercise produces a response larger than either alone, and taking them **before** training beats taking them after because exercise-driven blood flow multiplies delivery. Longitudinal outcome evidence — actual gains in lean mass, walking speed, grip and leg strength — is real but comes almost entirely from **older and clinically compromised populations**. The stand says so plainly, and then recommends more research in athletes. There is no dose recommendation for a healthy trained lifter in this document, because there is no evidence base for one.

## Bottom line

- **Nine EAAs**: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine. Arginine is conditionally essential.
- **MPS is stimulated from as little as 1.5 g** of oral EAA ([Wilkinson 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29031484/)) and **plateaus at 15–18 g** per dose ([Tipton 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15539275/)).
- **Non-essential amino acids add nothing.** Adding NEAAs to 18 g of EAAs made no difference to the MPS response ([Volpi 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12885705/)).
- **3 g of EAAs matched 20 g of whey isolate** for MPS in older women ([Bukhari 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25827594/)); 1.5 g and 6 g doses matched **40 g of whey** in the same population ([Wilkinson 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29031484/)).
- **Adding EAAs to whey beats whey alone** in young healthy adults ([Park 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32041644/)).
- **15 g of EAAs does not blunt the anabolic response to a meal three hours later** ([Paddon-Jones 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15572657/)) — unlike a protein isolate pre-load, which does.
- **Take them before training, not after.** Same dose pre- or post-exercise both raised phenylalanine by 130%, but pre-exercise gave roughly **3× greater amino acid delivery** and a much larger net uptake ([Tipton 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11440894/)).
- **Older adults need a higher leucine fraction.** At the same 6.7 g total EAA dose, a **40% leucine** blend produced ~**50% more MPS** than a 27% leucine blend ([Katsanos 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507602/)).
- **Dieting raises the requirement.** Five days at a **30% calorie deficit** required a **3-fold increase in EAA intake** to hold whole-body protein balance positive ([Gwin 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32768315/)).
- **Safety:** more than **100 g/day of supplemental EAA** sits within published safe upper limits on top of a typical ~40 g habitual intake. A reasonable single dose is **≤15 g**.
- **BCAAs are not a substitute.** BCAAs and their keto acids raised muscle FSR for 0–2 h and then **failed to sustain it at 2–5 h**, while milk protein did — because the other EAAs ran out ([Fuchs 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31250889/)).

## Why "all nine" is the load-bearing claim

This is the most useful argument in the document and it applies directly to the BCAA aisle.

Translational elongation — the physical assembly of a protein — only runs to completion if **every** required amino acid is present. A relative shortage of any one EAA terminates elongation early. So a supplement containing leucine alone, or the three BCAAs, can switch on the signalling machinery (mTORC1) and produce a transient 1–2 h bump in measured synthesis, but the amino acids needed to finish the job have to come from somewhere. First from free intracellular and extracellular pools, and once those are drained, **from accelerated breakdown of existing muscle protein** — which cancels the net gain.

Fuchs demonstrated exactly this in older adults: BCAAs, branched-chain keto acids and milk protein all stimulated FSR over 0–2 h, but only milk protein held it at 2–5 h, and plasma phenylalanine **fell** in the BCAA arms ([Fuchs 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31250889/)). The stand draws the methodological conclusion: only trust MPS studies with a measurement interval of **3 h or more**, because shorter windows will flatter incomplete amino acid mixtures.

Signalling follows the same ranking. After resistance exercise, activation of S6K1 and 4E-BP1 ran **placebo < leucine < BCAAs < EAAs** ([Moberg 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27053525/)).

## Dosing, and where the numbers come from

|---|---|---|
| Minimum that stimulates MPS at rest | **1.5 g** | [Wilkinson 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29031484/) |
| Plateau — no further gain per serving | **15–18 g** | [Tipton 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15539275/) |
| Reasonable practical single dose | **≤15 g** | Stated position |
| Older women, 3 months, lean mass gain | **15 g/day** | [Dillon 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19208731/) |
| Glucose-intolerant older adults, 16 weeks | **11 g twice daily between meals** | [Børsheim 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18294740/) |
| Women ≥75 y, 3 months, walking speed | **3 g twice daily** | [Kim 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22142410/) |
| Low-function older adults, 12 weeks | **12 g EAA + 3 g flavouring** | [Azhar 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33475727/) |
| 10-day bed rest, functional preservation | **3 × 15 g/day** | [Ferrando 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19419806/) |

Bolus versus spread did not matter: the same dose given as one bolus or **five smaller doses** produced the same MPS ([Mitchell 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25644339/)). And a **second bolus one hour after the first duplicated the anabolic response** of the first — the machinery is not refractory ([Børsheim 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12217881/)).

## Why EAAs beat protein per gram — and why that is less impressive than it sounds

The stand's headline efficacy claim is that free-form EAAs stimulate MPS more than an equal weight of high-quality protein, whether as an isolate ([Paddon-Jones 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16310330/)) or inside a meal ([Kim 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31212940/)).

The stated mechanism is straightforward and, once you see it, deflationary: a free-form EAA product is **100% EAA by weight**, while whey is roughly 50%. So "3 g of EAAs matched 20 g of whey isolate, which contains approximately 10 g of EAA" is a genuine and interesting result — but the gram-for-gram framing is doing a lot of the work in the comparison. The rest is kinetics: free EAAs need no digestion, absorb through the jejunum rapidly ([Adibi 1974](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4412534/)), and produce a **three-fold or greater** rise in plasma EAAs, which correlates with the size of the MPS response ([Church 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33276485/)). A 70 g mixed beef-protein meal raises plasma EAAs only minimally by comparison.

One counterweight the stand includes to its credit: the relationship between peak plasma EAA and MPS is **not agreed upon**. One study found no difference between bolus and split dosing ([Mitchell 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25644339/)), which is hard to reconcile with a pure speed-of-rise mechanism, and the stand says so.

One counterweight it leaves out. In elderly subjects, **15 g of whey protein beat free amino acids matched to whey's exact composition** for leg phenylalanine balance — the intact protein raised accrual significantly, the free amino acids did not ([Katsanos 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19083472/)). That is the reverse of the stand's general claim, in the population the stand most relies on. It does not appear anywhere in this document's 127 references, though the same author's other work is cited three times. It *is* cited in the [2017 protein stand](protein-2017.md) (ref 148).

## EAAs and exercise: the one genuinely strong mechanistic finding

Resistance exercise **alone does not produce anabolism.** It raises both synthesis and breakdown, and net muscle protein balance stays negative ([Biolo 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7900797/)). Amino acid delivery alone raises transport and synthesis. The **combination** is interactive rather than additive — the response exceeds the sum of the parts ([Biolo 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9252488/)), because exercise-driven limb blood flow multiplies the delivery of an elevated arterial EAA concentration.

Only the EAA fraction is needed: after lower-body resistance exercise, oral EAAs improved net balance to the **same extent as a complete amino acid mixture** ([Tipton 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10198297/)). Timing within an hour after training still works ([Rasmussen 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10658002/)), but the pre-exercise advantage is the practical takeaway — and even that has a contradicting study ([Fujita 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18535123/)), which the stand acknowledges.

The effect is not limited to lifting. Ninety minutes of cycling or loaded treadmill carriage with EAAs every 30 min raised MPS in both, with **weight-bearing loading giving the larger effect** ([Pasiakos 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26474292/)). In older adults, 24 weeks of 15 g/day EAA plus supervised aerobic training beat EAA alone for muscle quality and 400 m walking speed ([Markofski 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29750251/)).

## What this stand does not support

The most useful thing for a trained reader is the negative space.

**No HIIT benefit.** The three trials closest to a healthy training population all came back null. In untrained overweight/obese adults, 8 weeks of HIIT increased thigh muscle size, cross-sectional area, volume and quality — and **3.6 g of EAA twice daily added nothing** to any of it ([Hirsch 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33369879/)). VO₂ improved with HIIT, not further with EAA ([Hirsch 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34427732/)). No acute or chronic effect on time to exhaustion or workload progression ([Hirsch 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35512775/)). The stand's own summary: *"there is not enough evidence to draw conclusions."* Note the dose used — 3.6 g — sits at the very bottom of the effective range.

**No athlete outcome data.** The stand states it directly: *"Less work has documented the long-term effects/outcomes associated with EAA utilization in athletic populations."* Two of its fifteen final position points are requests for research rather than positions.

**No recommended profile.** There are effectively limitless nine-EAA combinations and almost no head-to-head comparisons, so the stand explicitly declines to discuss optimal profiles — beyond "more leucine for older adults."

## Safety

Consumption of EAAs has not been reported to cause adverse responses. The published safe upper limits, expressed as intake **above habitual**, total more than 100 g/day across the nine — for example leucine at **657 mg/kg/day** ([Cynober 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23096009/)). Against a typical American habitual EAA intake of ~40 g/day, even three 15 g doses is unremarkable.

Two real cautions: inborn errors of EAA metabolism (maple syrup urine disease), which would already be known to the individual; and **impaired kidney function**, where the stand states plainly that there is **insufficient data** to determine safety.

## Strength of the evidence

**Strong.** The mechanism. Translational elongation requires all nine EAAs; incomplete mixtures produce transient responses that do not sustain past ~2 h; measurement windows shorter than 3 h will overstate leucine-only and BCAA products. This is well-argued, well-cited ([Fuchs 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31250889/), [Moberg 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27053525/)), and it is the most practically valuable content here — it is a solid case against buying BCAAs.

**Strong.** The interactive effect of EAAs and exercise, and that resistance exercise alone leaves net balance negative. This rests on classic tracer work ([Biolo 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7900797/), [Biolo 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9252488/)) that has been repeatedly reproduced.

**Solid, but population-limited.** The longitudinal functional outcomes. Dillon, Børsheim, Kim, Azhar and Ferrando are real randomised trials with meaningful endpoints — lean mass, 6-minute walk distance, grip and leg strength — and the Azhar trial is the standout, with EAAs beating an equal-weight whey isolate on three of four measures in 92 subjects ([Azhar 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33475727/)). **Every one of them is in older, sarcopenic, glucose-intolerant, bed-rested or otherwise anabolically resistant people.** Anabolic resistance is precisely the condition a rapid, digestion-free, high-EAA bolus is built to overcome. Nothing here transfers automatically to a 25-year-old lifter already eating 2 g/kg/day.

**Thin.** The headline "EAAs beat protein" claim as it applies to anyone healthy. It is a per-gram comparison against a protein that is roughly half EAA by weight. The one published study that matched free amino acids to a protein's **exact composition** found the intact protein won ([Katsanos 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19083472/)), and it is absent from this stand's reference list. The economically relevant comparison — an EAA supplement versus an equal-EAA or equal-cost dose of whole food — is never made.

**Weakest.** Position point 12: *"EAA supplementation is efficacious in the vast majority of clinical studies and conditions."* This rests on a single paragraph listing roughly twenty conditions with sixty-odd citations, **no effect sizes, no study quality appraisal, no mention of any negative trial, and no systematic search**. It is a citation dump presented as a conclusion, and it is the sentence most likely to be quoted by a supplement seller.

**Also thin.** The safe upper limits table. Each of the nine values comes from a single proposal paper, and the arithmetic that turns them into "more than 100 g/day is safe" simply sums nine independently derived limits, which is not how tolerable upper intakes normally combine.

**Self-citation.** This is the most important thing to hold in mind while reading. The lead authors — Ferrando, Wolfe, Church, and their Arkansas group — are authors on a large share of the primary studies the document relies on, including several of the anchor citations ([Ferrando 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19419806/), [Church 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33276485/), [Park 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32041644/), [Azhar 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33475727/), [Kim 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31212940/), and the Biolo/Tipton/Wolfe tracer series). The text captured here carries **no competing-interests or funding statement**. The document is, in substance, a review of one laboratory's programme written by that laboratory and ratified by the society. The mechanistic claims are robust enough to stand independently; the efficacy-versus-protein claims are the ones to discount.
