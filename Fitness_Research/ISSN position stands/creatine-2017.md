# Creatine: the ISSN's 2017 Safety and Efficacy Stand

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2017 · [PMID 28615996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z)
**Authors:** Kreider RB, Kalman DS, Antonio J, Ziegenfuss TN, Wildman R, Collins R, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 (<https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>)
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

Creatine monohydrate is, in the ISSN's words, "the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement currently available" for high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass. Load at **~0.3 g/kg/day for 5–7 days**, maintain at **3–5 g/day**. Nothing beats plain monohydrate — every branded alternative tested has failed to show better retention. On safety the stand goes further than any previous edition: **up to 30 g/day for 5 years** has produced no detrimental effects in any population studied, from infants to the elderly, and the authors argue that the under-18 warnings on product labels are legal cover rather than science. Roughly half the document is about medicine rather than sport — neuroprotection, sarcopenia, cognition, pregnancy, creatine synthesis deficiencies — and that is where the evidence quality varies most.

## Bottom line

- A normal diet supplying **1–2 g/day** leaves muscle creatine only **60–80% saturated**; supplementation lifts muscle creatine and PCr by **20–40%** ([Hultman 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8828669/), [Harris 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1327657/)).
- Fastest route to saturation: **5 g four times daily (~0.3 g/kg/day) for 5–7 days** ([Hultman 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8828669/), [Harris 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1327657/)).
- Then **3–5 g/day** to maintain; **5–10 g/day** for larger athletes in heavy training ([Kreider 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12701816/)).
- Skip the load and take **3 g/day for 28 days** and you get to the same place, more slowly, with "less supported" early performance effects ([Hultman 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8828669/)).
- Total muscle creatine pool averages **~120 mmol/kg dry mass**, ceiling **~160 mmol/kg**. Vegetarians start at **90–110** and gain more ([Hultman 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8828669/), [Brosnan 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26874700/)).
- Performance in high-intensity and repeated efforts rises **10–20%** after loading, scaled to how much PCr you actually gained ([Kreider 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12701815/)).
- Stores return to baseline in **4–6 weeks** after stopping, and never fall *below* baseline — endogenous synthesis is not suppressed ([Vandenberghe 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9390981/), [Kreider 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12701816/)).
- Co-ingesting **carbohydrate**, or carbohydrate + protein, improves retention — but not necessarily performance ([Green 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8944667/), [Steenge 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10956365/)).
- Novel forms — citrate, serum, ethyl ester, buffered, nitrate — have **never** beaten monohydrate on retention ([Jäger 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21424716/), [Spillane 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19228401/), [Jagim 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22971354/)).
- The only consistently reported side effect across ~1,000 studies is **weight gain**.

## Dosing, in practice

| Protocol | Dose | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loading | **~0.3 g/kg/day** (≈5 g × 4/day) | 5–7 days | Fastest saturation ([Hultman 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8828669/)) |
| Maintenance | **3–5 g/day** | indefinite | **5–10 g/day** for large athletes in hard training ([Kreider 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12701816/)) |
| No-load | **3 g/day** | 28 days | Same endpoint; early performance effect "less supported" |
| General health, ageing | **3 g/day** | lifespan | The authors endorse [Wallimann 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21448658/)'s recommendation |
| Clinical / deficiency syndromes | **0.3–0.8 g/kg/day** (21–56 g/day at 70 kg) | years | Used in AGAT/GAMT deficiency ([Braissant 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21390529/), [Stöckler-Ipsiroglu 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24268530/)) |

Creatine degrades to creatinine in solution — faster at low pH and high temperature (**4% at pH 5.5, 21% at pH 3.5 after 3 days at 25 °C**) — which is why it is sold as a powder. In the gut this does not happen to any meaningful extent; absorption into blood is **near 100%** ([Jäger 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21424716/)).

## Beyond strength: what else the stand claims for training

**Glycogen and recovery.** Creatine loading before glycogen loading produced greater glycogen supercompensation than carbohydrate alone ([Nelson 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11445755/)). Co-ingesting 5 g creatine with 95 g glucose improved both creatine and carbohydrate storage ([Green 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8944667/)).

**Muscle damage.** Cooke found **+10% isokinetic** and **+21% isometric** knee extension strength during recovery from damaging exercise, with plasma CK **84% lower** at days 2–7 ([Cooke 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19490606/)). In marathon runners, loading before a 30 km race attenuated CK (**−19%**), PGE2 (**−61%**) and TNF-α (**−34%**) ([Santos 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15306159/)).

**Overreaching.** 0.3 g/kg/day for 4 weeks of deliberately excessive volume preserved performance that otherwise declined ([Volek 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14685870/)) — the most directly useful finding here for a lifter running a hard block.

**Heat.** Creatine holds **~0.5–1.0 L** of extra water, proportional to the acute weight gain. Loading (20 g/day, 7 days) increased intracellular water and lowered heart rate, rectal temperature and sweat rate during prolonged exercise in the heat ([Kilduff 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15467102/)). At 21.6 g/day it did not compromise hydration or thermoregulation in dehydrated men ([Watson 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16619091/)).

**Immobilisation and rehab.** Two weeks of leg casting followed by 10 weeks of rehab: the creatine group gained **+10% fibre cross-sectional area** and **+25% peak strength** over placebo ([Hespel 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11600695/)). Not all such studies agree — creatine did nothing for strength recovery after ACL surgery, and nothing for 800 m wheelchair performance in trained SCI athletes.

## Older adults

This is where the 2017 stand is strongest outside pure performance. Two meta-analyses found greater gains in muscle mass and strength when creatine was added to resistance training in people averaging **64 years** — 357 participants in one ([Devries & Phillips 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24576864/)), 405 in the other ([Candow 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24190049/)). A **low dose of 0.1 g/kg/day** with protein raised lean mass and upper-body strength in men aged 59–77 ([Candow 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18685526/)), and 12 months at 0.1 g/kg/day preserved femoral neck bone mineral density in postmenopausal women ([Chilibeck 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25386713/)). Note the dose: **0.1 g/kg/day is roughly 7 g for a 70 kg person**, and it worked.

## Brain and cognition

Creatine raises brain creatine content by only **5–15%** — far less than muscle. Reported effects: reduced mental fatigue at 8 g/day for 5 days ([Watanabe 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11985880/)); improved working memory and processing speed at 5 g/day for 6 weeks ([Rae 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14561278/)); less decrement after sleep deprivation at 20 g/day for 7 days ([McMorris 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16416332/)). Guanidinoacetic acid at 3 g/day raised brain creatine more than monohydrate in one pilot ([Ostojić 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27560540/)).

## The kidney question

The concern traces to a handful of case reports, not trials. Against them: 20 g/day for 5 days had no effect on creatinine clearance, GFR or glomerular membrane permeability ([Poortmans 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9404870/)); up to 10 g/day for 10 months to 5 years, likewise ([Poortmans & Francaux 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10449011/)); 5–10 g/day for **21 months** in American footballers produced no difference in renal markers versus controls ([Kreider 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12701816/)); 12 weeks in type 2 diabetics, no effect on kidney function ([Gualano 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20976468/)). Serum creatinine rises are an artefact of creatine metabolism, not a signal of damage — Kreider's football data showed nearly every athlete looked "renally distressed" on creatinine alone, creatine users and non-users equally.

## Strength of the evidence

**Well supported.** Everything in the dosing and saturation section rests on direct muscle-biopsy and MRS work from the 1990s, replicated repeatedly. The ergogenic claim rests on hundreds of trials plus concordant position stands from the ADA, Dietitians of Canada and ACSM. The renal-safety claim is genuinely well evidenced for the doses athletes use. The equivalence of monohydrate and novel forms is settled.

**Weaker than the confident phrasing suggests.**

- **"Up to 30 g/day for 5 years has no detrimental effects."** True of the populations where those doses were used — children with creatine synthesis deficiencies, and neurodegenerative-disease patients in the [Bender & Klopstock 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26748651/) review (1,687 patients, mean 9.5 g/day, 5,480 patient-years). It is an extrapolation to healthy adults, who have never been studied above ~25 g/day long term. The claim is defensible; the framing conflates two evidence bases.
- **Injury reduction.** "Athletes who take creatine experience a lower incidence of injuries" rests almost entirely on non-randomised observation within American college football programmes, largely from this author group ([Greenwood 2003a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12701814/), [Greenwood 2003b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14608430/), [Kreider 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12701816/)). Users self-select. Treat this as "creatine doesn't cause cramping or injury", which is what the data support, rather than "creatine prevents injury".
- **Neuroprotection.** The concussion and spinal-cord section is rats and mice almost throughout. The document ends by warning sport organisations that restricting creatine may expose them to "legal liability" over head injury — that is advocacy resting on rodent infarct volumes, and it is the least defensible passage in the stand.
- **Pregnancy.** Explicitly hypothesis-generating; animal data plus human observational changes in maternal creatine. Presented as promising, which is fair, but there is no human trial.
- **Women.** The stand concedes most work is in men and that "some studies suggest women may not see as much gain". It does not resolve this.
- **Ergogenic tables.** Tables 1 and 2 — which sports benefit, which mechanisms — are "adapted from Kreider and Jung" and from a book by three of the authors, not derived from a systematic synthesis.

**Conflicts of interest.** Set out in full in the section below. In short: the manuscript was prepared at the request of, and with support from, the Council for Responsible Nutrition, and several authors declare ties to companies that sell creatine. None of that makes the creatine conclusions wrong — creatine is the best-evidenced supplement in sport — but the safety language ("label advisories are likely unnecessary") is the kind of regulatory position an industry-adjacent panel has an interest in taking, and a reader should notice.

**What the stand does not address at all.** Searching the full text: no mention of **non-responders**, no mention of **DHT, hair loss or androgens**, no mention of **caffeine interaction**, and no discussion of **cycling on and off**. These are the four questions lifters actually ask. The 2007 edition at least discussed cycling protocols; 2017 dropped them. Popular evidence-based coverage (see `Jeff Nippard videos/creatine.md`) fills those gaps from primary literature the ISSN never cites.

## What changed since the 2007 stand

The 2007 edition ([PMID 17908288](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17908288/)) was nine numbered points and a literature review aimed at debunking myths. The 2017 edition is a much larger document with a different centre of gravity. Concretely:

**Doses that moved.**

| | 2007 | 2017 |
|---|---|---|
| Loading duration | "at least **3 days**" at 0.3 g/kg/day | "**5–7 days**" at ~0.3 g/kg/day |
| No-load protocol | **2–3 g/day** over 3–4 weeks | **3–5 g/day** over 3–4 weeks |
| Maintenance | 3–5 g/day | 3–5 g/day, **5–10 g/day for larger athletes** |
| Stated safe ceiling | "no scientific evidence of detrimental effects" (no number) | **up to 30 g/day for 5 years**, and 0.3–0.8 g/kg/day in clinical use |

The loading window lengthened and the low-dose route was raised — both movements toward more creatine, not less. The 2007 text was explicit that **2–3 days of loading may suffice** if taken with protein or carbohydrate; 2017 drops that shortcut.

**Claims dropped.** Cycling is gone. 2007 described cycling protocols — loading doses for 3–5 days every 3–4 weeks — as "effective in increasing and maintaining muscle creatine content"; 2017 does not mention them. The whole "Ethics of Creatine" section, and the DSHEA/FDA regulatory point that was position statement #8 in 2007, are gone. So is the 2007 claim that **adding β-alanine to creatine may produce greater effects than creatine alone** on strength, lean mass and body fat — a claim that rested on two studies from within the same author network and which the ISSN's own [2015 beta-alanine stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26175657/) later declined to endorse.

**Claims strengthened.** 2007 said supplementation in young athletes "is acceptable" if seven conditions are met, including parental approval. 2017 keeps four conditions, drops the parental-approval requirement, and adds an entirely new assertion: that under-18 label warnings are "likely unnecessary" legal cover. That is a real strengthening on the thinnest part of the evidence base. 2007's cautious "possibly beneficial in regard to preventing injury" hardened into "athletes who take creatine experience a lower incidence of injuries" — on essentially the same studies.

**New evidence that shifted the position.** Three areas barely present in 2007 carry most of the 2017 document's new weight: the **ageing/sarcopenia meta-analyses** ([Devries & Phillips 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24576864/), [Candow 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24190049/)) and the bone-density work ([Chilibeck 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25386713/)); the **cognition** literature; and the large negative clinical trials in Parkinson's and ALS ([Bender & Klopstock 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26748651/)) — which the 2017 stand reports honestly as showing no clinical benefit, while using their enormous patient-year total as safety evidence. 2007 could only say medical uses were "promising"; 2017 can say the therapeutic trials mostly failed but proved the compound harmless at high dose for years. That is the single most important change between editions, and the stand handles it well.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** The funding line is the one that matters: **"Support to prepare this manuscript was provided by the Council for Responsible Nutrition"** — the US dietary-supplement industry's trade association. The competing-interests section states the same relationship from the other side: R.B.K. (Kreider) "prepared this position stand update at the request of the Council for Responsible Nutrition and ISSN." Kreider also declares that he is a co-founder of the ISSN, has received externally funded industry grants to research creatine, serves as a scientific and legal consultant, and is a university-approved scientific advisor for Nutrabolt. D.S.K. (Kalman) is an ISSN co-founder working for the contract research organisation QPS, which "has received research grants from companies who sell creatine," and sits on an advisory board (Post Holdings) to Dymatize, "that sells creatine." H.L.L. (Lopez) has research grants from companies who sell creatine and co-founded Supplement Safety Solutions LLC, consulting for "companies who sell creatine"; he is also a co-inventor on multiple supplement patent applications. A.L.A. (Almada) is CEO of Vitargo Global Sciences Inc. R.W. (Wildman) is Chief Science Officer for Post Active Nutrition. T.N.Z. (Ziegenfuss) receives royalties on sports nutrition products and is a co-inventor on supplement patent applications. J.A. (Antonio) is CEO and co-founder of the ISSN. The remaining authors declare no competing interests.

**Does it bear on the conclusions?** Yes, and it is the most structurally significant declaration in this set — not because any single tie is unusual for the field, but because of the commissioning chain. A supplement-industry trade association requested the document and paid to prepare it; the author who wrote it advises a supplement manufacturer; three co-authors are tied to companies that specifically sell creatine. None of that touches the biopsy and saturation data, which is decades old and independently replicated, or the ergogenic effect, which rests on hundreds of trials and concordant stands from the ADA, Dietitians of Canada and ACSM. It does bear on the parts of the stand that read as policy rather than physiology: that label advisories are "likely unnecessary", that restricting creatine may expose sport organisations to legal liability, and the confident framing of long-term safety at doses never studied in healthy adults. Those are regulatory positions, and they are the positions a trade association would want a scientific document to arrive at.
