# Energy Drinks and Energy Shots (2023): It's the Caffeine, and the Dose Has a Threshold

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2023 Dec · [PMID 36862943](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36862943/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2023.2171314)
**Authors:** Jagim AR, Harty PS, Tinsley GM, Kerksick CM, Gonzalez AM, Kreider RB, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 (<https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>)
**Topic:** Supplements

Revision of the [2013 stand](energy-drinks-2013.md).

## The position, in one paragraph

Energy drinks (ED, 12–16 fl oz) and energy shots (ES, 2–5 fl oz) work because of **caffeine**, and secondarily **carbohydrate**. Ten years on from the first stand, the additive value of every other ingredient — taurine, ginseng, guarana, carnitine, tyrosine, theanine, the B vitamins — "remains to be determined," largely because manufacturers do not disclose the doses. The effect has a threshold: **≥3 mg/kg bodyweight of caffeine** is where lower-body power reliably improves; below that, results go mixed-to-null. Aerobic performance and cognition improve fairly consistently; anaerobic capacity mostly does not. The safety language is materially more cautious than 2013: the stand no longer calls these drinks safe, and says the long-term consequences of habitual use "are currently unknown, and therefore such habits are currently not recommended."

## Bottom line

- **Caffeine is in 100% of the 75 bestselling products**, at **174 ± 81 mg per serving** (ED **159 ± 74 mg**; ES **217 ± 87 mg**) ([Jagim 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35057494/)).
- **Aim ≥3 mg/kg** if you want the performance effect. **1 mg/kg does nothing**; 3 mg/kg raised half-squat and bench press peak power ([Del Coso 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22569090/)). Nearly every null power study used **<2 mg/kg**.
- **Timing: 10–60 min before.** The stand does not narrow this further.
- **Lower-body power: 9 of 13 studies positive.** Jump height and jump power improve in trained athletes at 3 mg/kg.
- **Maximal strength in multi-joint lifts: no.** 1RM bench and 1RM leg press were unaffected even at **4.1 mg/kg** ([Eckerson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23880655/), [Harty 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32831109/)).
- **Anaerobic capacity (Wingate): largely null**, across four independent trials.
- **Endurance: mostly positive but mixed.** ~**4.7%** faster cycling TT ([Ivy 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19403954/)); ~**2.8%** in a longer TT ([Quinlivan 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25710190/)); several well-run null trials.
- **Cognition, reaction time, mood, alertness: the most consistent benefits in the document.**
- **Sugar: 45.3% of products, 19.9 ± 18 g per serving.** A typical ED is a **~14% carbohydrate solution**; **6–8%** is what's recommended during exercise, so **dilute it** if you're using it as a sports drink. **76%** contain an artificial sweetener, judged safe at beverage doses.
- **Blood pressure goes up.** Meta-analysis: **+4.44 mmHg systolic** (95% CI 2.71–6.17) and **+2.73 mmHg diastolic** (1.52–3.95) acutely ([Shah 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27340146/)).
- **Not recommended** for children 2–12, pregnancy/breastfeeding, or caffeine-sensitive people. Adolescents 12–18: caution and parental guidance.

## What's actually in the can

| Ingredient | Prevalence | Typical amount | Ergogenic at that amount? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Caffeine | **100%** | **174 ± 81 mg** | Yes, if ≥3 mg/kg BW |
| Vitamin B6 | 72.0% | 367% DV (~6.2 mg) | No |
| Sodium | 70.7% | 120 ± 118 mg | No (there for palatability/shelf life) |
| Niacin / B12 | 66.7% each | 121% DV / 5,245% DV | No |
| Sugars | 45.3% | 19.9 ± 18 g | Contributes to peri-exercise carbs |
| Taurine | 37.3% | **not disclosed** | Unknown — meta-analysis supports 1–6 g/day ([Waldron 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29546641/)), but few products state a dose |
| Ginseng | 30.7% | **not disclosed** | Mostly no |
| Guarana | 25.3% | **not disclosed** | Only as a caffeine source |
| Tyrosine / L-theanine | 22.7% / 17.3% | **not disclosed** | Plausible with caffeine; doses unknown |
| Carnitine | 16.0% | **not disclosed** | No |

The recurring word in that table is *not disclosed*. The stand's own view is that "companies who sell EDs or ES … should fully disclose the ingredients and amounts" — the single most useful practical point in the document. You cannot evaluate a proprietary blend, and neither can the authors.

## The dose threshold, which is the real finding

Across 13 studies on lower-body power, nine were positive and four were null. The nulls used **~1.2 mg/kg** ([Kammerer 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25389379/)), **~2.4–2.5 mg/kg** (Campbell et al.) and **~3.12 mg/kg** ([Jacobson 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28723815/)). The positives clustered at **3 mg/kg** and came overwhelmingly from one research group: countermovement jump in male semi-pro footballers ([Del Coso 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22348079/)), female footballers ([Lara 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24615239/)), sprint swimmers ([Lara 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26279580/)), rugby sevens ([Del Coso 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23462927/)), male volleyball ([Del Coso 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24664858/)), elite female volleyball ([Pérez-López 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25051390/)), adolescent basketball ([Abián-Vicén 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24599611/)) and badminton ([Abian 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25530454/)).

The stand's own summary — "at least 3 mg/kg BW caffeine … consistent with the current consensus in caffeine research" — is another way of saying the drink is a caffeine delivery vehicle.

## Where they do and don't work

| Outcome | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Lower-body power / jump | **Yes at ≥3 mg/kg**, mainly in athletes |
| Handgrip MVC | Mostly yes, some nulls |
| 1RM bench / 1RM leg press | **No**, even at 4.1 mg/kg |
| Muscular endurance (reps to fatigue) | Mixed — positive ([Forbes 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18046053/), [Duncan 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22124354/)); null ([Eckerson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23880655/), [Magrini 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27990227/), [Harty 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32831109/)) |
| Wingate anaerobic capacity | **Largely null** |
| Repeated sprint | **Null or negative** ([Astorino 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21461905/), [Jeffries 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29065052/)) |
| Cycling / running time trial | Mostly positive, several nulls |
| Team-sport distance at high speed | Yes (Del Coso group, 3 mg/kg) |
| Running economy / substrate use | **No effect** ([Peveler 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27386963/)) |
| Memory, attention, vigilance | Yes, fairly consistent |
| Reaction time | Mostly yes; **smallest dose gave the largest gain** ([Howard 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21186930/)) |
| Rating of perceived exertion | Inconsistent |
| **Aiming stability (marksmanship)** | **Impaired.** Avoid before shooting sports ([Monaghan et al., unpublished](#references)) |

## Cognition and mood

The most reliable thing an energy drink does is make you feel more alert and react slightly faster. Working memory improved in elite esports players despite null results on most other measures ([Thomas 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31443435/)); attention, vigilance and memory improved after a **157 mg** shot ([Wesnes 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23587521/)). One detail worth holding onto: working and episodic memory improved with a **sugar-containing** Red Bull but not the identical **sugar-free** version ([Wesnes 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28168925/)) — the carbohydrate is doing part of the cognitive work. Effect sizes are small: a 300 mg ED shifted psychomotor vigilance reaction time from **482.4 ms to 473.8 ms** ([Antonio 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33467362/)) — statistically real, and about 9 milliseconds.

## Ongoing use, body composition and safety

Only **three studies** have followed ED use beyond a single day.

- **7 days**, two cans daily: systolic BP **+8% (day 1), +10% (day 7)**, diastolic **+7%/+8%**, heart rate **+8%/+11%**, no ECG changes ([Steinke 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19299320/)).
- **28 days**, low-calorie ED vs diet soda: small favourable shift in fat mass, no differences in clinical safety markers ([Roberts 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18950510/)).
- **10 weeks**, sedentary men: **ED alone changed nothing.** Only ED **plus** an exercise programme beat placebo-plus-exercise ([Lockwood 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19816213/)).

On cardiovascular safety the 2023 stand is far better resourced than 2013 and reaches a more restrained conclusion. Four doses in a day raised 24-hour systolic (**123.2 vs 117.4 mmHg**), diastolic (**73.6 vs 68.2**) and mean arterial pressure (**90.1 vs 84.8**) versus caffeine-matched placebo ([Franks 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22298600/)). Most importantly, against a **caffeine-matched** control an ED raised corrected QT interval and kept systolic BP higher at six hours (**+4.72 vs +0.83 mmHg**, p = 0.01) ([Fletcher 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28446495/)) — the one clean signal that something beyond the caffeine is acting, and it is a cardiovascular signal, not a performance one. Prolonged QTc is an established cardiac risk factor ([Karjalainen 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9227299/)). On mental health, a prospective cohort found men who became ED users had increases in depression, anxiety and stress scores of **6.09**, **3.76** and **3.22** ([Kaur 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32845046/)); the stand fairly notes reverse causality is plausible.

## What changed since the 2013 stand

| | 2013 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Position points | 9 | 13 |
| Dose guidance | none | **>3 mg/kg BW caffeine** stated three times |
| Anaerobic claim | "improve … anaerobic performance" | same wording kept, plus a new bullet limiting it to **lower-body power** |
| Safety verdict | "consumption of ED and ES are **safe** in healthy populations" | **no safety claim at all**; long-term effects "currently unknown, and therefore such habits are currently not recommended" |
| Children | "with parental approval" | **not recommended for ages 2–12**; also not for pregnancy, trying to conceive, breastfeeding, or caffeine-sensitive people |
| Adolescents | folded in with children | separate point: caution, parental guidance, **not >400 mg** |
| Cardiometabolic disease | "should **avoid** … unless approved by their physician" | "should **exercise caution and consult** with their physician" |
| Overconsumption | "especially if **more than one serving** per day" | "especially if **multiple servings** per day … or with other caffeinated beverages" |
| Glycaemic warning | addressed to **athletes** | addressed to **individuals** generally, plus a new note that less active people should weigh regular high-GI intake |
| Method | Medline search | scoping review; capsule/powder pre-workouts and thermogenics **explicitly excluded** |
| Ingredient data | tables of label names | quantified prevalence and dose from a 75-product survey ([Jagim 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35057494/)) |
| New sections | — | sex differences and menstrual-cycle effects; artificial sweeteners; sport-specific performance; reaction time as its own topic |

Three things to notice. First, dropping the affirmative safety claim is the most substantive edit in the revision. Second, the tightening was applied to children and adolescents while guidance for adults with diabetes and cardiovascular disease was **loosened**, from "avoid unless approved" to "exercise caution and consult" — and the 2023 evidence on blood pressure and QTc is stronger than 2013's, not weaker. Third, the overconsumption threshold moved from "more than one serving" to "multiple servings," a quieter loosening in the same direction.

## Strength of the evidence

**Better than 2013, and still not strong.**

On solid ground: the caffeine dose-response, inherited from a large independent caffeine literature and its own [ISSN caffeine stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33388079/); the acute blood-pressure rise, which has a meta-analysis behind it; the ingredient prevalence data, which is a straightforward product survey.

Not on solid ground: nearly everything specific to *energy drinks as products* rather than to caffeine. The positive power and team-sport results are dominated by one Spanish research group testing largely one formulation; the stand names their studies eleven times. The three chronic-use studies total 15, 60 and 38 participants. Sex differences get a section built almost entirely on caffeine and menstrual-cycle research, not on energy drinks at all. And the one head-to-head that matters most — ED versus a caffeine-matched control — found **no performance difference** ([Eckerson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23880655/)) while finding a **cardiovascular difference** ([Fletcher 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28446495/)).

Where the position statement outruns its own review:

1. **"Consuming ED and ES can improve endurance, repeat sprint performance, and sport-specific tasks."** The stand's own anaerobic section reports repeated-sprint results as null ([Astorino 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21461905/), Campbell et al.) and cites a paper titled *"Energy drink doses of caffeine and taurine have a null or negative effect on sprint performance"* ([Jeffries 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29065052/)). Asserting improved repeat sprint performance in the position list is not supported by the section that reviewed it.
2. **"Anaerobic performance"** is retained in position point 4 despite the anaerobic-capacity section concluding "largely null findings reported."
3. **Point 8 on weight control** rests on the same three chronic studies, one of which found ED alone did nothing.

Two errors worth flagging in the document itself:

- **[Quinlivan 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25710190/) is listed in both the positive-findings and the null-findings sentences** for endurance, three paragraphs apart. (The likely truth: positive versus placebo, null versus caffeine — but the stand does not say so.)
- The **[Roberts 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18950510/)** control-group fat mass is printed as **19.1 → 18.4 kg**, i.e. a **0.7 kg loss**, larger than the ED group's 0.6 kg — while the text claims the ED group lost statistically more. The 2013 stand printed the same control group as **18.1 → 18.4 kg**, a gain. One of the two is a transcription error, and the 2023 version as printed contradicts the claim it is used to support.

**On funding and conflicts — the document contradicts itself.** It carries a **"Disclosure statement: No potential conflict of interest was reported by the author(s)"** and a **"Funding Statement: The author(s) reported there is no funding associated with the work."** Immediately below sits a separate **"Conflict of interest"** section reporting, among others:

- **R.J. (Jäger) "owns shares of a Nootropic/Energy Drink company, Update, and is co-founder and co-owner of Ingenious Ingredients (ING2), which has commercial interest in the caffeine metabolite paraxanthine,"** and holds patent applications on paraxanthine.
- **J.A. (Antonio) "is the CEO of the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN); the ISSN receives support and sponsorship at times from companies that sell caffeine-based ergogenic aids or dietary supplements."** The society adopting the position is funded in part by the industry the position covers.
- **R.B.K. (Kreider)**: sponsored research "including caffeinated energy drinks and pre-workout supplements," honoraria for presenting on energy drinks, paid expert in energy-drink cases, consulting on caffeinated product development. **S.M.A. (Arent)**: patents on an ingredient in a performance coffee product, paid coffee-company consultant. **A.R.J. (Jagim, lead author), C.M.K. (Kerksick), A.S.R. (Smith-Ryan)**: consulting and external funding from dietary-ingredient companies. **J.R.S. (Stout)**, **B.C. (Campbell)**: industry grants, consulting, honoraria.
- Three of the thirteen authors — **Tinsley, Gonzalez and VanDusseldorp** — appear nowhere in the conflict-of-interest text at all.

A blanket "no potential conflict of interest was reported" printed alongside declared equity, patents and society sponsorship in this exact category is the most important thing to know about the document. It does not make the caffeine dose-response wrong — that is corroborated elsewhere. It does mean weighing the judgement calls independently: what got softened for adults with cardiovascular disease, what got asserted about repeat sprint performance, and the decision to leave "energy drinks work" as the headline when the review keeps arriving at "the caffeine works."

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares — twice, and inconsistently.** The disclosure statement reads in full: **"No potential conflict of interest was reported by the author(s)."** The funding statement: "The author(s) reported there is no funding associated with the work featured in this article." A separate **Conflict of interest** section then declares that R.J. (Jäger) "owns shares of a Nootropic/Energy Drink company, Update, and is co-founder and co-owner of Ingenious Ingredients (ING2), which has commercial interest in the caffeine metabolite paraxanthine," and is an inventor on numerous paraxanthine patent applications. R.B.K. (Kreider) reports sponsored research "including caffeinated energy drinks and pre-workout supplements," honoraria for presenting research on energy drinks, paid expert work in energy-drink cases, and consulting on caffeinated product development. S.M.A. (Arent) "holds patents for an ingredient used in a performance coffee product" and has been a paid consultant for a coffee company. A.R.J. (Jagim, lead author), C.M.K. (Kerksick) and A.S.R. (Smith-Ryan) consult for and receive external funding from dietary-ingredient companies. J.A. (Antonio) is CEO of the ISSN, which "receives support and sponsorship at times from companies that sell caffeine-based ergogenic aids or dietary supplements." J.R.S. and B.C. report industry grants, consulting and honoraria. P.S.H. declares none. Three authors — Tinsley, Gonzalez and VanDusseldorp — appear nowhere in the conflict-of-interest text.

**The contradiction, side by side.** "No potential conflict of interest was reported by the author(s)" and "R.J. owns shares of a Nootropic/Energy Drink company" are printed in the same article, a few lines apart. Both stand. The second is the one to read.

**Does it bear on the conclusions?** Yes — this is the clearest case in the ISSN set. Equity in an energy-drink company, commercial interest in and patent applications on a caffeine metabolite, and a society sponsored by sellers of caffeine-based ergogenic aids, in a position stand on caffeinated energy drinks. It does not touch the caffeine dose-response, which is corroborated by an independent literature. It does bear on the judgement calls: keeping "improve repeat sprint performance" in the position list when the review section found null results, and leaving "energy drinks work" as the headline conclusion when the evidence keeps arriving at "the caffeine works."
