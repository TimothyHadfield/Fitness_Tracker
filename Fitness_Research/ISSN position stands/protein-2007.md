# Protein and Exercise (2007): The Original 1.4–2.0 g/kg Stand

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2007 Sep 26 · [PMID 17908291](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17908291/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-4-8)
**Authors:** Campbell B, Kreider RB, Ziegenfuss T, La Bounty P, Roberts M, Burke D, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 2.0 — <http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0>
**Topic:** Protein & macronutrients

> Superseded by the [2017 revision](protein-2017.md). Read this one to understand what the society believed before the 2010s wave of dose-response and timing research, and what it quietly stopped saying.

## The position, in one paragraph

Exercising people need **1.4–2.0 g of protein per kg of bodyweight per day** — roughly **0.64–0.91 g/lb** — which is well above the RDA of 0.8 g/kg. Where you sit in that range depends on your training mode: endurance at the bottom, intermittent team sports in the middle, strength/power at the top. That intake is safe; the standard warnings about kidneys and bone loss do not apply to healthy exercising people. Get it from whole food where you can, and when you supplement, use a product containing **both whey and casein**. Eat protein in the window around your training session. Take BCAAs before, during and after exercise, aiming for at least **45 mg/kg/day of leucine** in a 2:1:1 ratio with isoleucine and valine.

## Bottom line

- **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day (0.64–0.91 g/lb)** for anyone exercising. This is the headline number and it survived intact into the 2017 revision.
- **Endurance: 1.0–1.6 g/kg/day**, scaling with intensity, duration and training status ([Lemon 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11023001/), [Tarnopolsky 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15212749/), [Meredith 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2745350/)).
- **Intermittent sports (soccer, basketball, MMA): 1.4–1.7 g/kg/day** — extrapolated from a single soccer review ([Lemon 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8072059/)).
- **Strength/power: 1.6–2.0 g/kg/day**, highest early in training and during sharp volume increases ([Tarnopolsky 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1474076/), [Lemon 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1400008/)).
- **Active elderly need the same 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** and it is safe for them. The stand makes this a formal position, not an aside.
- Nitrogen balance — the method every one of these numbers rests on — **underestimates requirements**, because it measures nitrogen equilibrium, not performance or tissue accretion ([Rand 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12499330/)).
- **No evidence of renal harm** in healthy exercisers at these intakes; the fear traces to animal models and patients who already had kidney disease ([Martin 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16174292/)).
- **No evidence of bone harm.** The urinary calcium rise from high protein appears to be intestinal in origin, not bone resorption ([Kerstetter 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15546911/)).
- Supplement with **whey + casein together** — the only source recommendation the stand makes.
- **≥45 mg/kg/day leucine, ≥22.5 mg/kg/day each of isoleucine and valine.** For an 80 kg lifter that is **3.6 g leucine and 1.8 g each of isoleucine and valine** daily.

## Why 0.8 g/kg is the wrong target for a lifter

The RDA exists to cover 97.5% of healthy adults for nitrogen equilibrium ([IOM 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12449285/)). It was never built to cover the **1–5% of exercise energy cost** that comes from amino acid oxidation, nor to supply substrate for new tissue or repair of exercise-induced damage. The stand cites eight separate lines of work showing exercising people run higher requirements than 0.8 g/kg regardless of mode or training status ([Forslund 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10329992/), [Phillips 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8307870/), [Lamont 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2203721/), [Friedman 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2722324/), among others).

One honest hedge appears and then disappears from the later literature: **protein requirements may actually fall as training continues**, because trained muscle retains nitrogen more efficiently ([Rennie & Tipton 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10940342/)). The 2017 revision does not repeat this.

| Population | 2007 target | For an 80 kg / 176 lb lifter |
|---|---|---|
| Sedentary (RDA) | 0.8 g/kg/day | 64 g/day |
| Endurance | 1.0–1.6 g/kg/day | 80–128 g/day |
| Intermittent/team sport | 1.4–1.7 g/kg/day | 112–136 g/day |
| Strength/power | 1.6–2.0 g/kg/day | 128–160 g/day |
| Active elderly | 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day | 112–160 g/day |

## The safety case, as it stood in 2007

This is the weakest-evidenced section of the document, and it is worth seeing why, because the 2017 revision fixes it properly.

**Kidneys.** The concern originates with the hyperfiltration hypothesis ([Brenner 1982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7050706/)), whose supporting data came from animals and from patients with existing renal disease. Against it the stand offers: a prospective cohort finding no renal decline in women with normal kidneys ([Knight 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12639078/)); and observations that vegetarians, who eat less protein, show the same age-related decline in renal function as omnivores ([Blum 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2912408/)). That is one cohort study and one small cross-sectional comparison — not a controlled trial in sight.

The stand does add a caveat that later disappears: **people with mild renal insufficiency should monitor protein intake closely**, because epidemiological data link intake to progression of existing disease ([Lentine & Wrone 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15073493/)).

**Bone.** The acid-load-leaches-calcium story rests on early studies with small samples, methodological errors and purified protein doses ([Ginty 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15018487/)). Stable isotope work indicates the extra urinary calcium is dietary in origin, absorbed from the gut, not stripped from bone ([Kerstetter 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15546911/)). Older adults — the group most at risk of osteoporosis — may need **more** than 0.8 g/kg/day to optimise bone mass ([Dawson-Hughes 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15001604/)).

## Protein quality and the whey/casein recommendation

Quality is judged by PDCAAS, which scores amino acid composition against a reference pattern and corrects for digestibility ([Darragh & Hodgkinson 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10867062/)). Whey isolate, casein, egg white and soy isolate all score 1.00; lentils score 0.52 and wheat gluten 0.25.

The whey-vs-casein evidence available in 2007 was genuinely equivocal, and the stand says so. Whey produces a sharp fast rise in plasma amino acids; casein produces a moderate rise sustained over **7 hours** ([Boirie 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9405716/)). On outcomes: one study found net muscle protein balance **similar** between the two ([Tipton 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15570142/)); one favoured whey ([Dangin 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12665610/)); two favoured casein ([Boirie 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9405716/), [Dangin 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11158939/)).

The society's resolution of that tie is to **recommend both together**. It is a reasonable hedge on genuinely mixed data — and it is the recommendation the 2017 revision abandons.

## Timing and the immune claim

The timing position — "consume high quality protein within the time period encompassing exercise (before, during, and after)" — leans on a small set of studies ([Willoughby 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16988909/), [Cribb 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277594/), [Esmarck 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11507179/)) plus the mechanistic finding that elevated blood amino acids after resistance training drive synthesis ([Biolo 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9252488/)). Esmarck's study is in **elderly** subjects; that is not made prominent.

The most striking claim in the whole document is the immune one. In 387 US Marine recruits over 54 days, a post-exercise supplement containing 10 g protein produced **33% fewer total medical visits, 28% fewer infection visits, 37% fewer orthopaedic visits and 83% fewer heat-exhaustion visits** than placebo, plus less muscle soreness ([Flakoll 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14657039/)). The supporting mechanism offered is whey's cysteine content driving glutathione production — demonstrated **in mice** ([Bounous 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2743633/)). An 83% reduction in heat exhaustion from 10 g of protein is an extraordinary claim from a single trial, and the 2017 revision drops it entirely.

## BCAAs — the section that vanished

2007 gives BCAAs an entire section and an explicit dosing recommendation. The reasoning:

- BCAAs make up about **one third of skeletal muscle protein** ([Mero 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10418071/)) and leucine is the strongest single stimulator of synthesis ([Kimball & Jefferson 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16365087/)).
- Adding free leucine to carbohydrate + protein after resistance exercise increased synthesis over the same carbohydrate + protein alone ([Koopman 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15562251/)).
- The central fatigue argument: BCAAs compete with tryptophan for brain transport, so more plasma BCAA means less serotonin and less perceived fatigue ([Blomstrand 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16424144/), [Newsholme 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1360309/)). BCAAs during a marathon improved times for **slower** runners (3:05–3:30) but not faster ones ([Blomstrand 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1748109/)).

The stand's own honesty is worth quoting in spirit: most of the leucine-and-synthesis work was done in **animal models**, and one human trial found leucine before and during anaerobic running and strength work did nothing for performance ([Pitkänen 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12836063/)). Its fallback — "at the very minimum, it seems apparent that supplementation with BCAAs does not impair performance" — is an unusually low bar for a formal recommendation.

It also notes, correctly, that whole proteins and quality protein powders are already **~25% BCAA**, which rather undercuts the case for buying them separately.

## Strength of the evidence

**Reasonably solid.** The 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day range itself. It is corroborated by eight-plus independent nitrogen balance and tracer studies across endurance and strength modes, and the direction of the error in nitrogen balance (underestimation) means the true requirement is more likely above the range than below it. This number has held up for nearly two decades.

**Thin.** The mode-specific tiers. The intermittent-sport band of 1.4–1.7 g/kg/day comes from a **single review of soccer** ([Lemon 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8072059/)) and is then extended by assertion to "other types of intermittent sports". There is no evidence a basketball player and an MMA fighter share a protein requirement.

**Thin.** The safety case. Correct in its conclusion but built on observational data and analogy rather than controlled trials — a gap the 2017 revision closes with four RCTs.

**Very thin.** The Marines immune result. One study, one population, an extreme effect size, never replicated, and supported mechanistically only by mouse data.

**Overreach.** The BCAA dosing recommendation. Specific numbers (≥45 mg/kg/day leucine in 2:1:1) are given on the basis of one narrative review's suggestion, with the human performance evidence explicitly described as discrepant and the strongest supporting work explicitly described as animal. The document then concedes whole food already supplies 25% BCAA. The recommendation should not have been made in this form, and by 2017 the society had stopped making it.

**Disclosure.** The paper declares: *"The author(s) declare that they have no competing interests."* What that blanket line is worth on a document recommending whey/casein blends and BCAA products is discussed under *Disclosures and funding* below.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** One sentence: "The author(s) declare that they have no competing interests." No funding statement appears in the record. There are no per-author entries and no companies named — standard for BMC-era papers of this vintage, and thin by any modern measure.

**Is any declared tie material?** None is declared, so there is nothing to assess, and that absence is the finding. This is a document that recommends buying supplemental whey and casein and, in its BCAA section, specific amino-acid products; it is published by a society that is substantially industry-funded; and several of its authors declare extensive supplement-industry relationships on later ISSN stands in this same library. A blanket no-competing-interests line on that document is a weak disclosure. It is not evidence of misconduct and it was normal practice in 2007. It simply means the record cannot tell you the thing you would want to know, and absence of a declaration should not be read as absence of ties.

**Read it against the conclusions.** The core claim — that active people need more protein than 0.8 g/kg — is robust, independently replicated and untouched by any of this. The product-shaped parts are where the missing disclosure matters: the whey/casein blend recommendation, and the BCAA advice that quietly disappeared from later editions.
