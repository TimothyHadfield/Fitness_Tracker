# Energy Drinks and Energy Shots (2013): Caffeine and Sugar in an Expensive Can

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2013 Jan 3 · [PMID 23281794](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23281794/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-10-1)
**Authors:** Campbell B, Wilborn C, La Bounty P, Taylor L, Nelson MT, Greenwood M, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 2.0 (<http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0>)
**Topic:** Supplements

> Superseded by the [2023 revision](energy-drinks-2023.md). Kept for the record and because the two documents differ in ways worth seeing.

## The position, in one paragraph

Energy drinks contain a long list of ingredients, but only two of them do anything you can measure: **caffeine** and **carbohydrate**. Everything else — taurine, glucuronolactone, ginseng, guarana, carnitine, the B-vitamin dusting — is either present below an effective dose, or has no established effective dose. Taken **10–60 minutes before exercise**, an energy drink can improve alertness, reaction time, resistance-training volume and endurance performance. The stand judges them safe for healthy adults, tells diabetics and people with cardiovascular disease to stay away, and asks for parental approval before minors use them.

## Bottom line

- The two active ingredients are **caffeine** and **carbohydrate**. The rest are in the can for marketing, at doses "generally much lower than the typical concentrations associated with an ergogenic effect."
- Most of the studies used only **~2 mg/kg caffeine** — below the **3–6 mg/kg** the ISSN's own caffeine stand says is required ([Goldstein 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20205813/)). The stand treats this as evidence of ingredient synergy. It is at least as easily read as evidence of underpowered trials.
- Resistance training: **+6% bench reps across 3 sets** at 2 mg/kg ([Forbes 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18046053/)); **+12% leg press volume** with no bench effect (Campbell et al. 2010, *Med Sci Sports Exerc*); **+11.9% reps** across 4 sets at 80% 1RM ([Gonzalez 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24149870/)).
- Dose matters and the threshold is visible: **1 mg/kg did nothing; 3 mg/kg raised peak power ~7%** in half-squat and bench press ([Del Coso 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22569090/)).
- Wingate power and agility: **no effect**, repeatedly ([Forbes 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18046053/), [Hoffman 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19126213/), [Astorino 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21461905/)).
- Endurance: **4.7% faster** cycling time trial at 2.3 mg/kg ([Ivy 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19403954/)); **8–14% longer** cycling time at 80 mg caffeine ([Alford 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11665810/)).
- Carbohydrate concentration is wrong for use as a sports drink. ED run **~11–12%**; the recommendation for exercise is **6–8%**. Above 10% delays gastric emptying. **Dilute it or alternate with water.**
- Caffeine per can is **~75–200 mg** (range across products **100–286 mg**). **400 mg/day** is the ceiling for healthy adults ([Nawrot 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519715/)); children under **3 mg/kg/day** ([Higdon 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507475/)).
- Longest safety study the authors could find: **10 weeks**. That is the whole long-term evidence base.

## What actually improves, and by how much

| Outcome | Verdict | Best evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Lifting volume (reps to failure) | Improves | +6% to +12%, 2 mg/kg, 45–60 min pre |
| Maximal power (squat/bench) | Improves at ≥3 mg/kg only | +7% ([Del Coso 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22569090/)) |
| Wingate peak/mean power | No effect | 3 independent trials |
| Repeated sprint / agility | No effect | max difference 1.15%, ns ([Astorino 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21461905/)) |
| Cycling time trial | Improves | 4.7% ([Ivy 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19403954/)) |
| Run time-to-exhaustion | Mixed | +12.5% ([Walsh 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20398312/)); null ([Candow 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19528841/)) |
| Reaction time / alertness | Improves | consistent across trials |

The timing conclusion the stand draws for endurance — that **10–40 min** before works and **60 min** may be too long — rests entirely on the fact that the single null aerobic study ([Candow 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19528841/)) happened to dose at 60 minutes. That is one study reinterpreted as a timing rule, not a timing experiment.

## Mood, focus and reaction time

This is the most consistent finding in the document. Reaction time, concentration and subjective alertness improve across trials ([Alford 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11665810/), [Hoffman 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19126213/), [Scholey 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15549275/)). Two details cut against the "more is better" reading:

- In a dose-comparison, **the smallest dose (1.8 mL/kg) produced the largest gains** in reaction time, with benefit diminishing as dose rose ([Howard 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21186930/)).
- When the non-caffeine ingredients were tested separately, they added nothing to the metabolic response ([Pettitt 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23037611/)).

## Fat loss

Weak, and the stand mostly says so. **28 days** of a calorie-free ED cut fat mass from **18.9 to 18.3 kg** — statistically significant, about **600 g** ([Roberts 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18950510/)). A 10-week trial reported **−6.6% vs −0.35% fat mass** with pre-workout ED ([Stout 2008](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-5-S1-P8)). The thermogenic literature the stand leans on used **200–500 mg caffeine**, well above what a normal energy drink delivers, and mostly tested "thermogenic" products rather than energy drinks ([Higgins 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21037046/)). A full-sugar can will out-calorie any of this.

## Safety, as the document itself reports it

- **+3.8 mmHg** mean arterial pressure, **+13.7%** platelet aggregation, and reduced endothelial function one hour after 250 mL of a sugar-free ED in 50 young adults ([Worthley 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20103032/)).
- Heart rate **+7.8%** on day 1 and **+11.0%** on day 7; blood pressure **+7%** acutely on day 1 ([Steinke 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19299320/)).
- Caffeine side effects reported from **250–300 mg**. Lethal dose typically **>5 g** (~42 cups of coffee). Canada caps ED at **180 mg**; the US FDA's 0.02% soft-drink limit is not enforced for energy drinks.
- Avoid entirely: known cardiovascular disease. Avoid the high-calorie versions: diabetes, metabolic syndrome.

## Strength of the evidence

**Weak, and the conclusions run ahead of it.**

The performance literature here is a set of small single-site crossovers, most with 12–17 participants, most testing one commercial product at a caffeine dose below the established ergogenic threshold. Where a dose-response was actually run, the effect appeared only at 3 mg/kg and vanished at 1 mg/kg — which is the simplest available explanation for the whole body of mixed results, and undercuts the "synergistic blend" framing the stand offers instead.

Three specific places where the document is more permissive than its own evidence:

1. **Conclusion 3** lists "anaerobic performance" among the things energy drinks improve. The stand's own anaerobic section concludes ED "has no effect on repeated high intensity sprint exercise, or on agility performance." The only anaerobic gains found were lifting volume.
2. **Conclusion 5** (low-calorie ED "may promote a small amount of additional fat loss") rests on one 28-day trial showing a 600 g difference and one **conference abstract** ([Stout 2008](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-5-S1-P8)) on a named commercial product — an abstract co-authored by JR Stout, who is also an author of this position stand.
3. **"Consumption of ED and ES are safe in healthy populations."** The stand reaches this after citing acute platelet, endothelial, heart-rate and blood-pressure changes, noting that only "a few studies (acute or long term)" exist, and that the longest available was 10 weeks. Judging a product safe on 10 weeks of data is a judgement call presented as a finding.

**On funding.** The stand's competing-interests section is unusually candid and directly on-point. JA (Jose Antonio) "is a Sports Science Advisor to VPX/Redline in Weston FL" — VPX is an energy drink manufacturer, and this is a position stand on energy drinks. RBK (Kreider, who prepared the source presentation and assembled the final draft) "currently serves as a scientific advisor for Woodbolt International," a supplement company. TNZ (Ziegenfuss) is a scientific advisor to Biotest Laboratories and receives industry funding through a contract research organisation; HLL (Lopez) is at the same organisation and advises Nordic Naturals; DSK (Kalman) works for a CRO funded by supplement and pharmaceutical industries; JRS (Stout) advises Abbott Nutrition; BC, CW and LT all report industry funding and speaking honoraria. Three of the twelve authors declare nothing. Read the "safe in healthy populations" conclusion with that in view — not because it is necessarily wrong, but because it is a judgement, made on thin data, by a group with declared commercial relationships to the category.

## Disclosures and funding

**No structured disclosure record for this article came back from PubMed Central.** That is common for the older BMC-era papers in this corpus, where such statements sit in the article body rather than in a tagged section, and an absence in the record should never be read as an absence of ties. Here the body does carry a Competing interests section — it is the source of the account in the section above — and there is **no funding statement of any kind** anywhere in the paper.

**What the body declares.** J.A. (Antonio) "is a Sports Science Advisor to VPX/Redline in Weston FL." T.N.Z. (Ziegenfuss) "currently serves as a scientific advisor to Biotest Laboratories" and takes supplement-industry research funding through a contract research organisation; H.L.L. (Lopez) is "scientific and medical advisor to Nordic Naturals, Inc." and is funded through the same organisation. J.R.S. (Stout) "serves as a science advisor for Abbott Nutrition." R.B.K. (Kreider) "currently serves as a scientific advisor for Woodbolt International" and has served as a legal expert. B.C. (Campbell, lead author), C.W. (Wilborn), L.T. (Taylor) and M.G. (Greenwood) report academic and industry supplement funding and speaking honoraria; P.L.B. (La Bounty) is paid for contributing to sports nutrition books; D.S.K. (Kalman) works for a contract research organisation funded by the pharmaceutical and nutritional industries. M.T.N., S.S. and R.C. declare none.

**Does it bear on the conclusions?** Yes. An advisory role with an energy-drink manufacturer, held by an author of a position stand on energy drinks, is directly in-category — and it sits on a document whose weakest conclusion, that energy drinks "are safe in healthy populations," is a judgement call made on a literature whose longest study ran ten weeks. The other declared ties are supplement-industry-wide rather than specific to this product category.
