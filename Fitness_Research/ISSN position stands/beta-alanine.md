# Beta-Alanine: the ISSN's 2015 Stand

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2015 · [PMID 26175657](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26175657/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-015-0090-y)
**Authors:** Trexler ET, Smith-Ryan AE, Stout JR, Hoffman JR, Wilborn CD, Sale C, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 (<https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>)
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

Beta-alanine is not itself ergogenic. It is the rate-limiting precursor to **carnosine**, an intramuscular proton buffer, and taking **4–6 g/day in split doses for at least 2–4 weeks** raises muscle carnosine by roughly **40–60%**. What that buys you is performance in efforts hard enough and long enough to be limited by acidosis — **roughly 1 to 4 minutes**, with the effect fading below 60 s (where acidosis isn't limiting) and above ~25 min (where it isn't either). The median performance gain across the literature is about **2.85%**. For a lifter specifically, the stand is unusually blunt: beta-alanine increases **training volume** and reduces perceived fatigue, but "current research does not indicate an additive benefit on strength gains during resistance training." It is a conditioning supplement that happens to live in your pre-workout.

## Bottom line

- Dose: **4–6 g/day**, split into servings of **2 g or less** — ideally **6 g in 4 doses** if using a non-sustained-release form ([Stellingwerff 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22358258/)).
- Minimum **2 weeks** to get a **20–30%** carnosine rise; **4 weeks** for **40–60%** ([Baguet 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19131472/), [Stellingwerff 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21847611/)).
- Longer-run figures: **+64% after 4 weeks** ([Harris 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16554972/)); **+80% after 10 weeks** ([Hill 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16868650/)).
- **Responders vary a lot.** At 4.8 g/day for 5–6 weeks, high responders gained **55%**, low responders **15%** ([Baguet 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19131472/)) — driven partly by baseline carnosine and fibre type.
- **Take it with a meal** — meal co-ingestion increases carnosine loading ([Stegen 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439427/)).
- Median performance improvement across the meta-analysis was **2.85%**, from an average cumulative intake of **179 g** ([Hobson 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22270875/)).
- Best in tasks lasting **60–240 s**; **no effect under 60 s** ([Hobson 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22270875/), [Sweeney 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19935102/), [Derave 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17690198/)).
- **Washout takes 6–15 weeks** ([Baguet 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19131472/)) — long enough that a missed week doesn't matter, and long enough that cycling makes little sense.
- Only side effect is **paraesthesia**, above **~800 mg** in a non-sustained-release dose; gone within **60–90 min** ([Harris 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16554972/), [Stellingwerff 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22358258/)).
- **No safety data beyond 1 year exists.** The stand says so.

## Dosing, in practice

| | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Daily total | **4–6 g** (6 g preferred for loading, especially non-sustained-release) |
| Per serving | **≤2 g** — larger boluses cause paraesthesia, are excreted faster, and have not improved performance |
| Split | **4 × 1.5 g** for a 6 g total |
| Timing | With a meal; time of day irrelevant — this is a saturation supplement, not an acute one |
| Duration to effect | **2 weeks** minimum, **4 weeks** for the full loading effect |
| Maintenance dose | **Unknown.** The stand explicitly declines to give one |

That last row matters. The authors state plainly that "the maximal concentration or retention of carnosine in human muscle is not well known; thus, we cannot yet provide information on the optimal loading or maintenance doses." No upper limit on muscle carnosine has been identified.

**Baseline carnosine** ranges **10–40 mmol/kg dry weight**, typically **20–30**. It is higher in men than women ([Mannion 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1735411/)), higher in fast-twitch fibres, higher in sprinters and resistance-trained bodybuilders ([Tallon 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16287364/)), lower in vegetarians and probably lower with age. Supplementation raises it regardless of where you start.

## What it actually does

Carnosine has a **pKa of 6.83**, which makes it a better proton acceptor across the physiological pH range than bicarbonate (6.3) or inorganic phosphate (7.2). Its buffering role was shown in 1953 ([Severin 1953](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13095299/)).

How much of muscle buffering it accounts for is genuinely unsettled, and the stand is honest about the spread: early animal estimates put it at **40%** ([Davey 1960](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13814256/)); more recent human work puts it as low as **7%** ([Mannion 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1735411/)). That is a six-fold uncertainty at the heart of the mechanism. Secondary roles — free-radical scavenging, transition-metal chelation, anti-glycation — are real chemistry but almost entirely in vitro.

Note that **oral carnosine does not work**: serum carnosinase degrades it before it reaches muscle. Beta-alanine is the only practical route in humans.

## Performance, by duration

The stand presents "relative effect" (RE) values rather than effect sizes — RE > 100 means beta-alanine beat placebo on open-ended tests, RE < 100 means it beat placebo on fixed-endpoint tests like time trials.

**Under 60 s — no.** No improvement in repeated 5-second power bouts ([Sweeney 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19935102/)); no improvement in 400 m sprint time averaging 51.3 s ([Derave 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17690198/)). Acidosis isn't what limits you there.

**60–240 s — the sweet spot.** Cycling time to exhaustion at 110% of maximal power (≈104 s) improved markedly, RE **115.2** ([Hill 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16868650/)); 13–14% TTE increases replicated by [Sale 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21407127/) and [Danaher 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24832191/). In women running at 90% and 100% of vVO2max (268 s, 132 s), RE **129.3** and **117.0** ([Smith-Ryan 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22797003/)).

**But not consistently.** The same Smith-Ryan study gave RE **81.1** and **87.1** in *men* at the same intensities, and [Jagim 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22476168/) gave RE **95.1** — i.e. placebo won. The stand attributes all three to large placebo-group improvements of 8–15%, which is a fair observation and also a warning about how noisy these tests are.

**4–25 min — modest but possibly meaningful.** RE **103.1** in a graded exercise test after 6 weeks of interval training ([Smith 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19210788/)); RE **102.6** after 28 days ([Stout 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17136505/)). In 2,000 m rowing, **4.3 s faster** than placebo ([Baguet 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20671038/)) and **2.9 s faster** ([Ducker 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23239676/)) — RE of 98.8 and 99.0, small in percentage terms, potentially decisive in a race.

**Over 25 min — no.** Eight weeks at 2–4 g/day did not beat placebo on TTE ([Van Thienen 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19276843/)). In a one-hour cycling time trial, muscle carnosine **doubled** and performance did not improve — in fact both groups got worse ([Chung 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24457999/)). That is the cleanest negative result in the document: the mechanism engaged fully and the outcome did not follow.

## Neuromuscular fatigue — and the age effect

Physical working capacity at fatigue threshold (PWCFT) responds more consistently than anything else here. **+16.9%** in men after 28 days, RE 119.5 ([Stout 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17194255/)); **+14.4%** in women, RE 118.2 ([Stout 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17136505/)); **+5.6%** on a heart-rate-threshold variant ([Smith-Ryan 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24308676/)).

The effect is largest in **older subjects**: RE **123.0** from just **1,200 mg/day** fortified into a nutritional supplement over 12 weeks in 70-year-olds ([McCormack 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23832078/)), and a **37.3%** PWCFT improvement after 90 days in a 73-year-old cohort ([Stout 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18992136/)). Note the dose — a fifth of the athletic dose produced a larger relative effect.

The counterexample is instructive: [Smith 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18989693/) found a **20.4%** improvement in EMG fatigue threshold with beta-alanine — and a **25.5%** improvement with interval training alone. RE 95.9. A large absolute gain that the supplement did not cause.

## For lifters specifically

This is the section most people will want, and it is the weakest for beta-alanine.

- Short studies show **no significant strength improvement**, but do show **increased training volume and lower subjective fatigue** ([Hoffman 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18548362/), [Hoffman 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19083385/)).
- Fatigue was attenuated across 5 sets of 30 maximal knee extensions, while isometric endurance was unaffected ([Derave 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17690198/)); a separate study found isometric endurance *did* improve ([Sale 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22697405/)).
- Eight weeks in wrestlers and footballers: **no improvement** over placebo ([Kern & Robinson 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21659893/)).
- Ten weeks: significant improvements in isokinetic force, whole-body strength, arm-curl reps and body composition — **with no difference between beta-alanine and placebo** ([Kendrick 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18175046/)).
- Creatine and creatine + beta-alanine both improved squat and bench 1RM over placebo; only the combination improved body composition and training volume, but **not significantly more than creatine alone** ([Hoffman 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17136944/)).

The stand's own summary: beta-alanine "may improve indices of training volume and fatigue for resistance exercise, but more long-term studies are needed". Its formal position point is that current research **does not** indicate an additive benefit on strength gains.

## Stacking

**Sodium bicarbonate.** A modest additive effect, mostly demonstrable only via magnitude-based inference rather than conventional statistics: 70% probability of benefit ([Sale 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21407127/)), 64% ([Hobson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23535873/)), 71.8–78.5% ([de Salles Painelli 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23668760/)), and one study with a conventional significant effect using an unusual 7-day 0.5 g/kg/day loading protocol ([Tobias 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23595205/)). Against: [Mero 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24215679/), [Ducker 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23524361/), [Saunders 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24225816/), [Bellinger 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22330016/). Weigh a small benefit against bicarbonate's headache and GI side effects.

**Creatine.** Two studies showed additive effects but **neither included a beta-alanine-only arm** — an abstract by Harris and [Hoffman 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17136944/). Studies that did include such an arm found **no synergy** ([Stout 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17194255/), [Kresta 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25505854/)).

**This directly contradicts the ISSN's own [2007 creatine stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17908288/)**, which asserted that adding beta-alanine to creatine "may produce greater effects than CM alone" on strength, lean mass and body fat — citing those same two studies. Stout and Hoffman are authors of both stands. The 2015 correction is the right call; it is just worth knowing that the earlier claim circulated for eight years.

**Pre-workouts.** Typically contain **2–4 g of beta-alanine as a single bolus**, taken once daily. Acute multi-ingredient benefits cannot be attributed to beta-alanine — carnosine loading takes weeks. Over 4–8 weeks a pre-workout containing 2–4 g/day may contribute; over 8 days it will not ([Outlaw 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25302053/)). The tingle you feel is not the supplement working.

## Strength of the evidence

**Well supported.** Carnosine loading itself — dose, timescale, magnitude, washout, responder variability — rests on direct muscle biopsy and MRS measurements and is not in dispute. So is the paraesthesia dose threshold. The 60–240 s performance window is backed by a meta-analysis and multiple independent groups. The negative findings are reported honestly, including the ones that undercut the mechanism.

**Weaker.**

- **The buffering mechanism is under-quantified.** 7% versus 40% of muscle buffer capacity is an unresolved order-of-magnitude question, and [Chung 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24457999/) showed doubled carnosine with no performance effect. [Hannah 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25539942/) ruled out the leading alternative mechanism (calcium sensitivity).
- **Relative effects are not effect sizes.** The RE metric used throughout has no confidence intervals, no weighting for study size, and is dominated by placebo-group behaviour — as the authors themselves note when explaining away three inconvenient results. It looks quantitative and is closer to descriptive.
- **Safety.** No data beyond one year, in any population. The stand's argument that risk is low because beta-alanine is endogenous is reasonable but is reasoning, not evidence. The taurine-depletion question is dismissed on the grounds that a **~50% fall in circulating taurine in animals** ([Dawson 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12107759/)) "would not be of physiological significance" when extrapolated to humans — an assertion with no human muscle-taurine data behind it.
- **Tactical performance** is promoted to a position point from **two studies by one author group**, which disagree with each other on marksmanship, and which came after an independent expert panel concluded there was insufficient evidence to recommend it for military personnel ([Ko 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24697258/)). [Hoffman 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24716994/) found improved marksmanship; [Hoffman 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25510839/) did not. A third study found no effect on brain carnosine or cognition at all ([Solis 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25875297/)).
- **A units error.** In the sodium-bicarbonate section the stand reports supplement dosages "ranging from **4.8–6.8 g/kg/day** of beta-alanine". That is wrong by roughly a factor of body weight — it should be **g/day**; 4.8 g/kg/day would be over 300 g daily for a 70 kg person. An obvious typo, but it survived peer review in a document whose entire practical value is its dosing numbers.

**Conflicts of interest.** The declaration is set out under *Disclosures and funding* below. Six of these authors (Stout, Hoffman, Wilborn, Kreider, Campbell, Kalman) appear on the ISSN's [2013 HMB stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23374455/), where they disclosed industry grants, supplement-company advisory roles, paid speaking and contract-research employment. Stout and Hoffman are also authors of a substantial share of the primary studies this stand evaluates — including most of the PWCFT and tactical-athlete work, which are the two areas where the stand is most enthusiastic and the evidence is thinnest. That pattern is worth holding in mind, particularly since beta-alanine's commercial home is the pre-workout category.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** No funding statement appears in the record we have. The competing-interests declaration is specific in the place it matters. **JRH** (Hoffman) "has been funded by **Natural Alternatives Inc.** in support of research with beta-alanine" — NAI is the patent holder behind CarnoSyn, the dominant commercial beta-alanine. **TNZ** (Ziegenfuss) has received research support from companies to study beta-alanine and **has co-formulated products containing beta-alanine**. **JRS** (Stout) has received grants to examine the efficacy of BA. **CPE** is a research scientist at Texas A&M University and **Director of Research for Nutrabolt**; **RBK** currently serves as a scientific consultant for Nutrabolt and has served as a legal and scientific consultant. **AESR** has received grants as principal investigator to evaluate the efficacy of dietary supplements. **BC** is compensated by media outlets on sports nutrition topics, has received supplement research funding, and sits on a sports nutrition company's advisory board for product compensation. **DK** works for a contract research organisation running trials for the pharmaceutical nutrition industry. ETT, CDW, CS, RJ, LB and JA declare none.

**Is it material? Yes, moderately.** Three authors have been funded by industry specifically to study beta-alanine, one of them by the ingredient's principal commercial sponsor, and one has co-formulated beta-alanine products. These are ties to the substance under review, not general supplement-industry ties, and beta-alanine's commercial home is the pre-workout category the stand's recommendations feed.

**It lines up with the weakest evidence, not the strongest.** As noted above, Hoffman and Stout authored a large share of the primary studies here, including most of the physical working capacity at fatigue threshold and tactical-athlete work — the two areas where the stand is most enthusiastic and the trials are thinnest. The core case, four to six weeks of loading raising muscle carnosine and buffering capacity, is broad, replicated and independent of these authors; it survives this section untouched. The PWCFT and tactical claims are where a declared sponsor, a self-authored evidence base and an over-confident conclusion coincide.
