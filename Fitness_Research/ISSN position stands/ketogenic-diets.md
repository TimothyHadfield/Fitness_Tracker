# Ketogenic Diets for Athletes: Worse for Endurance, Neutral for Strength, Confounded for Fat Loss

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2024 Dec · [PMID 38934469](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38934469/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2024.2368167)
**Authors:** Leaf A, Rothschild JA, Sharpe TM, Sims ST, Macias CJ, Futch GG, et al
**Licence:** CC BY-NC 4.0 (<https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/>)
**Topic:** Body composition

## The position, in one paragraph

Ketogenic diets do exactly what their advocates claim metabolically — they raise fat oxidation dramatically — and it does not translate into performance. Across 16 controlled endurance trials, one favoured keto, eight showed impairment, and seven were null; every trial in elite athletes showed a performance decrement. The mechanism is not mysterious: fat costs more oxygen per unit of ATP, so movement economy falls, and real competition happens at intensities where carbohydrate supplies most of the energy anyway. Strength training is a different story — ten controlled trials, nine null. Body composition looks favourable for keto, but no trial controlled the diet, keto groups ate ~0.5 g/kg/day more protein, and the standard body-composition tools are distorted by the glycogen-water shifts a ketogenic diet causes. The stand is unusually willing to say all of this out loud.

## Bottom line

- **Ketosis is serum ketones >0.5 mM**, generally reached below **50 g carbohydrate/day**. Normal mixed-diet levels are ≤0.2 mM. Ketoacidosis is **12–15 mM** and is a different phenomenon — the negative feedback loop that prevents it fails only when insulin signalling is absent.
- High protein does **not** necessarily break ketosis in active people: 1.8 mM at **2.2 g/kg/day protein** in elite race walkers ([Burke 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28012184/)) and 1.0 mM at **1.7 g/kg/day** in resistance-trained men ([Wilson 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28399015/)).
- Fat oxidation rises **37% to 500%**, reaching **~1.5 g/min** in keto-adapted athletes against **0.46 g/min** average in 300 mixed-fitness adults ([Venables 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15333616/)). Keto-adaptation is fast — maximal diet-induced fat oxidation within about a week ([Hall 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27385608/)).
- **Of 16 controlled endurance trials: 1 favoured keto, 8 showed impairment, 7 found no difference.** All five elite-athlete trials showed decrements.
- **Movement economy falls consistently.** Fat costs more oxygen per unit of energy ([Krogh 1920](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16742941/)). The impairment appears above **70% VO₂max** but not below 60% ([Shaw 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31033901/)) — i.e. precisely at competition intensity.
- Carbohydrate supplies roughly **80%** of energy at competitive marathon and half-marathon pace ([Hawley 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26553495/)), and blocking fatty acid availability does **not** impair running at half-marathon pace ([Leckey 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26586912/)).
- **Strength is unaffected.** Nine of ten controlled trials found no difference in 1RM, jumps or calisthenics over 4–12 weeks. The exception favoured the higher-carbohydrate control.
- **Body composition favours keto but the trials are uncontrolled.** Body weight **−3.2 kg** and fat mass **−2.3 kg** on average versus control; fat-free mass **−0.9 kg** on average. Statistical significance in only 5–8 of 19 trials.
- Keto groups ate **1.8 vs 1.3 g/kg/day** protein and about **75 kcal/day less**. No trial fed the participants; **17 of 19 relied on food logs**, which athletes under-report by an average of **19%** ([Capling 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29207495/)).

## Why fat oxidation doesn't buy performance

The "keto-adaptation" thesis ([Volek 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25275931/)) is that shifting to fat as fuel spares glycogen and therefore extends endurance. The metabolic half of the claim is correct and large — in a 12-week crossover, keto athletes oxidised **1.6 vs 0.7 g fat/min** and reached maximal fat oxidation at a higher intensity, **86% vs 80% of VO₂max** ([Prins 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36845048/)).

Three things break the chain from there to performance.

**Oxygen cost.** Producing ATP from fat requires more oxygen than from glucose. Every study that measured movement economy found it worse on keto, in both elite ([Burke 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28012184/), [Burke 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32497061/), [Burke 2020b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32697366/), [Whitfield 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33027214/)) and recreational athletes.

**Rate ceiling.** Free fatty acid oxidation supports ATP resynthesis at about **0.40 mol/min**; glycogen breakdown supports **1.0–2.0 mol/min**. Fat is a bigger tank feeding a narrower pipe.

**Enzymatic lock-in.** Pyruvate dehydrogenase activity normally rises with intensity ([Spriet 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11991544/)), but low-carbohydrate high-fat diets suppress that rise ([Peters 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11701428/)) — and crucially the suppression **persists through carbohydrate loading and glycogen restoration** ([Stellingwerff 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16188909/)). You cannot carb up out of it on race morning.

The intensities involved are not hypothetical. Professional cyclists race for over 30 min at or above 90% VO₂max ([Lucía 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11039652/)); recreational marathoners run at ~85% VO₂max ([Gordon 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29200895/)); elite race walkers hold 85–90%. This is where keto costs you.

## The one positive study, examined

[McSwiney 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29108901/) is the only controlled trial reporting a performance benefit, and it is the one that gets cited. Twenty recreational male endurance athletes, 12 weeks, keto (41 g carbohydrate) versus control (400 g). Both improved the 100-km time trial by 1–3%. The keto group additionally improved **6-second sprint peak power by 6%** and **3-minute critical power by 17%**.

The stand takes it apart in one paragraph. Power was expressed **relative to body weight**. The keto group started **10 kg heavier** and lost **seven times more weight** than the control group, all of it fat. In **absolute** terms the keto group's sprint peak and average power *fell* **3%**, and critical-power average output *fell* **4%**. Since relative power tracks lean mass rather than body weight ([Maciejczyk 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25353079/)), expressing it per kilogram of lean mass would show the keto group *worse*. Calories and protein were also unmatched.

## What a resistance-training athlete should take from this

**Strength is fine, in the medium term.** Ten controlled trials, 4–12 weeks, covering bench, squat, leg press, Olympic lifts, jumps and calisthenics. Nine found no difference. The exception was [Vargas-Molina 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32276630/) in 21 trained women over 8 weeks — the control group gained **4.8 kg on bench and 15.6 kg on squat** versus **1.5 kg and 5.6 kg** on keto, a significant difference. That it was the only all-female trial *and* the only one showing a difference is a coincidence the stand flags rather than explains.

**This is surprising, because lifting is glycolytic.** Ten reps at a 10RM load draw **64–71%** of energy from anaerobic glycolysis, rising from 46–54% in the first five reps to **81–85%** in the last five ([Gorostiaga 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20976067/)). Blood lactate hits **7 mM** after five reps at 10RM, **17 mM** taken to failure, and **25 mM** after five such sets ([Gorostiaga 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22808209/)). Sixteen sets of lower-body work deplete quadriceps glycogen **26%** in bodybuilders ([Tesch 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3758035/)) and **33%** in untrained subjects ([Koopman 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16369816/)); even three sets of biceps curls drop local glycogen **24%** ([MacDougall 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10364416/)). A typical session costs **25–40%** ([Murray 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29444266/)).

Four weeks of eucaloric keto at 85% fat cut resting muscle glycogen **46.8%** versus a high-carbohydrate control ([Phinney 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6865776/)). The FASTER study reported normal resting glycogen in athletes after ~20 months on keto ([Volek 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26892521/)), but the stand calls that result "anomalous and unreplicated," notes it has been challenged ([Burke 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32358802/)), and cites a contradicting 12-week trial showing a **14% glycogen decrease** ([Miller 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32985255/)).

So the honest position is: glycogen falls, and measured strength somehow does not, over 4–12 weeks. No trial exceeds 12 weeks in trained strength athletes. The stand recommends monitoring individual response "due to its potential to suboptimize training adaptations in the long-term."

## Body composition, and why the numbers can't be trusted at face value

Across 19 controlled trials the direction is consistent:

| Outcome | Keto vs control (mean) | Range | Trials reaching significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body weight | **−3.2 kg** | −0.3 to −7.8 kg | 8 of 19 |
| Fat mass | **−2.3 kg** | −0.3 to −5.3 kg | 5 |
| Body fat % | **−2.6%** | −0.3 to −4.5% | 5 of 11 reporting |
| Fat-free mass | **−0.9 kg** | −0.2 to −2.3 kg | 5 |

Four things undercut this.

**No dietary control anywhere.** Not one trial fed participants or ran a live-in design. Seventeen of nineteen used food logs, and athletes under-report intake by an average of **19%**, up to 36% ([Capling 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29207495/)). Ketogenic diets are more satiating, especially higher-protein ones ([Johnstone 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18175736/)), so spontaneous under-eating is the obvious alternative explanation for the fat loss.

**Protein was not matched.** Keto groups averaged **1.8 g/kg/day** against **1.3** in controls. Fat-free mass gains are maximised at **≥1.6 g/kg/day** ([Nunes 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35187864/)), and the ISSN's own protein stand recommends **1.4–2.2 g/kg** ([Jäger 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)). Only six control groups reached the recommended range against twelve keto groups. The stand's inference is sharp: the keto groups *still* lost more lean mass **despite** eating more protein, so the higher protein was probably masking a larger effect.

**Energy deficits weren't matched either.** A meta-regression puts the threshold at which resistance training stops adding lean mass at about **500 kcal/day deficit** ([Murphy 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/)).

**The measurement tools are compromised by the intervention itself.** Each gram of liver glycogen carries ~**2.4 g of water** ([Nilsson 1973](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4771101/)) and each gram of muscle glycogen at least **3 g** ([Fernández-Elías 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25911631/)). Depleting glycogen lowers DXA-determined fat-free mass and repletion raises it ([Bone 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28410328/)); a **5% shift in fat-free-mass hydration changes DXA body fat percentage by nearly 3 percentage points** ([Prior 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9262461/)). BIA is worse: a **3% hydration change alters measured body fat by 2%** ([Saunders 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9624647/)).

[Wilson 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28399015/) demonstrates the artefact directly. After 10 weeks, both groups gained similar fat-free mass and the keto group lost significantly more fat. After **one additional week of 3 g/kg carbohydrate**, fat loss was now similar between groups and the keto group showed significantly *greater* fat-free mass gain. Nothing changed but hydration and glycogen.

## Sex differences

The stand gives this a full section, unusually. Women oxidise a higher proportion of energy from fat regardless of intensity or mode, and reach maximal fat oxidation at a **higher relative intensity — 52% vs 45% of VO₂max** ([Venables 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15333616/)). The mechanistic basis is substantial: oestradiol raises maximal activity of fat-oxidation enzymes ([Tarnopolsky 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18317381/)), women carry higher levels of β-oxidation enzymes ([Maher 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20700461/)), and there are documented sex differences in CD36 abundance and mitochondrial ADP sensitivity.

The plausible prediction — that women benefit more from a fat-based fuel strategy — is not what the data show. Metabolic shifts on keto were **more evident in males** up to 80% VO₂max ([Durkalec-Michalski 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30953522/)), and males show larger body-composition benefits ([Koerich 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35757868/)). The stand adds a genuine safety flag: keto's satiating effect can drive low energy availability, which downregulates kisspeptin signalling and the reproductive axis, with consequences for hormonal and bone health ([Mountjoy 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29773536/), [Sims 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/)). Its formal position is that there is **insufficient evidence** to say the diet affects the sexes differently, but a **strong mechanistic basis** for expecting it does.

## Strength of the evidence

**Strongest claim in the document: keto impairs elite endurance performance.** Four trials in elite race walkers, run inside a professional training camp with researcher-controlled diets and training, all showing impaired 10-km time trial performance with a coherent mechanism (reduced movement economy, higher oxygen cost) measured directly. This is as good as applied sports nutrition evidence gets, and the conclusion is negative.

**Weakest: the strength conclusion.** Nine null trials sounds solid until you note the durations (4–12 weeks), sample sizes, and total absence of trials in highly trained strength athletes beyond 12 weeks. Null results in short, small trials are weak evidence of no effect. The stand says this itself and recommends caution rather than confidence.

**Body composition is confounded, and the stand says so.** "No study has used a diet-controlled design, meaning that differences in body composition are likely due to differences in energy and protein intake." That is a stronger self-criticism than most position stands issue about their own headline finding.

**Methodological quality of the underlying trials is modest across the board.** Reliance on food logs, no exclusively-female endurance trials, only two endurance trials longer than 10 weeks despite 11 performance outcomes, and heavy use of time-to-exhaustion protocols the stand notes have "low reliability" ([McLellan 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7742769/), [Faude 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28128633/)) and use submaximal intensities that do not resemble competition.

**The gap the stand leaves open.** Nobody has tested keto in the one setting where it is theoretically most defensible — ultra-distance events below 70% VO₂max where carbohydrate intake is limited by gut tolerance or logistics (open-water swims >10 km, ultramarathons >80 km, multi-day races). The stand says there is currently insufficient evidence to support it there, but flags it as the outstanding research need.

**Where it does not overreach.** Notably, this stand argues *against* a popular commercial diet, quantifies its own uncertainty (counts of significant versus non-significant trials, ranges alongside means), states its inclusion and exclusion criteria and lists every excluded trial with the reason. Its disclosures are set out below, and they run against the direction of its conclusions.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** The Position Paper "was not funded." The disclosure statement opens: **"All authors declare that they have no competing interests concerning this Position Paper."** A paragraph of declared interests follows immediately. D.D. (D'Agostino) "is an inventor on USF patents related to applications of therapeutic ketosis, advisor to Levels Health, and co-owner of Ketone Technologies LLC, which does research, consulting and public speaking events." T.M.S. (Sharpe) and C.J.M. (Macias) "are shareholders in and are on the scientific advisory board of Outplay Inc.," a transdermal delivery company. A.L. (Leaf, lead author) has been a paid supplement-industry consultant and is compensated for writing health and nutrition material. J.A.R. (Rothschild) is on the scientific advisory board of Applied Behavior Systems Ltd. B.I.C., S.M.A., J.R.S., C.M.K., R.B.K. and M.J.O. report the familiar supplement-industry grants, consulting and advisory roles. J.A. is CEO of the ISSN, "which receives grant support from a variety of companies in the sports nutrition category." Two authors add product-specific denials: S.T.S. (Sims) "has no financial conflict of interest with ketogenic diets or ketone-related products," and R.B.K. says the same.

**The blanket line does not survive its own paragraph.** "All authors declare that they have no competing interests concerning this Position Paper" is printed directly above a declaration that an author holds patents on applications of therapeutic ketosis and co-owns a ketone company. Both sentences stand; the second is the accurate one.

**Does it bear on the conclusions?** D'Agostino's ketosis patents and ketone-company ownership are in the product category, so the answer is formally yes — but the direction matters. This stand's central conclusions are negative: keto impairs elite endurance performance, does not improve strength, and the body-composition literature is confounded. A declared commercial interest pointing one way while the conclusions go the other is the least troubling configuration available. Sims's and Kreider's specific carve-outs are the right way to disclose. Outplay, Levels Health and Applied Behavior Systems have no ketogenic product line stated in the declaration.
