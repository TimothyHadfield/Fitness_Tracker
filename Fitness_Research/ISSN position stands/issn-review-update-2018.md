# The ISSN's 2018 Supplement Grading Chart: What Works, What Might, What Doesn't

**Source:** ISSN review update (**not** a position stand — see below), *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2018 Aug 1 · [PMID 30068354](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30068354/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-018-0242-y)
**Authors:** Kerksick CM, Wilborn CD, Roberts MD, Smith-Ryan A, Kleiner SM, Jäger R, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

**This is not a position stand.** Every other document in this folder is a consensus statement on one narrow topic, ending in a numbered list of assertions the Society formally votes to endorse. This one is different: it is the ISSN's periodic **review update** — a survey of the whole field, roughly 34,000 words over 738 references, with no numbered position statement at the end. It is the single broadest document in the corpus, covering supplement regulation, how to read a supplement study, energy needs, all three macronutrients, vitamins and minerals, hydration, and nutrient timing. Its centrepiece — and the reason to keep it — is a **graded catalogue of 44 supplements sorted into three evidence tiers along two separate axes: muscle building and performance enhancement**. Nothing else in this library gives you a single chart of that scope. Treat it as an index rather than an authority: on any compound where a dedicated ISSN position stand exists, the position stand is the better source, and on several compounds the tiers here have since been superseded.

## Bottom line

- The ISSN's advice on how to use its own chart: **recommend only Category I. Category II is experimental — tell the athlete so. Category III the ISSN does not support taking at all.**
- The tiers run on **two axes, not one.** A compound can be Category I for building muscle and Category II for performance. **HMB and EAAs both sit in that split**, and both splits matter.
- **Creatine monohydrate is the only compound in Category I on both axes.** The review calls it "the most effective nutritional supplement available to athletes" — **0.3 g/kg/day for 5–7 days** then **3–5 g/day**, or just 3–5 g/day for 3–4 weeks ([Kreider 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/)).
- Protein: **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** for most exercising people, **1.7–2.2 g/kg/day** in high-volume training; the meta-regression it leans on puts the ceiling at **1.62 g/kg/day** ([Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)). During a deficit, **2.3–3.1 g/kg fat-free mass/day** ([Jäger 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)).
- Per-dose protein: **0.25–0.55 g/kg** or **20–40 g**, containing **700–3,000 mg leucine**, every **3–4 h**.
- Carbohydrate: **5–8 g/kg/day** for moderate training, **8–10 g/kg/day** for high volume; **30–60 g/h** during exercise over 90 min in a **6–8%** electrolyte solution.
- Fat: about **30%** of calories, up to 50% in high-volume training, down to **0.5–1 g/kg/day** when cutting — the review warns that going low hurts testosterone.
- **Nineteen of the 44 entries are Category III for muscle building.** That list includes most of the supplement aisle: arginine, glutamine, chromium, CLA, ZMA, tribulus, boron, D-aspartic acid, fenugreek, ecdysterones, prohormones.
- **Glutamine and arginine are Category III on both axes** — the clearest "don't bother" verdicts in the whole document.
- The review's own quality checklist is worth more than any single verdict in it: was the study done in trained humans, was it randomised and double-blind, was it *replicated by an unaffiliated lab*, and do the marketing claims match what the paper actually reported.

## Doses for the Category I compounds

| Compound | Dose the review gives | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Creatine monohydrate | **0.3 g/kg/day × 5–7 days**, then **3–5 g/day**; or 3–5 g/day from the start for 3–4 weeks | Only side effect reported is weight gain. Up to **30 g/day for 5 years** studied with no detriment. No evidence for cramping or dehydration risk |
| Protein | **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day**; **1.7–2.2** in high-volume training; **2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM/day** in a deficit | 20–40 g per dose, every 3–4 h |
| Essential amino acids | **6–12 g** to stimulate MPS; **≥2 g leucine** | *Muscle building only* — see the tier split below |
| HMB | **1.5–3 g/day**, split into 3 × 1 g; 6 g adds nothing over 3 g | Effect **+0.5–1 kg** lean mass over controls in untrained subjects over 3–6 weeks. *Muscle building only* |
| β-alanine | **4–6 g/day in divided doses for 28 days**; efficacy shown up to 12 g/day | Effects most pronounced in efforts lasting **1–4 min** |
| Caffeine | **3–9 mg/kg, 30–90 min pre-exercise** | Above **9 mg/kg** urinary caffeine can exceed doping thresholds. Habitual users see less benefit |
| Sodium bicarbonate | **0.3 g/kg 60–90 min pre-exercise**, or 5 g twice daily for 5 days | Efforts of **1–3 min**. GI distress is the common complaint — trial it in training |
| Sodium phosphate | **1 g four times daily for 3–6 days** | Aerobic capacity **+5–12%**, anaerobic threshold **+5–10%**. Female response inconsistent ([Buck 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25177171/)) |
| Carbohydrate | **5–8 g/kg/day**; **30–60 g/h** during long exercise | Mouth-rinse effect is real but inconsistent |
| Water / sports drinks | **12–16 fl oz every 10–15 min** in heat | The cheapest ergogenic aid in the document |

## The two tier splits that matter most

**HMB: Category I to build muscle, Category II to perform.** The muscle-building case rests on a coherent body of work — **1.5–3 g/day** adding **0.5–1 kg** of lean mass over controls in untrained subjects ([Gallagher 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11128859/), [Nissen 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941534/)), plus three trials in trained athletes from a single Polish group ([Durkalec-Michalski 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26225130/), [2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26849784/), [2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28708126/)). The performance case is genuinely mixed: [Kreider 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10606212/) found no body-composition or strength effect at 3 or 6 g in trained lifters. The review's own explanation — that HMB works when training intensity is maximised and supplementation runs at least 6 weeks — is a post-hoc rationalisation of heterogeneous results, not a finding.

**EAAs: Category I to build muscle, Category II to perform.** But read the muscle-building entry closely and it undercuts its own tier. The review says "limited evidence exists to demonstrate that supplementation with non-intact sources of EAAs... positively impacts fat-free mass accretion", notes that intact whey outperformed free-form amino acids at a matched EAA dose ([Katsanos 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19083472/)), and concludes by **encouraging the prioritisation of intact protein sources over free-form amino acids**. A Category I placement sits awkwardly on top of that.

**BCAAs are Category II on both axes** — and the reasoning is the cleanest piece of writing in the document. The mechanism is real: leucine drives mTORC1. But when BCAAs alone were tested, myofibrillar protein synthesis rose only **~20–22%**, roughly **half** what a dose of whey delivering the same BCAAs produces ([Jackman 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28638350/)); eight weeks of 9 g/day with resistance training changed nothing ([Spillane 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24620007/)). Mechanism without translation. The review's separate note that BCAAs *do* help recovery from damaging exercise is the more defensible claim.

## Energy, macronutrients and timing, condensed

**Energy.** General fitness: **25–35 kcal/kg/day**. Moderate-to-hard training: **40–70 kcal/kg/day**. Large athletes in heavy phases: **6,000–12,000 kcal/day**. The review is blunt that many athletes chronically undereat and that carbohydrate needs in particular go unmet.

**Nutrient timing** (imported from the ISSN's own stand, [Kerksick 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919842/)):
- Glycogen maximised at **8–12 g/kg/day** carbohydrate; rapid restoration via **~1.2 g/kg/h** of high-GI carbohydrate, or **0.8 g/kg/h carbohydrate + 0.2–0.4 g/kg/h protein**.
- Minimum energy floor of **27–30 kcal/kg** with **1.6–1.8 g/kg/day** protein.
- Pre-exercise meal **4–6 h** out; a light **50 g carbohydrate + 5–10 g protein** snack **30–60 min** before.
- Post-exercise **1 g/kg carbohydrate + 0.5 g/kg protein** within 30 min — but the review is careful to say this only matters when glycogen must be restored fast or daily carbohydrate is inadequate (<6 g/kg/day).
- Carbohydrate load: taper **30–50%** for 2–3 days and add **200–300 g/day** carbohydrate.

**Health supplements**, discussed outside the tier system: a low-dose multivitamin, vitamin D dosed against a measured baseline, glucosamine/chondroitin for joint pain, omega-3 at **3,000–5,000 mg/day**, and probiotics and beta-glucan for upper-respiratory infection risk ([Cox 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18272539/), [Talbott 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24149590/)). The review flags the tension honestly: high-dose antioxidants may blunt training adaptation ([Gleeson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23765353/)).

## Where the tiers have since been contradicted

The corpus contains later ISSN position stands on several compounds this review graded. Two disagree.

- **Essential amino acids.** 2018 placed free-form EAAs at Category II for performance and, in the muscle-building entry, explicitly recommended intact protein over free-form amino acids. The [2023 ISSN EAA position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37800468/) reverses this, asserting that "EAAs stimulate muscle protein synthesis **more than** an isonitrogenous protein isolate" and that anabolic responses to EAA plus exercise are "consistently reported." That is a direct flip on the same question in five years, from largely the same society, and neither document explains the change. The 2023 stand's own evidence is still heavily weighted toward clinical and aging populations rather than athletes.
- **HMB.** 2018 split HMB I/II. The [2025 ISSN HMB position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39699070/) broadly upholds the split — body-composition effects at **38 mg/kg/day** across ages and training states, performance effects in trained athletes still "mixed" — but goes further than 2018 in claiming a positive effect on **aerobic** performance in trained athletes, with mechanisms it concedes are unknown. A modest upgrade on a thin base.
- **Probiotics** got a [full ISSN position stand in 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31864419/) despite never appearing in the 2018 tier table at all — mentioned only in the general-health paragraph. Not a contradiction, but a reminder that the chart's coverage is not the field's coverage.
- Sodium bicarbonate, caffeine and creatine all later received dedicated stands consistent with their Category I placement here.

## Strength of the evidence

- **The tier framework is the most valuable thing here, and it is honestly constructed.** Category III explicitly folds together "doesn't work" and "may be unsafe", which is a real limitation — a reader cannot tell from the table whether tribulus is inert or hazardous. Category II explicitly certifies *no safety signal*, which is a meaningful and often-overlooked distinction.
- **Category I placements are not equally strong.** Creatine, protein and carbohydrate rest on hundreds of trials across many labs. **Sodium phosphate** sits in Category I on roughly a dozen small studies, with the review itself conceding that female athletes may not respond ([Buck 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25177171/)) and that several trials found nothing. **HMB's** Category I rests substantially on three crossover trials from one research group. Treat Category I as a range, not a uniform grade.
- **Self-citation is heavy and structural.** Nine of the sources leaned on for the recommendations are prior ISSN position stands, and the author list overlaps with those stands' author lists. On creatine, protein, HMB, beta-alanine, caffeine and nutrient timing, this document is largely the ISSN grading the ISSN. That is not disqualifying — those stands are separately reviewed — but it means the chart is less independent than it looks.
- **The document's own advice on conflicts of interest is better than its practice.** It devotes a long passage to warning readers about industry-linked authors, unreplicated single-lab results, and journals owned by supplement companies — then places compounds in Category I partly on single-lab evidence. The passage is worth reading on its own merits; apply it to the chart it sits in.
- **The evidence quality checklist is the most portable content in the paper.** Was the study in trained humans or rats? Randomised and double-blind? Statistically significant, or a trend? Do the marketing claims match the paper? **Was it replicated by an unaffiliated lab?** That last question does more work than any tier.
- **One outright reference error.** The phosphatidic acid section attributes an eight-week resistance-training study to "Joy et al. [262]". Reference 262 in the source list is *Nakane & Yoshimura, "Universality and diversity in the signal transduction pathway that regulates seasonal reproduction in vertebrates," Frontiers in Neuroscience* — a paper about seasonal breeding in vertebrates, unrelated to anything in this document. The intended paper is not recoverable from the reference list, so the 2.4 kg lean-mass figure attributed to it should be treated as unverified.
- **The document is eight years old and shows it.** Phosphatidic acid, arachidonic acid and agmatine were live questions in 2018 and have largely gone quiet. Ketogenic diets are covered in two paragraphs here and now have their own 2024 stand. Anything not confirmed by a later position stand should be checked against current literature before acting on it.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** No funding statement beyond "Authors of this paper have not received any financial remuneration for preparing or reviewing this paper." Then: "All authors report the following competing interests." C.M.K. (Kerksick) consults with and receives external funding from companies who sell supplemental protein, and has served as an expert witness. S.M.K. (Kleiner) is a paid industry consultant, takes speaking and lay-writing fees, receives book royalties, and "receives commission and has stock in companies that sell products produced from several ingredients discussed in this paper." R.W. (Wildman) "currently works as the Chief Science Officer for Dymatize Nutrition." R.C. (Collins) "is the attorney for numerous companies in the dietary supplement industry" and is paid for consultancy and lay writing on supplements. C.D.W. (Wilborn) has served as consultant, advisor and spokesperson for nutrition companies. M.D.R. (Roberts) reports academic and industry funding and states he holds no patents, stock or ownership in companies doing business on the topics discussed. R.J., L.M.L., M.B.C., M.G., A.S.R. and R.B.K. report supplement-industry grants, advisory roles or honoraria. J.N.D. and E.G. report none.

**Does any of it bear on the conclusions?** More than for any other document in this set, because of what this document is. It is not a stand on one compound; it is a tier list that sorts the supplement market into "works", "unclear" and "doesn't work or may be unsafe" — a chart with direct commercial value to whoever is placed where. Two declarations meet that directly: an author holding stock and commission in companies selling ingredients the chart grades, and an author serving as Chief Science Officer of a supplement manufacturer. Both are declared, which is the system working. But set it against the point made in the section above: this is the document that devotes a long passage to warning readers about industry-linked authors, single-lab evidence and supplement-owned journals, and then places compounds in Category I partly on single-lab evidence, with two authors commercially exposed to where compounds land. Apply the paper's own checklist to the paper.
