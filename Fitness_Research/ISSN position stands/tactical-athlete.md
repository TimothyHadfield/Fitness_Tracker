# Tactical Athlete Nutrition: Military, Police and Firefighters

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2022 · [PMID 35813846](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35813846/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2022.2086017)
**Authors:** Gonzalez DE, McAllister MJ, Waldman HS, Ferrando AA, Joyce J, Barringer ND, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Populations

## The position, in one paragraph

"Tactical athlete" covers military personnel, police and firefighters — jobs that demand athletic output under conditions nothing like sport. The stand splits into two problems that barely resemble each other. **Military:** sustained energy deficits of **2,500–4,500 kcal/day**, chronic sleep loss, load carriage of **31–44 kg**, and the resulting protein catabolism; the interventions are calories, essential amino acids, caffeine and creatine. **First responders:** the opposite — long sedentary stretches punctuated by maximal exertion, with **75% of firefighters overweight or obese** and heart attacks causing about **45% of on-duty firefighter deaths**; the intervention is basic cardiometabolic health, not performance nutrition. The stand's own summary: almost every recommendation is extrapolated from general athletic populations because occupation-specific research barely exists.

## Bottom line

- Military energy expenditure spans **2,342 kcal/day** (female combat support) to **7,122 kcal/day** (male Marines) ([Hoyt 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1917738/)); special operations selection runs **4,100–6,317 kcal/day** ([Barringer 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29632452/)).
- Sustained operations produce deficits of **~3,000 kcal/day**, ~**3.1% body mass loss**, **~3.6 h sleep over 72 h**, and measurable strength and power loss ([Nindl 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12439088/), [Nindl 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762372/)).
- Army Regulation 40-25 sets protein at **0.8–1.6 g/kg/day**; the stand says use the ISSN's **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day (0.65–0.9 g/lb)** instead ([Jäger 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)).
- Under a **30% energy deficit**, **0.3 g/kg EAA** maintained whole-body protein balance *and* stimulated MPS; **0.1 g/kg (~8 g)** only maintained balance ([Gwin 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32768315/)).
- **Once the deficit reaches ~40%, the anabolic effect of protein disappears** — EAAs get burned as fuel ([Carbone 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30596808/)). No supplement fixes a large enough calorie hole.
- Caffeine: **100–200 mg every 2–4 h**; **200–300 mg** improves marksmanship and reaction time; up to **800 mg/day** in 200 mg doses at 2–3 h intervals for sustained sleep deprivation ([Lieberman 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12424548/), [Kamimori 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25527035/)).
- Creatine **0.3 g/kg/day for 5–7 days, then 0.1 g/kg/day (~3–5 g)**; but **10–20 g/day for 2+ weeks** is what raises *brain* creatine by 5–10% ([Dolan 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30086660/), [Roschel 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33578876/)).
- Beta-alanine **6 g/day for 4 weeks** improved shooting accuracy and engagement speed in 20 soldiers, with **no cognitive change** ([Hoffman 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24716994/)); 30 days improved a 50 m casualty carry ([Hoffman 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25510839/)).
- Omega-3: warfighters with low DHA were **62% more likely** to have died by suicide ([Lewis 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21903029/)); **2 g/day EPA+DHA for 12 weeks** cut suicidal ideation 45% and depression 30% ([Hallahan 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17267927/)).
- Rehydrate at **150% of body mass lost**; firefighters at **0.4–0.8 L per hour of activity**, with electrolytes above 2 h or in heat. **~1 in 2 athletes underestimate sweat loss** in heat ([O'Neal 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32796724/)).

## Load carriage: the stressor our library doesn't cover

PPE alone runs **10–50 kg**: **26 kg** for structural firefighters, **6–20 kg** wildland, **~10 kg** general-duty police against **~40 kg** for SWAT ([Maupin 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29899197/)), and **31–44 kg** for warfighters. Loads have climbed steadily over two decades.

- **Energy cost rises** with load, speed and gradient; PPE adds **6–21%** to the metabolic cost of law enforcement tasks ([DiVencenzo 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25102956/)).
- **Physical ability falls ~1.5% per kg of PPE** in firefighters ([Lesniak 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31714455/)).
- **Gait mechanics change and neuromuscular function drops** ([Fallowfield 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22575491/), [Thomas 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29120978/)).
- **Lighter people are disproportionately penalised** by absolute loads; higher body fat independently reduces load-carriage capacity ([Ricciardi 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17691688/)).
- **Low back injury is the dominant consequence**, and an Army Physical Fitness Test score below **200** predicts it ([George 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22355317/)).

The fitness/injury relationship under load generalises to anyone rucking, carrying or doing manual work — being fitter is protective. The energy figures do not: the stand is explicit that the caloric cost of occupational tasks in PPE is unknown, and names it a research priority.

## Protein under deep energy deficit — the genuinely novel finding

As the energy deficit deepens, whole-body and muscle protein catabolism accelerate. During sustained training or combat the deficit routinely reaches **40–70%** of requirement. Two controlled studies define the dose-response:

| Condition | EAA dose | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 30% deficit, 5 days, post-exercise | **0.10 g/kg** (~8 g) | Whole-body protein balance maintained only |
| 30% deficit, 5 days, post-exercise | **0.30 g/kg** | Balance maintained **plus** MPS stimulated; whole-body synthesis **2× higher** |

([Gwin 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32768315/))

A follow-up gave **35 g protein** in three forms after ~50 min of military task work under the same 30% deficit. **Muscle protein synthesis did not differ between forms**, but whole-body protein balance was greatest with EAA-enriched whey, then whey alone, then a mixed meal ([Gwin 2021b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33413462/)). The proposed mechanism is delivery speed: peak plasma EAA and EAA area-under-curve are the strongest predictors of synthesis rates ([Church 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33276485/)).

Dose landmarks: **3–6 g** EAA is efficacious with exercise, **10–15 g** gives the maximal free-form response, **25–30 g intact protein** gives maximal MPS ([Tipton 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15539275/), [Moore 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25056502/)). The operating recommendation is **10–12 g EAA** per feeding.

**The honest limit:** at roughly a **40% deficit the anabolic response is gone entirely** — the amino acids go to energy ([Carbone 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30596808/), [Margolis 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27054679/)). Protein spares muscle in a moderate deficit; past a threshold it does not, and nothing substitutes for calories.

## Sleep deprivation and caffeine

Chronic sleep restriction is treated here as a nutritional problem because of what it does to intake and metabolism: **leptin down, ghrelin up**, more high-fat and high-carbohydrate eating, worse insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance ([Spiegel 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15583226/)), with downstream links to cardiovascular disease, musculoskeletal injury, substance abuse and PTSD. Caffeine is the countermeasure with the strongest data:

| Purpose | Dose |
|---|---|
| Operational utility, sleep deprivation | **100 mg per 2 h** or **200 mg per 4 h** (Army Reg 40-25) |
| Marksmanship, reaction time | **200–300 mg** |
| Sustained wakefulness, 24 h | **800 mg total**, as 200 mg every 2–3 h |

During Navy SEAL Hell Week, **200 mg** caffeine cut distance from centre of mass by **23%**, misses by **44%**, tightened shot groups **47%** and improved sighting time **23%** versus placebo; 200–300 mg attenuated reaction-time errors by **73–98%** ([Lieberman 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12424548/)). Military personnel already average **212–285 mg/day**.

Two cautions most caffeine guidance skips. **Avoid any dose within ~6 h of sleep**, especially above 200 mg — the thing that makes it useful is the thing that wrecks recovery. And in firefighters specifically, **6 mg/kg raised a coagulation response** during drills (Wassell et al. 2020, *J Caffeine Adenosine Res* — no PMID or DOI in the source reference list), while high caffeine raised core temperature in full PPE ([Kellawan 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19851910/)). Energy drinks are a separate flag: **1 in 6 warfighters** report 24 oz or more per day, associated with mental health problems, aggression and fatigue ([Toblin 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30169675/)).

## Creatine beyond muscle

The muscular case is imported wholesale from the ISSN creatine stand ([Kreider 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/)). What's specific here:

- **Injury reduction:** lower incidence of cramping, strains, heat illness and total injuries in collegiate athletes ([Greenwood 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14608430/)); faster force recovery after eccentric damage ([Cooke 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19490606/)).
- **Heat tolerance:** increased intracellular water with reduced heart rate, rectal temperature and sweat rate exercising in heat ([Kilduff 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15467102/)) — relevant to working in PPE, though the stand notes it has never been tested in firefighters during fire suppression.
- **Cognition and neuroprotection:** benefits during "neural vulnerability" from stress and sleep loss ([Dolan 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30086660/)). But brain creatine rises only **5–10%**, and only at **10–20 g/day for 2+ weeks** ([Roschel 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33578876/)) — four times the maintenance dose people actually take. Different goal, different protocol; don't conflate them.

## First responders: a health problem wearing a performance costume

The stand's most useful move is refusing to treat firefighters and police as athletes needing fuelling strategies.

- **~75% of career and volunteer firefighters are overweight or obese** ([Poston 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21386691/)); **~40.5% of US police officers** may be obese ([Can 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24694574/)); the US Army reports **17.3% obese and 52.9% overweight**.
- **Heart attacks cause ~45% of on-duty firefighter deaths**, and line-of-duty CVD events occurred **exclusively in firefighters with pre-existing, non-job-related disease** ([Soteriades 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21646874/)). Risk of death is **10–100× greater** after active fire suppression than after non-emergency calls ([Kales 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17377158/)). The job doesn't create the disease; it triggers events in people who already have it.
- Police officers are roughly **twice as likely** to have CVD as the general population ([Ramey 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18306648/)), and **~77% of on-duty sudden cardiac deaths** happen during non-routine tasks — the sudden-exertion pattern ([Varvarigou 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25406189/)).
- A firefighting simulation drove core temperature from **37.7 to 39 °C** and heart rate to **153 bpm**; structural firefighting hits **84–100% HRmax** with lactate of **6–13 mmol/L** ([Sothmann 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1506937/), [Gledhill 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1325260/)), and produced a **73% increase in thrombus production** two hours later ([Hunter 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28373523/)). The aerobic standard is **42 mL/kg/min**.

**Dietary approaches tested.** All small, all in firefighters, none in police:
- **Mediterranean diet** — inversely related to weight gain (OR 0.57), raised HDL, lowered LDL ([Yang 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24503596/)); also the pattern firefighters view most favourably ([Yang 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26331100/)).
- **Time-restricted feeding (14:10)** — reduced inflammation, oxidative stress and cortisol response to a fireground test over 6–8 weeks ([McAllister 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33136772/), [McAllister 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33928938/)); no harm to strength, jump height or task time, and ventilatory threshold rose from **65.2% to 70.0% VO2peak** ([Gonzalez 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33769400/)).
- **28-day carbohydrate-restricted diet**, n = 15 — **−2.4 kg fat mass**, systolic **−6 mmHg**, diastolic **−5 mmHg**, 2.41 km run **−3%**, max pull-ups **+15%** ([Waldman 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31469768/)).

The stand's reading of that last result is refreshingly deflationary: it is "likely not the decrease in carbohydrates but rather the increase in protein consumption" that made it work — satiety and calorie reduction, not carbohydrate per se ([Waldman 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32740289/), [Aragon 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28630601/)). It states flatly that **no dietary pattern can be recommended over another**; every effective approach works through energy deficit.

## Hydration, and one tool worth stealing

Baseline: **2.7 L/day** total water for women, **3.7 L/day** for men; keep dehydration under **2%** of body mass. Pre-mission, **5–7 mL/kg** 4 h out; post, **450–675 mL per 0.45 kg (1 lb)** lost, or **150%** of mass lost. Firefighters: **0.4–0.8 L per hour of activity**, with electrolytes past 2 h or in heat. PPE is the specific problem — it blocks evaporative and convective cooling while giving a continuous cue for sweat production, so the wearer dehydrates fast with no cooling return.

**The three-step morning check** (Cheuvront & Sawka 2005, *Sports Sci Exchange* — no PMID or DOI in the source reference list; urine colour chart from [Armstrong 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7987361/)): are you thirsty; what colour is your urine; is morning body mass below normal? One box ticked, possibly hypohydrated; two, likely; three, very likely. It costs nothing and generalises to anyone — **half of athletes underestimate their sweat losses** ([O'Neal 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32796724/)), and tactical populations are advised against using thirst alone.

## What generalises, and what doesn't

**Generalises:** the EAA dose-response under deficit (**0.3 g/kg beats 0.1 g/kg** when calories are short; past ~40% deficit protein stops being anabolic at all) applies directly to aggressive cutting. So does the finding that faster-absorbing forms improved *whole-body* balance while MPS was identical — "MPS unchanged" doesn't mean "nothing changed." Also: sleep restriction's effect on leptin, ghrelin and food choice; the three-step hydration self-check; caffeine timing discipline (nothing within 6 h of bed); and creatine for cognition needing **10–20 g/day**, not 5 g.

**Doesn't generalise:** **800 mg/day caffeine** is a countermeasure for people who cannot sleep, not a training aid — likewise 200–300 mg for marksmanship. The expenditure figures (**4,100–7,122 kcal/day**) describe selection courses and combat. **Deficits of 40–70%** are an operational reality, never a plan. The firefighter cardiometabolic findings describe a population with unusually high baseline disease burden and a sedentary-to-maximal work pattern. Beta-alanine improving marksmanship has no civilian analogue.

## Strength of the evidence

This stand is unusually candid about its own thinness: "most of the available recommendations are derived from both general and athletic populations." Read the recommendations through that lens.

**Reasonably strong.** The EAA-under-deficit work is the best evidence here — controlled crossover designs with real protein kinetics, right population, right deficit ([Gwin 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32768315/), [Gwin 2021b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33413462/)). The caffeine-and-sleep-deprivation literature is genuinely military, controlled, and replicated across marksmanship, vigilance and reaction time. The firefighter cardiovascular epidemiology is large-scale and consistent.

**Thin.** The beta-alanine tactical case rests on **two studies by the same group, one with 20 subjects**, in which the physical outcome improved and cognition explicitly did not — yet the closing recommendations list beta-alanine among supplements to "aid cognitive performance." A direct overreach on the cited data.

Creatine's neuroprotective claim is stated more confidently in the summary than the evidence supports: brain creatine rises only 5–10%, and the TBI work is largely preclinical. Omega-3's TBI evidence comes from **American football players, not military personnel** ([Oliver 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26765633/), [Heileson 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34579748/)), measuring a biomarker (neurofilament light chain) rather than a clinical outcome. The stand acknowledges "minimal human data exists" and then quotes an expert panel saying it would be "unethical not to attempt to increase n-3 status" ([Coulter 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25373106/)) — an appeal to authority doing work evidence should be doing. The suicide association ([Lewis 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21903029/)) is case-control; the intervention trial ([Hallahan 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17267927/)) was in patients with recurrent self-harm, not warfighters. L-tyrosine rests on three small studies, two of them cold-exposure protocols — and the stand's own text undercuts it by noting the inverted-U between prefrontal dopamine and cognition.

**Absent.** No energy expenditure data for occupational tasks in PPE for any first responder group. No supplement use data for first responders at all. No dietary intervention trials in law enforcement — every diet study cited is in firefighters and assumed to transfer. The first responder recommendations amount to "use Mifflin-St Jeor and follow general population guidance": honest, but not a position stand's usual product.

**Conflicts worth naming.** Self-citation density is high. Barringer authored the SOF energy prediction equation cited as the basis for special operations feeding ([Barringer 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29632452/)). McAllister, Waldman and Gonzalez — three of the first ten authors — authored essentially every time-restricted-feeding and carbohydrate-restriction study in the first responder section ([McAllister 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33136772/), [McAllister 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33928938/), [Gonzalez 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33769400/), [Waldman 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31469768/), [Waldman 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32740289/)). Ferrando's group produced the EAA studies. Partly unavoidable in a small field — but for the first responder half, "the current evidence" and "our lab's evidence" are close to the same thing.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** No funding was provided, and the authors state they received no financial remuneration for preparing or reviewing the paper. The competing-interests declaration, carried inside the acknowledgments, names most of the author list — DEG, MJM, HSW, AAF, JJ, NDB, JJD, AJK, TH, TNZ, AZ, JLT, JLH, DSK and BIC — as having no competing interests. Five entries follow. **CMK** (Kerksick) consults with and receives external funding from companies, is paid to present and to write on nutrition and protein, and has served as an expert witness. **JRS** (Stout) has conducted industry-sponsored research on creatine and other nutraceuticals over 25 years and has received financial support for presenting on nutraceuticals at industry-sponsored conferences. **RBK** (Kreider) has conducted industry-sponsored creatine research, received financial support for presenting on creatine, and served as an expert witness in supplementation cases. **TAV** has received supplement-related research grants. **JA** is CEO of the ISSN. The published article also carries a contradictory line of publisher boilerplate — "No potential conflict of interest was reported by the author(s)" — which is plainly wrong given the paragraph above it.

**Is any of it material?** Only mildly, in one place. The stand recommends creatine to tactical populations, and the two authors with the deepest declared creatine-industry relationships are among those who conceived the position stand. But the creatine evidence base is unusually large and substantially independent of them, and this stand's creatine advice is conservative and consistent with the wider literature. Nothing declared here should change what a reader believes.

**The real limitation is undeclared and structural.** As set out above, the first-responder sections rest heavily on studies authored by McAllister, Waldman and Gonzalez, who are among the first ten authors and who declare no competing interests. That is not a conflict of interest, and no disclosure statement is designed to catch it. It is a narrower problem: for that half of the document, "the current evidence" and "our lab's evidence" are close to the same body of work.
