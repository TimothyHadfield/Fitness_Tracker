# Diets and Body Composition (2017): Why the Diet Wars Are Mostly Settled

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2017 · [PMID 28630601](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28630601/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y)
**Authors:** Aragon AA, Schoenfeld BJ, Wildman R, Kleiner S, VanDusseldorp T, Taylor L, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Body composition

## The position, in one paragraph

Fat loss is a function of a sustained calorie deficit; the macro split you use to get there is a matter of adherence and preference, not metabolism. Keto and low-carb do not beat higher-carb diets when calories and **protein** are matched — every apparent advantage they have shown traces back to their higher protein content or their appetite-suppressing effect. Intermittent fasting is equivalent to daily restriction. The three things that genuinely change body composition are **enough protein, resistance training, and an appropriate rate of weight loss**. The metabolic slowdown people fear is real but has mostly been demonstrated in studies that combined aggressive restriction with low protein and no lifting — a design the stand calls "a perfect storm for the slowing of metabolism."

## Bottom line

- **No isocaloric, protein-matched, inpatient comparison has ever shown a fat-loss or thermic advantage to low-carb or ketogenic diets.** Across 32 controlled feeding studies spanning **1–83% carbohydrate and 4–84% fat**, the small edge actually went the other way: **+26 kcal/day** energy expenditure and **+16 g/day** fat loss in the *higher*-carb arms ([Hall & Guo 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28193517/)). The stand states this "invalidates the carbohydrate-insulin hypothesis of obesity."
- **Protein is the variable doing the work.** A **5% or greater** protein difference between diets at 12 months was associated with a **threefold greater effect size for fat loss** ([Clifton 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24472635/)). Matching protein removes the low-carb advantage ([Soenen 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22935440/)).
- **The single best trial in the document:** in a deficit with HIIT plus lifting, **2.4 g/kg/day** produced **+1.2 kg lean mass and −4.8 kg fat**, versus **+0.1 kg and −3.5 kg** at 1.2 g/kg/day. Four-compartment model, all food provided ([Longland 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/)).
- **Rate of loss matters as you get leaner.** **0.7% of bodyweight per week beat 1.4%** for retaining lean mass and strength in athletes ([Garthe 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/)); **0.5–1.0%/week** is the recommended range for contest prep ([Helms 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)).
- **Intermittent fasting is not magic.** The largest systematic review — 40 studies, 12 direct comparisons — found "apparently equivalent outcomes" versus continuous restriction ([Seimon 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26384657/)).
- **Keto's real advantage is appetite.** Eaten *ad libitum* at matched 30% protein, a ketogenic diet produced a spontaneous **−294 kcal/day** ([Johnstone 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18175736/)); a meta-analysis found KD suppresses appetite more than very-low-energy diets ([Gibson 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25402637/)).
- **Keto costs you high-intensity output.** Fat-adaptation impaired 1 km sprint power ([Havemann 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16141377/)), and in elite race walkers it **worsened exercise economy** and produced no performance gain while both high-carb arms improved ([Burke 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28012184/)).
- **Muscle burns far less than you think: 13 kcal/kg/day.** Five kg of new muscle adds roughly **65 kcal/day** ([McClave & Snider 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11224660/)).
- **Adaptive thermogenesis is real but preventable-ish.** A ≥10% weight loss drops TDEE by **20–25%**, of which **10–15%** is beyond what body composition change predicts ([Rosenbaum & Leibel 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20935667/)). Resistance training plus adequate protein circumvented it even on 800 kcal ([Bryner 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10204826/)).
- **Self-report is the biggest confounder in the whole field.** "Diet-resistant" obese subjects under-reported intake by **47% (1053 kcal/day)** and over-reported exercise by **51%** ([Lichtman 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1454084/)).

## The carbohydrate question, closed

The proposed mechanism for carb restriction is that lower insulin releases the brake on lipolysis and raises fat oxidation. The stand takes this apart in three steps.

**Step one — the direct test.** Hall put subjects on 4 weeks of a 300 g-carb diet, then 4 weeks of a 31 g-carb ketogenic diet. Ketones plateaued at ~1.5 mmol/l within two weeks. Energy expenditure rose by ~**100 kcal/day** for a little over a week and then went away. Insulin dropped fast and hard. And **body fat loss actually slowed** during the first half of the keto phase ([Hall 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27385608/)).

**Step two — the pooled test.** Hall & Guo's pair of meta-analyses restricted inclusion to **isocaloric, protein-matched, controlled-feeding studies where all food was provided** — no self-selection, no self-report. Thirty-two studies, the full macronutrient range. No thermic or fat-loss advantage to lower carb; a trivial advantage to higher carb ([Hall & Guo 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28193517/)).

**Step three — explaining the apparent wins.** Where low-carb diets *have* outperformed, protein was higher. Soenen showed systematically that the higher protein content, not the lower carbohydrate content, drove the greater weight loss ([Soenen 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22935440/)). Protein is the most satiating macronutrient; raising it from 15% to 30% of energy *ad libitum* cut spontaneous intake by **441 kcal/day** and produced **−4.9 kg in 12 weeks** ([Weigle 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/)).

The "you didn't allow enough time for ketoadaptation" defence fails on its own terms — the rise in fat oxidation plateaus **within the first week** ([Hall 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27385608/)), and higher fat oxidation is not net fat loss when fat intake and intramuscular storage rise alongside it. And the dedicated low-carb meta-analysis is instructive on effect size: significantly greater fat loss than controls, but only for "very low" carb (~50 g) in subgroup analysis, with **mean differences of 0.57–1.46 kg** in obese subjects ([Hashimoto 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27059106/)). Statistically real; practically small.

## What each archetype is actually good for

| Diet | Definition used | Genuine strength | Real cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VLED / LED** | 400–800 / 800–1200 kcal/day | Fast initial loss, which predicts long-term success ([Nackers 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20443094/)) | ~**25% of weight lost is lean** even at 50 g protein ([Saris 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11707557/)); no long-term edge over LED ([Tsai & Wadden 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16988070/)); hair loss the most common complaint |
| **Low-fat** | 20–35% fat (AMDR) | Largest health-outcome evidence base; Cochrane review of **32 RCTs, ~54,000 subjects** shows modest consistent fat reduction ([Hooper 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26250104/)) | Implies fat is the enemy; very-low-fat versions are barely adhered to |
| **Low-carb** | 50–150 g, or <40% energy | Defaults to higher protein; flexible | The advantage is the protein, not the carb restriction |
| **Ketogenic** | ≤50 g or ≤10% energy | Best appetite suppression of any archetype; simplifies decisions | Impairs high-intensity output; no advantage when protein and calories matched |
| **High-protein** | ≥25% energy, or 1.2–1.6 g/kg+ | The best-supported single intervention in the document | Can suppress appetite so much it fights a bulk; cost |
| **Intermittent fasting** | ADF / whole-day / 16–20 h TRF | Equivalent results, better hunger control, no tracking on ad lib days | No advantage; caution around performance |

Two adherence findings worth more than they look. In the A TO Z trial, the Ornish group was assigned **≤10% fat** and actually ate **21.1% rising to 29.8%** over 12 months ([Gardner 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17341711/)); in POUNDS LOST the 20%-fat target was met at **26–28%** ([de Souza 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22258266/)). Extreme macro prescriptions are not followed, which is itself part of why these trials come out even. POUNDS LOST also carries a lesson for reading "high protein" trials: it found no lean-mass advantage to its high-protein arm — but the arms were **1.1 and 0.7 g/kg**, both below anything a lifter would consider.

## Protein: the numbers that matter

The stand endorses the [2007 ISSN protein range of 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day](protein-2007.md) as having gained further support, then layers three refinements on top.

**In a deficit, more is better, up to a point.** Doubling the RDA (1.6 g/kg) beat the RDA repeatedly for preserving lean mass and reducing fat ([Layman 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16046715/)). *(The source article attributes this to "Lemon et al." while citing two Layman papers — an author mix-up in the published text.)* Tripling it (2.4 g/kg) did **not** beat doubling it for lean mass in one 21-day trial ([Pasiakos 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23739654/)) — but Longland, using a 4-compartment model, full food provision, and hard training, found 2.4 g/kg produced actual **lean mass gain** in a deficit while 1.2 g/kg only produced maintenance ([Longland 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/)). Longland is the better-designed study.

**Express it on lean mass when you are lean.** For resistance-trained athletes in hypocaloric conditions, **2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass** ([Helms 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/)). The stand notes this is one of very few papers to scale protein to FFM rather than total bodyweight — which matters most for people carrying more fat.

**Very high intakes appear inert or mildly favourable.** The Antonio series in trained lifters: **4.4 g/kg/day** for 8 weeks — an extra ~**800 kcal/day** — produced no body-composition change and **no weight gain** ([Antonio 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24834017/)); **3.4 vs 2.3 g/kg** with periodised training gave equal lean gains (1.5 kg each) but **−1.6 kg vs −0.3 kg fat** ([Antonio 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26500462/)); a one-year crossover at 3.3 vs 2.5 g/kg found no difference and no adverse clinical markers ([Antonio 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27807480/)).

The counterpoint the stand includes, to its credit, is the **metabolic ward** study: sedentary subjects overfed at 5%, 15% and 25% protein all gained the **same 3.51 kg of fat**, while lean mass went **−0.7, +2.87 and +3.98 kg** ([Bray 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22215165/)). Under supervision, surplus calories still become fat; protein changes the *partitioning*, and training status is what appears to separate Bray's result from Antonio's.

## Bulking: how big a surplus

Two studies bracket the answer, and training status is the variable.

- **Elite athletes**, +544 kcal/day for 12 weeks with nutritional counselling: **+1.7 kg lean, +1.1 kg fat**, versus ad libitum's **+1.2 kg lean, +0.2 kg fat**. The extra 0.5 kg of muscle cost 0.9 kg of fat ([Garthe 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23679146/)).
- **Untrained subjects**, a massive **+2010 kcal/day** for 8 weeks: **+2.9 kg lean, +0.2 kg fat** ([Rozenek 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12094125/)).

The recommendation follows: **larger surpluses for novices**, who partition well and are primed for rapid gains; **smaller surpluses for advanced trainees**, who are at higher risk of undue fat gain. And the honest caveat — some novices need less, some advanced lifters need more.

You cannot skip the surplus entirely. Ten days at 80% of energy requirements significantly lowered muscle protein synthesis and the associated signalling ([Pasiakos 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20164371/)), so lean gains in a deficit are possible but not optimal.

**Why some people cannot gain weight.** Overfeed non-obese adults by 1000 kcal/day for eight weeks and on average **432 kcal are stored and 531 burned** — nearly two-thirds of the burn (**336 kcal**) attributable to NEAT, which ranged as high as **692 kcal/day** ([Levine 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9880251/)). Between two similar-sized people, NEAT can differ by **2000 kcal/day** ([Levine 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17697152/)). The "hardgainer" is often unconsciously spending the surplus.

## Intermittent fasting and time-restricted eating

The two TRF training studies point in opposite directions, and the reason is protein.

**Tinsley** ran a 20-hour fast / 4-hour feed, 4 days a week, in untrained but active subjects on a standardised lifting programme. TRF ate **667 kcal less** on fasting days. Biceps and rectus femoris cross-sectional area rose similarly in both groups, but lean soft tissue moved **+2.3 kg in controls versus −0.2 kg in TRF** — not significant, but a large effect-size gap ([Tinsley 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27550719/)). Protein was **1.0 g/kg in TRF and 1.4 g/kg in controls** — both sub-optimal, and the confound runs against TRF.

**Moro** ran 16/8 in trained subjects with protein matched at **1.93 vs 1.89 g/kg**. TRF lost significantly more fat (**1.62 vs 0.31 kg**), no lean mass change either way — but with **decreased testosterone and triiodothyronine** ([Moro 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27737674/)).

Whole-day fasting carries the same adherence caveat: Harvie found WDF beat daily restriction for 3-month fat loss (**3.7 vs 2.0 kg**) — while **70% of fasting days were completed** versus the daily group hitting its target only **39%** of the trial ([Harvie 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23591120/)). That is an adherence result dressed as a metabolic one. Verdict: **choose linear or non-linear deficits by preference and tolerance**, not by expected result.

## Metabolic adaptation, in proportion

TEF is **8–15% of TDEE** and differs sharply by macronutrient — protein **20–35%**, carbohydrate **5–15%**, fat lowest ([Halton & Hu 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15466943/); [Jéquier 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12174324/)). BMR is **60–70%** of TDEE, EAT **15–30%**, and NEAT **15%** in sedentary people rising to **50%+** in very active ones ([Levine 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15102614/)).

The most useful debunk here: heart and kidneys each burn **~400 kcal/kg/day**, brain 240, liver 200 — four organs accounting for **70–80% of resting expenditure**. Muscle burns **13 kcal/kg/day**, so **5 kg of hard-won muscle raises RMR by about 65 kcal/day** ([McClave & Snider 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11224660/)). Muscle buys you no dietary licence. It matters on a *net* basis only because there is so much of it — which is why large lean-mass losses genuinely hurt.

Maintaining a ≥10% weight loss drops TDEE by **20–25%**, of which **10–15%** is beyond what the lost tissue predicts, and **85–90% of that excess comes from non-resting expenditure** ([Rosenbaum & Leibel 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20935667/); [Leibel 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7632212/)). The stand's rebuttal is fair: Leibel's subjects were on an **800 kcal liquid diet at 15% protein with no exercise at all**, while Bryner's — same 800 kcal but **40% protein plus three full-body resistance sessions weekly** — actually **increased** RMR ([Bryner 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10204826/)), and Donnelly's gained fibre cross-sectional area on 800 kcal with lifting ([Donnelly 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8379514/)).

## Strength of the evidence

**Very strong.** The rejection of the carbohydrate-insulin hypothesis. Hall & Guo's meta-analyses are restricted to inpatient, isocaloric, protein-matched, fully-provided-food studies — the most rigorous inclusion criteria in this entire document, and the design that eliminates the self-report problem the stand itself identifies as the field's biggest confounder ([Lichtman 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1454084/)). The conclusion has only strengthened since 2017.

**Very strong.** The high-protein-in-a-deficit case. Multiple converging meta-analyses ([Clifton 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24472635/), [Leidy 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926512/)) plus Longland's tightly controlled 4C-model trial. If you take one thing from this stand, take this.

**Strong.** The IF-equals-daily-restriction conclusion, from the largest systematic review available ([Seimon 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26384657/)).

**Solid but narrow.** The keto-impairs-performance finding. Havemann and Burke are well-designed, but Burke is in **elite race walkers** and the strength/power picture is genuinely open — Paoli found no strength decrement in gymnasts ([Paoli 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22835211/)), though protein was skewed **2.9 vs 1.2 g/kg** in keto's favour, and Wilson found similar strength and power gains in a protein- and calorie-matched comparison ([Wilson 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28399015/)). The stand's own conclusion — "effects of carbohydrate restriction on strength and power warrant further research" — is the right one.

**Weak, and presented as stronger than it is.** The super-high-protein (>3 g/kg) recommendation. All four supporting studies come from **one laboratory**, use **self-reported intake**, are unblinded, and produce a result the stand cannot explain — an extra 800 kcal/day producing zero weight gain. The document lists six speculative mechanisms and then adds, correctly, *"there might have been a misreporting of energy intake."* That concession belongs in the conclusions, where instead these findings appear as an "emerging evidence basis." Bray's metabolic-ward study is the better-controlled contradiction and it is under-weighted.

**Self-citation to note.** Two recommendations lean on the authors' own work. The "protein pacing" endorsement (4–6 meals/day, >30% protein per meal) cites **Arciero's** studies ([Arciero 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23703835/), [Arciero 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27187451/)) and Arciero is a co-author of this stand. The 0.5–1.0%/week rate-of-loss recommendation cites **Helms, Aragon & Fitschen** ([Helms 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)) and Aragon is the lead author. Neither claim is unreasonable, but both are the authors citing themselves into a society position.

**Disclosure.** The version of the text captured here contains **no competing-interests statement**. The author list includes scientists with commercial sports-nutrition affiliations. That does not undermine the carbohydrate or protein conclusions — those rest on independent trials by Hall, Longland and others — but it is relevant to the protein-pacing and very-high-protein recommendations specifically.

**The best-calibrated thing in the document** is its own closing metaphor: total daily macronutrition is the cake, timing and distribution are the icing, supplements are the sprinkles. Everything else in this note is an argument for that ordering.
