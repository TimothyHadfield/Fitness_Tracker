# Weight Cutting for Combat Sports: What Fighters Do, What Works, and What Is Reckless

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2025 Dec · [PMID 40059405](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40059405/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2025.2467909)
**Authors:** Ricci AA, Evans C, Stull C, Peacock CA, French DN, Stout JR, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Body composition

## The position, in one paragraph

Combat athletes routinely shed **5–10% of body mass** in the week before a weigh-in and then try to put it back on before they fight. This stand does not tell them to stop. Instead it argues that the practice is happening anyway, mostly unsupervised, and that a staged approach — lose most of the weight slowly as fat during an 8–10 week camp, then take only **2–4% off by dehydration in the final 24 hours** — is safer and performs better than the alternative. It sets a target starting point (**12–15% above the weight class** between fights), ranks the acute methods by how well they work and how controllable they are, and gives a detailed rehydration and refeeding protocol for the window between weigh-in and competition. It explicitly refuses to endorse diuretics, laxatives, enemas, diet pills and induced vomiting. Its central limitation is that almost none of this rests on controlled trials in combat athletes, and much of the "safe" framing is the authors' own clinical judgement.

## Bottom line

- Keep walk-around weight at **12–15% above the weight class**. Above that, the deficit needed to get down in 8 weeks starts driving testosterone below clinical reference range and suppressing resting metabolic rate ([Lebron 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38392975/)).
- Lose **0.5–1 kg/week** during camp. At roughly **0.7% body mass per week**, 1RM, sprint and jump performance are essentially preserved ([Garthe 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/)).
- Floors during the descent: carbohydrate **3–4 g/kg/day**, protein **1.2–2.2 g/kg/day** (**1.6–2.2** for lean mass retention), fat **0.7–1.3 g/kg/day**.
- Real UFC data across 600+ cuts: fighters sat **6.7% over** the class at 72 h and **4.4% over** at 24 h before weigh-in ([Peacock 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36547661/)). That 4.4% is the stand's implicit benchmark for how much dehydration is actually needed.
- **Glycogen depletion** (<50 g/day carbohydrate, or 2–3 g/kg/day to prevent refill) yields **1–2% body mass** because glycogen binds water at **1 g : 2.7 g** ([Bergström 1972](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5068307/)).
- **Low fibre** (>30 g/day down to <10 g/day for 4 days) yields another **~1–2%**; measured losses were **0.4%** at day 4 and **0.74%** at day 5 in healthy men ([Foo 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35313275/)).
- **Water loading** works: 100 ml/kg/day for 3 days then 15 ml/kg/day restriction produced **3.2%** loss vs **2.4%** in controls, with no problematic blood chemistry ([Reale 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29182412/)).
- **Hot water immersion plus wraps** is the single most potent acute method: two rounds of 20 min at 39–39.5 °C plus 40 min wrapped gave **4.5 ± 0.7%** body mass in one session block ([Connor 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33795918/)). Epsom salts added nothing.
- Sweating off **10% of body mass in 24 h is "ill-advised and potentially dangerous."** Cap heat exposure below a core temperature of **40 °C / 104 °F** ([Wasserman, StatPearls](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29083764/)).
- Rehydrate with an oral rehydration solution at **1–1.5 L/h**, sodium **50–90 mmol/dL** if the loss exceeded 3% of body mass, carbohydrate **≤60 g/h** ([Maughan 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9232555/)).
- Post-weigh-in carbohydrate **8–12 g/kg** after a heavy depletion, **4–7 g/kg** after a modest one. Aim to regain **≥10% of body mass** before competing.
- Not endorsed by the ISSN in any form: diuretics, laxatives, enemas, diet pills, induced vomiting, clinical bowel-prep formulas ([Barley 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31117325/), [Holte 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15484356/)).

## The weigh-in window determines everything

How much dehydration is defensible is a function of how long you have to fix it. The stand's own schedule:

| Time from weigh-in to competition | Net sweat loss the stand deems appropriate | Glycogen status going in | Sports |
|---|---|---|---|
| **< 4 h** | **0–3%** from a euhydrated start | Do **not** fully deplete — leaves 1–2% still available | BJJ (IBJJF, ADCC), NCAA and Olympic wrestling |
| **4–12 h** | **2–4%** | Do not fully deplete | Amateur boxing, amateur Muay Thai |
| **12–24 h** | **3–5%** | May be depleted | IJF judo, Olympic boxing, Olympic taekwondo |
| **24–36 h** | **4–6%** | May be depleted | Professional MMA, professional boxing, kickboxing, Muay Thai |

The practical consequence is the most useful thing in the paper: a BJJ competitor or wrestler weighing in 90 minutes before stepping on the mat has **no viable rapid-weight-loss strategy at all**. Rehydration to the tune of 125–150% of fluid lost is simply not achievable in that window. Those athletes have to arrive at the class through body composition, not dehydration.

## What athletes actually do (the survey data)

In 637 combat athletes across judo, BJJ, boxing, MMA and Muay Thai/kickboxing ([Barley 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29283792/)):

- **87%** restricted food (often to <22 kcal/kg)
- **85%** increased training
- **83%** water-loaded
- **79%** restricted fluid
- **51%** used a sauna
- **43%** used sweat suits or plastics
- **12%** reported diuretic use (5% in judo, 12% in taekwondo)

Separately, **72.9%** of 314 MMA athletes water-loaded always or sometimes ([Hillier 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31142173/)); **76%** of Irish MMA athletes used hot baths always or sometimes ([Connor 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31505745/)); **50%** of 580 Brazilian grapplers and strikers used saunas ([Brito 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22349031/)). Pharmacological diuretics are on the WADA prohibited list, both as performance agents and as masking agents ([Cadwallader 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20718736/)).

## Ranking the acute water-loss methods

Sweat rates of up to **2 L/h** are achievable ([Sawka 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16028570/)), which is why sweating is the dominant method. What the evidence supports:

| Method | Measured loss | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Hot water immersion, 39–40 °C, 15–20 min | **1.66–1.71 kg** (~2% BM) per protocol; **4.5%** when paired with 40-min wraps | Most effective; controllable; salt adds nothing ([Connor 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33795918/)) |
| Dry sauna, ~90 °C, 4 × 10 min | **0.65 kg** ([Podstawski 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30800676/)); **0.25–0.82 kg**, i.e. **0.5–0.9% BM** ([Podstawski 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25614882/)) | Effective, but modest per session |
| Wet sauna / steam room | **0.25 kg** vs 0.37 kg dry in women ([Pilch 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24899780/)); **0.36 kg** vs 0.72 kg dry in men ([Pilch 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31599875/)) | **Avoid.** Less sweat, worse thermal strain, evaporative cooling blocked, sweat rate untrackable |
| Mummy wraps | +2.79 kg over immersion alone in the Connor protocol | Never used alone; extends sweating at lower heat stress |
| Infrared / portable sauna / blanket | Not directly studied | Head stays out, so cooling and sweat tracking are easier — a reasonable pick on ergonomics, not evidence |
| "Floating" (respiratory water loss) | ~250–350 ml/day; 0.8→2.7 ml/min as humidity drops | Trivial in magnitude; relevant only for the last few ounces |

Passive methods spare muscle glycogen and avoid the cardiovascular load of exercising while hypohydrated. Active methods (pad work, sauna suits) burn glycogen too, but a depleted, hypohydrated athlete has reduced plasma volume and impaired cardiac output — the stand is explicit that skills training and sparring become both less efficient and less safe in that state, with a real risk of orthostatic intolerance and syncope ([Yanovich 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32293229/)).

## The rehydration and refeed window

This is the part with the clearest, most usable protocol.

**0–2 h post weigh-in.** Gastric emptying is already impaired by intense exercise, by hypohydration above 3% of body mass, and by the food and fluid restriction that preceded the weigh-in. Emptying plateaus around **600 ml** and slows above **1,000 ml** ([Costill 1974](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4436193/)). So: an initial bolus of **300–500 ml**, then **240–350 ml every 30 min**. Total oral rehydration solution **1–1.5 L/h**. Sodium **50–90 mmol/dL** when hypohydration exceeded 3% BM, ordinary sports drink (<30 mmol/dL) below that. Carbohydrate at a **2–3% solution** (15 g/500 ml), total **≤60 g/h**.

**3–6 h.** Fluid must still carry electrolytes. Reintroduce simple starchy carbohydrate — white rice, potato, pasta.

**6 h+.** Prioritise carbohydrate, low-to-moderate protein, avoid fat. Total post-weigh-in carbohydrate **8–12 g/kg** if depletion was aggressive, **4–7 g/kg** if not. Above 60 g/h, mix glucose and fructose to use both gut transporters ([Jeukendrup 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19000102/)).

**Fight day.** Carbohydrate **1–4 g/kg**, scaled to the hours available — a 70 kg fighter with 6 h takes 210–280 g, with 12 h takes 420–560 g. Protein in **20–30 g** servings. **No fat within 3 h** of competition. Reintroduce fibre as little as possible; it is contraindicated post weigh-in.

The stand wants at least a **10% regain in body mass** before the athlete competes, framed as both a performance and a harm-reduction target.

## What the stand refuses to endorse

Named and rejected: fasting, diet pills, enemas, laxatives and bulimia as weight-loss tools. Clinical bowel-prep formulas are rejected specifically because they reduce exercise capacity ([Holte 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15484356/)); a low-fibre diet achieves comparable gut clearance with no performance cost. Steam rooms are to be avoided in favour of controllable modalities. Sweating off 10% of body mass in 24 h is called out as dangerous, though 5–8% is described only as having "varying degrees of impact" and remaining "inconclusive". Sodium should not go below the **2,300 mg/day** RDA even during fight week, on the grounds that combat athletes lose far more sodium in sweat than the general population the RDA was written for.

## Strength of the evidence

Weak, and the stand mostly says so. Rate this document as a codification of expert practice, not a synthesis of trials.

- **What is genuinely well-supported:** the macronutrient floors and the weekly rate of loss, which import from the broader athlete-nutrition literature ([Garthe 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/), [Longland 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/)); gastric emptying and rehydration physiology, which come from decades of exercise-science work outside combat sports; the supplement recommendations, which lean on existing ISSN stands for caffeine, creatine, beta-alanine, sodium bicarbonate and HMB.
- **Single small studies carrying enormous weight:** the water-loading protocol rests essentially on one crossover ([Reale 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29182412/)) plus a 13-wrestler Korean study; the hot-water-immersion protocol on **eight** MMA athletes ([Connor 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33795918/)); the low-fibre protocol on 19 healthy non-athlete men ([Foo 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35313275/)). Mummy wraps, infrared saunas and "floating" have no direct study at all.
- **Pure authorial judgement, labelled as such:** the 12–15% walk-around figure is called "anecdotal" and "not yet fully substantiated". The entire weigh-in-window table is stated to be "based upon anecdotal observations from the authors working with thousands of combat athletes". The heat-acclimation protocol (sauna 3–4×/week, 15–25 min) is a protocol "the authors propose... derived from methods currently adopted by combat athletes."
- **Unpublished proprietary data.** Several load-bearing claims — the 8-week descent example, the Weight Making Preparedness Score, much of the framing — cite a UFC Performance Institute publication (UFC PI, *A Cross-Sectional Performance Analysis and Projection of the UFC Athlete*, vol. 2, 2021 — reference [3], with no PMID or DOI) that is a Zuffa LLC book, not peer-reviewed literature. Two co-authors work in that setting. The stand's disclosure statement, set out below, does not mention this.
- **The conspicuous omission.** For a document about a practice that has killed athletes, this stand never uses the word death, never discusses mortality, never mentions a case of exertional heat stroke or renal failure, and never engages with disordered eating beyond listing "bulimia" once as a method it does not endorse. The safety argument runs entirely through performance decrement and a single core-temperature ceiling. That is a real gap, and a reader should not take the absence of harm data as evidence of absence of harm. The stand's own closing point concedes that "the long-term effects of frequent weight cuts on health and performance are unknown."
- **Two citation errors worth knowing about.** The water-loading section attributes the key study to "Reid et al. [109]" — reference 109 is Mujika & Padilla on tapering, and the study is Reale et al., correctly cited two sentences later as [116]. The HMB paragraph attributes Tartibian and Rezaei to [68], which is a dietary nitrate review; the correct reference is [90]. A third is murkier: the dry-vs-wet sauna comparison is described as being in "young (22–24y) females" citing [186], but the English title of reference 186 says healthy *men*. Individually these are typographic rather than substantive, but together they mean the printed marker cannot be trusted blind.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** No funding: "the author(s) reported that there is no funding associated with the work featured in this article." The disclosure statement names three authors. **CK** (Kerksick) reports no direct conflicts with this article, but has received grants, honoraria and consulting fees from companies doing business in exercise and nutrition. **BIC** (Campbell) has received grants and contracts to research dietary supplements, served as a paid industry consultant and as an expert witness, is a member of the International Protein Board, and receives compensation for speaking, lay writing and educational services. **JA** (Antonio) is CEO of the ISSN, an academic non-profit that receives funding from companies that sell, market and manufacture dietary supplements.

**Does any of it bear on the conclusions?** No. This stand recommends dehydration limits, weight-descent timelines and refeeding volumes — not products. None of the declared ties is to a company selling anything the stand recommends, and the ISSN's supplement funding has no obvious purchase on advice about how much water to lose and how fast to put it back. Nothing declared here is material.

**The independence problem is a different one.** The declaration covers three names on a longer author list, and it does not mention the commercial setting that supplies several of the stand's load-bearing claims. The eight-week descent example, the Weight Making Preparedness Score and much of the framing come from a UFC Performance Institute volume (reference [3]) published by Zuffa LLC — not peer-reviewed, not indexed — and co-authors work in that setting. That is not a declared conflict and it is not misconduct. It is simply the point at which the stand stops citing literature and starts citing an employer's in-house document, and the disclosure statement does not flag it.
