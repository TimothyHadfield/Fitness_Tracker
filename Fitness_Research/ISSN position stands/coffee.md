# Coffee and Sports Performance: Where Coffee Isn't Just Caffeine

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2023 Dec · [PMID 37498180](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37498180/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2023.2237952)
**Authors:** Lowery LM, Anderson DE, Scanlon KF, Stack A, Escalante G, Campbell SC, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

Coffee works as a pre-exercise ergogenic aid at the same caffeine dose as capsules — **3–6 mg/kg, about 60 minutes out, roughly two to four cups** — but it is not a caffeine delivery vehicle and shouldn't be treated as one. Caffeine is only **1–2%** of coffee by mass; the rest is chlorogenic acids, melanoidins, lipids, minerals and hundreds of unidentified compounds, some of which appear to have effects of their own (alertness, blood flow, glucose disposal, mood) and at least one of which may **antagonise** caffeine. The stand's own honest closing point is that the coffee-specific literature is thin, "coffee" is not consistently operationally defined, and sweeping generalisations should be avoided. Its practical advice: if you want a precise, reproducible caffeine dose, use anhydrous caffeine; if you want coffee, dose by measured caffeine content per serving, not by cups.

## Bottom line

- **Dose: two to four cups (473–946 ml / 16–32 oz)** of typical medium-roast hot-brewed or instant Arabica, delivering **3–6 mg/kg (1.4–2.7 mg/lb)** caffeine plus roughly **100–400 mg chlorogenic acids per cup**.
- **Timing: final sip ~60 min before exercise**, allowing a **15–30 min** drinking window. Salivary caffeine peaks at about **42 min** after coffee, faster than capsules ([Liguori 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9329065/)).
- **Coffee is ~1–2% caffeine.** Non-caffeine components represent **over 60%** of the compounds ([Graham 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9729561/)).
- **Caffeine content per serving varies wildly.** Across 20 espressos: **51 mg to 322 mg** per serving, and chlorogenic acids **24 mg to 422 mg** ([Crozier 2012](https://doi.org/10.1039/C1FO10240K)). This variance — not any potency deficit — is why sport dietitians often prefer anhydrous caffeine.
- **Head-to-head at matched dose, coffee equals anhydrous caffeine for endurance.** 5 mg/kg as instant coffee and as capsules both improved cycling time trial by **~5.0%** over decaf and placebo, with no difference between them ([Hodgson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23573201/)).
- **The one landmark study saying otherwise had nine subjects.** At 4.45 mg/kg, only the capsule condition improved run time to exhaustion ([Graham 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9729561/)); plasma caffeine was the same in all conditions.
- **Coffee does not dehydrate you.** Urine output after coffee matched water over four hours ([Maughan 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26702122/)); a meta-analysis found exercise negates caffeine's minor diuresis ([Zhang 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25154702/)).
- **Sprints don't respond.** No effect on Wingate peak or mean power in three separate coffee studies, and no effect on 25 m swim sprint.
- **Decaf is not an inert placebo.** Decaffeinated and high-chlorogenic-acid coffee improved alertness and mood on their own ([Cropley 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21773723/)), which undermines most of the field's control condition.
- **Chronic loading doesn't work.** Two weeks of high-CGA coffee changed nothing on a 50 km time trial ([Nieman 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29035597/)). The benefit is acute.
- **Coffee blocks iron.** Instant coffee's polyphenols reduce non-haem iron absorption, by **60–90%** in some studies ([Hurrell 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10999016/)). Separate from iron-containing meals by at least an hour.

## Where coffee and anhydrous caffeine differ

This is the reason the document exists, so it's worth being precise about which differences the stand actually asserts.

**Differences the stand supports:**

| | Coffee | Anhydrous caffeine |
|---|---|---|
| **Dose precision** | Poor. 51–322 mg per espresso serving; roast, bean, grind and brew all shift it | Exact. This is the whole argument for capsules |
| **Chlorogenic acids** | **100–400 mg per cup**, falling with longer roasting ([Mills 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23993490/)) | None |
| **Absorption speed** | Salivary Tmax ~**42 min** | ~**67 min** for capsules ([Liguori 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9329065/)) |
| **Effects without caffeine** | Real — decaf and high-CGA green coffee improved alertness, reduced fatigue and shifted mood ([Cropley 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21773723/)). Isolated chlorogenic acid alone did **not**, which points to the matrix rather than one compound ([Camfield 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24349389/)) | None |
| **Iron, calcium, vitamin D** | Polyphenols inhibit iron absorption; possible effects on calcium balance and osteoblast vitamin D receptors | Not applicable |
| **Autonomic response** | Neutral-to-beneficial HRV effects in habituated/active consumers; **3 and 6 mg/kg** from coffee left HRV unaffected in caffeine-naive female athletes ([Karayigit 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33374947/)) | Some studies show delayed parasympathetic recovery post-exercise |
| **Fluid balance at high dose** | **6 mg/kg** from coffee produced an acute diuretic effect at rest; **3 mg/kg** was no different from water ([Seal 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28868290/)) | Same caffeine-driven effect, but coffee is mostly water to begin with |

**Where the stand says they don't differ:**

- **Epinephrine response.** 4.45 mg/kg produced similar epinephrine increases whether delivered as capsules, decaf + added caffeine, or regular coffee — and exercise abolished the differences entirely ([Graham 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9729561/)).
- **Plasma caffeine and paraxanthine.** Same across all caffeine-containing conditions in the same study.
- **Endurance time trial performance at matched dose.** No significant difference between coffee and anhydrous caffeine ([Hodgson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23573201/)).
- **Substrate use.** Neither coffee nor anhydrous caffeine changed carbohydrate or fat oxidation during 30 min of steady-state cycling ([Hodgson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23573201/)).
- **The mechanism itself.** The stand says the primary ergogenic route for coffee *is* caffeine's adenosine A1/A2A antagonism. The extras modulate; they don't substitute.
- **Anxiety.** "Anxiety exacerbation is likely due to the caffeine content of coffee."

**The unresolved bit.** Chlorogenic acid derivatives from roasting inhibit the human adenosine transporter *in vitro* ([de Paulis 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12065074/)) — the stand frames this as chlorogenic acids antagonising caffeine's receptor binding, which is a slightly looser reading than the source paper supports. But it then immediately reports that in vivo concentrations are too low to matter ([Hodgson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23573201/)). So the "coffee blunts caffeine" story survives as a hypothesis with a plausible mechanism and no confirmed clinical relevance.

## The glycogen-sparing story, buried properly

The founding coffee-and-exercise study gave nine cyclists caffeinated coffee (330 mg) versus decaf and found cycling time to exhaustion rose from **75.5 to 90.2 min**, with higher free fatty acids and lower RER ([Costill 1978](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/723503/)). That result launched the fat-oxidation/glycogen-sparing hypothesis and has been cited over 1,100 times. The 2023 stand notes flatly that the mechanism "has had very little data to support it" ([Graham 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11583104/)), that pooled data across studies using 5 or 9 mg/kg found **no difference in muscle glycogen use** between caffeine and placebo ([Graham 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19088793/)), and — a detail worth keeping — that Costill's design had **no true control**, only decaf, so it can't separate caffeine effects from coffee-matrix effects at all.

Two other proposed muscular mechanisms are dismissed on dose grounds: calcium release and phosphodiesterase inhibition require caffeine around **1000 µM**, while two to three cups of coffee produce plasma concentrations of only **20–50 µM**, and **200 µM** is already in toxic territory.

## What coffee does and doesn't improve

| Performance window | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Reaction time / alertness** | Improves — and partly without caffeine | **65 mg** caffeinated coffee improved simple reaction time ([Smith 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23015541/)); a whole coffee cherry extract with **1.8 mg** caffeine cut reaction time and raised BDNF in older adults with cognitive decline ([Robinson 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31829793/)) |
| **Ballistic / explosive (<5 s)** | Probably improves upper body; lower body unclear | A series of **abstract-only** studies with 328 mg instant coffee consistently improved bench-press bar velocity and peak power. Squat responded less |
| **30-s Wingate** | **No effect** | No change in peak power, mean power or fatigue index in experienced cyclists ([Anderson 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29912858/)), physically active students ([Hoffman 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17530975/)), or relatively untrained males ([Clarke 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29910293/)) |
| **Sprint swimming** | **No effect** | 250 mg before a 25 m freestyle: no improvement (Alkatan 2020, *JOPER* — the reference carries no PMID or DOI, so no link) |
| **Multi-set resistance** | Mixed, upper vs lower body inconsistent | Coffee raised total weight lifted in the **squat** but not bench; decaf + anhydrous caffeine beat everything ([Richardson & Clarke 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26890974/)). A separate trial found coffee raised **leg press 1RM** but not bench 1RM or reps to failure ([Trexler 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26394649/)) |
| **1-mile run** | Improves | **1.3%** faster than decaf, **1.9%** faster than placebo at 3 mg/kg ([Clarke 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29140142/)) |
| **5 km cycling TT** | Improves, equally in both sexes | Average **9 s** (men) and **6 s** (women) faster at 3 mg/kg ([Clarke 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31731467/)) |
| **~45 min cycling TT** | Improves ~5% | Coffee and anhydrous caffeine equal ([Hodgson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23573201/)) |
| **50 km TT after 2 weeks loading** | **No effect** | ([Nieman 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29035597/)) |

An umbrella review of 21 meta-analyses concluded coffee is likely effective for resistance and sprint exercise at **3–6 mg/kg**, while noting coffee itself is "relatively under-explored" ([Grgic 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30298476/)). A coffee-specific endurance review put the working range at **3.0–8.1 mg/kg (1.36–3.68 mg/lb)** taken **≥45 min** beforehand ([Higgins 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26568580/)).

## Habituation, genotype, sex

- **Habituation doesn't appear to matter much.** High (>6 mg/kg/d) and low (<3 mg/kg/d) consumers both improved on a 5 km cycling TT ([Clarke & Richardson 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33260141/)), and a meta-analysis found habitual intake did not affect the ergogenic response ([Carvalho 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35536449/)). Tolerance is "incomplete" even after a week of 200 mg twice daily ([Watson 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12392588/)).
- **The dose-shifting proposal is a proposal, not a finding.** [Sökmen 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18438212/) proposed **2–5 mg/kg for non-users, 3–6 for average users, 7–10 for heavy users**. The 2023 stand reports this as a theory; nothing in the coffee literature validates the 7–10 mg/kg tier.
- **Withdrawal contaminates the research.** Symptoms peak at **24–48 h** after cessation and resolve in **4–7 days**; most coffee studies used **12–48 h** abstinence, i.e. precisely inside the peak-withdrawal window. Subjects may be starting trials with headaches.
- **CYP1A2:** AA fast, AC/CC slow. In 101 competitive male athletes, 2 and 4 mg/kg improved endurance only in the **AA** group, with performance **impaired** in CC carriers ([Guest 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29509641/)). The stand adds the right caveat: this was anhydrous caffeine, and coffee's other constituents may change the picture. *"More coffee-specific research is needed."*
- **Sex:** performance improvements are similar in men and women once adjusted for baseline and body mass ([Clarke 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31731467/), [Mielgo-Ayuso 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31574901/)). But **oral contraceptives cut caffeine clearance by ~40%** ([Fazio 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2719518/)) and half-life roughly doubles in the late luteal phase ([Lane 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1483492/)). That matters for evening dosing more than for performance.

## Side effects and interactions

- **Cancer:** the acrylamide scare is resolved — California's OEHHA concluded the dose in coffee is too low to be a carcinogenic risk, and the FDA backed that "strongly." Separately, habitually drinking **very hot** beverages is associated with oesophageal cancer risk ([Islami 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19415743/)), which is about temperature, not coffee.
- **Cardiovascular:** the old risk conclusion has been reversed by more recent cohorts and meta-analyses ([Chrysant 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26141200/)). People with a family history of supraventricular arrhythmias should still be careful with dose.
- **Iron:** the strongest practical interaction. Coffee polyphenols inhibit non-haem iron absorption by up to **60–90%**; adding milk doesn't fix it ([Hurrell 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10999016/)). Iron-deficient athletes should keep coffee away from meals.
- **Aspirin** absorption is accelerated by coffee, via increased gastric acid secretion.
- **Sleep:** limit caffeinated coffee to morning/afternoon; switch to decaf later. Standard advice, and the stand offers no coffee-specific refinement.

## Strength of the evidence

**Genuinely well supported.** The hydration conclusion (a randomised beverage-hydration trial plus a meta-analysis), the acute endurance effect at 3–6 mg/kg, and the demolition of glycogen sparing. Also the honest admission that "coffee" as a research variable is barely standardised.

**Weakest link: the control condition.** Most coffee studies use decaffeinated coffee as placebo. The stand's own reaction-time section shows decaf and high-CGA coffee produce real alertness and mood effects, which means the standard placebo is an active comparator. It also reports that subjects correctly identified the caffeinated drink **56–72%** of the time, so blinding largely failed too. Given that a placebo dose-response was demonstrable in cyclists who received placebo in *every* trial ([Beedie 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17146324/)), this is not a minor methodological quibble — it puts a question mark over a large share of the coffee-specific results.

**Thin.** The explosive/ballistic strength material rests substantially on a series of studies published **as abstracts only**, from the lead author's own laboratory. The stand labels them "(a) = abstract only" in its table, which is proper disclosure, but they carry a lot of the weight in that section. Repeated same-day dosing has no data at all — the stand says so. Training adaptations, the "super-training effect," and body-composition effects are all flagged as speculation.

**Overreached slightly.** The claim that older women drinking more than two cups a day over 12 years had a **69% higher fracture rate** is cited to a review of coffee's effects on *drug pharmacokinetics* ([Belayneh & Molla 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32775441/)) — a secondary source well outside its own subject. The stand does immediately say more research is needed in athletes, but the number shouldn't be carried forward as if it came from a bone study.

**Consistent with the caffeine stand, with one useful correction.** The 2023 coffee stand and the [2021 caffeine stand](caffeine-2021.md) agree on dose (3–6 mg/kg), timing (60 min), hydration, and genotype. Where they part company is emphasis: the caffeine stand still frames coffee as the awkward option, while the coffee stand shows that at matched dose the two perform the same, and that the real problem is knowing your dose. Both positions are downstream of the same 1998 nine-subject study, and both note the difficulty of standardising coffee's caffeine content as the actual reason practitioners reach for capsules.
