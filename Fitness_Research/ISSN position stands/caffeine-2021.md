# Caffeine and Exercise Performance: Doses, Genotype, Timing and Sleep (ISSN 2021)

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2021 Jan 2 · [PMID 33388079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33388079/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4)
**Authors:** Guest NS, VanDusseldorp TA, Nelson MT, Grgic J, Schoenfeld BJ, Jenkins NDM, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

Caffeine reliably improves endurance performance by about **2–4%** at doses of **3–6 mg/kg** taken roughly an hour before exercise, and produces smaller but real gains in muscular strength, muscular endurance, jumping and single-sprint power. The effect is central, not metabolic — the old "glycogen sparing" story is explicitly dismissed. What the 2021 revision adds over its 2010 predecessor is the honest acknowledgement that the average hides enormous individual variation (**−3.0% to +15.9%** across time trials), that some of that variation tracks the **CYP1A2** genotype, and that the costs — disturbed sleep, anxiety, jitters — scale linearly with the dose. The practical upshot is: use the lowest dose that works for you, test it in training first, and take it early in the day.

## Bottom line

- **3–6 mg/kg body mass** (**1.4–2.7 mg/lb**) is the ergogenic range, taken **~60 min pre-exercise**. Doses of **≥9 mg/kg** add nothing and multiply side effects.
- Endurance improves **2–4%** across dozens of studies ([Ganio 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19077738/), [Southward 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29876876/)). A meta-analysis of 46 studies found mean power **+2.9 ± 2.2%** (ES 0.22) and time-trial completion **+2.3 ± 2.6%** (ES 0.28) ([Southward 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29876876/)).
- **Low doses (1–3 mg/kg) are also ergogenic** and produce no measurable change in RER, lactate or glucose — the strongest single argument that the mechanism is CNS, not fuel ([Spriet 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25355191/), [Talanian 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27426699/)).
- **Strength gains are small: ES 0.16–0.20, roughly 2–7%** across three meta-analyses ([Warren 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20019636/), [Grgic 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30217692/), [Grgic 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29527137/)). **Muscular endurance is a bit better: ES 0.28–0.38 (6–7%)**.
- **Repeated-sprint performance is not improved** — total work, best sprint and last sprint were all unaffected in meta-analysis ([Lopes-Silva 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31036532/)). Single and intermittent sprints do respond.
- **RPE falls ~5.6%**, and that reduction statistically explains **up to 29%** of the performance improvement ([Doherty & Smith 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15773860/)).
- **Fast metabolisers (CYP1A2 AA) get about 6.8% at 4 mg/kg; slow metabolisers with CC got 13.7% worse** ([Guest 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29509641/)). This is the most consequential finding in the whole document and it rests on one large trial.
- **Trained and untrained respond similarly.** The 2010 stand's claim that caffeine only works in trained athletes is walked back ([O'Rourke 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17544329/), [Warren 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20019636/)).
- **Side effects rise linearly with dose** ([Pallarés 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23669879/)). Caffeine at **18:00 caused insomnia in 6 of 13** subjects; the same dose in the morning affected **1 of 13** ([Mora-Rodríguez 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24816164/)).
- Caffeine is **not banned** — WADA removed it in 2004 and now only monitors it. The **12 µg/ml** urine threshold corresponds to about **10 mg/kg** taken over several hours, more than triple the ergogenic dose.
- You **cannot "outcaffeinate" poor sleep** — the stand says so in those words.

## What changed since the 2010 stand

Eleven years apart, and the two documents disagree on more than they agree on in mechanism and population. The 2021 paper cites the 2010 one directly ([Goldstein 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20205813/)).

| Question | 2010 said | 2021 says |
|---|---|---|
| Dose | **3–6 mg/kg**, nothing above 9 mg/kg | Same headline, but adds that **1–3 mg/kg works too**, and prefers the low end because side effects scale with dose |
| Mechanism | Substantial weight on **fat oxidation and glycogen sparing** | That hypothesis was "challenged and **eventually dismissed**". Mechanism is adenosine antagonism in the CNS |
| Untrained athletes | "**Exceedingly apparent** that caffeine is not effective for non-trained individuals" in high-intensity exercise | Effects are "**similar in both trained and untrained**" |
| Strength | "The literature is **inconsistent**" | **Three meta-analyses all find an effect**, but a small one (2–7%) |
| Coffee vs anhydrous | Anhydrous is "**more powerful**" than coffee | Coffee at **3–8.1 mg/kg** benefits endurance; the preference for anhydrous is now framed as a **dose-standardisation** problem, not a potency one |
| Genotype | **Not mentioned at all** | An entire section: CYP1A2, ADORA2A, anxiety, sleep sensitivity |
| Sleep | Two sentences, in passing | A full section, plus a side-effects section |
| Doping status | Framed as "the **IOC mandates an allowable limit of 12 µg/ml**" — stale by six years | States plainly that the IOC/WADA **removed caffeine in 2004**; it is monitored, not prohibited |
| Delivery forms | Capsules and coffee | Gum, mouth rinse, nasal spray, inspired powder, gels, bars, energy drinks, pre-workouts |
| Individual variation | Barely discussed | Foregrounded: **−3.0% to +15.9%** across 56 endurance time trials ([Shen 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30170953/)) |

**Dose recommendations.** The headline range didn't move, but the reasoning underneath it did. 2010 argued for 3–6 mg/kg because higher doses stopped adding benefit — a plateau argument. 2021 argues for the same range because lower doses in it are *equally* ergogenic and produce "substantially fewer side-effects" ([Spriet 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25355191/), [Pallarés 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23669879/)). The practical shift is downward: if 3 mg/kg works, there's no case for 6.

**The genotype material.** Over 95% of caffeine is cleared by the CYP1A2 enzyme ([Begas 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17221922/)), which converts it to paraxanthine (~84% of elimination), theobromine (~12%) and theophylline (~4%); only **3–5%** leaves unchanged in urine. The **−163A>C (rs762551)** polymorphism alters enzyme inducibility ([Ghotbi 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17370067/), [Djordjević 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18157525/)): **AA = fast metaboliser, AC/CC = slow.** The pivotal trial gave **101 competitive male athletes** placebo, 2 mg/kg or 4 mg/kg before a 10-km cycling time trial ([Guest 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29509641/)):

| Genotype | 2 mg/kg | 4 mg/kg |
|---|---|---|
| AA (fast) | improved | **+6.8%** |
| AC | no effect | no effect |
| CC (slow) | no effect | **−13.7% (impaired)** |

An earlier study agreed ([Womack 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22420682/)), and a resistance-training crossover found more total repetitions only in AA carriers ([Rahimi 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29532291/)). Against that, some studies found no genotype effect or benefits only in slow metabolisers. **ADORA2A** (adenosine A2A receptor) is the second gene: **rs5751876 TT** carriers appear more caffeine-sensitive — more anxiety ([Childs 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18305461/), [Rogers 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20520601/)) and, in a **12-subject pilot**, all six TT women benefited ergogenically versus one of six C-carriers ([Loy 2015](https://doi.org/10.1089/jcr.2014.0035)). CC/CT carriers show more caffeine-induced sleep disruption ([Rétey 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17329997/), [Bodenmann 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21950736/)).

**Habituation and withdrawal.** This is where 2010 and 2021 land in nearly the same place — "we don't know" — but 2021 shows its working. The evidence pulls three ways:

- **Tolerance develops:** 28 days of low-dose caffeine in previously low consumers abolished the acute ergogenic effect ([Beaumont 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27762662/)).
- **Tolerance doesn't develop:** 20 consecutive days maintained the ergogenic effect, though the effect size attenuated ([Lara 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30673725/)).
- **Habitual intake doesn't matter:** 40 cyclists split into low (58 ± 29 mg/d), moderate (143 ± 25 mg/d) and high (351 ± 139 mg/d) consumers all improved equally at 6 mg/kg ([Gonçalves 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28495846/)).

The stand's most useful contribution here is the **withdrawal-reversal confound** ([James 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24892519/)): short abstinence periods (12–48 h) mean the "caffeine" trial is partly just relief from withdrawal symptoms, which by itself looks like an ergogenic effect. It criticises the Gonçalves study on exactly this ground — a 24-h washout. Two studies imposed longer withdrawal and still found benefit: 3 mg/kg improved cycling regardless of a 4-day withdrawal in habitual users ([Irwin 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21279864/)), and endurance improved at 6 mg/kg after 0, 2 or 4 days of abstinence in **six** recreational athletes ([Van Soeren 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8226485/)). Animal work shows adenosine receptor upregulation with chronic intake, but *"changes in adenosine receptor number or activity have not been studied in humans."* That sentence is the honest bottom of the tolerance question.

**What 2010 got walked back.** Three claims: (a) that caffeine is ineffective in untrained people, (b) that glycogen sparing explains the endurance effect, (c) that anhydrous caffeine is inherently more powerful than coffee. Also quietly dropped is 2010's "Caffeine and Women" section, which leaned partly on an unpublished master's thesis; 2021 states instead that the benefits are not influenced by sex.

## Practical dosing and timing

- **60 min pre-exercise** is the default because plasma caffeine peaks around then. Tmax across studies ranges **30–120 min**.
- **Later may be better for long events.** Effect size grows with time-trial duration ([Shen 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30170953/)) — caffeine helps most when you're already fatigued. A low dose taken *toward the end* of a race (the flat-cola trick) matched 5–6 mg/kg taken 60 min beforehand ([Cox 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12183495/)), and the effect was the caffeine, not the carbohydrate.
- **The source changes the optimal timing.** Caffeinated gum absorbs faster (Tmax **44–80 min** vs **84–120 min** for capsules, with comparable bioavailability — [Kamimori 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11839447/)) and worked when taken **immediately** before cycling but not 1 or 2 h beforehand ([Ryan 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22476164/)).
- **Half-life is 4–6 h on average but ranges 1.5–10 h.** Hormonal contraceptives and pregnancy slow clearance; smoking and heavy use speed it up.

## Where caffeine works, and where it doesn't

| Task | Effect | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Endurance (cycling, running, skiing, swimming) | **+2–4%** | Strong — dozens of RCTs, several meta-analyses |
| Muscular endurance (reps to failure) | **ES 0.28–0.38, ~6–7%** | Moderate; inconsistent across individual trials |
| Maximal strength (1RM, isokinetic) | **ES 0.16–0.20, ~2–7%** | Moderate; meaningful mainly for powerlifting/weightlifting |
| Vertical jump, throws | **ES 0.17–0.22, ~2–4%** | Moderate |
| Single Wingate | mean power **+3%**, peak **+4%** | Contested — one meta-analysis finds it ([Grgic 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29087785/)), another finds nothing |
| Repeated sprints (short rest) | **No effect** | Reasonably clear ([Lopes-Silva 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31036532/)) |
| Vigilance under sleep deprivation | Robust | Strong — military field studies, repeated **~200 mg** doses |
| Cognition when well-rested | Weak to absent | Several null trials |

Sport-specific results are patchier than the summary conveys: caffeine increased free throws made and rebounds in basketball ([Puente 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925969/)) but not sprint time or dribbling speed, and it improved **no** NFL Combine anaerobic test at all ([Woolf 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19620930/)).

## Sleep, anxiety and the cost side

The 2021 stand is unusually willing to price the downside. Common side effects are tachycardia, palpitations, anxiety, headache and disturbed sleep, and their incidence and severity are **primarily determined by dose**, rising roughly linearly ([Pallarés 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23669879/)). Caffeine before an evening Super Rugby match delayed sleep onset and shortened sleep duration that night ([Dunican 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29431593/)); objectively, caffeine worsens sleep latency, wake-after-sleep-onset, efficiency and duration ([Clark & Landolt 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26899133/)).

Two practical consequences the stand draws: skill-dependent athletes (tennis, biathlon shooting) may be net losers from caffeine-induced arousal while a football lineman may not; and athletes prone to competition anxiety — particularly ADORA2A TT carriers — may want to avoid caffeine around competition entirely.

## Environment, hydration and stacking

- **Hydration is a non-issue.** At recommended doses caffeine does not meaningfully impair hydration or thermoregulation; even **9 mg/kg** did not substantially alter heat balance at 40 °C ([Ely 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21411837/)).
- **Altitude:** all four existing hypoxia studies found benefit at **4–6 mg/kg** ([Berglund 1982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7152771/), [Smirmaul 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27864638/)) — but that is four studies total.
- **Creatine:** caution is advised with **chronic high-dose (>9 mg/kg) caffeine during creatine loading**, on the basis of hypothesised opposing effects on Ca²⁺ handling and muscle relaxation time ([Vandenberghe 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8929583/), [Hespel 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11796658/)). That is a hedge, not a finding.
- **Carbohydrate:** caffeine + CHO beats CHO alone ([Conger 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21411838/)), but the increment is smaller than caffeine-vs-placebo. Post-exercise, **8 mg/kg** with **4 g/kg** CHO raised glycogen resynthesis ([Pedersen 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18467543/)) — at that dose and timing the sleep cost usually outweighs it.

## Strength of the evidence

**Solid.** The endurance claim. It rests on multiple independent meta-analyses of randomised placebo-controlled trials, the effect sizes are consistent (small, ~0.2–0.3), and the dose-response is well characterised. The dismissal of the glycogen-sparing mechanism is also well grounded — low doses that change no metabolic marker still improve performance.

**Reasonable but oversold in the summary.** Strength and power. Effect sizes of 0.16–0.22 are genuinely small, and the summary line "caffeine is ergogenic when consumed in doses of 3–6 mg/kg" doesn't distinguish a 3% endurance gain from a 2% jump-height gain that may not survive the next meta-analysis.

**Thin.** The ADORA2A story rests on **one 12-subject pilot** for the performance claim. Altitude rests on four studies. Caffeinated bars rest on **one** study. Nasal sprays showed no performance benefit at all, and are included anyway.

**Overreached.** The sport-specific list states "Basketball — increased jump height, but only in those with the AA version of the CYP1A2 gene [240]", citing a paper whose title is *"The CYP1A2 −163C>A polymorphism **does not alter** the effects of caffeine on basketball performance"* ([Puente 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29668752/)). The body text later concedes "even though there was **no caffeine-genotype interaction effect** for this outcome" — so the bullet asserts as a genotype finding something the underlying paper reports as a null. A reader who only reads the bullet list is misled.

**The genotype question is not settled.** [Guest 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29509641/) is a good study — 101 athletes, three conditions — and a **13.7% impairment** in CC carriers is a startling result that, if real, matters enormously. But it is one trial, contradicted by several others, and the stand's explanation for the contradictions (small samples, wrong test type) is post hoc. Treat "get genotyped" as a hypothesis, not a recommendation. It's worth noting that the lead author's research programme is in nutrigenomics; the extracted text of this article carries no competing-interests statement either way.

**On cycling off caffeine.** The stand does **not** recommend cycling off, and the evidence it reviews doesn't support the practice: no study tested "abstain for N days, then retest and see the response restored". What exists is one 28-day tolerance study, one 20-day no-tolerance study, and two abstinence studies (4 days max, n = 12 and n = 6) that found caffeine ergogenic *regardless* of washout. The stand's own framing cuts the other way — short abstinence may make caffeine look better than it is, by letting it reverse withdrawal.
