# Protein and Exercise (2017): Daily Total, Per-Meal Dose, and Why Timing Got Demoted

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2017 · [PMID 28642676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8)
**Authors:** Jäger R, Kerksick CM, Campbell BI, Cribb PJ, Wells SD, Skwiat TM, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Protein & macronutrients

This is the ten-year revision of the [2007 stand](protein-2007.md), and the single most cited document in this library's protein material. The daily number did not move. Almost everything around it did.

## The position, in one paragraph

Eat **at least 1.4–2.0 g of protein per kg of bodyweight per day (0.64–0.91 g/lb)** — and treat that as a floor, not a target, especially if you are dieting. Split it into **3–4 or more feedings of 20–40 g each (0.25 g/kg per meal)**, spaced roughly **three hours** apart. Prefer fast-digesting, leucine-rich sources; whey is the best-studied. Intakes up to **3.3 g/kg/day** have been shown safe for a year in trained lifters, and intakes above 3 g/kg may help body composition. Timing around the workout is a convenience, not a lever: **total daily protein is the strongest predictor of hypertrophy**, and once you are at 1.6 g/kg/day or more, the peri-workout window stops mattering much. Adding protein does not improve endurance performance, though it reduces soreness and damage markers.

## Bottom line

- **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day is a minimum**, not a range to aim at the middle of. Higher may be needed when restricting calories to hold fat-free mass. Fits inside the IOM's 10–35% protein AMDR ([Wolfe 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28298271/)).
- **Per meal: 0.25 g/kg, or 20–40 g absolute** — carrying **10–12 g of EAAs** and **1–3 g of leucine**. 20 g was the plateau at rest with egg ([Moore 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19056590/)) and with whey after resistance exercise ([Witard 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24257722/)).
- **But 40 g beat 20 g after whole-body training** ([Macnaughton 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27511985/)) — the finding that broke the "20 g ceiling" as a general rule.
- **Older lifters need ~40 g per dose** to max out synthesis ([Yang 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22313809/)); **~70 g** is needed to meaningfully suppress protein breakdown ([Kim 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26530155/)).
- **Space meals ~3 h apart.** 4 × 20 g over 12 h beat both 8 × 10 g and 2 × 40 g for myofibrillar synthesis ([Areta 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23459753/)).
- **Pre-sleep: 30–40 g casein, 30 min before bed.** Raises overnight MPS ([Res 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22330017/)) and over 12 weeks added muscle and strength ([Snijders 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926415/)).
- **Timing is demoted.** Total protein is the strongest predictor of hypertrophy; timing effects "appear to be minimal" in non-athletes, and the benefit shrinks once daily intake is **≥1.6 g/kg/day**.
- **Supplementation meta-analysis:** +**0.69 kg fat-free mass** (95% CI 0.47–0.91) and +**13.5 kg lower-body strength** (95% CI 6.4–20.7) versus placebo across 22 trials, n=680 ([Cermak 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23134885/)).
- **In a 30% deficit, 1.6 and 2.4 g/kg both beat 0.8 g/kg** for keeping lean mass: the 1× RDA group lost the most total weight but the highest proportion of it was lean tissue ([Pasiakos 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23739654/)).
- **Safety: up to 3.3 g/kg/day for one year, no harm** to kidney function, liver function or blood lipids in trained men ([Antonio 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27807480/)).
- **Endurance:** extra protein does not improve endurance performance. **0.25 g/kg per hour** of endurance exercise, on top of normal carbs, for damage markers and soreness only.

## Daily intake: what the number actually means

The RDA's 0.8 g/kg/day is not in dispute as a nitrogen-equilibrium figure; the argument is that nitrogen balance **systematically underestimates** requirements. Phillips' 2004 regression put trained-athlete needs at **1.2 g/kg/day**, rising to **1.33** at the upper 95% CI ([Phillips 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15212752/)). The stand's response is pointed: the two included studies that prescribed **2.4 and 2.5 g/kg/day** produced the highest positive nitrogen balances in the entire dataset ([Tarnopolsky 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3356636/), [Tarnopolsky 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1474076/)). If your goal is an anabolic environment rather than break-even, the ceiling of the balance data is the wrong reference point.

For strength specifically, the observational signal is large: college footballers self-selecting **above 2.0 g/kg/day** gained **22% more squat and 42% more bench** strength than those at the recommended 1.6–1.8 g/kg/day ([Hoffman 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24149229/)). This is not a randomised comparison and the stand does not pretend otherwise.

## Timing: read this section before you rearrange your day

This is where the 2017 stand does its most careful work, and where it most visibly retreats from 2007.

The mechanistic case remains: muscle is **sensitised to protein for up to 24 h** after a resistance bout ([Burd 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21289204/)), MPS peaks within 30–60 min of the signalling response and stays elevated for 24–72 h, and net balance stays negative without feeding ([Biolo 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7900797/)).

But the training-outcome evidence does not follow the mechanism:

- Pre- versus post-exercise ingestion: Tipton found pre-exercise superior for delivery and MPS ([Tipton 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11440894/)); Fujita, using the same design with a different tracer approach, found **no difference** ([Fujita 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18535123/)); Tipton's later work found 20 g whey isolate produced the same MPS immediately before or after ([Tipton 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15570142/)). A training study found **similar muscular adaptations either side** ([Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28070459/)).
- The timing meta-analysis found protein within one hour post-exercise gave a small significant hypertrophy benefit — **which disappeared once total protein was controlled** (the timed groups were eating ~1.7 vs ~1.3 g/kg). Total intake was the strongest predictor ([Schoenfeld 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299050/)). *(Note: the source article cites this as ref [84], which is actually Bosse & Dixon; the meta-analysis is its ref [32]. A numbering error in the published paper.)*
- The anabolic window is more like **five to six hours**, not one ([Aragon & Schoenfeld 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23360586/)) — so a pre-workout meal eaten less than five hours before you finish training largely removes the need for immediate post-workout protein.

The stand's own summary: *"the benefits of protein timing in relation to the peri-workout period seem to be lessened for people who are already ingesting appropriate amounts of protein (e.g. ≥1.6 g/kg/day)"*, and the honest practical framing — a post-workout meal is worth eating because **there is no downside**, and because it is another chance to raise your daily total.

**Post-workout carbs are not required for the protein response.** Insulin's effect on MPS depends on amino acid availability, not on insulin itself ([Trommelen 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25646407/)); its anti-catabolic effect saturates at **15–30 μIU/mL**, achievable with 45 g of whey isolate alone ([Power 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18679613/)); and carbohydrate added to protein produced **no further MPS** ([Staples 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131864/)). Carbs matter for glycogen, not for muscle protein.

## Sources ranked, and how the stand ranks them

Two criteria decide everything: **leucine content** and **digestion rate**.

| Source | Leucine | Digestion | Verdict in this stand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whey | 11% | Fast | Best studied; distinct MPS advantage |
| Casein | 9.3% | Slow | Good; best for pre-sleep |
| Egg | 8.6% | Fast (whites) | Excellent, under-researched ("less funding relative to dairy") |
| Meat | ~8% | Fast if >95% lean | Good; also iron, B12, creatine, carnitine |
| Soy | Lower | Intermediate | Beats casein at rest, loses to whey |
| Wheat, rice, pea | Low | Medium–slow | Need larger doses; rice matched whey at 48 g/day |

The decisive comparison: whey stimulated MPS about **93% more than casein and 18% more than soy at rest**, and **122% more than casein and 31% more than soy after resistance exercise** ([Tang 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19589961/)). Ten weeks of whey versus casein in trained bodybuilders produced **5.0 kg vs 0.8 kg** lean mass ([Cribb 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17240782/)). And the rate itself matters independently of the amino acids: a 25 g bolus of whey beat the **identical** 25 g delivered as ten 2.5 g sips ([West 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21795443/)).

Plant sources are not written off. At high doses the difference collapses: 48 g/day of rice protein matched 48 g/day of whey for strength, muscle thickness and body composition over 8 weeks ([Joy 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23782948/)). Beef, chicken and whey at 46 g/day all produced the same strength gains, lean gains and fat loss ([Sharp 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28399016/)). Milk beat soy over 12 weeks for lean gain ([Hartman 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17684208/)).

## Safety: settled, on real trials this time

Four controlled investigations, culminating in a **one-year crossover in trained men at 3.3 versus 2.5 g/kg/day** — no adverse effects on kidney function, liver function, metabolic markers or blood lipids ([Antonio 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27807480/)). Earlier entries in the series ran 8 weeks at **4.4 g/kg/day** ([Antonio 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24834017/)) and **3.4 g/kg/day** ([Antonio 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26500462/)) with the same result. The WHO and the Australia/New Zealand nutrient reference panel are both cited as finding no evidence linking high protein to renal disease in healthy people.

The caveat the stand keeps: people **in renal failure** do benefit from protein restriction ([Fouque & Laville 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19588328/)). Extending that to healthy trained people is what it calls inappropriate.

## What changed since the 2007 stand

The revision presents itself as continuous — *"in alignment with our previous position stand"* — and on the headline number it is. Underneath, five of the 2007 document's positions were revised, softened, or dropped without comment.

**1. The daily number held, but its status changed.**
2007: 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day, framed as *the* range, with a specific place in it depending on your sport. 2017: the same numbers, reframed as *"a minimum recommended amount while greater amounts may be needed for people attempting to restrict energy intake."* And a genuinely new addition: *"there is preliminary evidence that consuming much higher quantities of protein (>3 g/kg/d) may confer a benefit as it relates to body composition."* In 2007 that intake would have been outside the recommendation entirely.

**2. Sport-specific tiers were replaced by per-meal dosing.**
This is the largest structural change. 2007 tiered intake by mode — endurance 1.0–1.6, intermittent 1.4–1.7, strength/power 1.6–2.0 g/kg/day. 2017 abandons that tiering (the endurance and intermittent bands simply do not appear) and states explicitly that *"protein needs are being recommended within this position stand on a per meal basis"* — 0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g, every ~3 h. Ten years of dose-response tracer work ([Moore 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19056590/), [Witard 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24257722/), [Yang 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22313809/), [Areta 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23459753/)) made distribution the more useful axis than sport.

**3. Timing was walked back from a formal position to a convenience.**
2007's position was affirmative and unqualified: *"exercising individuals should consume high quality protein within the time period encompassing their exercise session (i.e. before, during, and after)."* 2017's corresponding key point: *"Total protein and calorie intake appears to be the most important consideration ... and the impact of timing strategies (immediately before or immediately after) to heighten these adaptations in non-athletic populations appears to be minimal."* The stated rationale for eating around training is reduced to the fact that it is *"an opportunity to eat, thus elevating one's total daily protein intake."* That is a substantial retreat, and it is the change most likely to alter what a reader actually does.

**4. The whey-and-casein blend recommendation was dropped.**
2007 made an explicit product recommendation: *"we recommend that the protein contain both whey and casein components."* By 2017 the whey-versus-casein data had resolved in whey's favour ([Tang 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19589961/), [Cribb 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17240782/)), and the criterion became fast digestion plus high leucine. Blends get a hedge — they *"might afford a favorable combination"* but *"more research is needed"* — and the direct recommendation is gone. Casein survives in one new and specific role: pre-sleep.

**5. The BCAA section was deleted entirely.**
2007 devoted a full section to BCAAs and issued numeric dosing: **≥45 mg/kg/day leucine plus ≥22.5 mg/kg/day each of isoleucine and valine**, taken before, during and after exercise, on a 2:1:1 ratio. It also promoted the central-fatigue/tryptophan rationale for BCAAs in endurance work. **None of this appears in 2017.** Leucine is discussed only as a marker of whole-protein quality, with the explicit warning that *"supplementation should not be with just leucine alone"* and that *"a higher total dose of all EAAs ... seems to be most suited for sustaining the increased rates of MPS."* No BCAA dose is recommended anywhere in the 2017 document. This is a reversal, and it is not flagged as one.

**6. The immune claim disappeared.**
2007 reported the Marine recruit trial — 33% fewer medical visits, 83% fewer heat-exhaustion visits, from a supplement containing 10 g protein ([Flakoll 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14657039/)) — and concluded protein was *"beneficial for ... sustaining immune function during high-volume training periods."* 2017 mentions whey's bioactive peptides, lactoferrin and α-lactalbumin in passing, but makes **no immune recommendation** and does not cite the Marine study. An extraordinary single-trial result was quietly retired.

**7. The renal-insufficiency caveat was dropped.**
2007 warned that *"those individuals with mild renal insufficiency need to closely monitor their protein intake."* 2017's safety key points contain no equivalent — only that people in outright renal failure benefit from restriction. Given the much stronger safety evidence in 2017, this is defensible, but it is a caveat lost rather than a caveat re-examined.

**8. The "requirements may decrease with training" hedge was dropped.**
2007 noted that adaptations improving net protein retention might **lower** requirements over time ([Rennie & Tipton 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10940342/)). 2017 does not repeat it. Every directional statement in the revision points upward.

**9. Genuinely new territory in 2017, with no 2007 analogue:** endurance performance (a clear negative finding plus the 0.25 g/kg/h damage-and-soreness recommendation); pre-sleep protein; protein distribution and pacing across the day; plant protein sources beyond soy; hydrolysates; filtration methods; digestive enzymes; and controlled long-term safety data.

**The pattern.** The revision keeps every 2007 claim that later evidence supported, and silently removes the ones it did not — the BCAA dosing, the immune effect, the blend recommendation, the strong timing position. That is the right outcome, but a reader comparing the two documents would not be told any of it happened. The 2017 conclusion opens with *"in alignment with our previous position stand"*, which is true only of the headline number.

## Strength of the evidence

**Strong.** The daily 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day floor, and the finding that protein supplementation improves lean mass and lower-body strength. The Cermak meta-analysis ([Cermak 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23134885/)) pools 22 trials with 680 subjects and reports confidence intervals that exclude zero comfortably. The lean-mass effect (0.69 kg) is real but modest; note the 13.5 kg lower-body strength figure has a very wide CI (6.4–20.7) and is an absolute-load figure, not a percentage.

**Strong.** Safety at high intakes. Four controlled trials including a one-year crossover is a far better evidence base than 2007's cohort studies. The caveat worth keeping in mind: all four are from **the same laboratory** (Antonio and colleagues), all use resistance-trained subjects, all rely on self-reported intake, and Antonio is a co-founder of the ISSN. Independent replication would strengthen this considerably. The conclusion is almost certainly right; the evidential independence is thinner than the citation count suggests.

**Solid.** The demotion of timing. This is the stand contradicting a claim its own predecessor made, on the basis of a meta-analysis whose effect vanished under control for total protein and a training study finding no pre/post difference. It is the intellectually honest move and it is well supported.

**Mixed.** Per-meal dosing. The 20 g figure is robust for isolated-limb work at rest; the 40 g figure after whole-body training rests essentially on **one study** ([Macnaughton 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27511985/)), and the 70 g breakdown-suppression figure on one more ([Kim 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26530155/)). The 3-hourly distribution recommendation comes from a **single 12-hour acute study** using a total of only 80 g of protein — which the stand itself concedes is *"quite low"* — and it acknowledges the longitudinal work testing distribution patterns gives **mixed** results ([Arnal 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10867039/), [Tinsley 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27550719/)). The recommendation is described as "pragmatic", which is the correct word.

**Thin.** Pre-sleep protein. The 12-week Snijders trial is the anchor, and the stand flags the problem itself: the protein group ate **1.9 g/kg/day versus 1.3** in the placebo group ([Snijders 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926415/)). That is a total-protein study, not a timing study. The one trial that **did** equate total protein found no difference between morning and evening casein ([Antonio 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28515842/)) — and the stand spends more words on that study's non-significant 1.2 kg vs 0.4 kg trend than on its null result. That is selective reading.

**Weakest.** The "protein pacing" recommendation. Four of the five supporting citations in that paragraph are Arciero's own work, and Arciero is a co-author of this stand. The paragraph also miscites: the body attributes protein-pacing findings to refs [116, 141], but [116] is Kinsey's pre-sleep casein study, not Arciero's. Ref [106] is likewise miscited as supporting whey's immune effects when it is Staples' carbohydrate study. There are at least three citation-numbering errors in the published document.

**Not addressed at all.** The stand does not confront the trial in its own reference list that found *"protein supplementation has minimal effects on muscle adaptations during resistance exercise training in young men"* in 68 subjects over 12 weeks ([Reidy 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27466602/)). It is cited only for the whey-versus-blend comparison, not for its headline null finding. And the honest observation it does make — that **protein intake may need to rise ~59% above baseline** before fat-free mass changes appear ([Bosse & Dixon 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22958314/)) — undercuts many of the supplementation trials it cites, since most did not achieve a spread that large.

**Disclosure.** Set out in the section below. The safety evidence base is drawn from a single ISSN-affiliated laboratory; weigh the safety conclusion — which independent bodies also endorse — separately from the source recommendations.
