# Sodium Bicarbonate: 0.3 g/kg, 60–180 Minutes Out, for Efforts of 30 Seconds to 12 Minutes

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2021 Sep 9 · [PMID 34503527](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34503527/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-021-00458-w)
**Authors:** Grgic J, Pedisic Z, Saunders B, Artioli GG, Schoenfeld BJ, McKenna MJ, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 (<https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>)
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

Sodium bicarbonate is baking soda, it has been studied since **1931**, and it works — but only inside a narrow window. It raises extracellular buffering capacity, pulling H⁺ out of working muscle via monocarboxylate transporters, which helps exactly one kind of effort: **hard, glycolytic work lasting longer than 30 seconds and up to about 12 minutes**. Take **0.3 g/kg, 60–180 minutes before**. It improves high-intensity cycling, running, rowing, swimming, combat sports and multi-set resistance training. It does **not** improve maximal strength, and it does **not** improve a single 30-second Wingate. The main practical obstacle is your gut: bloating, nausea and vomiting rise with dose and can wipe out the benefit entirely. Roughly **30% of the effect appears to be placebo**, which the stand says out loud.

## Bottom line

- **Optimal single dose: 0.3 g/kg** (~**0.14 g/lb**). **0.2 g/kg is the practical minimum**; 0.1 g/kg does nothing; **0.4–0.5 g/kg adds no benefit and more side effects** ([McNaughton 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1331493/)).
- **Timing: 60–180 min before.** Individualising to peak plasma bicarbonate is theoretically better but needs a blood gas analyser, and the timing is not reproducible between administrations anyway ([Oliveira 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32102054/)).
- **Effort duration is the deciding variable.** 10 s and 30 s: nothing. 120 s and 240 s: yes ([McNaughton 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1331494/)). Wingate meta-analysis: **d = 0.02** — nil ([Grgic 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33314967/)).
- **Typical magnitude: 1–3%.** 800 m run **1.8% faster** ([Wilkes 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6312244/)); 4-km cycling TT **~2%** ([Gough 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30196448/)); 2000 m rowing **1.4%** ([Turnes 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31034295/)); 200–400 m swimming **1.3%** ([Grgic & Mikulic 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34151681/)).
- **Repeated bouts benefit more than single bouts**, and the benefit shows up in the *later* reps — sprints 3–5, not sprint 1 ([Bishop 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15126714/), [Artioli 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17507744/)).
- **Resistance training: muscular endurance yes (d = 0.37), maximal strength no (d = −0.03)** ([Grgic 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32096113/)).
- **Multiple-day loading works** at **0.4–0.5 g/kg/day for 3–7 days**, split across meals — and moves the side effects off competition day.
- **Side effects are the main failure mode.** In one trial the ergogenic effect only appeared after excluding the 4 of 21 participants with GI distress ([Saunders 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24155093/)).
- **Stacks with beta-alanine and creatine.** Caffeine: only **1 of 8** studies found additive effects. Nitrates: possibly counterproductive.

## Dosing, in grams you can actually measure

| Bodyweight | 0.2 g/kg (minimum) | **0.3 g/kg (optimal)** | 0.5 g/kg/day (loading, split 3×) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 kg / 132 lb | 12 g | **18 g** | 30 g |
| 70 kg / 154 lb | 14 g | **21 g** | 35 g |
| 80 kg / 176 lb | 16 g | **24 g** | 40 g |
| 90 kg / 198 lb | 18 g | **27 g** | 45 g |

That is a lot of capsules. The alternative — powder in water — is bitter and salty, which is why almost no study using it maintained a credible blind.

## Where it works, and by how much

| Task | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 10–30 s all-out (Wingate) | **No** | meta d = 0.02 ([Grgic 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33314967/)) |
| 400–1500 m running | **Yes**, 1.1–2.9% | [Bird 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8558626/), [Goldfinch 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2830108/), [Wilkes 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6312244/) |
| 1–2 min treadmill to fatigue | **Yes**, 77.4 → **82.3 s (+6%)** | [Van Montfoort 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15235332/) |
| 4–12 min cycling | **Yes**, ~2% | [Gough 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30196448/), [Mueller 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23531361/) |
| 20–68 min continuous | **Inconsistent** — 3 null, 3 positive | [Stephens 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11932569/) null |
| Long race with a finishing sprint | **Yes** — 3-h race, **+3%** in the final 90-s sprint | [Dalle 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34756350/) |
| Repeated 1-min cycling | **Yes** — final sprint extended 113 → **160 s** | [Costill 1984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6094373/) |
| 5 × 6-s sprints | **Yes**, in sprints 3–5 only | [Bishop 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15126714/) |
| Yo-Yo IR2 | **Yes**, meta **+16%** | [Grgic 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31466868/) |
| 2000 m rowing | **Yes**, **+1.4%** (90% CI 0.1–2.6%) | [Turnes 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31034295/), [Hobson 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23579002/) |
| 100 m swimming (50–60 s) | **No** | [Grgic & Mikulic 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34151681/) |
| 200–400 m swimming | **Yes**, **+1.3%** | same meta; [Lindh 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18004687/) |
| Judo (Special Judo Fitness Test) | **Yes**, **+2 throws (~6%)** in bouts 2–3 | [Artioli 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17507744/) |
| Boxing sparring | **Yes**, more punches over 4 rounds | [Siegler & Hirscher 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19625976/) |
| Multi-set resistance to failure | **Yes** — 157 → **164 reps (+4.5%)** | [Carr 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22941193/) |
| 1RM strength | **No** — meta **d = −0.03** | [Grgic 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32096113/) |

Note the pattern in the marginal cases: 1–3% is small in absolute terms and large in competitive ones. The stand argues, reasonably, that **0.3%** in rowing and **0.4%** in swimming are enough to change placings.

## Resistance training specifically

The single most useful finding for lifters is that the effect grows across sets. In back squats at 80% 1RM, set 1 was identical (**12 reps** both conditions), set 2 was **11 vs 7 reps**, set 3 was **9 vs 6** ([Duncan 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24126895/)). A single set lasting ~40 s showed nothing at all ([Webster 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8396707/)). Same rule as everywhere else in this document: **too short, no effect**.

It does not make you stronger. What it may do is stop you getting weaker within a session — the decline in knee-extensor strength across a basketball game was **9% with bicarbonate vs 15% with placebo** ([Ansdell & Dekerle 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29023327/)). The stand's inference that this should translate into greater long-term strength gains is a mechanistic argument, not a finding.

## Long-term use

Three studies, two positive:

- **8 weeks** of interval training with 0.4 g/kg before every session: lactate threshold improved **26% vs 15%**, time to fatigue **164% vs 123%** — and the tests were run *without* acute supplementation, so this is a training adaptation, not an acute effect ([Edge 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16627675/)).
- **6 weeks** of HIIT: relative peak power **+21% vs +10%** ([Wang 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30987663/)).
- Two null studies, both with **4–6 participants per group** ([Driller 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22899814/), [Siegler 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29718968/)).

## Managing the side effects

The document is unusually good on this, because the side effects genuinely determine whether the supplement works.

1. **Use 0.2–0.3 g/kg**, not more. Incidence and severity rise linearly with dose ([McNaughton 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1331493/)).
2. **Take it with a high-carbohydrate meal** (~1.5 g/kg carbohydrate). This produced alkalosis with the lowest GI symptom incidence of any protocol tested ([Carr 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21719899/)).
3. **Push timing out to ~180 min** if you're symptomatic. GI discomfort was lower at 180 min than at 60 or 120 min despite equivalent alkalosis ([Siegler 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21964428/)); the worst incidence clusters around **90 min** ([Carr 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21719899/)).
4. **Enteric-coated capsules.** Same performance benefit, **3 of 11 vs 7 of 11** participants with GI discomfort ([Hilton 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32388584/)). Won't help with osmotic diarrhoea.
5. **Or load over 3–7 days** and take nothing on competition day.
6. **Trial it in training first.** The stand's explicit recommendation, and the right one.

One more variable: **leave time between warm-up and event**. A hard sprint-based warm-up with only **10 min** recovery abolished the ergogenic effect ([Gurton 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33831843/)); the same warm-up with **30 min** recovery did not ([Jones 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34480008/)). You spend the buffer in the warm-up and need time to rebound.

## Stacking

| With | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Beta-alanine** | Additive. 12% (BA) + 7% (SB) → **16% combined** ([Sale 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21407127/)); meta: **d 0.43 vs 0.18** for BA alone ([Saunders 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27797728/)). Mechanistically sensible — intracellular plus extracellular buffering. |
| **Creatine** | Probably additive; a handful of positive studies ([Mero 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15142001/), [Barber 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23254493/)). |
| **Caffeine** | **Unclear.** Only **1 of 8** studies showed additive effects ([Grgic 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32583697/)). |
| **Nitrates** | **Possibly counterproductive** — alkalosis may impair nitrate→NO conversion. One study, no effects for either alone or combined ([Callahan 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27834492/)). |

## Strength of the evidence

**The strongest of the three drink/buffer stands, and the most self-critical document of the set.**

This is a 231-reference review of one of the most-studied ergogenic aids in sport, and it leans on meta-analyses rather than narrative cherry-picking for its central claims: the Wingate null, the muscular endurance effect, the strength null, the rowing and swimming effects, the beta-alanine interaction. Several of those meta-analyses are by the lead author, which is worth knowing, but they are published, quantified and reported with confidence intervals — including the ones that report nothing.

Three things it does that most position stands don't:

- It reports **null and negative findings by name**, including for its own recommendations.
- It devotes a full section to the **placebo effect** and concludes that around **30% of the benefit of buffering agents is placebo-driven** ([Marticorena 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587551/)) — a sham placebo designed to mimic bicarbonate's gut discomfort improved cycling capacity **9.5%** ([Higgins & Shabir 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26863442/)), and a placebo *described* as bicarbonate improved 1000 m running time **1.5%** against the real thing's **1.7%** ([McClung & Collins 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17876973/)). Position point 13 states this plainly.
- It flags that **most studies did not verify blinding**, which given the taste and the gut effects is a real limitation across the whole literature.

Where it is thinner than it sounds:

1. **"Improves exercise performance in both men and women."** True as far as it goes, but only **~20% of studies included women**, and only **11 studies** provided women-only analyses ([Saunders 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33487131/)). The pooled female effect (0.37) is real; the evidence base behind it is a fifth the size.
2. **The lower duration bound in the position statement is stated too generously.** Point 1 says effects are established for tasks "between **30 s** and 12 min," while the review concludes the task "needs to be longer than 30 s" and that a 30-second Wingate shows nothing. Read the bound as **>30 s**.
3. **Minimum dose is inconsistent between sections.** The dose section says 0.15 g/kg "may be the minimal ergogenic dose" (1 of 2 studies); the position statement says 0.2 g/kg. The position statement is the more defensible of the two.
4. **Long-term training adaptation** rests on two positive studies against two null ones, and every one of them is small.
5. **Kidney effects of habitual use are explicitly unknown**, and the stand notes the sodium load at 0.3 g/kg may push habitual users past the Tolerable Upper Intake Level for sodium. That is an open question, not a reassurance.

**On funding and conflicts.** Cleanest of the three stands in this batch, and the disclosure reads as though it was written to be checked. **No funding** was received for the manuscript (Saunders holds unrelated FAPESP and Universidade de São Paulo grants). The **six lead authors — Grgic, Pedisic, Saunders, Artioli, Schoenfeld and McKenna — declare no competing interests at all**, and they are the ones who wrote it. The later authors report the usual sports-nutrition industry ties (Kreider: industry-sponsored studies and consulting; Stout: 25 years of industry-sponsored nutraceutical research; Arent, Bishop, VanDusseldorp: research grants; Lopez and Ziegenfuss: consulting, patents and royalties; Antonio: CEO of the ISSN; Burke: IOC Sports Nutrition Diploma). Two authors — Kalman and Campbell — disclose sitting on Dymatize Nutrition's scientific advisory board **and specifically note that Dymatize was not producing sodium bicarbonate supplements at the time**. That is the right way to disclose. It also reflects the underlying commercial reality: this is baking soda, nobody has a patent on it, and there is very little money riding on the conclusion.
