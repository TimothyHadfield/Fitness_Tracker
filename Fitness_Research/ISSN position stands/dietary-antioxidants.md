# Dietary Antioxidants: Which Ones Help Recovery, and Which Ones Blunt the Adaptation

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2026 Dec 31 · [PMID 41701327](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41701327/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2026.2629828)
**Authors:** Gonzalez DE, Dickerson BL, Roberts BM, Kurtz JA, Waldman HS, Gonzalez AM, et al (12 authors)
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

Exercise generates reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, and that signal is not a side effect to be mopped up — it is a large part of *why* training works. The relationship is an inverted U (hormesis): too little stress and nothing adapts; too much and you get damage, fatigue and immunosuppression. Dietary antioxidants sit on both sides of that curve. Used to correct a genuine deficiency, or short-term during congested competition or an overreaching block, they can speed recovery. Used chronically at high doses, some of them measurably suppress the adaptations you trained for. The stand's practical answer is food first, supplement only to fill a gap or handle a defined high-stress window, and pick compounds that have been shown *not* to blunt adaptation — chiefly creatine, omega-3, tart cherry and astaxanthin.

## Bottom line

- The core mechanism is **hormesis**: moderate ROS drive PGC-1α, mitochondrial biogenesis, MAPK signalling and the upregulation of your own antioxidant enzymes. Blunt the signal and you blunt the adaptation ([Powers 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32380253/), [Powers 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26893258/)).
- The canonical blunting result: **1000 mg/day vitamin C + 400 IU/day vitamin E for 4 weeks** in 39 untrained young men suppressed PGC-1α induction, mitochondrial biogenesis and endogenous antioxidant enzyme expression ([Ristow 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19433800/)). Replicated in trained subjects by [Paulsen 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24492839/).
- **Resveratrol 250 mg/day** for 8 weeks *attenuated* VO₂max and cardiovascular gains in older men doing high-intensity training ([Gliemann 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23878368/)). Not recommended.
- The four compounds the stand ranks top-tier: **creatine 0.1 g/kg/day**, **omega-3 1000–6000 mg/day EPA+DHA for 6–12 weeks**, **tart cherry 480 mg powder or 60–90 mL juice concentrate for 7–14 days**, **astaxanthin 4–12 mg/day for 4–12 weeks** — moderate-to-high evidence, and none shown to interfere with training adaptation.
- **Astaxanthin 12 mg/day for 4 weeks** cut 24 h muscle soreness ≈**57%** and perceived damage ≈**60%** while leaving resistance-training outcomes untouched ([Barker 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40757570/)).
- **NAC works only if you're deficient.** In people with low baseline glutathione, 1.2–2.0 g/day improved VO₂max, time-trial and anaerobic power by **11.4–15.4%**; in those with normal glutathione, nothing ([Paschalis 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29233792/)).
- **NZ blackcurrant** 105–210 mg anthocyanins, 7 days, taken 1–2 h pre-exercise: a small but real **0.45% mean performance effect** (95% CI 0.09–0.81) across 9 studies — comparable to caffeine's 0.41% ([Braakhuis 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32460873/), [Christensen 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28536531/)).
- **Curcumin 180–500 mg/day for 3–7 days** is the evidence-supported window; long-term data are thin ([Suhett 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32282223/)).
- **Green tea EGCG above 800 mg/day carries liver-injury risk** ([EFSA 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32625874/)), and high-dose EGCG taken immediately pre-training disrupts redox signalling. The stand says outright it may not be a good choice for athletes.
- Roughly **1 in 3 people show minimal, no, or even *reductive* stress** after damaging exercise ([Margaritelis 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24634834/)). Blanket antioxidant dosing assumes a problem many individuals do not have.

## The cold-water-immersion parallel

Our existing material establishes that post-training cold water immersion blunts hypertrophy adaptation. This stand describes the same failure mode arriving by a different route. In both cases the intervention is not harmful in itself — it is *effective at removing a stress signal*, and the stress signal is the message the training was sending. CWI suppresses the inflammatory/anabolic signalling cascade; high-dose antioxidants suppress the redox-sensitive one (NF-κB, Nrf2, PGC-1α). The stand states the principle explicitly: exercise-induced ROS "can support adaptation at moderate levels", and "chronic high-dose use may blunt training adaptations."

The practical rule transfers cleanly. Use it when recovery beats adaptation — congested fixture lists, tournament weeks, back-to-back competition, deliberate overreaching, austere environments (altitude, smog). Avoid it during blocks where the adaptation *is* the point.

Be precise about what is implicated, though. The stand does not condemn antioxidants generally:

| Implicated in blunting | Not implicated (evidence of no interference) |
|---|---|
| Vitamin C **1000 mg/day** + vitamin E **400 IU/day**, chronic ([Ristow 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19433800/), [Paulsen 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24492839/)) | Astaxanthin 12 mg/day, 4 wk ([Barker 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40757570/)) |
| Resveratrol **250 mg/day** ([Gliemann 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23878368/)) | Tart cherry concentrate 60 mL/day, 2 wk ([Jackman 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29890270/)) |
| High-dose EGCG immediately pre-workout | Sulforaphane 30 mg/day ([Sato 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34004418/), [Komine 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34927380/)) |
| Chronic high-dose antioxidants generally, per the stand's own conclusion | Creatine, omega-3 |

Two honest complications. First, the vitamin C/E story is not uniform: [Zoppi 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18500971/) gave soccer players **800 mg vitamin E + 1000 mg vitamin C daily across a three-month pre-season** and found reduced lipid peroxidation and muscle damage *without* hindering adaptation, and a review of ten chronic-training trials found only **2 of 10 ergolytic, 2 ergogenic, 6 null** ([Nikolaidis 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22928084/)). The blunting effect is real but not guaranteed. Second, the "does not blunt" evidence for astaxanthin and tart cherry rests on small numbers of short trials — absence of a detected effect in a 2–4 week study is weaker than it sounds.

Sulforaphane is the interesting case mechanistically: it works *indirectly*, by inducing Nrf2 to build up your own antioxidant enzymes, rather than by scavenging radicals directly. That preserves the hormetic signal instead of erasing it, which is why the stand treats it as a non-blunting recovery aid.

## What actually has support

The stand grades each compound High / Moderate / Low on both performance and antioxidant outcomes, using the 2018 ISSN framework ([Kerksick 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30068354/)).

**Worth using:**

- **Tart cherry** — best-evidenced recovery agent here. A meta-analysis of 14 studies found improved strength recovery, reduced DOMS and lower CRP and IL-6, but **no effect on CK or TNF-α** ([Hill 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33440334/)). A separate meta-analysis of 10 studies found endurance performance benefits when taken for 7 days pre-event and up to 1.5 h before ([Gao 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31986108/)). Optimal acute timing is **90 min pre-exercise**, matching peak phenolic metabolite exposure, with benefits larger in trained than recreational subjects ([Wangdi 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39213288/)). Protocols: 200–500 mg powder (66–257 mg anthocyanin), 60–90 mL concentrate (≈550–820 mg anthocyanin), or 300–400 mL juice (≈80 mg anthocyanin).
- **Astaxanthin 4–12 mg/day.** Soreness and CK reductions in soccer players at 4 mg/day over 90 days ([Djordjevic 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22828460/)), antioxidant enzyme increases of ≈17–42% ([Baralic 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23192897/)). Only notable side effect is red-tinted stool from unabsorbed pigment.
- **Omega-3, ≥1000 mg/day mixed EPA+DHA.** 6000 mg/day (2400 EPA + 1800 DHA) for ~7 weeks attenuated CK, LDH and soreness 72 h after eccentric work in trained men ([VanDusseldorp 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32727162/)); a 39-trial meta-analysis in >28,000 people found improved total antioxidant capacity and GPx with reduced MDA ([Heshmati 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31563611/)).
- **Creatine** — included as an indirect antioxidant, but the stand is candid that this is an add-on. Human data are mixed and one report suggests it may act as a *pro*-oxidant. Take creatine for what it actually does.

**Situational:** nitrate from beetroot (**300–600 mg nitrate, 2–3 h pre-exercise**) improves functional recovery — countermovement jump, soreness — but four studies by Clifford and colleagues found **no effect on any oxidative-stress or inflammation biomarker** ([Clifford 2016a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26537365/), [Clifford 2016b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27548212/), [Jones 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34151694/)). Cocoa flavanols **400–500 mg, 2 h pre-exercise** ([Decroix 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29299877/)). Curcumin short-term. Ashwagandha 240–600 mg/day for ≥6 weeks, but mostly studied in untrained people ([Bonilla 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33670194/)).

**Skip:** resveratrol (may blunt), green tea catechin extracts (liver risk, weak benefit), CoQ10 (reliable biomarker changes, no performance effect across a large body of trials), spirulina, quercetin (poor bioavailability, inconsistent), glutathione (poorly absorbed).

## Strength of the evidence

This is a thorough, unusually self-aware document, and its central claim — hormesis, and the risk of blunting adaptation with chronic high-dose supplementation — is the best-supported thing in it. That claim rests on a coherent mechanistic literature plus at least two well-controlled human RCTs with hard outcomes ([Ristow 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19433800/), [Paulsen 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24492839/)). Take it seriously.

Below that, the evidence thins quickly and the "top-tier" framing outruns it in places:

- The **astaxanthin** case is built on small trials, several from the same two research groups (Baralic/Djordjevic in Serbian soccer players; Barker in one 4-week study). The most striking claim in the section — **a 55% increase in barbell squat repetitions** after 6 months of 4 mg/day — comes from a 2008 paper in *Carotenoid Science*, a journal with **no PubMed record and no DOI in the article's own reference list**. That is not a citation you should lean on, and the stand presents it without qualification.
- The **NZ blackcurrant** performance claim ultimately traces to a small literature dominated by one group using one commercial product (CurraNZ™), and one of the cited trials ([Willems 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-11-S1-P14)) is a **conference abstract**, not a full paper. The 0.45% pooled effect is honestly reported with its confidence interval, but the same meta-analysis found no biomarker effects and the authors dismissed that as too few studies — a convenient asymmetry.
- **Creatine's** inclusion as an antioxidant is thin by the stand's own account, and it says so.
- **Ashwagandha, pycnogenol, urolithin A, fucoxanthin, spirulina, selenium and zinc** sections mostly summarise single trials or non-athlete populations. Two of the fucoxanthin trials cited are by authors of this stand (Dickerson, a listed author, on [Dickerson 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38613023/)); so is one of the tactical-athlete curcumin studies ([McAllister 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30252723/), McAllister being a listed author). Self-citation is normal in position stands but worth knowing when a section is short on independent replication.

**Two errors in the published article.** First, the quercetin section cites references [258], [259] and [260] for quercetin's neuromuscular effects and Nrf2 activation — but all three are Emami *et al* papers on **coenzyme Q10 and pre-cooling in swimmers**. The citations are wrong. Second, the footnote to Table 2 contains an **unremoved peer-reviewer comment** in the published text: "The dosing and duration ranges presented for several supplements are extremely broad… I recommend revisiting these values and potentially narrowing them to typical or commonly used ranges in RCTs." The reviewer was right, and nobody acted on it or deleted the note. Treat the dose ranges in that table as the full published span, not as recommendations.

**On funding and competing interests:** set out in the section below. Given that the stand names specific branded products (CurraNZ™, Pycnogenol®, Nigrita®, Mi136), read that section before acting on any single-product recommendation here.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** No funding: "No funding was provided," and the authors state they received no financial remuneration for preparing or reviewing the paper. Three authors — B.L.D. (Dickerson), J.A.K. (Kurtz) and J.L.H. (Heileson) — declare no competing interests. Thirteen others declare research grants or external funding related to dietary supplements. Named roles: B.I.C. (Campbell) is a paid industry consultant, sits on the International Protein Board, and has served as an expert witness for plaintiff and defence in supplement cases; S.M.A. (Arent) holds a patent on a theaflavin-enriched black tea extract; A.M.G. (Gonzalez) is a Scientific Advisor for Shifted LLC, "a manufacturer of sports supplements"; K.A.H. is an employee of AstaReal Inc.; J.A. (Antonio) is CEO of the ISSN and sits on the advisory boards of CREATE, Bear Balanced, Liquid Youth and Enhanced, and "the ISSN receives grants on occasion from companies that manufacture, sell, and market dietary supplements."

**Does any of it bear on the conclusions?** One does, directly. The declaration does not say what AstaReal sells; the company supplies astaxanthin, and astaxanthin is one of the four compounds this stand names as safe to use without blunting adaptation. That placement rests, as the section above sets out, on small trials from two research groups plus a 55%-squat-repetitions figure published in a journal with no PubMed record. An author employed by an astaxanthin supplier does not make the placement wrong, but it is the one recommendation here where a declared tie and a conclusion line up exactly. Arent's black-tea-extract patent is adjacent — polyphenol antioxidants are in scope — though theaflavins are not among the compounds recommended. The rest is category-wide rather than compound-specific: supplement-industry grants, consulting, expert-witness work and the society's own sponsorship bear on the document's general enthusiasm for supplementation, not on any individual ranking.
