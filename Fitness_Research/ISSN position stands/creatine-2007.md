# Creatine: the ISSN's 2007 Stand (superseded by the 2017 edition)

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2007 Aug 30 · [PMID 17908288](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17908288/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-4-6)
**Authors:** Buford TW, Kreider RB, Stout JR, Greenwood M, Campbell B, Spano M, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 2.0 (<http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0>)
**Topic:** Supplements

## The position, in one paragraph

Nine numbered points, written as much to kill myths as to give guidance. Creatine monohydrate is the most effective ergogenic supplement available for high-intensity capacity and lean mass; it is safe; it does not cause renal damage, cramping or dehydration; newer formulations are not better; and it is not unethical to use. The dosing advice is the familiar one — **~0.3 g/kg/day loading, then 3–5 g/day** — but the 2007 text is noticeably more flexible than the 2017 revision about how short the load can be and about cycling. This edition has been **superseded by [Kreider 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/)**; read it for what it says about the *state of the argument* in 2007, and for the two things 2017 quietly dropped.

## Bottom line

- Loading is **~0.3 g/kg/day** (≈5 g four times daily) for **5–7 days**, then **3–5 g/day**. The formal position statement wording is even looser: "at least **3 days**".
- Loading may need only **2–3 days** if creatine is taken **with protein and/or carbohydrate** ([Steenge 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10956365/), [Green 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8944667/)) — advice the 2017 stand removes.
- An alternative dose is **0.25 g/kg fat-free mass/day** ([Burke 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14600563/)).
- No-load works: **3 g/day for 28 days** ([Hultman 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8828669/)), or **6 g/day for 12 weeks** for size and strength ([Willoughby 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11581551/), [Willoughby 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12783039/)).
- **Cycling** — loading doses for 3–5 days every 3–4 weeks — is described here as effective. Stores fall back to baseline in **4–6 weeks** off ([Vandenberghe 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9390981/), [Candow 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15263100/)).
- How much you gain depends on where you start: low-meat eaters gain **20–40%**, people with high baseline stores only **10–20%**.
- **~70%** of several hundred trials found a significant performance improvement; none found an ergolytic effect ([Kreider 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12701815/)).
- Typical effect sizes: max power/strength **+5–15%**, work in sets of maximal effort **+5–15%**, single sprint **+1–5%**, repeated sprints **+5–15%**; long-term training gains **+5–15%** over placebo.
- Body mass rises **1–2 kg in the first week** of loading; over 4–12 weeks creatine users gain roughly **twice the fat-free mass** of placebo — an extra **2–4 lb** ([Stone 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10362452/), [Jones 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10573659/)).
- The only clinically significant side effect in the literature is **weight gain**.

## The carbohydrate/protein question

This is the most concretely useful part of the 2007 document, and it is more specific than 2017's version of the same material. Adding **93 g of carbohydrate to 5 g of creatine increased total muscle creatine by 60%** ([Green 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8944667/)). Adding **47 g carbohydrate + 50 g protein** was as effective as adding **96 g carbohydrate** ([Steenge 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10956365/)). D-pinitol at low doses also raised retention (Greenwood et al. 2001, *J Exerc Physiol Online* — no PubMed or DOI record; a non-indexed paper by the author group). The honest caveat is stated plainly: **several investigations found these combinations no more effective at improving strength, endurance or performance**. Better retention, not necessarily better results.

## Forms and combinations

Creatine phosphate, creatine + sodium bicarbonate, magnesium chelate, glycerol, glutamine, ethyl ester, effervescent and serum forms are all reported as **no better than monohydrate**. Two exceptions were flagged as promising but not recommended: creatine phosphate (one study, more expensive to make) and creatine + glycerol for hyperhydration (one study, no better than creatine alone on thermal responses — [Easton 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17460334/)).

Two combination claims from this edition did not survive:

- **Creatine + β-alanine.** 2007 states that adding β-alanine "may produce greater effects than CM alone" on strength, lean mass and body fat percentage, citing [Stout 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17194255/) and [Hoffman 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17136944/). The ISSN's own [2015 beta-alanine stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26175657/) later reviewed the same studies, noted neither included a beta-alanine-only arm, and concluded there was **no demonstrated synergy**.
- **Creatine + HMB.** Reported as more effective than either alone in one study ([Jówko 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11448573/)), contradicted by others, and correctly judged not to warrant a recommendation.

## Safety, and where the myth came from

The 2007 stand traces the renal panic to a single source: a 1998 case report of falling GFR in a 25-year-old **who already had glomerulosclerosis and steroid-responsive nephrotic syndrome** ([Pritchard & Kalra 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9643752/)), which the French sports paper *L'Équipe* reported three days later as creatine being dangerous to kidneys "in any condition", and which then spread across the European press. Against that: no detrimental effect at 5 days, 14 days, or 10 months to 5 years ([Poortmans & Francaux 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10449011/), [Poortmans 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9404870/), [Poortmans 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16260971/)); and Kreider's football cohort, where nearly *every* athlete looked renally distressed on serum creatinine alone regardless of supplement use ([Kreider 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12701816/)). The stated safe intake is **< 25 g/day in healthy adults**.

Long-term data cited: athletes up to 5 years, infants with creatine synthesis deficiency up to 3 years, and one cohort on **1.5–3 g/day monitored since 1981** for gyrate atrophy ([Sipilä 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7207523/)). Children have been given **4–8 g/day for up to 3 years** as adjunctive therapy, including Duchenne muscular dystrophy trials ([Felber 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10763500/)).

## Children and adolescents — seven conditions

2007 sets **seven** conditions before a young athlete should use creatine, including two that 2017 removes: the athlete must be **past puberty**, and **the parents must approve**. The rest are the familiar four (serious supervised training, good diet, informed use, no exceeding dosages) plus supervision by parents/coach/physician and use of quality supplements.

## Strength of the evidence

**Solid.** The dosing arithmetic, the saturation data, the retention effect of carbohydrate, and the refutation of the renal myth all rest on direct biopsy or clinical-chemistry work and are unchanged a decade later. The 5–15% effect-size range is a fair summary of a large literature.

**Thin or self-referential.** Several load-bearing citations are not indexed primary trials: the loading protocol itself is sourced to a **book** (*Creatine: The Power Supplement*) and a **book chapter**, both by members of this author group; the D-pinitol and creatine-retention comparisons are in a non-PubMed-indexed online journal edited within the same network; and the "20–40% vs 10–20%" responder split is sourced to a textbook chapter, not a study. None of these are wrong, but the reader cannot check them, and the pattern — society position stand citing its own members' non-peer-reviewed work for its central dosing advice — is worth noticing.

**Overreach.** The closing argument that banning creatine "would be unethical" because creatine reduces musculoskeletal injuries, heat stress and provides neuroprotection stretches its evidence. The injury claim rests on non-randomised observation in college football programmes run by the authors ([Greenwood 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12701814/), [Greenwood 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14608430/)). More pointedly, that sentence cites **[Tyler 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14977662/)** among the injury-reduction references — a trial the 2017 stand accurately describes as finding that creatine **did not** significantly affect strength or functional capacity after ACL reconstruction. Either that is a misattribution or it is a very loose reading of a null result.

**Missing.** As with 2017: nothing on non-responders, nothing on DHT or hair loss, nothing on caffeine interaction. Position statement point 8 is a summary of US dietary-supplement *law* (DSHEA), which is a strange thing to find in a scientific consensus document and was dropped from the 2017 edition.

**Conflicts of interest.** The extract we hold carries no competing-interests statement. Overlapping authors (Kreider, Stout, Ziegenfuss, Lopez, Kalman) disclosed substantial supplement-industry ties in the ISSN's [2013 HMB stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23374455/) six years later.

## Disclosures and funding

**No competing-interests or funding statement is retrievable for this article at all** — not from the PubMed Central record and not from the captured full text, which runs from the abstract to the conclusion and stops. This is common for the older BMC-era papers in this corpus, and its absence should not be read as an absence of ties: overlapping authors (Kreider, Stout, Ziegenfuss, Lopez, Kalman) declared substantial supplement-industry relationships in the ISSN's [2013 HMB stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23374455/) six years later, and Kreider's own [2017 creatine update](creatine-2017.md) discloses that it was prepared at the request of, and with support from, a supplement-industry trade association.

**What to look at instead.** The pattern this document does make visible is self-reference, set out in the section above: the loading protocol is sourced to a book and a book chapter by members of this author group, the retention comparisons to a non-PubMed-indexed online journal edited within the same network, the responder split to a textbook chapter, and the injury-reduction argument to non-randomised observation in college football programmes the authors ran. That is a stand citing its own members' unreviewable work for its central dosing advice, and it is checkable from the reference list without any declaration at all.
