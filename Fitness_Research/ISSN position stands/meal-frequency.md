# Meal Frequency: Does It Matter How Many Times a Day You Eat?

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2011 Mar 16 · [PMID 21410984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21410984/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-8-4)
**Authors:** La Bounty PM, Campbell BI, Wilson J, Galvan E, Berardi J, Kleiner SM, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 2.0 (<http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0>)
**Topic:** Body composition

## The position, in one paragraph

Eating more often does not raise metabolic rate, does not increase the thermic effect of food, and does not by itself cause fat loss. The stand says this plainly and repeatedly. What it does claim is a set of secondary benefits that appear when calories are restricted: less hunger, better cholesterol and insulin numbers, and possibly less lean mass loss. For athletes specifically it argues — on very thin data — that higher meal frequency might help preserve muscle during a cut and might help partition nutrients better than in sedentary people. This is one of the older and thinner ISSN stands. Much of the body-composition section rests on cross-sectional epidemiology from the 1960s and 70s, and the athlete section rests on one small trial and one conference abstract.

## Bottom line

- Increased meal frequency does **not** raise resting metabolic rate or 24-hour energy expenditure. Five chamber-calorimetry studies comparing patterns from one to seven feedings all found nothing.
- It does **not** increase the thermic effect of food. One study found the *opposite* — a single large meal raised TEF more than six smaller ones ([Tai 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1951147/)).
- Under matched calories, weight and fat loss are the **same** whether you eat 1, 2, 3, 6 or 9 times a day, in both obese and normal-weight people.
- The classic inverse correlation between eating frequency and body fat is **observational** and mostly disappears once you adjust for under-reporting, restrained eating or physical activity ([Duval 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18996853/), [Summerbell 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8863011/), [Crawley 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9043972/)).
- **Cholesterol and insulin do improve.** In EPIC-Norfolk (n = 14,666), total and LDL cholesterol were **~5% lower** in people eating more than six times a day versus once or twice, after adjustment ([Titan 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11731392/)). 17 snacks versus 3 meals cut serum insulin **27.9%** ([Jenkins 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2674713/)).
- **One meal a day is actively worse.** Eight weeks of one-meal-a-day at maintenance calories raised blood pressure, total cholesterol and LDL, and increased hunger, versus three meals ([Stote 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17413096/)).
- **Hunger falls** with more frequent eating. A single pre-load meal led to **27% (~358 kcal) more** eaten at the next ad libitum meal than five smaller pre-loads of the same total energy — despite no difference in *subjective* hunger ratings ([Speechly 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10578205/)).
- **Protein per meal beats meals per day.** Nitrogen loss depended more on protein content (15% vs 10% of calories) than on 5 versus 1 meals ([Garrow 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7470437/)). Per-meal MPS appears saturated around **20–30 g protein / 10–15 g EAA** ([Moore 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19056590/), [Bohe 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12909668/)).
- **The one athlete trial:** boxers on 1200 kcal/day for two weeks lost the same weight on 2 or 6 meals, but the 6-meal group lost **less lean body mass** and had lower urinary 3-methylhistidine ([Iwao 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8960647/)).

## Where the "eat more often" idea came from, and why it doesn't hold

The origin is a small cluster of mid-century observational studies. [Fabry 1964](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14186149/) and [Hejda and Fabry 1964](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14209568/) reported skinfold thickness inversely related to meal frequency; [Metzner 1977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/855813/) found the same across 2,000 adults aged 35–60 in the Tecumseh study. Later work added [Ruidavets 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12439650/), [Ma 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12835290/) and [Drummond 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9504318/).

The stand then dismantles them, correctly. Three problems:

1. **Under-reporting.** People misreport intake, and overweight and obese people misreport more ([Bandini 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2393004/), [Heitmann 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7580640/), [Schoeller 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2200586/)). If heavier people also skip meals, frequency and adiposity correlate for reasons that have nothing to do with metabolism.
2. **Reverse causality.** Someone gaining weight may start skipping meals *because* they are gaining weight, which makes low frequency look causal ([Bellisle 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9155494/)).
3. **Confounding that survives adjustment.** Several inverse relationships vanished once investigators adjusted for under-reporters, restrained eaters, or physical activity — the last being the whole of [Duval 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18996853/)'s point. [Ruidavets 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12439650/) is the one that survived, which is why the stand does not dismiss the literature outright.

## The controlled trials

Under matched (and usually hypocaloric) intake, meal frequency does nothing to body composition. This has been tested from one meal a day up to nine, in obese and normal-weight subjects, since 1966 ([Bortz 1966](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5903121/), [Finkelstein and Fryer 1971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5090633/), [Young 1971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5116179/), [Garrow 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7470437/), [Verboeket-van de Venne 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8383639/)). The cleanest modern test is [Cameron 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19943985/): 16 obese adults, **−700 kcal/day for 8 weeks**, three meals versus three meals plus three snacks. Both lost **~5% of body weight** with identical changes in fat mass, lean mass and BMI, and no difference in hunger, fullness, ghrelin or PYY.

The single exception in the literature is [Fabry 1966](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4951520/) in schoolchildren, where three meals produced greater skinfold gains than five or seven — but only in the 10–16 year olds, not the 6–11 year olds.

## Metabolism: no effect, and the theory behind it was wrong

The "grazing stokes the metabolic furnace" idea does not survive contact with calorimetry.

| Measure | Finding |
|---|---|
| Thermic effect of food | No difference between 1 and 2 meals ([Kinabo 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2387273/)); **one large meal raised TEF more** than six ([Tai 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1951147/)) or three ([Molnar 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1484710/)); one study found the reverse ([LeBlanc 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8180882/)) |
| 24-h DIT, 2 vs 3 meals | No difference over 36 h in a respiration chamber, but **24-h fat oxidation was higher and carbohydrate oxidation lower** on 3 meals ([Smeets 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18053311/)) |
| RMR / 24-h energy expenditure | Null in every chamber or metabolic-unit study, across 1 vs 3 vs 5, 2 vs 3–5, 2 vs 7, and 2 vs 6 feedings ([Verboeket-van de Venne 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1905998/), [1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8399092/), [Taylor and Garrow 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11319656/), [Garrow 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7470437/)) |

This is the most solid conclusion in the document, and it is a negative one.

## Blood markers: the strongest positive case

More frequent eating consistently improves lipids and insulin. [Titan 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11731392/) is the largest dataset — 6,890 men and 7,776 women aged 45–75 in EPIC-Norfolk, with total and LDL cholesterol **~5% lower** at >6 eating occasions/day versus 1–2, after adjusting for obesity, age, activity and diet. [Edelstein 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1550041/) found the same pattern in 2,034 adults aged 50–89. [Jenkins 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2674713/) is the best controlled version: 17 snacks versus 3 isocaloric meals, no change in glucose but **insulin down 27.9%**.

Both large studies are cross-sectional, [Mann 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9155496/)'s review notes the positive intervention studies were all short, and three trials found no lipid benefit at all ([Jagannathan 1964](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14206197/), [Irwin and Feeley 1967](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6051146/), [Finkelstein and Fryer 1971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5090633/)).

## Protein, nitrogen balance, and the part that aged best

This section is the most useful for a lifter, and it is where the stand anticipates the protein-distribution work that later became mainstream.

Nitrogen balance results are mixed and mostly null. [Garrow 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7470437/) found less nitrogen loss on five meals than one, but also found that raising protein from 10% to 15% of calories mattered more, and concluded protein content beats meal frequency for preserving lean tissue. [Irwin and Feeley 1967](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6051146/) (3 vs 6 meals), [Finkelstein and Fryer 1971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5090633/) (3 vs 6, 60 days) and [Young 1971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5116179/) (1 vs 3 vs 6, 14 weeks) all found no difference.

The stand then makes the argument that matters: **nitrogen balance is whole-body protein flux, not muscle.** And in a fixed-calorie design, spreading intake over more meals *shrinks the protein bolus per meal*, potentially dropping each one below the threshold that maximally stimulates MPS — around **20–30 g protein or 10–15 g EAA** ([Moore 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19056590/), [Bohe 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12909668/), [Dangin 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12665610/)). Irwin compared three ~20 g protein meals to six ~10 g meals; on this reading it was rigged against the higher frequency. The counter-design is [Paddon-Jones 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15572657/): three 850 kcal meals **plus** three 180 kcal snacks containing 15 g EAA gave **~23% greater** mixed muscle protein synthesis than the three meals alone — added protein at added frequency, not frequency alone, but the right shape of experiment.

The stand also notes the typical US pattern is skewed — roughly **10–14 g protein at breakfast** against **29–42 g at dinner** — so MPS is likely maximised once a day. Its supporting even-distribution experiment is a rodent study published as a FASEB abstract (Wilson et al. 2010, no PMID or DOI), which is very weak support for a claim it leans on.

## Athletes

The honest summary of this section is that it contains three data points.

1. **[Iwao 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8960647/)** — boxers, 1200 kcal/day for two weeks, 2 versus 6 meals. Same weight loss; the 6-meal group lost **less lean mass** and had lower urinary 3-methylhistidine, suggesting an anti-catabolic effect of spreading intake during a hard cut. This is the only controlled athlete trial in the document.
2. **[Deutz 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10731010/)** — elite female gymnasts and runners, hour-by-hour energy balance. Across all athletes, an ~**800 kcal daily deficit**, with **7.5 hours per day** spent more than 300 kcal in deficit against only **~3 hours** more than 300 kcal in surplus. Hours in deficit correlated positively with body fat percentage. The stand states outright that the method "is not fully validated," and the study did not measure meal frequency at all.
3. **Benardot 2005** — a conference abstract (no PMID or DOI). 60 college athletes given three 250 kcal between-meal snacks for two weeks lost **1.03% body fat**, gained **1.2 kg lean mass** and improved 30-second Wingate power, while total daily intake rose only **128 kcal** because they ate less at meals. Values returned to baseline four weeks after the snacks stopped.

The stand builds its "athletes may be the population where meal frequency actually helps" hypothesis on these, and is upfront that no experimental study has tested why. Observationally, endurance athletes eat **5–10 times a day** anyway ([Burke 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1844570/), [Hawley 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1777785/), [Lindeman 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2365944/)).

## Strength of the evidence

**This is a thin document, and much thinner than its confident section headings suggest.** Three things are worth being explicit about.

**First, how much of it is epidemiology.** The entire "Body Weight and Body Composition" case — the reason anyone reads a meal frequency paper — opens with two tables of cross-sectional and prospective studies and no trials. The stand deserves credit for then arguing *against* that evidence rather than for it, but the structure means most of the citations in the section that matters most are observational, self-reported, and in several cases sixty years old. The dietary intake data underpinning them come from food-frequency questionnaires and recall, methods the stand itself spends four paragraphs discrediting.

**Second, how old and small the controlled trials are.** The experimental body-composition literature is 1966, 1971 (×2), 1981, 1993, and then Cameron in 2010. The metabolic chamber studies are 1991–2001. Sample sizes are frequently in single digits — Gwinup's foundational lipid work used **five hospitalised subjects** with, as the stand notes, "no statistical analyses made between or amongst the participants" ([Gwinup 1963](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14067443/)).

**Third, the athlete recommendations are extrapolations, and the stand admits it every time.** Each "Application to Nutritional Practices of Athletes" box opens with a variant of "athletic and physically active populations have not been independently studied in this domain," then makes a recommendation anyway. The blood-markers box goes furthest, concluding that "increasing meal frequency in athletic populations is warranted" purely by transferring findings from sedentary 45–89 year olds. That is not a defensible inference for a training athlete's diet.

**One internal inconsistency.** The conclusion lists "decrease nitrogen loss" among the benefits of higher frequency under energy restriction. That rests on a single arm of [Garrow 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7470437/) and is contradicted by three other nitrogen-balance studies cited in the same section — a fact the stand states in its own text and then does not carry into the conclusion.

**What is actually well supported.** Two things. Meal frequency does not change energy expenditure or the thermic effect of food — negative, but firmly established by chamber calorimetry. And extreme low frequency (one meal a day) worsens blood pressure, lipids and hunger ([Stote 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17413096/)).

**Practical read.** Eat often enough to hit your protein target in doses that actually stimulate MPS, and often enough to manage hunger during a deficit. Beyond that the number of meals is a scheduling preference, not a lever. The ISSN's own 2017 nutrient timing stand reached the same place and noted that the later meta-analysis suggesting a frequency benefit ([Schoenfeld 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26024494/)) collapsed to a single confounding study.

**Disclosure.** The authors declare no competing interests.

## Disclosures and funding

**No separate disclosure record for this article came back from PubMed Central** — common for the older BMC-era papers in this corpus, where such statements sit in the article body rather than in a tagged section. The body does carry one, and it is a single sentence: "The authors declare that they have no competing interests." There is no funding statement of any kind, and no company, grant or role is named anywhere in the paper. A blanket line on a document of this vintage is not evidence that no ties existed; several of these authors declare extensive supplement-industry relationships on later ISSN stands in this library.

**Does any of it bear on the conclusions?** Nothing declared does, and it would be a mistake to go looking for the problem there. What weakens this stand is inference, not interest: a body-composition case opening with two tables of cross-sectional epidemiology, controlled trials from 1966 to 1981 with single-digit samples, and athlete recommendations that each begin by conceding athletes have not been studied in the domain and then make a recommendation anyway. No disclosure statement would have caught any of that. Read the "Application to Nutritional Practices of Athletes" boxes with that in view rather than with the author list in view.
