# Nutritional Considerations for Single-Stage Ultra-Marathon Training and Racing

**Source:** ISSN position stand, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2019 Nov 7 · [PMID 31699159](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31699159/) · [doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-019-0312-9)
**Authors:** Tiller NB, Roberts JD, Beasley L, Chapman S, Pinto JM, Smith L, et al
**Licence:** CC BY 4.0 — <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/>
**Topic:** Populations

## The position, in one paragraph

An ultra-marathon is anything beyond 26.2 miles; this stand covers single-stage races up to **152 miles (245 km)**, typically lasting **6–48 hours**. The defining fact is that you cannot eat as much as you burn — a 70 kg runner covering 100 miles expends roughly **9,900 kcal** and will realistically take in a fraction of it. So the whole nutritional problem becomes: how large a deficit can you carry while still finishing, and what stops you from finishing first — glycogen depletion, dehydration, hyponatremia, or a gut that has stopped accepting food. The stand's answer is that **race completion tracks energy and fluid intake**, that gastrointestinal distress is the binding constraint on both, and that the gut is trainable. Uniquely among ISSN stands, every recommendation carries an explicit evidence grade (A through D), and the authors state plainly that they included studies "irrespective of any methodological concerns."

## Bottom line

- **Race intake: 150–300 kcal/h up to 50 miles; 200–400 kcal/h beyond.** Persistent intakes **below 200 kcal/h** in long races predicted non-completion ([Stuempfle 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22331688/)).
- Finishers of a 100-miler ate nearly **double the carbohydrate per kg per hour** of non-finishers (**0.98 vs 0.56 g/kg/h**) and **5× the total fat** (98 g vs 19 g) ([Stuempfle 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22331688/)).
- In-race carbohydrate target **30–50 g/h** — deliberately below the 90 g/h endurance ceiling, because 90 g/h means a race diet that is essentially nothing but carbohydrate and invites malabsorption ([Jeukendrup 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20574242/), [Costa 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30056753/)).
- Fluid **450–750 mL/h**, or 150–250 mL every 20 minutes; sodium **500–700 mg/L**, rising to **300–600 mg/h** in heat above 25 °C / humidity above 60% ([Sawka 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/)).
- **Post-training rehydration needs 150% of the fluid lost**, with at least **460 mg/L sodium** — plain water at 100% of losses does not restore balance ([Shirreffs 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8897383/), [Mitchell 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7808248/)).
- **Training-day macros: 60% carbohydrate (7–10 g/kg/day), 15% protein (1.3–2.1 g/kg/day), 25% fat (1.0–1.5 g/kg/day)**, total **~38–63 kcal/kg/day**.
- Protein **1.6–2.1 g/kg/day (0.7–0.95 g/lb)**; below 1.6 risks negative nitrogen balance in high-mileage endurance athletes ([Kato 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27322029/)). Up to **2.5 g/kg/day** when mileage or carbohydrate restriction is extreme.
- **~20 g protein every 3 waking hours** beats 10 g every 1.5 h or 40 g every 6 h ([Areta 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23459753/)).
- **GI distress affects 30–50% of ultra runners** on conservative estimates; in a 272-runner cohort at Western States, **96% had symptoms** and **44%** said it hurt their race ([Stuempfle 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25716739/)).
- **Caffeine: 1–2 mg/kg repeat doses (~50 mg/h)**, saved for the late stages and night running — not the 4–6 mg/kg bolus used in short events.
- **Do not take NSAIDs.** Up to **60–70%** of ultra runners use them; efficacy is equivocal, renal, GI and hyponatremia risks are real, and **93%** of endurance runners are unaware of any contraindication ([Kuster 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23604350/), [Warden 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20424410/)).
- Ketogenic diets: **insufficient evidence** for performance, despite a large substrate shift (fat oxidation **1.2 vs 0.75 g/min**) ([Volek 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26892521/)).

## The energy problem, quantified

| Scenario | Expenditure | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| 50 kg runner, 50 miles at 8.0 km/h (~10 h) | ~3,460 kcal | ~346 kcal/h |
| 70 kg runner, 50 miles, same pace | ~4,845 kcal | ~484 kcal/h |
| 50 kg runner, 100 miles at 6.5 km/h (~25 h) | ~6,922 kcal | ~277 kcal/h |
| 70 kg runner, 100 miles, same pace | ~9,891 kcal | ~395 kcal/h |

Running costs roughly **47–72 kcal/km** ([Applegate 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1844990/)). Observed race intake sits far lower: a mean of **183 kcal/h** across 213 runners at 44–112 km ([Martinez 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28322630/)), and **333 ± 105 kcal/h** in elites over sixteen 100-mile races ([Stellingwerff 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26061831/)). Some ultra-endurance athletes take in only **36–53%** of expenditure.

One counterintuitive advantage of going long: gastric emptying is only meaningfully impaired above **~70% VO2max**, and ultra pace sits well below that, so runners tolerate more food and more calorie-dense food than in a marathon. Carbohydrate-load at **~10 g/kg/day** in the 48 h before the race ([Thomas 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26920240/)).

## The gut is the real limiter

This is the section with the most transfer value to ordinary training.

- **Prevalence:** 30–50% conservatively ([de Oliveira 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24791919/)), with reports of 70–80% ([Riddoch 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3167507/)). At Western States, 96% of 272 runners had symptoms and **nausea was the most disruptive, in 60%** ([Stuempfle 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25716739/)).
- **Mechanism:** reduced mesenteric blood flow → gut hypoperfusion → tight-junction disruption → lipopolysaccharide leakage and endotoxin-driven symptoms. **81% of runners needing medical attention** after the 90 km Comrades had LPS above 100 pg/mL ([Brock-Utne 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3375945/)); an Ironman cohort showed a biphasic endotoxin response in **68%** ([Jeukendrup 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10600658/)).
- **The trap:** the runners with upper-GI symptoms were the ones eating *more* ([Glace 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12500985/)). The intake that gets you to the finish is the intake that makes you sick. Hyperosmolar solutions (**>500 mOsm/L, >8% carbohydrate**) are the main nutritional cause.
- **What to do:** train the gut in advance — the stand's category B recommendation and its most generalisable one ([Jeukendrup 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28332114/)). Use multiple transportable carbohydrates (glucose + fructose) to raise absorption and lower discomfort ([Jeukendrup 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20574242/)). Avoid NSAIDs and limit saturated fat pre-race ([Guy 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29910316/)). A short low-FODMAP period may help symptomatic individuals ([Wiffin 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30646926/)). Four weeks of a multi-strain *Lactobacillus*/*Bifidobacterium* probiotic reduced GI symptoms during a marathon ([Pugh 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30982100/)) and cut faecal zonulin ~25% ([Lamprecht 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22992437/)), though the stand labels the ultra evidence limited.
- **When nausea hits:** slow down and drop toward the low end of intake, but keep eating. If the nausea is hypoglycaemic, eating is what relieves it — and persistent sub-200 kcal/h in a long race is how people DNF.

## Hydration and the hyponatremia trap

Two failure modes pull in opposite directions and the stand refuses to let readers pick one.

**Dehydration.** At marathon distance, abstaining from fluid gave **3.3% and 5.3%** body mass loss in cool and warm conditions, while drinking 1 L/h held losses to 0.3–1.7%. Consequences: cardiovascular drift, reduced capacity, impaired cognition — the last mattering acutely on technical terrain or courses requiring navigation.

**Exercise-associated hyponatremia** (serum sodium <135 mmol/L) is potentially fatal and was first described in ultra runners and Ironman athletes ([Hew-Butler 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28316971/)). Sodium intake alone does not protect you if fluid intake is excessive ([Hew-Butler 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26102445/)). The stand is pointed about the common bad workaround: runners drink *less* rather than salting more ([Winger 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23412802/)), a practice it says "must be challenged."

Practical: measure your sweat rate by nude body mass before and after a session in race-like conditions. In-race, **450–750 mL/h** with **500–700 mg sodium/L**. Keep drink sodium under **~1000 mg/L** or palatability collapses and people stop drinking — hence swallowable capsules over effervescent tablets. Many commercial electrolyte products simply don't contain enough sodium. Away from key sessions, **drink to thirst** is adequate (category B/C), and the stand takes a swipe at "flushing the kidneys" as pseudoscience.

## Train-low, and the ketogenic question

**Train-low, compete-high** — periodically training with lowered (not depleted) glycogen to drive AMPK signalling and mitochondrial biogenesis ([Baar 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461390801919094)) — is endorsed cautiously. Twice-daily training every second day raised total work and time to exhaustion after resynthesis ([Hansen 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15361516/)). Liver glycogen falls up to **80%** overnight, so a pre-breakfast easy run is the simplest implementation. The caveats carry into any training context: chronic depletion raises cortisol and disturbs immune function ([Gleeson 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9534081/)), high-intensity work suffers, and the endpoint is low energy availability and RED-S ([Statuta 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28684389/)). The phrasing is precise: **moderation, not restriction.**

**Ketogenic diets** get a clean B/C verdict of "insufficient evidence." Keto-adapted ultra runners oxidised fat at **~1.2 g/min** against **~0.75 g/min** on high carbohydrate ([Volek 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26892521/)) — a large, real substrate shift with no demonstrated performance payoff. Against it: downregulated pyruvate dehydrogenase and worse oxygen economy at higher intensities ([Zinn 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28706467/)), plus reduced fibre and micronutrients. Notably Volek's own data showed the shift at a **less strict 80 g/day**, so even proponents' evidence doesn't require the strict version.

## Palatability, and why savoury wins late

By the late stages of long races runners reliably turn against sweet foods — gels and drinks — toward savoury, salty, higher-fat ones ([Glace 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12500985/)). This happens in thermoneutral conditions too, so it isn't just heat; the authors speculate it reflects real electrolyte and energy inadequacy. Two consequences: fat-dense savoury foods carry more calories per gram of pack weight *and* tend to carry sodium; and runners should carry their own food rather than trusting checkpoint stock. The stand also asks race organisers to publish feed-station menus in advance and serve at least one hot calorie-dense meal in races over 50 miles.

Its food table gives per-hour targets: **energy ~150–400 kcal/h, carbohydrate 30–50 g/h, protein 5–10 g/h, fat 1.1–17.7 g/h, fluid 450–750 mL/h, sodium >575 mg/L**. The protein-during-racing recommendation is unusual and rests largely on 100-mile finishers eating **131 g vs 43 g** total protein against non-finishers ([Stuempfle 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22331688/)).

## Supplements and drugs

- **Caffeine (category A).** Elites at a 100-miler reported **~912 ± 322 mg total** across 15–19 h ([Stellingwerff 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26061831/)). Dose response is non-linear ([Graham 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775331/)) and caffeine works taken late in endurance exercise ([Cox 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12183495/)). So: **1–2 mg/kg repeat doses, ~50 mg/h, reserved for night running.** Response varies by CYP1A2 genotype ([Womack 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22420682/)).
- **Ketone esters.** 500 mg/kg raises β-hydroxybutyrate to ~3 mmol/L and improved performance **2%** ([Cox 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27475046/)) — but in exercise under 120 min, and repeatedly refuted since, including a ketone diester *impairing* time trial performance in professional cyclists ([Leckey 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29109686/)). No ultra-specific evidence.
- **MCTs.** Must stay under **30 g** to avoid GI distress, and at that dose the effect is negligible ([Misell 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11447364/)).
- **Vitamins and minerals.** The single ultra trial found no performance difference ([Knechtle 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18586636/)). Vitamin C evidence conflicts: null at 1500 mg/day for 7 days ([Nieman 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11960947/)), but 600 mg/day cut post-race respiratory infection at Comrades ([Peters 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8185726/)), and 7 weeks of α-tocopherol + ascorbic acid protected against lipid peroxidation ([Mastaloudis 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15110397/)). **Chronic daily antioxidant use is discouraged** — it blunts adaptation ([Peternelj 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22060178/)).
- **L-glutamine.** Not recommended for immune support ([Gleeson 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18806122/)), but dose-dependently reduces gut permeability at **0.25–0.9 g/kg fat-free mass** pre-exercise in heat ([Pugh 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29058112/)).
- **NSAIDs — strongly discouraged.** GI bleeding risk is already elevated in distance runners ([Halvorsen 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3487825/)), renal risk is real ([Boulter 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22273028/)), and a causative role in hyponatremia has been raised. Paracetamol is better tolerated but masks pain signals indicating tissue damage. Separately, up to **15%** of legal supplements are contaminated with banned substances ([Geyer 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18563865/)).

## What generalises, and what doesn't

**Generalises to ordinary training.** Gut training — carbohydrate tolerance is trainable, and you rehearse fuelling in training — applies to anyone doing long sessions. Post-session rehydration at **150% of fluid lost, with sodium**, estimated by pre/post body mass: cheap, universal, and most people get it wrong. Protein at **~20 g every 3 hours** beats front- or back-loading, the same conclusion the hypertrophy literature reaches by another route. The train-low framing — moderate carbohydrate around easy sessions, fuel hard ones properly — transfers to any periodised programme. So do the warnings against chronic antioxidant megadosing and prophylactic NSAIDs.

**Does not generalise.** **30–50 g/h carbohydrate is a downward revision specific to ultra duration**, not a general endurance ceiling — under a few hours, 60–90 g/h still stands, so don't import the low number into a marathon plan. Deliberate **caloric deficits during the event** are unavoidable in a 25-hour race and bad practice anywhere else. **Sodium at 500–700 mg/L** is calibrated for multi-hour sweat losses. The **savoury food preference** is a phenomenon of 6+ hour efforts. And the muscle damage markers — **creatine kinase 4,970 U/L after 308 km against 274 U/L post-marathon** ([Shin 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27196469/)) — describe a stimulus no ordinary programme produces.

## Strength of the evidence

The grading system is this stand's best feature. It uses NHLBI categories ([NIH 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9813653/)) with a fourth level for case reports, applied per statement. The distribution is telling:

- **Category A** appears exactly once, on caffeine.
- **Category A/B** covers hydration monitoring and the 150% rehydration rule — both borrowed from the broader hydration literature, not ultra-specific work.
- **Category B** covers gut training, protein feeding frequency, and the moderate train-low position.
- **Category C** covers the entire in-race fuelling protocol: the **150–400 kcal/h**, the **30–50 g/h carbohydrate**, the **5–10 g/h protein**, the **450–750 mL/h fluid**. These are the numbers most readers will actually use, and they rest on observational field studies of finishers versus non-finishers.
- **Category D** — a single case-report-grade statement — covers the savoury food preference.

The finisher-versus-non-finisher design deserves a flag the stand does not fully raise. Finishers ate more, yes, but the causal arrow is ambiguous: people already failing — nauseated, cramping, walking — eat less *because* they are failing. The stand acknowledges this once ("GI distress and a lack of appetite in non-finishers may explain their lower overall intake") and then continues to present intake as a determinant of completion.

The authors also state up front that they included every relevant study "irrespective of any methodological concerns... (e.g., low sample sizes, short study durations, lack of randomization, lack of control measures, and other biases)." Defensible in a sport with tiny participant numbers and remote races, but it means several numbers come from single small field studies. One fat-oxidation figure (1.0–1.5 g/min after 6 h of running) comes from **a single subject**.

Where it is admirably restrained: it does not oversell ketogenic diets, declines to recommend MCTs, ketone esters, glutamine or multivitamins, and its NSAID position is firm and well-argued. It is also one of the few sports nutrition documents assigning responsibilities to **race organisers** — publish menus, provide protein and hot food, discourage NSAIDs — rather than putting everything on the athlete.

## Disclosures and funding

**What the document declares.** Very little, and what there is is clean. "No funding was received for the development of this manuscript." The competing interests section reads in full: "The authors declare that they have no competing interests." One line for a long author list, with no exceptions and no named companies — unusual for an ISSN stand, and unusual for a list that includes several authors who declare extensive supplement-industry ties on other documents in this series.

**Does any of it bear on the conclusions?** There is nothing declared that could. The stand recommends no branded product, declines to endorse MCTs, ketone esters, glutamine or multivitamins, and its most commercially useful available conclusion — that ultra-endurance athletes should buy more things — is the one it does not reach. It also puts obligations on race organisers rather than on athlete spending.

**One non-financial interest is declared, in an unexpected place.** The Authors' information note states that both corresponding authors "are accomplished ultra-endurance competitors," offered as a credential: their "nuanced appreciation of the physiological demands of the sport, enables them to make recommendations that are both evidence-based and pragmatic." It is a fair credential. It is also an interest, and it points the same way as the document's weakest inference — the Category C in-race fuelling numbers drawn from finisher-versus-non-finisher field data, where practitioner intuition is carrying weight the evidence grade does not.
