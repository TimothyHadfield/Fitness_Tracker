# Collagen Supplements: A Low-Quality Protein Sold At A High Price

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Does collagen live up to the hype? [New research 2025]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rmsQdP7QbJk)) · 2025-08-12 · 8 min
**Topic:** Supplements

## Bottom line
- Three recent independent studies found nothing: **30 g** hydrolysed collagen added no myofibrillar, connective-tissue, skin or whole-body collagen synthesis over training alone; **30 g/day** across a week of training including calisthenics did nothing; and **15 g/day** bioactive collagen peptides for **15 weeks** failed to improve patellar tendon size, stiffness, strain or any mechanical property.
- **Collagen is a terrible protein source** — protein score literally zero, and most research finds it fails to stimulate muscle protein synthesis at all.
- Independent studies finding acute collagen-synthesis benefits are **confounded by co-administered vitamin C**, which stimulates collagen production on its own.
- Ingested collagen is broken down to amino acids and peptides like any protein — it does not travel intact into your tendons.
- **The one exception he grants unreservedly:** undenatured type II collagen for **osteoarthritis**, which reduces joint pain. He argues it doesn't generalise to healthy lifters, whose injuries are usually tendon rather than cartilage.
- For hair the mechanism doesn't exist: hair is not connective tissue and contains no collagen.

## What the new research shows
The three *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* studies cover different windows:

- **Acute** (Aussieker): six sets of leg press and leg extension raised myofibrillar and connective tissue protein synthesis as expected — 30 g hydrolysed collagen added nothing on top, and nothing to skin protein synthesis or whole-body collagen turnover markers.
- **Short-term** (Kirmse): 30 g/day across a week including calisthenics — which loads connective tissue heavily and should be near-ideal for collagen production — showed no effect.
- **Long-term** (Balshaw): 15 g/day across 15 weeks of serious lower-body training produced no tendon adaptation benefit on any measure.

## The industry-sponsorship argument
The sharpest part of the video. The older studies showing collagen reduces joint pain were sponsored by **Gelita AG**, whose roughly billion-dollar revenue comes from collagen. His test of their credibility isn't the joint-pain data but a side finding from the same labs: industry-funded research reports 15 g of collagen builds more muscle than the same amount of protein from any other source. Since collagen is independently established as near-useless for hypertrophy, that finding isn't plausible — and he treats it as grounds for discounting everything else from those labs.

The counterweight: the only independent study in healthy people with joint pain found **10 g hydrolysed collagen for 12 weeks** had no effect on knee performance, knee pain, or inflammatory cartilage/bone biomarkers. He states his position as suspicion rather than proof — the research "does not fully debunk" collagen for joint pain, but five industry studies against one independent one is a movie he's seen before.

## Why the mechanism is weaker than it sounds
Collagen is just a protein source: digested into amino acids and short peptides, then reassembled, at which point the origin is irrelevant. Other protein sources raise collagen precursor amino acids just as well, and collagen supplementation works better in people with **low protein intakes** — consistent with a protein-adequacy effect rather than a collagen effect.

The steelman he offers then dismantles: collagen is unusually high in **glycine and hydroxyproline**, conditionally essential amino acids an otherwise-optimal protein intake might not cover. But if that mattered, protein-intake research should show it in lean body mass or muscle protein synthesis — and it doesn't.

**Verdict:** eat gelatin if you like it, but as a supplement collagen is expensive low-quality protein. He'd want a trial controlling for both protein intake and vitamin C before changing his mind.
