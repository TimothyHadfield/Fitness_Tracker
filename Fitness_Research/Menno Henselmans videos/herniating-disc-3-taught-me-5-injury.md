# Five Injury Lessons From a Year of Debilitating Back Pain

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Herniating a disc or 3 taught me these 5 injury tips" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rMuPG8-rEzI)) · 2024-10-31 · 18 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Most injuries heal on their own.** For a non-severe injury, self-management for the first couple of weeks is a defensible default — a 2023/24 meta-analysis found no clinically meaningful difference between one physiotherapy session and multiple, and a 2021/22 meta-analysis of "booster" follow-up sessions found no effect on function, pain-related disability or pain self-efficacy (only on pain catastrophising).
- **Imaging is usually unhelpful early on.** MRI and ultrasound produce large numbers of asymptomatic false positives; Henselmans showed his MRI to several leading back experts and got several different readings.
- **You generally don't need a diagnosis**, because it rarely changes the plan. Four things matter: where it hurts, how bad and what kind of pain, which movements provoke it, and whether it has happened before.
- **The treatment is almost always the same: load below your currently reduced recovery capacity and build back up.** There are no magic rehab exercises — study after study finds exercise selection barely matters.
- **Reinjury is the strongest predictor of injury.** Tissue keeps remodelling for months after pain has gone, so pain-free is not the same as healed.
- **Focus on what you can still do.** Muscle mass is very easy to maintain; even two weeks of not training a muscle group costs very little.

## What is personal experience and what is evidence

**Personal experience (n=1, no diagnosis):** the popping sound in Mexico, a year of pain severe enough that he could not shower standing or walk for more than a minute, consulting Stuart McGill's team and several evidence-based physiotherapists, being read as having anywhere from one to three herniated discs depending on who looked at the MRI, being extension-intolerant rather than the more typical flexion-intolerant, and eventually returning to squatting and deadlifting. None of this is evidence for anything general, and no one ever gave him a diagnosis he trusted.

**Evidence-backed:** the spontaneous-resorption literature, the two meta-analyses on physiotherapy dose, the asymptomatic-findings-on-imaging literature, and the "exercise selection doesn't matter much" trials. These carry the argument; the personal story is illustration. He notes his own case was severe enough that **seeing a professional was indicated** — the lessons target ordinary overuse injuries, not his own.

## Lesson 1: You often don't need to see a professional

The claim is not that physiotherapists are useless. It is that for most injuries the natural history is recovery, and much of the benefit of a consultation is the reassurance of being cared for rather than a specific intervention. He quotes Voltaire — "the art of medicine consists in amusing the patient while nature cures the disease."

Two meta-analyses do the work: no clinically significant differences in pain, function or quality of life between single and multiple physiotherapy sessions ([Dubé 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2023.09.017)), and no evidence that booster sessions after self-management improve function, pain-related disability or self-efficacy — the only thing they reliably reduced was pain catastrophising ([Buzasi 2021](https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000002302)). Even severe presentations often resolve: herniated discs and massive prolapses commonly reabsorb without surgery ([Zhong 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28072796/); [Benson 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19887021/)). He frames this as converging with a growing view among evidence-based physiotherapists that education and self-management should be first-line ([Lewis & O'Sullivan 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099198)).

His test for a good first visit: you should leave with **principles for interpreting your own pain signals**, not a list of prescribed magic exercises.

**Where a reader should see a clinician, plainly.** This is a YouTube video, not clinical advice. Get assessed rather than self-managing if you have: loss of complete range of motion; skin discolouration or visible deformity suggesting a full tissue rupture; electric, radiating or numb sensations, which he says indicate a large neurological component; severe pain, or pain not improving after a couple of weeks; or any recurrence of a previous injury. Bowel or bladder changes, saddle numbness, progressive weakness or unexplained weight loss alongside back pain are standard emergency signs he does not mention at all — his own red-flag example is a GP screen whose contents he admits he can't remember, which is itself a reason not to use this video as your filter. His own case, severe and year-long with radiating symptoms, is one he says warranted professional care.

## Lesson 2: Imaging is often not useful

MRI is accurate at seeing structures, but structures are not the problem. Scans miss things too small to see, miss things that are neurological rather than biomechanical, and — the bigger issue — find plenty of abnormalities in people with no symptoms at all, including large herniations and prolapses. His own MRI is the exhibit: consensus on an L4/L5 herniation, disagreement about everything else, and disagreement about whether his symptoms matched the scan at all.

Position: imaging is largely unhelpful early, and worth considering only for long, severe, unclear cases — while still expecting false positives. He says there is a "growing consensus that MRI scans are contraindicated" for musculoskeletal problems, which overshoots the guideline position (imaging is *not indicated* early absent red flags, rather than contraindicated).

## Lesson 3: You don't need to know what's wrong

Because the plan rarely changes with the diagnosis. His knee example: whether it's the ACL, MCL, patella or tendon, if twisting hurts you stop twisting. Four things are worth establishing: **where** the pain is, **how bad** it is and what quality it has (electric or radiating suggests a neurological component), **when** it hurts — tested cautiously, stopping at the first sign of discomfort, never by working up to a heavy single — and **whether it has happened before**.

The fourth matters most: **reinjury is by far the number one predictor of injury.** Tissue keeps remodelling long after pain resolves — elbow tendinopathy might stop hurting in one to two weeks while the collagen takes many months to reach full strength, leaving you vulnerable throughout. In some cases tissue never fully returns to its previous state.

## Lesson 4: The treatment is nearly always exercise

Load below your currently reduced recovery capacity, avoid what hurts, do what doesn't, and gradually increase. Strength training in particular, because force is what tissue adapts to.

Two things follow. First, **pain signals are a good guide** — he argues this is what pain evolved for, and that the reason his clients arrive injured is almost always that they trained through pain for months. "Can I handle this?" and "is there pain?" are different questions, and hardcore lifters habitually answer the first. Second, **there are no healing exercises.** Trials pitting rotator cuff work against plain rows, or hip-dominant against knee-dominant work for knee pain, generally find no difference, and this is especially well established for back pain ([Long 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15564907/); [Littlewood 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23954024/); [Littlewood 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26160149/); [Saito 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29351510/)). He includes McGill's Big 3: he did them, doesn't know if they helped, and rates them as a good stimulus-to-fatigue ratio for the spine rather than anything magical.

He acknowledges psychosomatic pain exists and that nearly all pain has a psychological component — fear and anxiety amplify it — but argues the treatment converges anyway: find pain-free work, build confidence and tissue capacity together, escalate gradually.

## Lesson 5: Focus on what you can do

A psychological point, and he treats it as one. The reframe: unless you compete in the lifts you've lost, the loss is smaller than it feels — he hasn't squatted or deadlifted much in two years and considers it fine.

The reassurance has a factual basis: maintaining muscle is very easy. Any training for a muscle group maintains it; a first week of nothing costs essentially no muscle, a second week a little, and muscle memory restores it rapidly. He had to fully avoid hamstring, glute and lower-back work for two weeks and treats that as trivial.

He also names a behavioural trap: a client asks for more variation, gets injured a week later, and immediately becomes desperate to do the exercise they'd just asked to drop. A forbidden-fruit effect, not a training need.

## Flags

- The "MRI is contraindicated" framing overshoots the guidance, which is that imaging is not routinely indicated early.
- "Research finds there are only four things you need to know about an injury" is stated as a research finding; the cited paper on assessing pain in a strength setting ([Meir 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000491)) is a practical strategy piece, not a study establishing that list. Treat it as his framework.
- The two meta-analyses concern *how many physiotherapy sessions*, not *whether to seek care at all*. They do not license skipping an initial assessment; he agrees a first visit can be useful.
