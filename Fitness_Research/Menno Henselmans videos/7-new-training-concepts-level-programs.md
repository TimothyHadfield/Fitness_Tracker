# Seven Programming Concepts

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "7 New training concepts to level up your programs" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A7UAY2fanuA)) · 2025-01-02 · 11 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Witness lift:** keep an exercise *out* of your program and test it occasionally. Getting stronger on a lift you don't train is decent evidence you actually got bigger, not just better coordinated.
- **Benchmark set:** use the **first set** of an exercise as your progress readout — later sets are confounded by rest interval length.
- **Think of volume like energy balance:** it's the flow over time that matters, not any single workout. This is why consistency beats exercise order and rest intervals.
- **Failure is continuous, not binary.** Most lifters hit *volitional* failure, not momentary muscle failure, and the fatigue cost of true failure varies enormously by exercise.
- **Reactive deloads beat scheduled ones** — deload the specific muscle showing signs of underrecovery, when it shows them.
- **Build the split last.** Choose volume and frequency per muscle first; whatever falls out doesn't need a name.
- **Systemic fatigue is mental fatigue.** Neuromuscular fatigue from lifting is essentially entirely local ([Behm 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01456-3)).

## 1. The witness lift

The problem: short-term strength gains are dominated by **neural adaptation**, so strength and size correlate poorly in the short term. They correlate strongly in trained lifters over the long term, but by then you can just look in the mirror.

His solution is an exercise you deliberately *don't* program, tested occasionally: front squats if you back squat, Arnold presses, barbell bench if you always use dumbbells, rows if you never row. Record a 10RM or reuse a logged weight and note whether it feels easier.

Two caveats. Carryover exists, but he notes he has been **repeatedly surprised by how specific strength is**, which is what makes the method work at all. And **pick something measurable** — not lateral raises or inverted rows, where standing six inches further from the cable changes your numbers. Machines work well, but you lose the benchmark if you change gyms.

## 2. The benchmark set

Use one designated set to decide whether you're progressing; he uses the **first set**, which also triggers his reactive deload decisions. Performance on sets two and three depends on how long you rested, so unless you time rest you can't tell improvement from a longer break.

## 3. Volume is like energy balance

Both are **flows over time** and both dominate their domain. Weekly volume is the number one determinant of hypertrophy; distribution across individual workouts is not. That explains why consistency matters more than exercise order or rest intervals, and it answers "I missed a workout, should I make it up?" — yes, the total is what counts. Likewise body fat settles at a level determined by total energy flow, not a genetic set point and not by any individual holiday.

## 4. Failure as a continuous variable

**Most people who say they trained to failure didn't.** Momentary muscle failure means you literally failed a rep; what most trainees reach is **volitional failure** — they stopped.

More useful: quantify closeness to failure as **loss of force output**, the strict definition of neuromuscular fatigue. Fatigue rises continuously toward failure, spikes at true failure, and rises further with partials or drop sets.

The payoff is that **the fatigue cost of failure differs by exercise**. On a full-ROM row, once the elbow passes behind the body the lats lose leverage — they can't hyperextend the elbow — which is why full-ROM rows are so much weaker. So failing one costs little fatigue, and you can safely push past it with partials back to the torso.

## 5. Reactive deloads

He cites a 2023 deload study (the sportRxiv preprint below; the transcript's rendering of the author name is unreliable, so it isn't given here) finding the traditional approach — a week off every four weeks — **cost strength gains with no offsetting benefit**, with the authors suggesting reactive deloads instead. He notes he is "the pioneer of reactive deloading."

The mechanic: you squatted 100 x 8, go for 102.5 x 8, get 4 — and judge that incomplete recovery rather than technique or lifestyle noise. Cut the load substantially for the remaining sets, skip them, or switch to speed work.

Two advantages over scheduled deloads: you deload only the muscles that need it (no reason to deload pecs because calves are cooked), and you deload on **actual evidence of overreaching** rather than guessing weeks ahead when your sleep and stress will be poor.

## 6. Splits are built backwards

People pick "push/pull/legs" and fill it in. He argues the reverse: decide **volume per muscle**, **frequency per muscle**, how often each exercise recurs, and how many days you train — then assemble. Named splits are simplifications that restrict the design space, and the program that falls out doesn't need a name.

## 7. Systemic fatigue is mental fatigue

His strongest claim, and the one most against the grain of coaching language. Exercise science, he says, is clear that the vast majority of neuromuscular fatigue is **local** — train biceps and your quads are unaffected — and that for strength training there is **no significant systemic component**. He cites [Behm 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01456-3) on non-local muscle fatigue and [Contessa 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00889.2015), which questions whether central fatigue rests on a solid foundation at all.

What does exist systemically is **mental fatigue** — a mechanism that stops you sinking effort into an unrewarding task. His conclusion: this should be **empowering**, since mental fatigue can safely be trained through, whereas physical fatigue would call for a reactive deload or a volume cut.

## Where the note has to push back

Point 7 is stated far more strongly than the cited evidence supports. "Entirely local" and "no significant systemic component" is a large claim to draw from a meta-analysis on **non-local muscle fatigue** and a paper arguing central fatigue is poorly founded — that is a case for the systemic component being smaller than assumed, not zero. "You can safely train through it" follows from neither paper, and readers with high stress, poor sleep or a heavy workload should not take it as licence to ignore accumulated fatigue.

The deload finding rests on a single unpublished preprint, and he is endorsing a study that endorses a method he invented — which he says openly, but which is worth flagging.
