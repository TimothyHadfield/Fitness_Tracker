# You Cannot Feel Mechanical Tension — Stop Picking Exercises by Feel

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This mistake can cost you 40% of your #gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z0jbhQJXbqE)) · 2024-07-25 · 11 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Golgi tendon organs do sense mechanical tension accurately — but that signal never reaches conscious awareness.** You are physiologically unable to feel how much tension a muscle is producing.
- What you actually feel is **stretch and pump**, neither of which tracks hypertrophy. The pump is the reason lifters historically believed in short rest intervals, mid-range partials and a 6–12 rep "hypertrophy zone" — all wrong.
- The one direct study on subjective vs objective muscle activity, in bodybuilders, found the sensation of contraction **inconsistent with objective activity**.
- In a study he co-authored, subjects **universally** felt their glutes more in hip thrusts than squats; squats produced **just as much glute growth**.
- Repeated pattern: the exercise people prefer or feel is not the one that grows the muscle — pushdowns vs overhead extensions (**~40% less growth**), lying vs seated leg curls, seated vs standing calf raises, squats for hamstrings, bench press for triceps, squats for rectus femoris.
- Conclusion: **select exercises from biomechanics and research, not sensation.** Keep listening to pain signals; discount everything else.

## The neurological argument
Growth is driven primarily by mechanical tension, so selecting exercises by feel requires sensing tension. We have the hardware — Golgi tendon organs measure muscle tension accurately. The problem is routing: that information goes to **subconscious** areas of the brain.

This is especially true of **reafference signals** — sensory feedback caused by your own motor commands. Two illustrations: **you can't tickle yourself**, because the brain issued the command and the surprise factor is gone; and **force escalation**, where two people squeezing each other's hands ratchet upward because each feels the other's force fully and their own only faintly (which, he notes, is why friendly sparring drifts out of hand).

## What the direct evidence shows
There is **only one study** directly comparing subjective sensation of contraction against objective muscle activity, done in bodybuilders. The group average showed sensation was **inconsistent** with objective activity. He says he's involved in a more comprehensive study; the present state of research is "not very promising."

The second piece is his own: the Plotkin hip thrust vs squat study he co-authored. Essentially every subject reported feeling their glutes more during hip thrusts. **Squats produced just as much glute growth** — a clean dissociation between felt activation and measured hypertrophy.

## What you're actually feeling
Mostly stretch, and above all **the pump**, which he argues is massively overrated as a signal. He traces three persistent myths to it:

- **Short rest intervals.** Incomplete rest produces more metabolic stress and pump, which felt like more stimulus. In reality longer rest allows more total volume and more mean tension, and research shows more growth.
- **Mid-range partials.** Excellent for pump; research shows full ROM, or partials in the **lengthened** portion, is better.
- **The 6–12 rep "hypertrophy zone."** Sets of five give little pump — heavy squats just feel generally crushing, with no localised sensation — so they were assumed ineffective, and high-rep sets feel like nothing early on. The effective range is more like **5–30 reps and possibly broader**, provided sets go close to failure.

The inverse holds too: **not feeling a muscle doesn't mean it isn't working.** "Glute amnesia" is largely a myth — plenty of people don't feel their glutes squatting, and squats are an excellent glute exercise. **Glute activation drills** he calls comprehensively debunked across multiple studies: focusing on and literally warming the glutes makes you notice them more, which is not the same as more motor activity, and doesn't improve performance.

## The exercise-selection track record
His list of cases where felt preference diverges from measured growth:

| What lifters feel/prefer | What research shows |
|---|---|
| Pushdowns are the default triceps exercise | Overhead extensions stimulate **~40% more growth** |
| Lying leg curls preferred, or no preference | **Seated leg curls far more effective** for hamstrings |
| Seated calf raise substituted for standing | Seated (bent-leg) takes the **gastrocnemius out entirely**; only the soleus is trained |
| Bench press is a great triceps builder ("why powerlifters have great triceps") | Presses barely train the **long head** — the biggest part — due to biarticular conflict |
| Squats are the best quad exercise | **Rectus femoris is barely trained** by squats, again a biarticular conflict; leg extensions train the whole quad |
| Squats are a great hamstring exercise | Squats are a **very poor** hamstring exercise |
| Close supinated grip chin-ups feel far better for biceps | Grip-width differences are **very small**, probably insignificant |
| Wide-grip bench substantially reduces triceps involvement | The effect is **massively overstated** — a slight decrease at most |

Nobody in the industry was claiming seated leg curls were superior before the research appeared — the finding was invisible to intuition. And on squats-for-hamstrings he points out that Rippetoe, who has studied biomechanics and coached enormous numbers of people, argued in its defence anyway: even very experienced people make gross errors about which exercises train what.

## The closing argument
His strongest point is almost offhand: it should be obvious we have no internal sense of what an exercise trains, because **the first question anyone asks about a new exercise is "which muscles does this train?"** If we could sense it, we wouldn't need to ask.

So: rely primarily on science and biomechanics. Be sceptical of what you feel in both directions — feeling a muscle may just be pump or stretch, and not feeling one doesn't mean your program is failing. He doesn't recommend ignoring feelings entirely, and specifically says **listening to pain signals is productive**.

**Overreach flags.** The headline "40% of your gains" is the pushdown-vs-overhead figure from a single study, generalised into a claim about exercise selection as a whole. The direct subjective-vs-objective evidence is **one study**, which he acknowledges. And "seated calf raises are almost entirely ineffective" is a strong statement — they are ineffective for the gastrocnemius specifically, which he does explain, but the phrasing overshoots.
