# Seven Caffeine Studies: Tolerance, Sleep, Placebo and the Missing Dose-Response

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "7 New studies you MUST know about #caffeine" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SBaX0jEYsjU)) · 2024-04-20 · 20 min
**Topic:** Supplements

## Bottom line
- **Caffeine has no meaningful dose-response for performance.** In well-trained sprinters and jumpers, **1, 3 and 6 mg/kg produced no significant differences** in jumping performance. He says this has now been replicated across multiple studies and calls it near-unique among drugs.
- **Habituation largely erases the ergogenic effect.** Three studies: soccer players habituated to ~360 mg/day got nothing from 100 or 200 mg; strength-trained men habituated to only ~2.2 mg/kg/day got nothing from 3 mg/kg on reps to failure; recreational cyclists got no benefit from even **6 mg/kg** unless they abstained for **24 hours** first.
- **Sleep costs are larger than most people assume.** A meta-analysis concluded coffee should stop **8.8 hours** before bed and a standard pre-workout (~200 mg) **13.2 hours** before bed to avoid clinically significant reductions in sleep time and quality.
- **Ten days of 450 mg/day (3 × 150 mg) reduced working memory performance** versus placebo — higher error rates, longer reaction times, reduced hippocampal activity — and the deficit persisted during acute withdrawal.
- **The placebo effect is large and may exceed the pharmacological effect.** Told an inert capsule was caffeine, subjects did more reps *and* reported lower RPE.
- His recommendation: **~1 mg/kg/day, or roughly 700 mg/week**, taken only pre-workout, as the ceiling for staying sensitive; **4–9 days of abstinence resensitises receptors**.

## Where this sits against the library
Our library holds that **no evidence supports cycling off caffeine**, and that the **ISSN gives 3–6 mg/kg** as the ergogenic dose. Henselmans **differs on both**:
- He argues tolerance is real, strong, and effectively negates the benefit in habitual users, and therefore explicitly recommends a **4–9 day washout** (cold turkey or tapered) followed by a low maintenance dose — that is a cycling protocol.
- He argues **3–6 mg/kg is unnecessary**, because the dose-response is flat and 1 mg/kg is equally effective while costing less sleep and less cognitive impairment.

He does not claim the tolerance literature is unanimous. He notes other studies and even meta-analyses still find benefits in habitual users, and gives two reasons why weaker designs may inflate that: they are often cross-sectional rather than within-subject, and they typically test 3–6 mg/kg in people habituated to ~1 mg/kg, so an effect is expected even under a shifted dose-response curve. His counterweight is largely argumentative — an appeal to how obviously daily users stop feeling caffeine — rather than a new dataset.

## 1–2: Cognition
A 10-day trial compared placebo, caffeine (150 mg three times daily = **450 mg/day**, roughly five cups of coffee), and caffeine followed by withdrawal on day 10. Against the short-term literature — where a single dose usually improves or does not affect working memory — chronic dosing **reduced** working memory performance: more errors, slower reaction times, and reduced hippocampal activity. The authors concluded daily moderate-dose caffeine leads to compromised working memory function that may remain reduced during acute withdrawal.

He links this to older work on chess performance under stimulants: attention and individual move quality improved, but players took longer per move and lost more games on time, so **overall performance was unchanged**. His framing is that caffeine selectively enhances some cognitive functions while degrading others, and is mainly useful for simple tasks where attention is otherwise hard to sustain. He is careful to note the working memory study is a single study and the effect is not dramatic.

## 3: Sleep
A meta-analysis concluded that to avoid reductions in total sleep time and sleep quality, coffee should be consumed at least **8.8 hours** before bedtime and a standard pre-workout serving at least **13.2 hours** before. Individual sleep metrics moved modestly, but the aggregate effect was judged clinically significant: harder sleep onset, reduced sleep efficiency, less deep sleep, more light sleep, and more time awake at night even when unremembered.

The practical implication he draws is stark: a 13-hour buffer means not training much later than about 12 hours before bed if you use a caffeinated pre-workout. He also notes that **subjective and objective sleep quality diverge** — one study found a single morning double espresso still objectively impaired that night's sleep — so "it doesn't affect my sleep" is not reliable self-report.

## 4: Placebo and nocebo
Three inert capsules were given with three different framings: cellulose (open placebo control), caffeine, and lactic acid (framed as harmful). The caffeine framing produced **more repetitions with a lower RPE** — subjects performed better while feeling they were working less hard. The lactic acid framing showed the opposite trend (fewer reps, higher RPE) but was **not statistically significant**.

He notes prior work in which the expectancy effect exceeded the pharmacological effect: people who thought they had caffeine outperformed people who actually had caffeine but were told it was placebo. He also corrects the premise of the nocebo arm in passing — muscles produce lactate, not lactic acid, lactate is a base, and it buffers rather than causes fatigue.

## 5: No dose-response
Well-trained collegiate sprinters and jumpers performed jump-based sessions at **1, 3 and 6 mg/kg**. No significant differences between doses. He stresses the magnitude of what is being equated: 1 mg/kg is roughly one Red Bull for an average man or half a weak coffee for many women; 6 mg/kg is about six Red Bulls. **The crucial condition is blinding** — this holds as long as subjects do not know which dose they received.

He balances this with an anecdote about accidentally drinking a friend's very strong coffee and nearly fainting, i.e. side effects clearly do scale with dose even if performance does not.

## 6–8: Habituation (three studies)
1. **Soccer players** habituated to ~360 mg/day (three to three and a half coffees) derived **no ergogenic benefit** from 100 or 200 mg, with no difference between the two doses. One exception: the 200 mg dose improved 1 of 6 test measures, which he treats as probably noise but possibly a hint that habituated users need higher doses for any effect.
2. **Strength-trained men** habituated to an average of only **2.2 mg/kg/day** got no increase in repetitions during a medium-load full-body session to failure from **3 mg/kg** of caffeine. He emphasises how modest the habitual intake was — about two coffees for a large man.
3. **Recreational cyclists** derived no benefit from pre-exercise caffeine unless they abstained for at least **24 hours**. At 8 hours' abstinence they had still had ~1.5 mg/kg that morning, and **6 mg/kg** then failed to improve cycling performance. The authors concluded that habitual users may not benefit from 6 mg/kg and that previous work may have overstated caffeine's value for habitual users.

## Practical protocol he gives
- **Resensitisation takes 4–9 days** of abstinence, depending on prior intake.
- After that, keep total intake to roughly **1 mg/kg/day or ~700 mg/week** — about one coffee or one energy drink — which he says is sustainable long-term without significant habituation or withdrawal.
- **Take it only pre-workout.** Caffeine earlier in the day will blunt the workout effect.
- Decaf coffee and herbal teas are fine as substitutes.
- Taking none at all is also fine: most of the effect is psychological, and **long-term studies do not find caffeine enhances muscle growth, fat loss or strength development**.
- The reasoning he chains together for low dosing: low doses work as well as high ones; high doses cost sleep and possibly working memory; much of the effect is expectancy anyway.

**Where the argument outruns the evidence:** the "1 mg/kg/day or 700 mg/week" threshold is not a study finding — no study he describes tested that maintenance dose against habituation. It is his synthesis of the flat dose-response plus the habituation studies, and should be read as a reasoned heuristic rather than an established number. The 4–9 day resensitisation figure is also given without a specific citation.
