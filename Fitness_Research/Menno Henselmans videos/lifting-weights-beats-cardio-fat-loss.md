# Lifting Beats Cardio for Fat Loss — as a Package Deal

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Why lifting weights beats cardio for fat loss [New research]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2h0sOlO8W8)) · 2026-02-17 · 7 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- New study: **304 dieters** (~500 kcal deficit, 1.5 g/kg protein) chose aerobic exercise ~200 min/week, strength training 2-3x/week, or no exercise, and dieted to a healthy body fat level — about **5 months** on average.
- The strength group had the **most absolute fat loss on every measure**, significant only for women's abdominal circumference. Statistically, fat loss was similar across groups. **Only the strength group gained lean mass; the aerobic group lost it.**
- A meta-analysis of 31 studies agrees: cardio produced greater absolute fat loss, but strength training added fat-free mass, so **body fat percentage changes were similar**. Equated for time, energy expenditure or work output, **even absolute fat loss was equal**.
- His conclusion isn't that lifting burns more fat — per minute spent it gives **equal body fat reduction plus muscle and strength**, so it's the better package.

## Why cardio isn't specially fat-burning
Cardio oxidises fatty acids, but total fat loss is set by **energy balance over time**, not by which fuel a given session burns. That's exactly what the meta-analysis shows: equate energy expenditure and all exercise modes come out the same in the short term.

Lifting then adds three things on top.

**Lost muscle is fat you didn't lose.** In a deficit the body burns its own tissue to cover the gap, and only burns each unit once. Every kilo of muscle catabolised is a kilo of fat spared — so muscle preservation matters for the fat number itself, not only appearance.

**Muscle raises expenditure, and it compounds.** He's careful here: maintaining an extra kilo of muscle costs only a few calories. The larger effect is **carrying that mass around all day**, so total daily expenditure rises more than resting expenditure. He cites a couple of percent change in resting expenditure after a few months of training, and roughly **15%** between bodybuilders and matched controls long term. Cardio's investment pays out for that session only; lifting's keeps paying.

**Constrained energy expenditure.** Expenditure doesn't rise linearly with activity — diminishing returns as the body compensates elsewhere, with some analyses putting the average shortfall around **28%** below what the added activity predicts. Low-intensity activity like walking is subject to this; **strength training appears not to be**, apparently too intense for the body to compensate away.

## Where he puts the emphasis anyway
Having argued lifting wins the comparison, he shrinks the stakes: over long periods the expenditure difference from *any* training is small next to diet. "You can't out-train a bad diet" and "abs are made in the kitchen" he calls straightforwardly true.

His practical order: spend the **first couple of hours of exercise per week on resistance training**, aimed at health, performance and muscle. Add cardio for extra expenditure only once more lifting runs into overreaching and recovery limits. Even then the durable lever is **sustainable food choices**.

## Note on the evidence
He flags the main weakness himself: exercise mode was **self-selected, not randomised**, chosen for adherence at the cost of selection bias. Worth adding that the headline is weaker than the title implies — the strength group's fat loss advantage was **not statistically significant** on almost every measure, and the meta-analysis found cardio better for absolute fat loss. The real finding is equivalence on body fat percentage plus a muscle bonus, which is what he argues in the body of the video even as the title claims lifting "beats" cardio.
