# Fibre-Type-Specific Training: The Rationale Changed, the Prescription Didn't

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Training different muscle fiber types" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4QSE0XT5DdY)) · 2022-12-19 · 5 min
**Topic:** Training

A clip from his appearance on the Revive Stronger podcast with Steve Hall ([full episode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WaTRx6Tov6U)).

## Bottom line
- He **abandoned fibre-type-specific hypertrophy programming years ago** — the American literature is a run of null findings — but he does not think the story is finished.
- His reconciliation of the conflicting evidence: the Russian studies that *did* find high reps preferentially growing type I fibres all used **"no relaxation" training — continuous tension, no pausing**. The American studies used normal (often artificially slow, four-second eccentric) tempos. He thinks **the continuous tension, not the rep number, is the active ingredient**.
- Blood flow restriction training is his supporting case: it produces **type I-specific growth**, which is backwards on an anaerobic-stress account but fits a long-time-under-tension-without-relaxation account.
- Type II-preferential growth would need **1–3RM, maybe 5RM at most** — and even then it may just be that time under tension is too short for type I fibres rather than genuinely better for type II. He calls this "not a winning scenario".
- **His practical method is unchanged: prescribe percentages of 1RM, not RM targets.** Say "80% of 1RM", let the lifter get whatever reps they get. The reason for doing it has changed, not the doing.

## Where he splits from the standard "fibre type doesn't matter" position
The mainstream line — the one this library holds — is that fibre type barely matters for rep selection, since reps at a given %1RM is largely fibre-type-independent. **He accepts that finding and argues the conclusion drawn from it is too strong.** His counter-arguments:

1. **Capillary density does predict reps at a given %1RM** even where fibre type doesn't. Real between-person variation exists in how a percentage load behaves — via a different mechanism than the one everyone was arguing about.
2. **Recovery, not hypertrophy, is where fibre type shows up.** He cites research that muscles with more type II fibres take longer to recover from high-rep work, with no such difference for low reps. A fast-twitch-dominant lifter gets hammered by high-rep training even if growth per set is identical.
3. **Coaching practice suggests something real.** Via David Epstein's *The Sports Gene*: top coaches found sprinters got injured and failed to recover on marathon-runner methods. Not hypertrophy evidence, but he treats it as evidence that fibre type does *something*.

He does **not** mention the soleus or any muscle-level exception — his whole discussion is about between-person variation, not between-muscle variation. So this doesn't contradict the library's soleus point (~90% type I); it simply doesn't engage with it.

He also flags where his earlier view came from: his first publicly written article was on fibre-type-specific hypertrophy, built on Charles Poliquin and Christian Thibaudeau's material. He dropped it when the research didn't pan out.

## The point he ends on
Whatever the cause — more type I fibres, more capillaries, or just preferring high reps — **lifters get better adaptations from the training they are good at**. That is why percentage-based prescription survives the collapse of its original rationale: it lets individual variation express itself without the coach needing to know which mechanism produced it.

**Flagged:** every empirical claim here is described rather than cited, including the BFR type I finding and the recovery-by-fibre-type study — the two doing the most work. The confidence in the "no relaxation" explanation runs well ahead of anything he presents for it; he offers it as his reading of why two literatures disagree, not as a tested hypothesis.
