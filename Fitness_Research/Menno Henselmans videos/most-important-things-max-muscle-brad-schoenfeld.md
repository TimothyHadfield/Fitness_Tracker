# Brad Schoenfeld on What Actually Matters for Muscle

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The most important things for max muscle - Brad Schoenfeld" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Vwk4xPkC5U)) · 2024-04-26 · 38 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Ranked fundamentals, in Schoenfeld's own order:** consistency, then **training hard — within about 2–3 reps of failure**, then **volume**. Everything else is conditional.
- **Load barely matters** — the variable he holds with the *most* confidence: 3–5 reps up to 30–40 reps give similar whole-muscle hypertrophy if you go close to failure. But he is **less confident of that than five years ago**; possible fibre-type effects mean mixing loading ranges may be marginally better.
- **The 45-set volume studies have been misread.** Total weekly volume there was ~105 sets *across all muscle groups* — about 10 per muscle if spread. Think of a **weekly budget of ~130–140 total sets**; high per-muscle volumes belong in specialization cycles. **Overtraining is systemic, not local.**
- **Lengthened partials are an advanced technique, not a paradigm shift**, and he sees "virtually zero evidence" that the mechanism is sarcomerogenesis.
- **Meal frequency: 3–4 for an advanced bodybuilder**, essentially unchanged despite the Trommelen 100 g protein study, which he calls interesting but singular.
- **He has reversed himself on deloads.** His unpublished nine-week trial found no resensitization from a week off, and a slight strength cost.

A friendly interview between two people who agree on most things — but the disagreements are real, and recorded below.

## Stretch-mediated hypertrophy and lengthened partials

Henselmans opens on the hottest topic in evidence-based fitness and Schoenfeld immediately cools it. He uses lengthened partials "to some extent," but does not think they are the be-all and end-all. What the literature supports, he says, is that **the lengthened position has particular importance** — not that this dismisses the shortened position. Evidence that lengthened partials beat full range of motion is "very limited," though he was involved in one study showing benefit at some measurement sites.

His argument for keeping the shortened half is about strength: there is a **range-of-motion-specific effect**, with generalisation extending roughly 15 degrees either side of the trained joint angle, so cutting the shortened portion may compromise strength there. Henselmans adds — uncontested — that the compelling studies are almost all **leg extensions and calf raises**, which may not generalise, and that the rest of the literature is "really flimsy."

Schoenfeld then takes apart the mechanistic story built on top of it. Some claim the effect is all **sarcomerogenesis** (sarcomeres added in series); he sees "virtually zero evidence" for that. The studies measure **thickness and cross-sectional area**, which implies in-parallel hypertrophy. Sarcomerogenesis is inferred from **fascicle length**, but a longer fascicle can equally reflect existing sarcomeres stretching — and he is suspicious of the measurements themselves, since many fascicles **terminate intrafascicularly** rather than running origin to insertion. He is collaborating on a paper questioning whether sarcomerogenesis occurs in resistance training at all; the animal evidence comes from extreme casting models, which is not what lifting does. His verdict: reading in-series hypertrophy out of thickness and cross-sectional data is "a leap," though muscle *volume* data might change that.

## Mechanisms, revisited by the man who wrote the model

Henselmans calls Schoenfeld's 2010 mechanisms paper his PhD dissertation; Schoenfeld corrects him — it was his **Master's capstone**. It proposed mechanical tension, metabolic stress and muscle damage as the leading candidate stimuli, and is why those three terms are in every lifter's vocabulary. Fourteen years on his position has shifted but not collapsed. **Mechanical tension he holds with very good confidence.** On the other two: "when I wrote that paper... there was more hope for evidence on that, which I don't think has manifested" — but he explicitly will *not* dismiss them.

- On **metabolic stress**, a lactate review he collaborated on found literature on both sides. Resistance training alone produces several dozen metabolites, and there is at least a rationale by which **acidosis could enhance the anabolic response**.
- On **muscle damage**, "the jury is still out" — but there is quite good evidence damage **initiates satellite cell activation**, which is heavily involved in growth. The caveat: that pathway might be **redundant**. He also doubts you can produce *any* training stress without mild damage, and notes that **extracellular matrix** damage, not just fibre damage, has demonstrated relevance to the anabolic response.
- The core methodological problem: **you cannot manipulate one mechanism without manipulating another.**

He also refuses the framing that mechanical tension means load. Tension is force acting on a muscle, manifesting as contraction *or* passive stretch. Magnitude is one factor; **mode** (eccentric, isometric, concentric) another; and there is a **time-tension integral** — "if it was all about tension, do five 1-rep maxes and that should be your maximal hypertrophy, and that's not what happens." Where the optimum lies, he says flatly, we have no idea.

## Soreness and the pump are not the mechanisms

Henselmans asks whether the popular translation holds — damage ≈ soreness, metabolic stress ≈ pump. Schoenfeld rejects it as a lay simplification, and this is where he is sharpest, with a pointed aside about **influencers with no background in the field oversimplifying topics they do not understand**.

Soreness does correlate with damage, but weakly: fibre damage and extracellular matrix damage are different things, and soreness in trained lifters mostly comes from **novelty of exercise selection**. Keep selection stable and you will not get sore — which does not mean you are not growing. In his own 8–10 week studies, trained subjects are sore early and report **virtually no soreness by the end**, and he does not believe they got all their growth in the first fortnight.

## The fundamentals, ranked

**1. Consistency.** Train, repeatedly, several times a week, over time. He puts this first without hedging.

**2. Training hard.** "The one thing we can conclude from the literature is that training in fairly close proximity to failure is going to be the requisite factor." Pressed on numbers, he rejects 5–10 RIR as far too far away and lands on **2–3 reps in reserve**, citing his own meta-analysis and a forthcoming study suggesting that within a couple of reps of failure you get results as good as going to failure. Further away and you are probably not stimulating the muscle sufficiently — excepting novices, for whom the stimulus is novel.

**3. Volume.** An important driver, supported by numerous meta-analyses — with the same confound as the mechanisms question: manipulate rest intervals and you have manipulated load, since holding a 10RM across one- and three-minute rests forces you to lighten the short-rest group.

**4. Everything else, conditionally.** Load heads this tier and draws the most confidence of any variable in the interview: **similar whole-muscle hypertrophy across roughly 3–5 reps up to 30–40 reps**, provided you train close to failure. The hedge follows — he is "less optimistic than five years ago" that load is irrelevant, because some evidence, including recent blood flow restriction work, hints at a **fibre-type-specific effect**, so mixing heavy and light work might optimise things.

He then draws a line around who this is for: optimisation matters "for you or me or people who are just looking to maximise every morsel of their genetic potential," but for most people, "get in the gym, train hard, even fairly minimalistic routines — you're going to get the majority of your gains."

## Reading the high-volume studies correctly

The 45-sets-per-muscle findings are the most misused result in his catalogue, and he spends the longest stretch of the interview correcting the reading. He has never prescribed 45 sets to anyone. The number people miss is that **total weekly volume in that study was 105 sets across all muscle groups** — over ten muscle groups, about 10 sets each, at the *low* end of what the volume literature suggests. The 45 went to one or two muscles at the expense of everything else.

So there is a **distinction between systemic and local volume ceilings**. 45 sets for *every* muscle group would not have panned out the same: you would trash the subjects, and it is impractical — 18 hours in the gym. What the studies lend credence to is **specialization cycles**: you can run that volume into one muscle group, perhaps two. He grounds this in a claim Henselmans agrees to: **non-functional overreaching is systemic, not local.** Do 100 sets for biceps and nothing else and you will not be overtrained per se.

The practical model is a **volume budget**: if ~130–140 total weekly sets is roughly the ceiling worth planning around, you apportion it like money — well-developed muscle groups get fewer sets so a specialisation target can get more. At the high end, **spread it by frequency**: he ran a bodybuilder at 30 weekly hamstring sets as 10 sets every third day, first in the workout, to good short-term effect. Both agree on why frequency matters more as volume rises — the marginal return on a 21st bench set in one session is poor, so splitting improves the stimulus-to-fatigue ratio.

He labels the rest speculation, twice saying outright that there is no objective data. On whether there is a **time limit** on sustaining a volume, he invokes the General Adaptation Syndrome (attributed to Hans Selye, whom he calls "an Austrian physicist" — Selye was a Hungarian-Canadian endocrinologist) and suggests building low to moderate to a peaking phase, then restarting. On **periodizing volume**: pushing into functional overreaching then backing off could help.

## Meal frequency after the 100 g protein study

Schoenfeld and Alan Aragon's influential paper killed the six-meals-a-day rule and landed on about four, on the then-current model of a sharp, capped MPS response of roughly 20–40 g per meal. He notes he personally leaned toward **three** even then, and that the paper was more about **even spacing** than count.

Then came the Trommelen study showing a **100 g protein bolus** exceeded the anabolic effect of 25 g, with utilisation running 12 hours and probably longer. His response is a good model of how to update: praise for the work and the lab, followed by "**one study never changes my opinion on anything**." It "shifted my thinking to think it probably is less than what I would have thought," and lines up with the intermittent fasting literature — though he distrusts free-living dietary compliance data generally. And it measured **muscle protein synthesis, not hypertrophy**: you cannot extrapolate an acute response over months, and there could be a refractory effect or a novelty response to an unusual load. Henselmans adds the design point Schoenfeld endorses — subjects ate one meal post-workout after an overnight fast, and showing the body can *use* 100 g there is different from showing a bodybuilder on four spread meals would do equally well on one.

Asked to commit to a number for an advanced bodybuilder, he says **three to four** — where he was before. The argument is logistical: a 90 kg bodybuilder in a surplus eats around 4,000 calories, and getting that into one or two meals is possible but miserable and not the best use of the calories.

## Two unpublished studies

**Exercise selection, within-subject.** Trained men and women, legs randomised: one leg leg extensions, the other leg press; one calf seated raises, the other straight-leg.
- **Leg extension produced greater rectus femoris growth; leg press greater vastus lateralis growth.** Takeaway: you need both multi-joint and single-joint work. (No squats were tested; he would have expected leg press to be at least as good, given the degrees of freedom.)
- **Straight-leg produced substantially greater medial gastrocnemius growth.** Lateral gastrocnemius was barely affected either way, and the soleus — where a decent seated advantage was expected — showed only a slight one: **"nothing that I would say wow, that you really need to do the seated to target the soleus."**

**Deloads, accepted for publication.** Nine weeks; one group trained straight through, the other four weeks, a week off, then four more — testing evidence that a short break resensitizes muscle. **It did not happen**: no resensitization for hypertrophy, and a slight strength detriment in the break group, preliminarily attributed to subjects feeling "off" coming back, possibly because a complete week off is harsher than a traditional volume-and-load reduction.

The important part is the position change. He had **previously favoured scheduled deloads**, on the grounds that lifters cannot auto-regulate well enough to notice they need one. He no longer thinks four weeks requires one — and this was hard training: 20 weekly sets each for quads, hamstrings and calves, every set to volitional failure. His caveat: only the lower body was supervised.

## Where they disagreed

- **Whether the very-high-volume ceiling generalises.** Henselmans raises the position (associated with Milo Wolf and others; the captions garble the names) that everyone may tolerate far higher volumes than assumed. Schoenfeld: "**I would disagree with that**." He accepts people manage more than the textbook figure, but calls extrapolating to ~180 total weekly sets "a real stretch" the existing studies cannot support — a hypothesis worth testing, not a finding.
- **The tone toward his own volume research.** Henselmans calls the 45-set study "famous, infamous." Schoenfeld pushes back: "**I don't know it's infamous** — it's been replicated by numerous different independent labs." He accepts "popular and controversial."
- **The strength of the lengthened-partials literature.** Henselmans calls everything outside leg extensions and calf raises "really flimsy." Schoenfeld does not endorse that; he was involved in a study showing benefit at some sites. He is *more* favourable to the lengthened-position case than Henselmans implies, and far more sceptical of its proposed mechanism.
- **The pump-and-soreness shorthand.** Henselmans offers the popular mapping of damage to soreness and metabolic stress to pump; Schoenfeld declines it as a lay simplification.
- **Mechanisms — implicitly, and the most important one.** This library holds Henselmans' own 2017 lecture, where he puts "muscle damage: rejected" on a slide and reduces metabolic stress to an indirect route through muscle activation. Schoenfeld will not go there. **The author of the three-mechanism model has not adopted Henselmans' verdict on it**, and Henselmans does not press the point.
- **Meal frequency.** Henselmans pushes downward — "are we talking two, three, one?" — and Schoenfeld holds at three to four.
- **A minor correction:** Henselmans calls the 2010 paper a PhD dissertation; Schoenfeld corrects it to his Master's capstone.

## Where the confidence outruns the evidence

Schoenfeld is unusually good about this himself, labelling the GAS-based volume progression, volume cycling and the fibre-type loading argument as speculation before anyone asks. Three things still warrant flagging:

- The **130–140 weekly set budget** is offered as a way to think, but stated as a number it reads as a recommendation, and no data is given for it.
- The **deload conclusion** rests on one unpublished trial in which half the training was unsupervised. By his own standard — "one study never changes my opinion on anything" — it deserves the replication demand he applied to the protein work.
- The **calf finding** cuts against a well-established anatomical rationale and was stated from unpublished data before peer review.
