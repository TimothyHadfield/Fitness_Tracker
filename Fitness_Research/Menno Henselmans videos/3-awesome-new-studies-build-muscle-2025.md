# Three 2025 Studies: Pre-Exhaustion, Mind-Muscle Connection, Lengthened Partials

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "3 Awesome new studies on how to build muscle in 2025" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kvJp2OOAB4k)) · 2025-07-15 · 12 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Pre-exhaustion loses.** Doing leg curls immediately before RDLs and leg extensions immediately before squats produced consistently (if non-significantly) less growth across eight measurement sites, marginally worse squat strength, and **30% lower total volume load** than the traditional order.
- **Rest at least 90 seconds** between sets of the same exercise, and do **compounds before isolation** for a given muscle.
- **The mind-muscle connection failed again.** Trained men focusing on their pecs during Smith machine bench presses at 50% 1RM, three sets of eight, repeated four times, could not raise pec EMG. No triceps effect either.
- **Lengthened partials were oversold.** A reanalysis summing all measurement sites found they did **not** significantly outperform full range of motion at the whole-muscle level. Only shortened partials clearly underperformed.
- The practical version: it matters that a muscle gets high tension at long lengths — not that it *only* gets tension at long lengths.

## Pre-exhaustion (preprint)
Both groups did leg curls, RDLs, leg extensions and squats to failure with two-minute rests; the pre-exhaust group simply reordered so each isolation exercise ran straight into its compound with minimal rest. After 8 weeks the traditional group had more growth at all sites, better squat strength, and got slightly leaner while the pre-exhaust group gained a little fat, with no dietary differences. These were trained lifters.

He is explicit that **statistically this is a null result**, then says he'd bet on it replicating with more subjects and a longer trial. His grounds are three converging mechanisms:

1. **Short rest cuts tension.** He debunked the "short rest is anabolic" idea in one of his first major publications with Schoenfeld ([Henselmans & Schoenfeld 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-014-0228-0)), followed by a trial showing longer rest improves growth ([Schoenfeld 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/)). Same load, more reps, more time under the same tension.
2. **Agonist supersets are worse than short rest.** Once a muscle has been trained successfully, high-threshold motor units drop out and force output collapses, so continued volume imposes very little additional tension — the same reason he rates post-failure training as a poor stimulus-to-fatigue trade.
3. **Isolation-before-compound creates a weak link.** Fry the quads before squatting and you under-stimulate glutes, lower back and adductors. EMG research predicted this exactly: pre-exhausting a muscle does *not* raise its activity in the subsequent compound — flies before bench don't increase pec activation, with a trend toward *more* triceps activity compensating.

The honest counterpoint: the pre-exhaust group finished in **36% less time**, though they reported the sessions as more effortful. His alternative for time-pressed lifters is shorter rest intervals (work capacity adapts) or **combo sets** pairing unrelated muscle groups.

## Mind-muscle connection
The design was deliberately favourable — light enough (50% 1RM, three sets of eight, Smith machine) that technique and activation patterns *should* have been changeable, and repeated four times to validate ([Nascimento 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/00315125251343156)). Internal focus on the pecs still produced no increase in pec activation.

His explanation is blunt: the motor cortex is extremely good at coordinating movement, better than anything roboticists have built. When a muscle is a prime mover or synergist in a well-chosen exercise, it is already activated as fully as the task allows, because maximum force output is what the brain optimises for. Focusing on anything else reliably *reduces* force output — try pulling a deadlift 1RM while thinking about your quads.

If it doesn't raise activation and it lowers force, he argues, there is no route to more growth. Emphasise muscles through **exercise selection and execution** instead — a wider bench grip to shift work from triceps to pecs. His one concession: some lifters use it to hold technique together, but research suggests cueing the technique directly ("keep your elbows at your sides") is more efficient than cueing the muscle. Note this against his earlier bodybuilding-myths video, which cited an attentional-focus trial finding *better* biceps gains under strict-technique-plus-internal-focus instructions; he reconciles them by treating the benefit as technique-mediated, not activation-mediated.

## Lengthened partials
The original study made lengthened partials explode in popularity. This is a **reanalysis of the same data** ([Pedrosa 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2025.2502895)) — the captions garble the name as "Pedro Satal" — which previously reported only regional hypertrophy and now sums all sites for whole-muscle growth. Shortened partials underperformed everything; among full ROM, lengthened partials and varied ROM there were **no significant differences**, with a non-significant trend favouring the latter two.

He calls this a textbook inflated first effect size followed by regression to the mean. He also flags the sample: untrained women at RPE 15/20 on the Borg scale, 3-6 sets of seven slow reps at 60% 1RM — very submaximal, though enough to grow.

His conclusion is deliberately moderate. Lengthened partials are still good, several studies show benefits, and nothing shows them being *worse* — combined with full ROM beating shortened partials, the case for training at long muscle lengths is strong. But the difference is small, and should mostly matter for exercises weak in the stretched position anyway. Leg extensions and calf raises, which most of this literature uses, barely load the bottom; exercises already designed for tension throughout the range, like a Bayesian curl or lat prayer, leave little room for partials to add anything.

**Overreach to note:** on study one he says "I would bet my money on these results panning out" immediately after conceding the study reads as a null. He labels it as a prediction from mechanism, but it carries more confidence than the data.
