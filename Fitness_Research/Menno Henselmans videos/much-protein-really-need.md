# How Much Protein You Really Need — and Why the 3.1 g/kg Meta-Analysis Doesn't Hold Up

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is how much protein you REALLY need" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R4vCVSrD36U)) · 2024-11-28 · 13 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The evidence-based plateau for maximising muscle and strength gains is **1.6 g/kg/day (0.7 g/lb)** of *total* protein on *total* body weight — not lean mass. Henselmans co-authored the 2018 meta-analysis that produced that number; a 2022 meta-analysis put the strength plateau at 1.5 g/kg.
- His **practical recommendation is 1.8 g/kg (0.8 g/lb)** — the 1.6 plateau plus roughly one standard deviation of error, as insurance.
- Of **45 studies** comparing protein intakes that controlled for meal frequency and were otherwise comparable (collected for his PT course), **none** found benefits above 1.6 g/kg.
- The 2020 meta-analysis claiming benefits up to **3.1 g/kg** should be discounted: it pooled 105 studies of which the authors themselves say only **four** compared doses under identical conditions, and it controlled for energy intake only by throwing weight change into a regression.
- **No extra protein is needed in an energy deficit**, and protein's satiety advantage also stops being significant at around 1.6 g/kg.
- Genuine exceptions where more may be warranted: some vegans (protein quality), lifters with muscle memory, high-volume concurrent athletes, and — by far the biggest — anyone on anabolic steroids.

## The number, and where it comes from
His 2018 meta-analysis found fat-free mass gains maximised at 1.6 g/kg/day with no further benefit above it. (On-screen he misspeaks the imperial conversion; the video's own correction note gives **0.7 g/lb**.) A 2022 dose-response meta-analysis on strength landed at 1.5 g/kg — a 0.1 g/kg difference. His own controlled trial comparing 1.8 vs 2.9 g/kg found no difference in soreness, muscle damage or strength recovery.

He frames all of this as the tail end of a consensus forming since the 1980s, built on the nitrogen-balance and body-composition work in strength athletes and bodybuilders (Tarnopolsky and colleagues), which converged on the same ~1.6 g/kg figure.

## The teardown of the 3.1 g/kg meta-analysis
This is the bulk of the video and the reason it exists — a new meta-analysis had been circulating claiming benefits nearly twice as high as the accepted figure. Four objections:

**Confounding.** The analysis lumped in essentially every available protein-comparison study without controlling for energy intake, nutrient timing or meal frequency. Many were weight-loss trials in which the control group's protein intake actually fell, so the contrast is "decrease protein" versus "increase protein", not a clean dose comparison. In some, carbohydrate was co-administered with the protein. He quotes the authors' own admission that only 4 of 105 articles examined the dose-response relationship under identical conditions.

**Controlling for weight change is not controlling for calories.** Adding weight change as a covariate rewards fat loss: a study where protein did nothing for lean mass but produced more fat loss registers as a positive protein effect, while a study with genuine lean mass gain gets penalised. Given how many included trials were weight-loss studies, this is not a small artefact.

**The high end is nearly empty.** The model's curve runs to 3.1 g/kg, but the highest *assigned* intake in the data was 240 g, with only one other group above 200 g, and the highest self-reported intake was 161 g. Two of the handful of high-dose studies ran only 2–4 weeks. Extrapolating to a 200 lb lifter would imply ~300 g/day on almost no supporting data.

**No data shown.** The published graph is the model's expected relationship, not the underlying effect sizes; no breakpoint analysis, no R² estimates, no model-fit graphs. He calls this "just trust me bro." Two model outputs he treats as face-invalid: protein supposedly mattered *more* for non-exercisers than exercisers, and untrained non-lifters supposedly benefited to 3.1 g/kg. When weight change was controlled, the untrained inflection point dropped to 1.3 g/kg. He also flags that the paper's title says "muscle mass" while the outcome measured is lean body mass — not merely semantic when a third of the sample doesn't lift.

His conclusion is narrower than the headline: the analysis shows a positive association above 1.3 g/kg, which nobody disputes. It never ran the breakpoint test that would be needed to claim 3.1. And the same research group's later, better-controlled strength analysis returned 1.5 g/kg.

## Where he stands relative to this library
His 1.8 g/kg sits inside our existing bulking range (1.6–2.2 g/kg) and inside the ISSN's 1.4–2.0 g/kg. Where he **breaks with the library** is cutting: he holds there is no good evidence for elevating protein in an energy deficit, which puts him against our recorded 1.8–2.7 g/kg cutting figure. He treats the upper half of that range as unnecessary.

## Overreach to note
The "45 studies, none found benefits above 1.6" claim is the strongest single assertion in the video and it rests on an unpublished in-house collection inside a paid course — it cannot be checked. The methodological criticisms of the 3.1 g/kg paper are well-specified and mostly fair; the confidence with which he dismisses the entire result is somewhat more than his own argument supports, since he concedes the analysis does establish a real positive association above 1.3 g/kg.
