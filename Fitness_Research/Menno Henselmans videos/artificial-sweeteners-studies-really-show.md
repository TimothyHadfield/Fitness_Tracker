# Artificial Sweeteners: Where the Scare Headlines Come From

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Artificial Sweeteners: What Do Studies Really Show? [2025 Review]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wRW0vFUhu8Y)) · 2026-01-20 · 6 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- A large, conflict-of-interest-free **umbrella review** of low- and no-calorie sweeteners — pooling roughly **2.8 million participants** across cohort studies and ~25,000 in controlled trials — finds the apparent harm signal is an artefact of study design.
- **Naive observational studies do show a negative association. Bias-adjusted analyses do not.** His explanation is reverse causality: people who are obese or diabetic start using sweeteners *because* of their health problems.
- **Controlled human trials consistently find no negative effects.** Against water or a neutral comparator, no harm and no benefit. Against sugar, positive effects — not because sweeteners are healthy, but because they displace calories.
- **Animal studies are structurally guaranteed to find harm**, because dose-escalation designs go up until something breaks and then apply a 100-fold safety margin. The human-equivalent doses are absurd.
- **Aspartame** breaks down into phenylalanine (50%), aspartic acid (40%) and methanol (10%) — all compounds you already get from milk, eggs, tomatoes and meat. It is not stored and does not accumulate. A meta-analysis of 101 articles found little to no effect short- or long-term.
- Sweeteners covered: aspartame, sucralose, stevia, acesulfame K, saccharin. **Sugar alcohols were excluded** because they carry non-trivial calories.

## Why the literature looks contradictory
His central methodological point is that the contradiction is between *study types*, not between *studies*. Cohort data without adjustment says one thing; the same data bias-adjusted, and randomised trials, say another. There is no genuine disagreement to resolve — there is a hierarchy being ignored.

What he adds to that, more combatively, is **who is ignoring it**. He singles out the **World Health Organization**, arguing its recent warning against low-calorie sweeteners leaned on exactly the naive association studies the umbrella review discounts. That is a direct swing at an official body, and it is the position in this video most worth flagging as his rather than the field's — the WHO's own reasoning is more qualified than his one-line characterisation allows.

His framing of animal research is similarly sharp: it is "literally designed to find health problems". That is a rhetorical description of standard dose-finding toxicology, which exists to establish a no-effect threshold, not to indict the compound. The practical conclusion he draws — that finding harm at extreme doses tells you nothing about normal intakes — is sound; the implied bad faith is his own colour.

## The case for sweeteners being a net positive
He is careful about the mechanism of benefit. Sweeteners aren't good for you. They **create room in an energy budget**, and the health effects follow from the resulting deficit and fat loss. Beyond that, he points to research finding that satisfying a sweet tooth **helps with diet adherence** — if a diet soda means skipping dessert, that is a real dietary win, not a physiological one.

His closing note is a fair one: the "eat what a hunter-gatherer would recognise" heuristic is generally useful, but **unnatural does not entail unhealthy**, and sweeteners are the case where the heuristic misfires. Refusing sweeteners and drinking sugar instead is the worse of the two options.
