# The Ceiling On Sets Per Muscle Per Session

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is the max volume you should do per workout" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SzGPYsyzuLs)) · 2025-07-08 · 9 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- A new meta-analysis of **67 studies** modelled the per-session dose-response curve and put the muscle-growth plateau at **11 sets per muscle group per session** (fractional volume counting). Henselmans's own earlier read of the same literature gave **9–13 sets**.
- For **strength**, the plateau came far earlier: **2 direct sets** of the specific exercise, or **3.5 sets** once fractional volume is counted. Strength wants intensity and frequency, not per-session volume.
- His own recommendation is well below the meta-analytic ceiling: **once you're into the 6–11 set range for a muscle in one session, move the extra sets to another day.** He cites James Krieger's unpublished meta-analysis, which found a true plateau at **6 sets per muscle per workout**.
- Practical consequence: **body-part splits and bro splits are probably suboptimal**, because they force you past the per-session ceiling. Full-body training is the easiest way to spread volume out, and in every study he's aware of it matches or beats a body-part split.

## What the new meta-analysis actually found
[00:31] The curve is the familiar sharply-diminishing-returns shape. The first few sets buy most of the growth; beyond that, extra sets still add a little, but the return per set collapses. The novelty is that the plateau arrives low enough to matter for how you lay out a week — this is a limit on **maximum productive session volume**, not the weekly maximum adaptive volume that Israetel's framework popularised.

[07:37] The definition of the plateau is worth knowing, because it is a convention rather than a physical wall: 11 sets is the point at which additional sets no longer had a **greater-than-50% probability of exceeding the smallest detectable effect size**. Henselmans says so himself — it means "low likelihood of further gains, and very small if any", not "zero gains after set 11".

[03:04] His own stated caveat: he'd like the analysis rerun **controlled for total weekly volume**, which it was not. That is a real limitation — a per-session dose-response drawn across studies with different weekly volumes cannot fully separate the two.

## Why a per-session ceiling is plausible
[03:33] Two mechanisms, both offered as supporting reasoning rather than proof.

**Muscle protein synthesis saturates.** The same "muscle full" logic that limits how much protein one meal can use appears to apply to training stimulus. The direct evidence he leans on is **rodent** work (Ogasawara 2017), where MPS plateaus at around 10 sets while **protein breakdown keeps climbing**. He explicitly warns against reading the exact numbers across species — but he still presents the ~10-set correspondence as "very interesting" confirmation, which is more weight than one rat study can carry. Past the crossover, net balance flattens and could go negative: junk volume, then overreaching.

**Neuromuscular fatigue degrades every later set.** [06:05] Activation and force output fall as the session goes on. On a ten-set bench day, the tenth set is not the same stimulus as the first — you drop the weight or drop from ten reps to five. Since accumulated mechanical tension is the best driver of growth, a five-rep set at reduced load is roughly half the objective stimulus of the first set. Spreading those sets across the week gets you the same set count at much higher quality.

## Where he lands relative to the number he quotes
The interesting tension in this video is that he reports 11 as the meta-analytic answer and then declines to use it. His working recommendation is nearer Krieger's 6, with 6–11 treated as the band where you should start redistributing rather than adding. He also concedes [07:06] that the true figure varies by individual, by proximity to failure and by how hard you train — the study found no such moderator, but he presumes one exists.
