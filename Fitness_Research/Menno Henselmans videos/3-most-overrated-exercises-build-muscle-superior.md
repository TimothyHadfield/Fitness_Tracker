# Three Overrated Exercises: Deadlifts, Rows and Cable Crossovers

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "These are the 3 most overrated exercises to build muscle (with superior alternatives)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RHwnvh-uJt8)) · 2025-09-02 · 14 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Deadlifts** are concentric-dominant with no stretch reflex, a plate-limited range of motion, and isometric upper-body work at short-to-medium lengths. Jack of all trades, master of none. Replace with **Romanian deadlifts (or good mornings, 45° hypers, pull-throughs) plus separate quad work**.
- He *defends* deadlifts on one point: **the "CNS frying" claim is not supported**. Multiple studies find deadlifts no harder to recover from and no more neuromuscularly fatiguing than squats or bench presses.
- **Rows** have a resistance curve that is the inverse of the strength curve — only the traps see high tension at long lengths, and even they are limited by the weak shortened position. Replace with **pulldowns/pull-ups, high rows, sidelying reverse flyes, lat prayers, diagonal or leaned-forward rows**.
- **High-to-low and low-to-high cable crossovers** turn a fly into an extreme incline or decline fly. Extreme angles *remove* a pec region rather than emphasising it. Use a **horizontal fly** — Bayesian, dumbbell, or pec deck.
- Rows and crossovers are where he collides hardest with the Nippard material in this library. See the comparison section.

## Deadlifts
Two structural complaints. First, **concentric dominance**: the lift starts from a dead stop, so there is no stretch reflex to potentiate the concentric, and eccentric contractions generate higher active and passive tension (titin spring-loading; actin-myosin cross-bridges cleaved mechanically rather than rebuilt biochemically). You can lower the weight under control, but most people don't, and you still lose tension at the floor — the longest muscle lengths and the transition that matters most.

Second, **range of motion is set by the radius of a 45 lb plate**. Deficit deadlifts help slightly, but the quads still never reach near-maximal length, and involving the quads means the hamstrings don't either. All the upper-body musculature works isometrically at medium-to-short lengths.

His fix: split quad work from posterior chain work. Romanian deadlifts suffer neither problem, and he calls them superior for every muscle group except the quads — which conventional deadlifts weren't good for anyway. Good mornings if you want less grip and trap involvement; 45° hypers or pull-throughs for less upper body altogether.

On CNS fatigue he pushes back. What people feel is high mental effort, anxiety and focus, plus cardiorespiratory stress and probably connective tissue strain — not a fried nervous system. He grants this could still make the stimulus-to-fatigue ratio poor, since muscular strain is no better than other exercises while connective-tissue and mental strain is higher.

## Rows
Rows train biceps, middle and lower traps, lats, sometimes erector spinae, and rear delts — none of them optimally.

The core problem is that the **resistance curve is the inverse of the strength curve**. You feel a row in the shortened position: you fail on the last inch to your chest while still able to grind reps just short of it. In the stretched position only the traps see high tension, and they can't be loaded properly because the shortened position caps the weight.

The mechanism: the lats are a primary mover only until the elbow reaches the line of the body. Past that they lose leverage (which is also why the lats slightly assist out of the bottom of a bench press) and the comparatively weak rear delts and traps drive the movement — so the weak link caps the load for every other muscle involved. For biceps, he cites a study finding dumbbell rows produced roughly **half the growth of curls**.

Alternatives: **high rows** if you want a row, taking lats and biceps out to focus on rear delts and traps, plus **sidelying reverse flyes** for rear delts at long lengths. For lats he calls isolation work like **lat prayers** unmatched, and among compounds prefers **pulldowns and pull-ups** — lean back for some eccentric overload, still get rear delt and trap involvement, and the resistance curve matches the strength curve. He also likes **motorcycle rows**, heavily leaned-forward Arnold-style rows, and **diagonal pulls**.

## Cable crossovers
Research finds a high incline or decline **takes a pec region out of the movement rather than emphasising another** — an incline press doesn't train the upper pecs more than a flat press, it trains the lower pecs less.

A **low-to-high crossover is therefore an extreme incline fly**: you remove the lower pecs, don't improve upper-pec training much, and substantially increase front delt involvement — and bodybuilders already show overdeveloped front delts relative to side and rear. (He says front raises would themselves be a serious contender for this list.)

A **high-to-low crossover is an extreme decline fly**, which he considers worse: upper pecs are usually the lagging region and lower pecs rarely are — and it isn't even good at isolating them, since a full stretch requires the elbows above shoulder height in internal rotation, which most people's shoulders won't tolerate.

Use a horizontal fly — Bayesian fly, dumbbell fly, or pec deck, citing research that pec deck work grows lower and upper pecs equally. With only fixed pulleys: lie on a bench between the low ones, or from the high ones bend over roughly **90°** to get 90° of shoulder flexion. Most people bend about 30°, which isn't enough.

## Where this contradicts the Nippard material in this library
The two sources are directly opposed on two of the three exercises.

**Deadlifts — broad agreement.** Nippard puts the conventional deadlift at **C tier as a back builder** (`Jeff Nippard videos/back-exercises-ranked.md`) and #14 of 25 in `only-25-exercises-need.md`, explicitly "not optimal for pure hypertrophy," kept in for movement-pattern and strength value. He ranks the **RDL #4 of 25** and prefers it to the conventional for being "much less fatiguing for equal or greater glute/hamstring/erector stimulus" — Menno's conclusion, reached from biomechanics. Nippard would keep deadlifts for erector thickness and strength; Menno wouldn't, but the hypertrophy verdicts converge.

**Rows — sharp disagreement.** Nippard's back tier list crowns the **chest-supported row S tier and the single best back exercise overall**, with close- and wide-grip cable rows and the Meadows row also S tier. `back-width-vs-thickness.md` states flatly that "everyone should have a row in the program," citing Lehman's finding that the seated row beat the lat pulldown for both lat and trap activation. Menno says almost the opposite: rows are a jack-of-all-trades he is "not a big fan of," and pulldowns/pull-ups plus lat isolation are the better lat stimulus. They do agree on the biceps point — the Nippard library records the same curls-beat-rows finding.

**Cable crossovers — disagreement on the mechanism.** Nippard ranks the standing cable crossover **A tier** and the seated cable pec fly **S tier**, his favourite chest isolation movement. More directly, he *programs the exact thing Menno says makes no sense*: a crossover ladder with cables low to bias the upper chest and high to bias the lower chest (`best-way-isolate-chest-growth.md`, `best-full-upper-body-workout-max-muscle.md`, `push-pull-legs-routine.md`). Menno's position is that changing the angle removes a region rather than biasing one, so the ladder buys nothing. Two partial overlaps: Nippard ranks **decline pressing only B tier**, calls it his least favourite angle and says lower pecs rarely need emphasis — supporting Menno's criticism of high-to-low specifically; and Menno's alternatives (Bayesian fly, dumbbell fly, pec deck) are exactly Nippard's S/A-tier flyes. The disagreement is narrower than it looks: it is about **extreme cable angles**, not flyes.

**Front raises — full agreement.** Nippard ranks front raises **D tier and "worst of the worst"** in `shoulder-exercises-ranked.md` and #3 worst overall in `top-10-worst-exercises.md`, on Menno's exact reasoning: redundant with pressing, front delts already overdeveloped relative to side and rear.

## Where the argument outruns the evidence
The three verdicts are reached almost entirely by biomechanical reasoning. Only two cited studies test the claims directly — the rows-vs-curls biceps comparison and the pec deck study showing equal upper/lower growth. There is no training study comparing deadlifts to RDLs for hypertrophy, none comparing rows to pulldowns for lat growth, and none comparing crossover angles. The CNS-fatigue rebuttal is the best-evidenced claim in the video.
