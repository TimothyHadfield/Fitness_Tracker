# The 5x5 Texas Method, Audited by Volume per Muscle

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The 5x5 Texas Method program review [science]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=atWXBA_0mas)) · 2023-01-16 · 24 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- He does not recommend the program **for almost anyone**. His verdict: useful as a template an intermediate powerlifter builds around, not as a program to run.
- The central failure is **volume balance**. Weekly volume for the powerlifts is adequate (6–9 sets each, deadlift excepted), but biceps get three sets of bodyweight chin-ups and that is all; rear and side delts, traps, hamstring knee flexion and the long head of triceps are close to untrained.
- The program feels brutal not because of muscular overload but because it is **almost entirely low-rep work on the same few technical lifts** — that punishes connective tissue and costs mental energy while delivering low volume per muscle.
- **Prescribing 5×5 at 90–95% of 5RM ignores individual differences in the load–rep relationship**, which differ systematically between men and women. And with **no standardised rest interval** (Rippetoe's figure is 8–10 minutes), lifters manufacture fake progress by resting longer over time.
- He rejects Rippetoe's premise that **"size is a side effect of strength"** — the causality mostly runs the other way.
- He rejects **Olympic lifts for non-weightlifters** entirely: high technique cost, high injury risk, little eccentric work, limited range of motion, poor return for hypertrophy or strength.

## Who the program is for, and its stated premise
Rippetoe positions it for intermediates who have stalled on something like Starting Strength, and warns it "will overtrain your ass quickly". Structure is three days: **Monday volume, Wednesday recovery, Friday intensity**.

Rippetoe's framing is that strength gains are the basis of size, and size is a side effect of strength. Henselmans says this is close to backwards. Strength is the product of both morphological adaptation (muscle size) and neurological adaptation — the car engine and the skill of the driver. You can build substantial strength with little size via the neural route. You cannot build size without gaining strength, because an increase in physiological cross-sectional area necessarily increases force output at equal skill.

He also rejects the idea that 5×5 is an optimised combination. Total weekly volume per muscle group is the main determinant of hypertrophy, and strength is more exercise-specific — so whether 5×5 is right depends on frequency and exercise selection. His broader point: 5×5, 10×10, 3×3 all look like round numbers chosen because they sound good rather than the output of any optimisation.

## Monday — volume day
Squat 5×5 at 90–95% of 5RM; bench or overhead press 5×5 at 90% of 5RM; deadlift 1×5 at 90% of 1RM.

**Load prescription.** Percentages of 5RM assume a shared work capacity that does not exist. Research shows large individual variation in how many reps a given relative load allows, and **women typically complete more reps at a given intensity than men** — so the program is implicitly written for men. He says some clients could do 5×5 with their 5RM while he personally would need his 8RM.

**Rest intervals.** None specified in the workout; Rippetoe elsewhere allows up to 8–10 minutes. That is both a very wide range and a very long rest. The failure mode he describes: a lifter starts at 5×5 with their 8RM, adds weight, and quietly extends rest until they are effectively doing five sets of a near-5RM. Without a standardised rest interval, rep-across-sets progression is not measuring what it claims to.

**The deadlift's single set.** Rippetoe justifies it on the grounds that deadlifts are uniquely hard to recover from. Henselmans agrees with the practice and disagrees with the mechanism: two or three studies now find **no difference in neuromuscular recovery between deadlift, squat and bench press**. His alternative explanation is connective tissue and joint stress, plus the particular fatigue cost of isometric contraction and mental load. Either way, limiting deadlift volume is prudent — but the missing volume should be made up **with other exercises**, not simply dropped.

## Wednesday — the "recovery" day
Squat 2×5 at 80% of Monday's weight; overhead press 3×5 (or bench 3×5 at 90%); chin-ups 3 sets bodyweight to failure with 5 min rest; back extensions or glute-ham raises 5×10.

He calls this day arbitrary:
- **Squats at 80% for 2×5** are almost certainly not a growth stimulus for an intermediate — at best technique and speed work.
- **The press at 90% for 3×5** is a real stimulus, so the day's "recovery" label applies to some muscle groups and not others.
- **Back extensions or glute-ham raises are not interchangeable.** GHRs train knee flexion (short head of biceps femoris, gastrocnemius) and load the erectors lightly; back extensions load either the erectors or the posterior chain depending on execution. Different muscles, so you cannot substitute one for the other on volume grounds. Rippetoe describes them as optional active rest — but **5×10 glute-ham raises is advanced-level work for most intermediates**, not active rest.
- **Chin-ups at "bodyweight to failure"** produce wildly different stimuli depending on the lifter. A 300 lb powerlifter may manage a couple of reps or none. He argues the program should specify an RM target or a lat pulldown substitute.
- **Alternating overhead press with bench press week to week** is treated as substitution, but they train different muscles (side delts vs pecs), so weekly volume for those muscles swings.

## Friday — intensity day
Squat worked up to a true 5RM (1×5); bench or press likewise; then power cleans 5×3 or power snatches 6×2 for explosiveness, with no load specified.

Because bench/press alternates by day rather than by workout, he expects most weeks to be lopsided toward one or the other, and would alternate by workout instead.

On Rippetoe's line that "if you want to call yourself a lifter you need to know how to clean and snatch": he takes the opposite position — **if you don't compete in Olympic weightlifting you have no business doing the Olympic lifts.** Highly technical, minimal eccentric loading, incomplete range of motion, performed fatigued at the end of a session, relatively injurious, and poor return for either hypertrophy or maximal strength.

## What he likes
Two things, both structural rather than specific:
1. **It has a structure at all.** He treats having a structured program as a major discriminator between lifters who reach an advanced level and those who don't.
2. **It forces progressive overload** through explicit rep and load targets, which many lifters need to actually train hard.

## What he dislikes
- **Volume imbalance** (his main objection). Powerlifts are covered; nearly everything else is not. Front delts are hammered every week by pressing while side and rear delts are barely touched, producing the tapering-to-the-back delt shape rather than a rounded one. Biceps, traps, hamstring knee flexion and the long head of triceps are effectively untrained. Regional hypertrophy is not considered anywhere.
- **Almost exclusively low-rep work.** He argues combining high and low rep work is likely better even for strength, citing Russian work suggesting high and low reps activate different growth pathways, plus Western studies showing at least a trend favouring the combination. He also notes daily undulating periodisation is well established for strength — and the Texas Method uses neither.
- **The perceived difficulty is misattributed.** Volume per muscle is low, so the program is not neuromuscularly overloading. The strain comes from repeatedly loading the same joint-unfriendly lifts at low reps plus the mental cost of technical lifts. His summary: **"more inadequate than excessive"** in terms of actual muscular stimulus.

**Where the argument outruns the evidence:** the claims about high-plus-low-rep combinations and about Russian research on separate growth pathways are given without citation and described only as "a trend" — they are the thinnest support in a video that is otherwise mechanistic argument rather than study-by-study evidence.
