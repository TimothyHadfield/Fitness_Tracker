# Three Testosterone Myths: It Doesn't Amplify Training, You Can't Feel It, and Boosters Don't Work

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "3 Testosterone myths every man must know" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MKnkRSOONoA)) · 2024-12-27 · 13 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Testosterone raises muscle mass **on its own**, dose-dependently, even within the normal range. But its effect and training's effect are **additive, not synergistic** — there is no interaction. Higher testosterone does not make you gain faster from lifting.
- Baseline testosterone in natural lifters does not predict rate of muscle or strength gain. Men have roughly **15× the testosterone of women** yet the *percentage* rate of gains from training is the same.
- In Bhasin's 600 mg/week trial, previously trained men who took testosterone and **stopped training for 10 weeks** gained slightly more than men who kept training without it (differences mostly non-significant; only squat strength favoured the training groups).
- Blinded participants **cannot tell they are on testosterone**. No mood, cognitive or psychiatric effects at high doses; roid rage is largely a placebo/expectation effect, with a susceptible minority the exception. **Libido** is the one outcome with strong support, mainly going from low to normal.
- A 2023 systematic review found most testosterone boosters **fail to raise total testosterone**. Even the best case is a 1–2 lb fat-free mass gain that disappears when you stop.
- The only levers worth pulling are the ones you should pull anyway: sleep, stress management, adequate micronutrients (he names magnesium and zinc), a decent diet, and exercise.

## Myth 1: testosterone amplifies your training
He concedes the obvious immediately — give people testosterone and they gain muscle, more at higher doses, and this holds even inside the physiological range (Wang's transdermal gel study in barely-hypogonadal men showed better recomposition at higher doses). His claim is narrower: that effect is **standalone** and does not multiply the gains you get from lifting.

Three lines of evidence:

**Interaction studies.** The famous Bhasin design had four groups — training or not, 600 mg/week testosterone or not. Both training and testosterone produced growth, but summing the two single-factor effects reproduced the combined group's result almost exactly. No interaction. The striking corollary is that previously trained men who took testosterone and took ten weeks off matched or slightly beat men who trained naturally, with the exception of squat strength — which he attributes to the squat's technical demands.

**Baseline testosterone.** Studies looking at whether higher natural testosterone predicts faster gains generally find it does not.

**Sex differences.** A large difference in testosterone with no difference in relative rate of gain. He is careful to separate rate from ceiling: maximum muscular potential in women does appear somewhat lower, but that's a different question.

The practical implication he draws is blunt: people on gear "just grow", and how they train matters less to their rate of gain than they think.

## Myth 2: you can feel your testosterone
In blinded trials participants did not know which group they were in, and reported no mood or cognitive changes. He extends this to roid rage: when people are blinded, testosterone does not make them more aggressive. A minority do experience manic or aggressive symptoms, and he does not claim to know why. He notes most of this research is on testosterone specifically — trenbolone and nandrolone plausibly differ.

The mechanism he proposes is expectation: people expect to feel more masculine, so they do, and behave accordingly. He backs this with coaching anecdote (clients raving about black-market gear that turned out to be bunk) and with a placebo-steroid study where well-trained collegiate lifters given fake Dianabol gained **8–15% strength in seven weeks** versus **2%** training as usual — from a group already benching close to three plates.

He also makes a point that lands squarely on this library's existing position: **body fat percentage matters more than testosterone** for how you feel. Coached athletes on gear still report flat libido in contest prep.

## Myth 3: testosterone boosters
A 2023 systematic review concluded most fail to increase total testosterone. Individual positive studies exist, but the pattern is that they don't replicate in healthy individuals, or carry sponsorship bias. D-aspartic acid is his cautionary tale: an early positive finding drove a supplement boom, later work found the effect trivial in healthy men, and animal research indicated testicular toxicity. He extends this to the sector generally, citing work on undeclared ingredients and hormone spiking in sports supplements.

His closing arithmetic is the strongest argument: even if a booster worked, the increase stays inside the natural range, which by the dose-response literature buys roughly 1–2 lb of fat-free mass — and by myth 1, none of it compounds with your training. You would have to keep taking it forever.

## Where this sits against the library
He **agrees** with our existing position that acute post-training testosterone spikes don't drive hypertrophy (McCall's acute-and-chronic hormonal response study is in his reference list), that natural levers are all small, and that body fat and calories dominate. He goes **further** than anything currently recorded here: not just that acute spikes are irrelevant, but that even large *chronic* differences in testosterone — supraphysiological ones included — fail to amplify training-driven gains.

## Overreach to note
The framing "testosterone helps you build muscle is a myth" is contradicted by his own first thirty seconds; the defensible claim is the much narrower "testosterone does not amplify training-driven gains." The no-interaction finding rests heavily on one 10-week trial, and he narrates several *non-significant* differences in it directionally, which is the same move he criticises other people for.
