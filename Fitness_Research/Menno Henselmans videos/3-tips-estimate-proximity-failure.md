# Three Tests for How Close to Failure You Actually Are

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "3 Tips to estimate your proximity to failure" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S3_NTXC4rhQ)) · 2023-02-05 · 6 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- His premise, from the description: **most people don't train nearly as close to failure as they think.**
- A study had experienced coaches watch training videos and estimate reps in reserve. Their accuracy from video was **roughly the same as lifters' accuracy about their own sets** — so proximity to failure is externally visible, and **you can audit your own sets on film**. Lifters who have never watched their own video simply do not know what they are doing.
- Test 1, **facial expression and redness**, correlates with genuine effort but is crude. Tests 2 and 3 are objective and he favours them.
- Test 2: **true failure is preceded by gradual velocity loss.** If bar speed isn't declining across the set, you are not near failure.
- Test 3: **true failure occurs in the sticking region**, not at a random point in the range of motion. Failing anywhere else means technical or motivational failure, not momentary muscular failure.

## Test 1 — the face
Research shows facial expression and how red someone turns tracks actual training effort. Stay expressionless through a set — "full RoboCop" — and you probably have more in the tank. Full tomato face is a decent sign the effort was real. He treats this as super rough and moves on.

## Test 2 — velocity loss
Plot bar velocity across a set (he shows a 6RM bench in trained lifters) and you see a progressive slowdown culminating in task failure. This is the most reliable of the three: with individual velocity data, research can estimate proximity to failure very accurately. The visible version is simply whether reps are getting slower.

**Exception:** lifters with a high proportion of fast-twitch fibres — many powerlifters and Olympic weightlifters — look explosive right up to the point where the rep doesn't happen. Measured velocity still shows the decline, just more subtly.

## Test 3 — where you fail
Failure is not a whole-body systemic event, as most lifters intuit. It is local and mechanical: **force production at a specific joint angle falls below the force the movement requires there.** Because a lifter's strength curve and an exercise's resistance curve are mismatched at a predictable point, failure has a predictable location.

- **Squat:** most fail at a hip angle of roughly **90-100 degrees**, about halfway up. Getting stuck in the hole is usually technical. Almost nobody fails near the top — if you do, it is volitional failure or a technique fault.
- **Bench press:** most fail as **the elbow crosses the body**; past that point most can lock out. Failing with the bar on the chest is more likely technical than neuromuscular.
- **Deadlift:** genuinely contested, with studies disagreeing; some report difficulty above the knees. His view is that most lifters have no sticking point at all — the bar either leaves the floor or it doesn't, and if it leaves they lock it out. Trained powerlifters show one more often, which he puts down to anthropometry.

The point is not the exact angle but the **consistency**: your sticking point should be in the same place every time. If your failure point moves around, effort or technique is the variable, not fatigue.

## Caveats
The deadlift section is his coaching observation against a mixed literature, and he says so. The velocity criterion is the one with real measurement behind it; the facial cue he rates as barely usable. None of this yields an RIR number — it separates "genuinely near failure" from "not really," which given the size of the typical self-estimation error is the more useful distinction anyway.
