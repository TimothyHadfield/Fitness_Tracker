# What Squats Actually Train, and Why Variations Barely Matter

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Squats don't train what you think they train" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t4v_iuEcMUM)) · 2025-06-10 · 12 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Squats train the **soleus, glutes, adductor magnus, the vasti of the quads, and the erector spinae**. They effectively do **not** train the gastrocnemius, the hamstrings, or the rectus femoris.
- The common thread is **biarticulate conflict**: a muscle that helps at one joint and drags you back down at the other gets little recruitment — gastrocnemius, hamstrings and rectus femoris alike.
- **The adductor magnus is a major, unrecognised hip extensor** in the squat — a new study proposes renaming it "extensor magnus."
- **High bar vs low bar vs front squat makes little difference to hypertrophy.** No significant EMG differences in target musculature, and no significant growth differences in long-term studies between squats and leg presses, front vs back squats, back squats vs single-leg squats, or lunges vs squats.
- **Feel is not activation.** Hip thrusts and squats produced equal glute growth in all regions despite every participant reporting the hip thrust "activated" their glutes far more.
- Tentative and new: **load may shift emphasis.** Quad force output stays high at any intensity, but glute contribution rises substantially with heavier loads — roughly twice the increase seen for quads across 70-90% of 1RM.

## The muscle-by-muscle audit
**Calves.** The soleus is trained reasonably well, at least in a deep squat where the knee travels forward and the ankle moves. The gastrocnemius is not: it plantarflexes but also flexes the knee, so contracting it would help push the knee back while pulling you deeper into the hole. EMG, biomechanics and long-term growth data agree it grows far less from squatting than quads or glutes.

**Hamstrings.** The same conflict — hip extensors, which squats need, but knee flexors, which pulls you back down. EMG activity and long-term growth are both very low. His recommendation is blunt: **do not count hamstrings as trained by squats at all.**

**Glutes.** Uncontested — the primary hip extensor, highly active across EMG, biomechanics and training studies. He answers the "but I don't feel my glutes" objection with his own hip thrust vs squat trial: equal glute hypertrophy in all regions, despite unanimous reports that the hip thrust felt far more activating ([Plotkin 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37877099/)); plus a study finding little correlation between subjective and objective muscle activity ([Sevilmiş 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/00315125241279086), on which he is a co-author).

**Adductor magnus.** The genuinely underrated one — a very large inner-thigh muscle running front to back, contributing substantial force. A recent paper concluded its contribution "has often been overlooked" and proposed renaming it *extensor magnus*, its hip-extension role possibly exceeding its adduction role ([Takahashi 2025](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00600.2024)).

**Quads.** Trained well, but not entirely. The vasti get the quad sweep and, in his account, the teardrop. The **rectus femoris** is barely recruited and barely grows, being a knee extensor *and* a hip flexor. For that, he says, you need leg extensions.

**Erector spinae.** Trained effectively despite contracting mostly isometrically — lifters who squat and deadlift heavy tend to get decent lower-back development without dedicated work.

> **Library note:** this library records that squat-only training produced no vastus medialis *or* rectus femoris growth in one trial. He agrees on rectus femoris and its mechanism, but disagrees on vastus medialis — he credits squats with the teardrop, and elsewhere calls VMO-targeting via narrow stance a debunked myth.

## Variations: the mainstream position he rejects
Conventional wisdom says upright/high bar squats favour quads and hip-dominant/low bar squats favour glutes. Research does not support this, he says. There *are* real biomechanical differences that change joint loading and slightly change range of motion, but the differences in force production and range are too small to meaningfully change hypertrophy over short periods. It is the same movement pattern with the same muscles; bar position changes leverage — how much you can lift, how far you lean — not which muscles do the work, especially close to failure where almost everything contracts to its full potential.

The evidence: EMG showing no significant differences in target musculature between front, high bar and low bar squats, plus long-term studies finding no significant hypertrophy differences across squats vs leg presses, front vs back squats, back squats vs bodyweight single-leg squats, and lunges vs squats. He does not oversell it — **all these studies have significant limitations and none is excellent**, with only the front-vs-back squat study singled out as well conducted, and a year of well-trained lifting might yet reveal a few percent. The one exception he grants: single-leg work grows the erector spinae less, which can be a feature with a back injury.

## The new rep-range finding
A study modelling force output in high-level powerlifters found quad force stays very high at any intensity, while **glute contribution rises substantially with load** — about twice the increase seen for quads across 70-90% of 1RM. This fits the forward lean and good-morning drift on heavy squats, which he calls natural and often efficient when not excessive.

His caveats are extensive and appropriate: the modelling carries many assumptions, and crucially **only a single repetition was performed at each percentage of 1RM**. What happens across a full set — where near failure it becomes "all hands on deck" — is untested. The speculative extension, labelled as such, is that high-rep squats might favour quads while heavy squats fully engage glutes; since glutes and quads usually grow about equally, he files this as fine-tuning for a future long-term study.

For powerlifters he is firmer: a heavy squat and a light squat are technically slightly different exercises because of the forward lean, so strength-focused lifters should spend most of their time at one to five reps near 1RM rather than at 60%.
