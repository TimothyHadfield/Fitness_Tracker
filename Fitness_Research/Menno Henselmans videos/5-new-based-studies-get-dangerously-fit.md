# Five 2025 Studies: Fasted Training, Steps, Meal Frequency, Fasting And Protein Safety

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "5 New Based Studies to Get Dangerously Fit [2025]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=revJj8JnG8Y)) · 2025-10-21 · 9 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Fasted training:** a meta-analysis found no significant differences in muscle growth or strength, and greater fat loss when fasted — but the fat-loss edge vanished in subgroup analysis, and **every effect size for growth and strength moderately favoured training fed**. Only four studies, all with major caveats.
- **Steps:** benefits across dementia, depression and all-cause mortality, with sharp diminishing returns and an **inflection around 5,000–7,000 steps/day**. He thinks chasing a daily step target is high-effort, low-reward for most people.
- **Meal frequency:** no differences in body composition or strength between protein spread over **three versus five meals** when total protein was matched. His rule of thumb: **three whole-food meals a day is enough**, four or more may add a little, **two is probably not enough**.
- **Fasting for health:** over a **full year**, an 8–10 hour eating window produced no advantage over the same energy deficit without fasting, in weight loss or inflammation markers. Fasting is a compliance tool, not a mechanism.
- **High protein and longevity:** in ~16,000 US adults followed to 2006, **no significant relationship between plant, animal or total protein intake and mortality of any kind, in any age group** — with a hint of *lower* cancer mortality at higher intakes.

## 1. Is it okay to train fasted?
[00:07] The meta-analysis was limited to **four** studies, several on Ramadan fasting specifically or in non-serious trainees, with volume load often equated. His reading: people eat slightly less when training fasted, which explains the fat-loss trend and the mildly worse muscle outcomes.

He then adds unpublished work from his own team, not yet in the meta-analysis: in serious lifters they found **a real difference in total training performance that widened over time**, associated with worse strength gains on some measures for the fasted group. This is worth flagging — the strongest evidence he offers against fasted training is his own unpublished study, which nobody can check. He still concludes fairly: if you prefer training fasted, the difference isn't big.

## 2. Optimal step count
[02:15] A meta-analysis of **31 studies**. More steps associate with better physical and mental health on essentially every marker, but the curve flattens hard at **5–7k/day**, particularly in younger people and those already exercising — the health benefits of steps and of training overlap, and there's a ceiling on how healthy you can get.

His caveats are the useful part. This is **associative** data, so no causation. Steps are a crude proxy for total physical activity. And behaviourally, a hard daily target that has you walking at 10pm out of obligation is questionable on both benefit and sustainability. He'd rather you make activity structurally easier: a nicer dog-walking route, cycling to work, a standing desk. If you want a number, **7,000** is defensible.

## 3. Optimal meal frequency
[04:11] Design flaw he names himself: both groups ate five meals, with most protein concentrated into either three or five of them. He'd have preferred three meals versus five. Total protein was matched; nothing differed after 8 weeks.

He extends it with the Norwegian Meal Frequency Project in strength-trained individuals and a Japanese study in competitive rowers. The reason old bodybuilding lore demanded six meals: those studies used **whey shakes**, which are absorbed fast and can't sustain post-meal muscle protein synthesis as long as a whole-food meal with fat, fibre and intact protein. Hence three whole-food meals a day is enough for most of the available gains — with a possible small benefit from four-plus based on MPS data, and two probably falling short. He cites a comparable study where protein in three of three meals showed a strong trend over protein in two.

## 4. Fasting for health
[06:06] A 12-month study found **no differences in weight loss or inflammatory markers** between an 8–10 hour eating window and a matched-deficit non-fasting diet. This is the same conclusion as his nutrition-myths video, and consistent with the wider literature: the benefits attributed to fasting are mediated by energy restriction. He notes he personally does intermittent fasting — because he isn't hungry in the mornings, not because it does anything extra.

## 5. Is high protein bad for longevity?
[06:53] The concern is chronic anabolism, IGF-1 and mTOR activation raising cancer risk. The study analysed dietary data from just under **16,000 US individuals** (late 1980s to early 1990s) against death records to **2006**, and found no significant relationship for plant, animal or total protein with any mortality outcome, with some signal of lower cancer mortality at higher intakes.

He is upfront that this is associative and confounding-prone. His mechanistic counter is the more interesting bit: **anabolism is tissue-selective and nutrition is mostly permissive** — high protein plus lifting elevates MPS in muscle; it doesn't make your organs grow indefinitely. He notes plant protein has previously been associated with lower mortality.

The rhetorical flourish about "protein" deriving from Greek *protos*, "of first importance", is etymology, not evidence, and he leans on it as though it were argument.
