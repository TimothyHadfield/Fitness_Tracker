# Four Training Myths: Indirect Volume, Machines, CNS Fatigue, And Moving Volume Between Muscles

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "These 4 broscience myths keep confusing everyone" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gPN2NikDCA4)) · 2024-11-21 · 13 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- There is no such thing as "indirect" volume. A biceps contraction in a row is the same kind of contraction as in a curl. Count contributing exercises as **fractional volume** (e.g. a dumbbell row = ~0.5 biceps sets), not as zero.
- **Machines and free weights are equally effective** for hypertrophy, including fixed-path machines, as long as the movement pattern and musculature match.
- **"CNS fatigue" is mostly mental fatigue.** Central fatigue is rare, and what fatigue there is from lifting is local. Heavy low-rep work causes *less* fatigue than high-rep work; endurance training, not heavy lifting, is what produces genuine central fatigue.
- **You cannot bank volume.** Cutting leg volume does not buy your upper body extra recoverable sets — growth and fatigue are locally regulated. If anything, more lower body training slightly helps upper body gains.

## Myth 1: direct vs indirect exercises
[00:00] Menno's objection is partly semantic and partly substantive. A row creates a demand for elbow flexion, so the elbow flexors contract — nothing about it is "indirect." A 2023 meta-analysis of single-joint vs multi-joint exercise found **no difference in muscle growth between the categories** ([Rosa 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/ssc.0000000000000720)), consistent with earlier work ([Gentil 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4592763/)).

[01:02] His illustration is the hip thrust, widely mislabelled an isolation exercise, which is multi-joint and produces meaningful quad involvement — feet tucked close recruits more quad, feet further out shifts work to the hamstrings, and the glutes get the same job either way ([Plotkin 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10349977/)). "The body is not so inefficient that just because you're also contracting another muscle, one muscle group cannot contract to its full potential."

[02:32] The practical output is fractional volume counting, which he says he has always taught: count a great exercise for a muscle as a full set, a partial contributor as a half or less. He cites a 2024 meta-analysis finding the **volume-growth relationship is significantly stronger when volume is counted fractionally** than when counted as direct-only or all-equal. That reference is the unresolved SportRxiv preprint listed below.

## Myth 2: free weights vs machines
[03:02] Muscles do not care what is generating the resistance, only how much force output the movement demands. A 2021 meta-analysis found free weights and machines **on average equally effective** for hypertrophy ([Heidel 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34609100/)). He pre-empts the objection that lat pulldowns are "not really a machine" by noting fixed-motion machines have also been tested with no difference found. Pulldowns are also his double illustration: a machine, and a compound, and still highly effective for biceps.

## Myth 3: CNS fatigue
[04:34] This is the most combative segment. His position: whenever you hear "CNS fatigue," substitute "mental fatigue" or "I feel tired" and you will usually be more accurate. Fatigue and growth are local. One study concluded force behaviour usually attributed to central fatigue "could be explained solely by peripheral factors" and questioned whether central fatigue needs to exist at all ([Contessa 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26655823/)); a meta-analysis of 52 studies found no general non-local muscle fatigue effect except on endurance outcomes ([Behm 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-021-01456-3)).

[06:36] His intuition pump: you are demonstrably *more* fatigued after a 20RM than after a 3RM, and fatigue tracks total work done. Deadlifts are not special — research on the powerlifts finds they do not differ meaningfully from other exercises in neuromuscular fatigue ([Belcher 2019](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2019-0004); [Pompermayer 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11332-021-00761-8)). What deadlifts *do* demand is technique, concentration and ego, which produces mental exhaustion.

[07:38] The terminological point is the useful one: the motor neuron's cell body sits in the spinal cord, hence "central," but its axon reaches only the fibres it innervates — so the fatigue is extremely local, often sub-muscle. Central nervous system fatigue is largely not central fatigue.

## Myth 4: reallocating volume between muscles
[09:40] If a muscle is below its maximum adaptable volume, adding sets helps it. But taking sets *away* from your legs does not raise your upper body's ceiling, because both growth and fatigue are locally regulated by tension on that fibre. He notes a few studies suggesting lower body training slightly *improves* upper body gains, mediated by hormones — then immediately says higher-quality work generally finds no effect and the mechanism doesn't hold up ([West 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20541030/); [Morton 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4967245/)). The only legitimate version of the trade is time and willpower, not recovery.

## Where the claim runs ahead of the evidence
"There is essentially no central contribution to fatigue in strength training" is stated more absolutely than the cited work supports — Behm's meta-analysis found no *general* non-local effect but did find one for endurance outcomes, and Contessa raises a question rather than settling it. Similarly, the fractional-volume recommendation rests on a preprint that had not passed peer review at recording.
