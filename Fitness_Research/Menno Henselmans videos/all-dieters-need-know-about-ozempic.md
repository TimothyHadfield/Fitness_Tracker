# GLP-1 Drugs for Fat Loss: They Work, and Five Reasons That Isn't the End of It

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "What all dieters need to know about Ozempic" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Py1YuPRbKN8)) · 2024-06-19 · 9 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

> Nothing here is medical advice. GLP-1 receptor agonists are prescription medicines. Whether to start, continue or stop one — and at what dose — is a decision for a doctor, who will also screen for contraindications and interactions the video does not discuss.

## Bottom line
- GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide: Ozempic and Wegovy injectable, Rybelsus oral) **work**: roughly **10% of body weight lost over about 6 months**. The most effective weight-loss treatment short of bariatric surgery.
- **Broadly safe** — serious adverse events are uncommon, and the medical consensus is net positive, since the health gains from the weight loss outweigh the drug's downsides.
- But **most users report side effects**, and the gastrointestinal ones are inseparable from the mechanism.
- **Lean body mass loss is unusually high** in trials — a third to 40% of the weight lost, versus a typical ~25%.
- **Stopping means regaining**: appetite returns to baseline and nothing about the diet has changed.
- His verdict: a **band-aid unless you either change your lifestyle or plan to take the drug indefinitely.**

## How they work
GLP-1 is a hormone produced by the intestines, and these drugs mimic it — his analogy is injected testosterone acting like endogenous testosterone. GLP-1 triggers insulin release, inhibits glucagon, and slows gastric emptying. Together these produce potent appetite suppression: you feel fuller and eat less.

Developed as anti-diabetes medication, they turned out to be so effective for weight loss that they are now marketed for both. He notes the market reaction — manufacturers' share prices have soared, and some investors have grown wary of fitness and adjacent medical sectors on the theory that a weekly injection might resolve the obesity epidemic.

## The five problems

**1. Nutritional deficiencies get worse.** Deficiency rates for iron, vitamin D and magnesium are already high. If someone eats less of the same food without shifting toward better choices, whatever deficiency they had is amplified. Appetite suppression addresses quantity, not quality.

**2. Lean mass loss.** In many trials, a third to 40% of the weight lost is lean body mass, against the roughly 25% seen in the general weight-loss literature and the near-zero achievable with resistance training and adequate protein. He is careful about attribution: the drugs do not inherently cause muscle loss. Heavy appetite suppression produces what amounts to an involuntary crash diet, usually on low protein and without training — a reliable recipe for losing lean tissue. The knock-on effects are lower energy expenditure, plus the plain accounting point that every kilo lost as lean mass is a kilo not lost as fat.

One clarification the video doesn't make: "lean body mass" on DXA includes water, glycogen and organ mass, so the figure overstates pure muscle loss to some degree.

**3. Cost.** Roughly $1,000 per month. Fine if insured, a serious barrier if not. (A 2024 US figure; pricing has moved since.)

**4. Side effects.** Safe is not the same as pleasant. The large majority of users report something — commonly nausea, heartburn, diarrhoea, constipation, occasionally more serious issues. These are hard to engineer away: slowed gastric emptying is both the source of the digestive complaints and part of what produces the appetite suppression. And they are not short-term if the drug is taken long-term.

**5. Regain on discontinuation.** Most people who come off regain the weight. Same failure mode as any fad diet: nothing about food choices has changed, so appetite returns to baseline, intake follows, and body weight settles back at its old equilibrium.

## The steroid analogy, and an inversion
He draws an explicit parallel to anabolic steroids: use them without training and you gain muscle; stop and you lose it. The effect lasts as long as the drug does.

That leads to an inversion he seems to enjoy. Since the drugs suit inherently unsustainable practices, the population they arguably fit is **bodybuilders in contest prep**, rather than the overweight general population they were marketed to. He grants immediately that a "this is cheating" argument applies, as it does to steroids.

## Where he lands, and where the argument thins
His closing advice is to think long term before starting: are you planning to use it for life, do you accept the side effects and cost, and are you going to change your lifestyle? Without lifestyle change you stay on the drug indefinitely to stay lean.

His final line — "if you are going to change your lifestyle, then why do you need the drugs in the first place?" — is rhetorically neat and the weakest part of the video. It assumes the lifestyle change and the drug are alternatives, when the clinical case for these medications is largely that appetite suppression is what makes the change achievable for people for whom it repeatedly wasn't. He also frames the whole video around dieters and physique goals and says almost nothing about the population these drugs are indicated for — type 2 diabetes, severe obesity, established cardiometabolic disease — where the risk-benefit calculation is not the same as for a lifter wanting to get leaner.

**Practically:** the two problems a reader can act on alone are muscle retention and nutrition — resistance training and adequate protein address problem 2, deliberate food quality addresses problem 1. Everything else, including whether the drug is appropriate at all, belongs with a prescriber.
