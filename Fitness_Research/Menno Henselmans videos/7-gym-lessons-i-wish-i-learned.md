# Seven Things He'd Tell His Younger Self

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "7 Gym lessons I wish I learned earlier" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6CM3OCC1TIY)) · 2025-10-28 · 15 min
**Topic:** Psychology & behaviour

## Bottom line
- **Progressing at all beats progressing optimally.** Hitting your natural ceiling a few months earlier changes little; spending years not progressing changes everything. Track objective benchmarks — calipers, waist, strength, annual progress photos at a matched body fat level.
- **Use all-time PRs, not PRs on new lifts.** Program hopping produces neural gains on unfamiliar movements that get mistaken for muscle growth.
- **Three to four meals a day is enough.** Frequencies above three aren't better for growth, fat loss or strength; below three appears to be too few.
- **Macros matter far less than food choices.** Get protein, some fat and fibre in; the carb/fat split barely matters for a moderate-volume lifter. Long-term success runs on the two S's — satiated and satisfied.
- **Do annual blood work and check your blood pressure.** A whole-foods diet is no guarantee.
- **Stop training through pain**, and remember that **lifestyle drives training and diet more than the reverse.**

## The long view, and how to know you're progressing

Reaching your natural muscular potential takes years even if you do everything right, and you won't. So the target is not the optimal rate but **consistent progression at all**, which he says most lifters never achieve. That requires objective benchmarks rather than feel: body composition, waist circumference, strength, and yearly progress photos compared at a similar body fat level.

The sharpest practical point is about using strength as a proxy. **Progress on a lift you recently added is mostly neural.** Program hoppers cycle through this indefinitely and never establish whether they built muscle. Two fixes: track **all-time PRs**, and keep a **witness lift** — a Zercher squat or leg press you don't normally train. If it improves without being trained, that's muscle.

His own biggest failure: bulking and cutting too aggressively, so a full cycle ends at the same body composition it started at, which he sees mostly in men. The mirror error, mostly in women and in lean men attached to being lean, is **never bulking at all** — which past the beginner stage means maintenance, which means maintenance.

## Meal frequency: an admitted 10-year mistake

He ate six times a day, every three hours, for years — to the point where he could tell the time from his hunger. He calls it a waste of time and effort.

Meal frequencies **above three per day** are not better for muscle growth, fat loss or strength ([Taguchi 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33361498/)). Below three appears to be too few, because there is a ceiling on how much muscle protein synthesis one meal can stimulate — the "muscle full" effect — and evenly distributing protein across three meals beats an uneven distribution ([Yasuda 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32321161/)). Behaviourally, three to four is where most people do best.

His technical caveat is the good bit: the studies that *do* find benefits of higher frequencies used **supplements only**. Whey is absorbed too fast to sustain an anabolic response across the day. In mixed meals with fat and fibre, digestion is slower and the response more prolonged, so it doesn't transfer.

## Food choices over macros

Beyond adequate protein, some fat and some fibre, the carb-to-fat split — 20%, 40%, even keto — doesn't much matter for a lifter training at moderate volume. What matters is the **two S's: satiated and satisfied.** Perfect macros while permanently hungry isn't sustainable, and neither is broccoli and protein shakes.

He goes further than most evidence-based coaches: for many people, **long-term macro tracking is not the way forward**. The endpoint is ad libitum eating from hunger signals, a skill best learned *after* developing calorie awareness by tracking. His own practice: no tracking except when bulking (too easy to dirty bulk, or to accidentally maintain) or in contest prep.

## Health, supplements, pain, lifestyle

**Health.** Annual blood work and blood pressure. He's seen high LDL in people eating whole-food diets with a poor fatty acid balance, and had his own iron run very high from iron-fortified food in Brazil.

**Supplements.** His analogy is the skincare industry — one shady study, "clinically tested", nothing happens, people keep buying. The only defensible categories are **filling dietary gaps (vitamin D, omega-3s) and creatine**, by far the most effective muscle-building supplement if you're a responder. Many quit because they can't notice a difference: "if you're not impressed with creatine, there's nothing legal and safe that will impress you."

**Pain.** Injuries are usually the lifter's own fault, himself included. A balance: people who take a week off for minor elbow discomfort don't train hard enough to succeed either, but people who never brake end up at 40 with a list of exercises they can no longer do. Same argument as his [injury video](herniating-disc-3-taught-me-5-injury.md), compressed.

**Lifestyle.** To a hammer everything looks like a nail, and in fitness everything gets blamed on nutrition. Happiness, stress and sleep influence diet and training far more than the reverse.

## Flags

The stress and sleep numbers outrun their citations. "High academic stress can double injury rates and double recovery time" and "50 to 80% deteriorations in nutrient partitioning" from sleep deprivation are presented as research findings, but the listed references cover **strength gains under life stress** ([Bartholomew 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18545186/)) and **muscle protein synthesis under sleep restriction** ([Saner 2020](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP278828); [Lamon 2021](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.14660)) — not injury rates, recovery time or body composition partitioning. The direction is well supported; the figures aren't sourced here. Two of the eight listed references don't resolve, including one of the two carrying the stress claim.
