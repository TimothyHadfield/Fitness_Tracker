# Combo Sets: Save Time With Overlapping Rest, Not Less Rest

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is the most time-efficient training technique" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C01Nw9uKRBM)) · 2024-11-07 · 10 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Supersets, tri-sets and circuits do cut session time by roughly a half or a third, and research generally finds equal muscle growth. But when the paired exercises are compound or high-rep, real-world performance drops, and it is **strength gains that suffer first** — hypertrophy usually survives.
- His fix is the **combo set**: the same group of 2–5 exercises, but you catch your breath between each one instead of running them back to back.
- The time saving comes from **overlapping rest intervals**, not from resting less. While you squat, your delts and back are resting for the press and the chin-up.
- A three-exercise combo costs about **1–2 extra minutes** over doing the squats alone, and buys you two extra exercises.
- The exercises in a combo must not share musculature. Squats + leg curls works; bench press + triceps extensions + front raises does not.
- Order is deliberately **flexible** — do whatever equipment is free. Worst case you fall back to straight sets, so there is no downside to trying.
- Realistic target: **~20 sets in 45 minutes** once your conditioning catches up.

## What the superset research actually shows
[00:30] The arithmetic is obvious — pair two exercises and you do two exercises in the time of one. The studies bear this out for muscle growth, muscle activity, force output and long-run strength.

[01:00] His caveat is about ecological validity: study participants get verbal encouragement and a bit of performance pressure that you don't have alone in a gym. Pair squats with bench press and the cardiorespiratory cost alone means you can't hit a genuine 10RM on the second exercise. Where studies do pair high-rep or compound movements, performance and long-term gains suffer — but the loss lands mostly on strength, with hypertrophy not statistically significantly worse. That leaves supersets a defensible trade even at their worst.

## The combo set
[02:31] Example: a set of squats, catch your breath in the rack, a set of chin-ups, catch your breath, strip the bar down for overhead press, catch your breath, then back to squats. Rest as long as you actually need before repeating.

[03:02] His students' usual objection — *isn't the point that you rest less?* — misses the mechanism. Shorter rest is a minor part of the saving. The dominant part is that all three muscle groups are recovering during the same wall-clock minutes. If squats alone eat 15–20 minutes of your session, two more exercises fit inside that window almost for free.

## Choosing what to combo
[04:02] The rule is non-overlapping musculature. His full-body example is squats + chin-ups + overhead press. He also likes **squats with leg curls**, on the grounds that squats are a poor hamstring exercise — a claim he asserts flatly and then declines to justify ("I'm not going to go into the biomechanics"). He reports leg curls done *before* squats can even improve squat performance, either via better-feeling knees or antagonist potentiation. Both of those are coaching observations, not cited findings.

[05:03] The failure case is obvious: triceps extensions, front raises and bench press together will degrade each other.

## Equal priority, and gym logistics
[05:33] A secondary benefit: in a normal session, whatever you do last gets your worst effort — if only in mental energy. A combo set gives three exercises roughly equal priority, because your first chin-up set comes after a single squat set rather than after twenty minutes of squatting. He is careful to say the first-vs-last exercise gap is smaller than most people assume.

[06:34] Unlike a prescribed superset, a combo is just *a group of exercises you may do in any order*. He describes his own approach as walking in, seeing what's free, starting there, and opportunistically slotting sets of the other two exercises into his rest periods while camping a squat rack. If the gym is packed you end up doing straight sets — the same as before, so nothing is lost.

## The cost
[08:06] Combo sets feel harder, and the extra difficulty is cardiorespiratory. He credits his own 30–45 minute sessions partly to conditioning from kickboxing, and says conditioning improves with practice — eventually to the point of doing isolation combos (lateral raises, curls, flyes) genuinely back to back with no rest.

## Where the argument outruns the evidence
- The four cited studies are all **supersets or circuits** — the very format he says degrades performance. "Combo sets", defined by *keeping* rest between exercises, are his own construct and are essentially untested as such. His claim that "research is very clear that combo sets like this don't hurt performance" is really a claim about García-Orea's upper–lower superset protocol with 45 s rest, which he concedes is "not a true superset but quite close".
- Squats being a "really really poor hamstring exercise", the leg-curl-before-squats effect, and the equal-priority benefit are all coaching claims with no reference attached.
