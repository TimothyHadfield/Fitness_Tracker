# Focus on the Movement, Not the Muscle

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is how you perform your reps for max muscle growth" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j4RJLssGB-4)) · 2024-10-08 · 15 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Despite the title, this is a video about **attentional focus and cueing**, not tempo or rep speed. The claim is that the mind-muscle connection does not survive contact with hard training.
- Internal focus raises EMG **only at very light loads, far from failure, lifted non-explosively**. At 80% 1RM the effect is essentially gone; it also vanishes when lifting explosively.
- In a rowing study the effect lasted **two to three reps**, then disappeared — and the lat gain came with a **rear delt loss**. Focusing on one muscle can cost another.
- Internal cues **reduce force production and long-term strength development**. One study found 1–2 extra reps on a 10RM squat/deadlift when focusing on moving the weight rather than on using the legs.
- His practical position: **cue technique and external outcomes**, not muscles. Exception: internal cues are genuinely useful in rehab, where lifting less weight for the same stimulus is a feature.

## Why the supporting studies do not hold up
[01:16] The EMG evidence is real but narrow. Bench press studies show pec focus raising pec activity and triceps focus raising triceps activity — at intensities as low as 20% 1RM. By 50–60% the effect is muddy and often non-significant; at 80% it is basically gone. Add explosive lifting and it disappears too. The effect exists in exactly the conditions under which nobody trains.

The seated row study is his cleanest illustration ([Fujita 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32448047/)): "pull from your back" raised lat activity and lowered rear delt activity for two to three reps, then both washed out — the negative effect lingering slightly longer. Across the whole set, no significant differences. And this was 70% 1RM stopped at velocity loss, not even to failure.

He pre-empts the objection that subjects are untrained lab volunteers, and notes that studies directly comparing trained and untrained subjects find no difference in mind-muscle connection efficacy.

## The mechanism he proposes
[04:33] First, **you never get feedback**. Skill improves through practice plus feedback. You feel something, but nothing tells you whether it is actually activation or tension, so repetition alone does not make you better at it.

Second, and more interesting: **the motor cortex is a Bayesian system** already solving the movement optimally, subconsciously setting every muscle's contribution, co-activation and timing. An internal cue is a **constraint on that optimisation** — the brain returns a less efficient solution. Less efficient means the same weight is harder, which is exactly why EMG goes *up* at light loads: relative to your now-lower effective 1RM you are lifting a higher percentage. His analogy is poking a motherboard with a kitchen knife to speed up the computer. It reframes the EMG finding neatly — the activation increase signals degraded efficiency, not extra stimulus.

## The one growth study, and how he handles it
[09:36] Only one study has tested attentional focus on long-term hypertrophy ([Schoenfeld 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29533715/)): 10 weeks of barbell curls and leg extensions, "get the weight up" vs "squeeze the muscle." Leg extensions showed no difference; **biceps grew more in the squeeze group**.

He does not dismiss it, but attacks it two ways. First, it may be a false positive — body composition, skeletal muscle mass and isometric strength, which normally track hypertrophy well, did **not** differ between groups. Second, and more persuasively: the leg extension is a fixed machine, so cueing cannot change the movement, and the movement is the whole causal path. The barbell curl is not constrained — "get the weight up" reliably produces elbows drifting forward, hip drive, reduced range of motion and a near-freefall eccentric. So the biceps result plausibly reflects **better technique, not better focus**, and he predicts you would get the same outcome by cueing technique directly.

He also notes the researcher's explanation (subjects couldn't establish a good mind-muscle connection with their quads) is not a scientific account.

## On tempo specifically
This video does not address rep tempo directly and offers no seconds-per-rep prescription, so it neither confirms nor contradicts this library's existing position (roughly 0.5–8 s per rep works at matched effort; slow negatives retracted by a major creator in December 2024; tempo the lowest-priority variable). Two touchpoints only:
- He treats **explosive lifting as normal and desirable**, noting it as one of the conditions that abolishes the internal-focus effect — implicitly comfortable at the fast end of the range.
- The one place eccentric speed appears is as a *confound*: a rushed, uncontrolled eccentric is part of what he thinks made the "get the weight up" curl group worse. That is a technique argument, not a tempo prescription, and it is consistent with treating control as the variable rather than seconds.

His practical hierarchy — technique and performance first, muscle focus optional — sits comfortably alongside tempo being low priority.

## Where he goes further than the evidence
The strength claim is well supported; the growth claim is not. He concludes internal cues "probably don't affect muscle growth," but the only direct hypertrophy study found a positive effect for the biceps, and his rebuttal is a plausible reinterpretation rather than data. He then speculates in the other direction — that better long-term strength from external cues might *improve* growth — which is a chain of inferences with no direct evidence.

To his credit the practical advice is permissive: if you are training purely for physique and you like feeling the muscle, he says that is fine, as long as it does not degrade technique.
