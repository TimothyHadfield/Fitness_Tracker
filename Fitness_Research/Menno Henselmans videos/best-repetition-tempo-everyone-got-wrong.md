# Rep Tempo: The Slow Eccentric Has No Evidence Behind It

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The best repetition tempo: everyone got it WRONG" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O1yzCzCmPFs)) · 2023-11-27 · 9 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- The bodybuilding staple of a **2–4 second eccentric is not supported by research**. Studies comparing eccentric durations mostly find nothing.
- **Slowing the lowering phase is not eccentric overload.** Overload means more force, which means more load. Research needs roughly **20% minimum, often ~50%** eccentric overload before hypertrophy differences appear.
- A slow eccentric buys time under tension but **costs repetitions**, so volume comes out neutral — and it forfeits the stretch reflex, making you weaker.
- His practical rule: **control the weight, don't deliberately slow it**, don't pause at the bottom, and go straight into the concentric.
- **Concentric: as fast and explosive as intended.** Clearly better for strength; for hypertrophy just complete as many reps as you can.

## Why "eccentric is better" doesn't imply "slower is better"
Studies comparing eccentric-only to concentric-only training do favour eccentric for growth. Henselmans's point is that this happens for a reason that doesn't transfer. In those studies the eccentric groups lift heavier loads, because you can lower more than you can lift, so the muscle is exposed to **higher tension** — and tension is what drives the adaptation. There may also be a titin-mediated stretch signalling component.

Lowering a given weight more slowly changes none of that. You are adding time under tension, not tension. And in the research, eccentric overload has to be substantial — about 20% at minimum, often around 50% — before it produces measurable hypertrophy differences. A tempo cue gets you nowhere near that.

## Two costs of going slow
**Fewer reps.** The extra time under tension is paid for with lost repetitions, so total volume ends up roughly unchanged.

**The stretch reflex.** When a muscle lengthens at speed, the signal goes to the brain and comes back as a strong potentiating contraction — the nervous system protecting the muscle from tearing. That reflex raises muscle activity and lets you lift more. Lower slowly and you lose it.

There is a third, more speculative cost he raises. On exercises where the weight would otherwise unload at the bottom — his example is a leaning cable lateral raise — descending at a reasonable speed means you have to actively decelerate the weight and then re-accelerate it. That produces high forces exactly at the longest muscle length, which may enhance stretch-mediated hypertrophy. Go very slow and you dilute the most valuable part of the rep.

So the intuitive benefit of the slow negative is, on his reading, both smaller than assumed and partly self-cancelling.

## What the data say
He quotes a review of 26 studies, whose conclusions he reads out:

- Eccentric tempos **below 2 seconds** improved subsequent concentric 1RM, velocity and power compared with tempos above 4 seconds.
- Tempos **above 4 seconds** increased time under tension but **reduced the volume completed**.
- There is **no clear difference between 2 and 6 second eccentrics** for hypertrophy or strength.

The review's overall recommendation is eccentric actions below 2 seconds, on the grounds that hypertrophy doesn't differ but performance does.

He then adds a randomised trial published after that review — Pearson et al., strength-trained men doing leg extensions with a 1-second or 3-second eccentric — which concluded both tempos produced similar muscular adaptations.

**Worth flagging:** the hypertrophy half of this is a set of null results, not a demonstrated advantage. The affirmative case for a fast eccentric rests on acute performance measures (1RM, velocity, power) and on volume, not on measured muscle growth. He is reasonably clear about this — he says the benefit of a slow eccentric is "probably very, very small" and likely cancelled out, i.e. neutral — but the video's title claims more certainty than the evidence delivers.

## The prescription
**Eccentric.** Retain muscular control — the muscles do the work, the weight does not free-fall. That's the whole requirement. On exercises with no tension at the bottom (lateral raises, dumbbell curls), decelerate the weight with the muscles rather than letting it drop, then accelerate immediately into the concentric. Don't pause, so the stretch reflex stays available.

**Concentric.** Near-universal consensus, and he agrees: fast and explosive, or at least with the *intent* to be explosive, since a heavy weight moves slowly regardless of intent. The strength evidence for this is strong. It may indirectly help hypertrophy by allowing more load and more volume, though again most studies find no significant tempo differences either way.

If your goal is hypertrophy, aim to complete as many reps as possible. If your goal is strength, aim to be explosive on every rep, maximising muscle activity and neural drive.
