# Only Two Macros Matter: Carb Intake Doesn't Move Gains, and Surplus Size Barely Does

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Stop Obsessing Over Your Macros. Only 2 Things Matter" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a5KNMbTMIf0)) · 2026-06-30 · 13 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- For most lifters, **carbohydrate intake does not reliably affect gym performance or long-term strength gains**, and the carb-to-fat split is fine-tuning once protein is adequate and fat isn't absurdly low.
- The only two things that matter nutritionally are **protein intake and total energy intake**. He adds weekly training volume as the third big lever overall.
- His new within-subject trial added ~33 g of carbohydrate (50 g intended) on top of a 215 g baseline in trained lifters over two 8-week blocks. DEXA body composition, ultrasound muscle size, squat 1RM, quad peak torque and fatigue index all fluctuated around zero.
- Traditional strength training doesn't deplete glycogen enough to matter — **no more than ~40%** even at 15–20 sets per muscle per session. Carbs start to matter above roughly **10 sets per muscle per session**, or if you play a concurrent sport.
- Outside an energy deficit, **extra calories mostly become fat**, not muscle. The relationship is asymmetric: deficits clearly hurt, surpluses barely help. So lean bulk on the smallest surplus that still produces progress — for an advanced lifter possibly ~100 kcal.

## The new study
A 16-week within-subject design: trained male lifters kept training and nutrition constant and took a supplement that always contained protein, with 50 g of extra carbohydrate added during one of the two 8-week blocks. He makes a methodological case for the design — each man is his own control, which removes the between-subject variation in genetics, motivation, effort and lifestyle that ordinarily forces studies to be large or long to detect anything.

He is upfront about the limits: eight weeks, trained lifters who won't gain much anyway, and a modest achieved contrast. He argues the modest contrast is the point, since 215 vs ~250 g is the realistic decision people actually face.

## The wider literature
His 2022 systematic review is the backbone: of the short-term studies, **7 of 7** found no significant effect on strength development; of the long-term studies, **15 of 17** found none, with one favouring higher carbs and — his favourite detail — one favouring lower. In acute performance studies the same null holds, with the exception showing up at very high per-session volumes (15–20 sets per muscle group), where glycogen depletion can plausibly become limiting in some type II fibres.

The physiology he gives for why lifting is so carb-insensitive: long rest periods, a large eccentric component that costs little glycogen, and at least 24 hours before that muscle is trained again — enough for resynthesis even on low carbohydrate intakes.

For hypertrophy specifically, 10 of 15 studies found no effect and 5 favoured higher carbs — but in all five the higher-carb group also ate more calories, usually because the low-carb arm was ketogenic and cut intake overall, often losing more fat. He points out the trap: those are exactly the studies people cite for high carbs, and if you accept them you also have to accept greater fat loss on low carb. Their 2026 meta-analysis of 11 studies controlling both protein and available energy found no significant effect of carbohydrate on muscle growth.

## The measurement artefact, and the anecdote it explains
Most studies measure growth as lean or fat-free mass, and higher carb intake increases glycogen storage plus associated water — so the outcome measure is **biased toward high carb by design**, without any change in contractile tissue. This is also why high carb makes you look fuller and get a better pump, and keto makes you look dry and ripped. Both are real; neither is muscle. His read on the anecdotal high-carb believers is that they see fullness and read it as growth, and that palatable carbs quietly raise total calories, which the research shows reliably.

## Where he breaks with consensus
He states directly that the high-carb default in bodybuilding — **including in evidence-based circles** — "is really not very evidence-based." That is the confrontational core of the video and worth logging as a clear disagreement with mainstream sports nutrition. His caveats are specific rather than face-saving: more than ~10 sets per muscle per session, concurrent sport (tennis, football, kickboxing — continuous, concentric, anaerobic, far higher depletion), and carbohydrate intolerance.

## Energy intake and lean bulking
The study was deliberately built to test calories too, since the higher-carb condition also carried higher energy intake with protein held equal. It produced **non-significant trends toward both more fat and more fat-free mass** — energy has to go somewhere. Across the wider literature he finds minimal effect of extra energy on muscle and strength outside a deficit, while in a deficit there is a meta-analysis and several studies showing that larger deficits suppress muscle protein synthesis, raise breakdown and reduce growth.

His practical rule follows from that asymmetry: a surplus as large as it can be without spilling into fat gain, judged week-to-week by skinfolds and waist circumference rather than a fixed percentage. Enhanced lifters, lifters with muscle memory and true beginners can absorb a large surplus. Past the newbie phase, the energy actually required to build the small amount of muscle you can still build is tiny — he estimates around **100 kcal** — so the goal is avoiding deficits rather than maximising surplus.

## Overreach to note
Almost the entire evidence base here is his own: the new trial, the 2022 systematic review and the 2026 meta-analysis are all papers he co-authored. That doesn't make them wrong, but the video presents a position and its supporting literature from a single research group as settled. The ~100 kcal surplus figure is back-of-envelope arithmetic, not a study result, and is stated with more precision than that warrants.
