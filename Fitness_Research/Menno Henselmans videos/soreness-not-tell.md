# Soreness Tells You Almost Nothing

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is what soreness does and does NOT tell you" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4hKuqBlNCcA)) · 2025-01-30 · 7 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- His answer to "what does soreness tell you?" is **nothing** — beyond the fact that you did something novel.
- Muscle damage is **not required for growth** and does not correlate with growth or strength gains. High responders to training do not get more sore than low responders.
- Soreness and muscle damage don't correlate well either — in several studies, not at all, and sometimes they move in opposite directions.
- Soreness has **no programming implications** for most people. You can train through it and it usually improves rather than worsens.
- The one common-sense exception: if after warming up you still can't perform an exercise through full range of motion, skip it.

## Where he lands in the soreness dispute
This library holds two competing positions — soreness as mostly novelty and a poor signal, versus the argument that the things producing soreness (volume, effort, eccentrics, long muscle lengths) are the things producing growth. **Menno lands entirely on the first side, and pushes past it.** He does not treat damage as an incidental by-product of a good stimulus; he treats it as a **cost to be minimised**. His stated principle is that good program design *minimises muscle damage relative to the growth stimulus*.

His mechanism: damage mainly raises protein **breakdown**, and raises synthesis only enough to replace what was broken down. More damage means more total turnover, not more net accretion. His worked example is training to complete failure — disproportionate damage and recovery cost, disproportionate fatigue in the rest of the session, for only a little extra muscle over stopping one rep short.

Note that he does not engage with the opposing framing at all. The video argues soreness and damage don't track growth; it does not address whether the *training variables* that cause soreness are themselves the growth drivers. That is where this dispute actually sits, and the video leaves it untouched.

## Why soreness is a bad proxy
- Trained weightlifters got slightly **more** sore than untrained people after a heavy leg day, despite less damage.
- Endurance work vs heavy strength work: the strength session caused far more and far longer-lasting strength loss, but **similar soreness ratings**. Soreness peaked at 24-48h, when strength had already returned to baseline after the endurance session.
- Soreness follows the same time course — onset within a day, peak 24-48h, mostly gone by ~day 3 — largely regardless of how hard the session was.
- Electrically evoked contractions cause more damage than normal lifting yet less soreness.
- High protein intake speeds recovery without changing soreness; ice and heat reduce soreness without speeding tissue healing.

Two reasons: soreness is a **sensation with a large neural component** (phantom limb pain being the extreme illustration), and it may originate in **connective tissue** rather than muscle at all.

## Novelty and the repeated bout effect
The repeated bout effect means the same stimulus produces far less soreness the second time, even much later. Menno says he rarely gets sore from lifting now — partly high training frequency, which has been shown to reduce soreness, mostly habituation. What does make him sore is snowboarding and surfing, from which he gains no muscle. That, he argues, is the whole informational content of soreness: novelty.
