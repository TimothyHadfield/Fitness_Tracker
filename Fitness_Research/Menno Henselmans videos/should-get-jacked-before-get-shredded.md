# Build The Muscle First: Why Getting Jacked Makes Getting Shredded Easier

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Why You Should Get Jacked Before You Get Shredded [Science]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bP1Yu-xtjiM)) · 2025-11-11 · 9 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- A new review found that **any intervention causing muscle growth tends to cause unintentional fat loss too**, in humans and animals — high-protein diets, growth hormone, exercise. Henselmans agrees with the practical conclusion but **rejects the authors' mechanism**: he attributes it to energy balance, not to a body-weight "gravitostat".
- A **meta-analysis of 31 studies** found **strength training is as effective as endurance training for lowering body fat percentage**, and equally effective for absolute fat mass when workload is equated. Some studies and prior meta-analyses have strength training winning outright.
- **Muscle loss is fat you didn't lose.** People who only diet or only do cardio typically lose about **25% of their weight as fat-free mass** — a double penalty, because you end up with less lean mass *and* more fat than someone who lost the same weight as pure fat.
- His concrete targets: **male novices should be able to reach ~15% body fat, intermediates ~10%**; women **~25%** and **~20%** respectively. Going lower is for advanced trainees with real muscle mass behind it.
- Long-term structure: **cut to a body fat you're happy with, not your ultimate target**, then lean bulk with mini cuts, alternating muscle-mass PRs with progressively lower body-fat PRs each cycle.

## Where he breaks with the paper he's reporting
[00:31] The review argues muscle growth *inherently* causes fat loss, as if the body defends a set weight, citing gravitostat research (weighted vests causing fat loss). Henselmans is blunt: he is "very skeptical of any mechanism that does not involve simple energy balance." Thermodynamics has to hold. A weighted vest causes fat loss because lugging extra mass around costs energy. This is a good example of him accepting a finding while refusing its stated explanation.

## The three reasons muscle helps fat loss
[01:02] **1. Leanness is a percentage, not an absolute.** Two men at 15% look completely different depending on muscle mass — the point his DEXA-scan video makes visually.

**2. Nutrient partitioning is underrated.** In a deficit the body burns tissue to fill the gap. With adequate protein and strength training, that tissue should be fat. Without them, roughly a quarter of the weight lost is lean mass. You only get to spend that deficit once.

**3. Muscle is a long-term investment in energy expenditure.** Cardio buys expenditure only on the day. Muscle growth is itself energetically expensive, and the resulting tissue raises expenditure permanently — not much at rest per pound, but more once you account for carrying and using it all day. Fat-free mass is the single biggest determinant of energy expenditure; he cites differences between individuals of **~5%**, and up to **20%** in the long run with substantial muscle gain.

That 20% figure is the one to be careful with. He doesn't name the study, and it's a much larger effect than the usual estimate for the metabolic cost of added lean tissue. Treat 5% as the defensible number.

## Why not just diet all the way down
[05:09] For clients without much muscle, he explicitly advises **not** going to their eventual target body fat on the first cut. Two reasons: you'll look emaciated rather than lean, and it will be harder than it needs to be.

The physiological argument is that **essential body fat is largely an absolute amount** — the nervous system and organs need it — so the leaner you get relative to a small total mass, the worse the hormonal cost. He notes natural bodybuilders on stage often have **castrate-level testosterone**: they look their best and feel their worst. More muscle means more absolute fat available at the same target percentage, so getting lean is healthier and more sustainable.

He also flags the reverse. He is **not** endorsing dreamer bulking: high body fat harms health, and multiple studies show overweight individuals have **worse recovery capacity**, which costs you muscle-building ability directly.

## What to actually do
- **If your goal is purely fat loss** (he suggests this is mostly women in his audience): strength train at minimum to maintain muscle. Getting to a low body fat percentage is far easier if you also build some. What laypeople call "toning" is exactly this — more muscle *and* less fat.
- **If you want a lot of muscle and very low body fat**: cut to a comfortable, not extreme, body fat level. Then lean bulk with mini cuts to hold that level while accumulating muscle. Each subsequent cut phase should end slightly leaner than the last — helped both by more muscle mass and by more dieting experience.

He closes by conceding that most of this is ultimately personal preference, and that the framework is a decision aid rather than a finding.
