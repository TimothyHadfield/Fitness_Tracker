# Twelve Bodybuilding Myths, Walked Through a Review Paper

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Avoid these 12 myths & build more muscle" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Im4lKSxNBOE)) · 2026-03-31 · 15 min
**Topic:** Evidence & methodology

## Bottom line
- A walkthrough of Roberts et al.'s "From Anecdote to Evidence" review, which lists twelve debunked bodybuilding beliefs. Henselmans endorses eleven and **openly disputes one**.
- **The disagreement is myth 4.** The review argues the interference effect from cardio may be a measurement artefact. Henselmans says it is real, demonstrated for hypertrophy, and that the debunking rests on untrained-subject studies where everything works.
- Consistent thread: most of these myths are **feelings rationalised after the fact**, then dressed up as mechanism.
- The practical residue is thin and familiar — progressive overload, three to four meals, total energy balance, don't over-engineer the rest.

## The twelve

| # | Myth | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fancy periodization maximises growth | **Busted** | Block periodization and volume-ramp-plus-deload models have no hypertrophy evidence. Daily undulating periodization helps *strength*; hypertrophy evidence for it is "quite slim." |
| 2 | You must confuse your muscles | **Busted** | Adaptation is the goal, not the enemy. Excess variation raises muscle damage and forces more work for the same gains, and it obstructs progressive overload. |
| 3 | Training to failure is necessary | **Busted** | Failure helps only insofar as it adds volume. Equate repetition volume between groups and the difference disappears. |
| 4 | Cardio kills your gains | **Partly true — he dissents** | See below. |
| 5 | Lifting makes women bulky | **Busted** | Building muscle is slow and hard; resistance training is in nearly every national health guideline at 1–2 h/week. |
| 6 | A good physique needs a monotonous, strict diet | **Busted** | Get protein in, keep energy balance right, avoid gross carb/fat deficiency, and almost any diet works. The failure mode is practical (hunger, deficiencies), not metabolic. His coaching rule: satiety and satisfaction. |
| 7 | Eat every 3 hours | **Busted** | Thermic effect of food has the same area under the curve regardless of meal count. **Three to four meals a day**, with a floor of three for hypertrophy; beyond that, preference. |
| 8 | The anabolic window | **Busted** | "More of a meteor crater" — elevated muscle protein synthesis lasts days. What matters is amino acid availability, not meal timing. His default: pre- and post-workout meals within a **5-hour window** around training. |
| 9 | Spot reduction | **Busted** | Fat distribution is largely a genetic blueprint per body-fat percentage. Stubborn areas just mean you need to get leaner. |
| 10 | Fasted cardio burns more fat | **Busted** | Confuses acute fatty acid oxidation with adipose tissue loss. Fasted cardio may even *reduce* fat loss by cutting performance and thus expenditure — though measured differences are trivial either way. |
| 11 | Unused muscle turns to fat | **Busted** | The tissues cannot interconvert. You can lose muscle and gain fat simultaneously — and, going the other way, recomposition works far further into intermediate training than most people assume. |
| 12 | Creatine is a steroid | **Busted** | It is a nitrogenous compound. Vitamin D *is* a steroid, which shows the category itself carries no verdict. |

## Where he breaks with the review: the interference effect
[03:33] The review treats the concurrent training effect as possibly artefactual. Henselmans says flatly he "would certainly disagree" — he thinks it is real and has been shown for muscle growth, not just strength.

His reconciliation: the effect is **overrated in the direction people fear and real at the top end**. Walking to the gym, commuting by bike, a low volume of cardio for health — none of it materially costs muscle. But cardio done *before* lifting, or at high enough volume to drive genuine endurance adaptations, does compromise growth and performance.

His methodological objection is the sharper part. The case against interference leans on **untrained subjects, in whom any stimulus builds muscle** — put a beginner on a bike and they gain mass. Around eight weeks in, the cellular signalling pathways for endurance and resistance training diverge; cycling stops building muscle and you have to lift. Only past that point does the interference effect show consistently. He names bodybuilders in contest prep as the group genuinely at risk: an hour of daily cardio, a deep deficit and limited recovery, stacked together.

## Notes on the rest
- **Myth 3** is worth flagging against the rest of this library: "failure only matters via volume" is a strong reductive claim, delivered here in thirty seconds with no citation, pointing at another of his own videos. The equated-volume literature is not as clean as the phrasing suggests.
- **Myth 9** is the one place he adds a caveat the review does not: two studies have found marginal spot reduction under a strength-plus-cardio combination. He calls their methodology weak and does not change the verdict, but he does not hide them.
- **Myth 11**'s recomposition claim ("much, much higher than most people in fitness realise") is sourced to his own clients and app users, not to research — he says so, but it is the one place practice is doing the arguing.
- **Myth 8** carries a jab at the supplement industry: immediate post-workout protein is the one behaviour that *requires* a shake, which he treats as the reason the belief was promoted. He generally prefers whole foods for satiety.
