# Autoregulatory Volume Training: One Benchmark Set, Then Let the Reps Fall

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Program your sets like this for faster gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kGsteOMkEz4)) · 2025-01-09 · 10 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Fixed prescriptions like "3×10 with your 12RM" or "5×5 at 80%" are a mistake because **work capacity varies enormously between people** — some can't complete 5×5 at 80%, others could do seven or eight sets of it.
- **Autoregulatory volume training (AVT)** has two parts. Step one: set a **percentage of 1RM**, do as many reps as you can (or to your chosen RIR), and **whatever number you get becomes your rep target** for that exercise.
- Step two: **set 1 is the benchmark set** — the only set you track progress on, because it is the only one uncontaminated by fatigue and unmeasured rest intervals.
- Sets 2 onwards are **volume sets** with no rep targets and no timed rest. You go when you feel ready and take what you get. This is where the autoregulation of fatigue actually happens.
- Progression rule: hit your benchmark rep target with progressively more weight. If you are hitting it, don't change the program. If you aren't, change something.
- He is candid that the physiological evidence for this specific method is "not very strong" — the case is that it is unlikely to hurt and is practically pleasant to run.

## Why percentages rather than rep maxes
[01:34] He leans on a 2019/2020 systematic review finding that programs prescribing load as a **%1RM** produced better average strength gains than programs prescribing by repetition maximum. He then immediately undercuts it himself: the review did not control for proximity to failure or total volume, so he says to take it with a grain of salt.

[02:05] The second argument is that people respond better to loading ranges they are genetically suited to. His example is a study in which carriers of one ACE genotype — the endurance-favouring variant — gained more strength at 12–15RM loads than at 8–12RM, inverting the normal pattern that heavier loads produce more strength.

[02:36] From there he builds a hypothesis: lifters with predominantly type II fibres and low capillary density may progress better on low reps, and endurance-suited lifters on higher reps. He is clear this is "a tentative hypothesis", supported mainly by the coaching observation that sprint-type athletes never become elite marathoners and vice versa.

[03:38] The mechanism of the method is that a fixed %1RM **automatically sorts people into their own rep range**. Give two lifters 80% of 1RM and one gets 12 reps while the other gets 5 — those become their respective targets, with no coach judgement required.

[04:08] It also autoregulates preference. Someone who hates high-rep squats will quit at 8–10 at 70% regardless, and he argues forcing them higher turns the session into cardio and indirectly hurts their results anyway.

## The benchmark set
[05:10] The core practical argument, and the strongest part of the video. If you don't time your rest intervals — and he thinks you shouldn't, because people hate it and it isn't necessary — then **later sets are confounded by rest length**. The 5×5 forum complaint of "my workouts now take an hour" is his illustration: those lifters have not necessarily got stronger, they have drifted from 80% of 1RM into five genuine 5RMs by resting longer. Measuring only set 1, when you are unfatigued and rest length is irrelevant, removes that confound.

The benchmark set is also what triggers programming decisions: reactive deloads, plateau breakers, whether the program needs updating at all.

## The volume sets
[06:41] On sets 2+ you shift attention from maximum reps toward technique. Two claimed benefits: technique degrades when every set chases a rep target, and dropping rep targets is what lets fatigue self-regulate. A brutal first set means fewer reps later; an easy first set means more. Rest intervals autoregulate the same way — you go when you feel ready to perform.

[09:44] Caveat he adds himself: if **strength** is your primary goal you probably do want to chase maximum performance on every set, and should build technique so that maximum performance and good technique coincide.

## Where the argument outruns the evidence
- The genetic-suitability case rests on **one 2005 ACE genotype study**. Building a programming principle on a single small candidate-gene study is exactly the kind of inference that has failed repeatedly in exercise genomics, and he does not flag this.
- He then slides from genotype to *preference* — "people like doing things they're genetically good at" — which is not an evidence claim at all, though it is presented in the same breath.
- The autoregulation studies he cites (Graham & Cleather, Ghobadi, Dorrell) test **RIR-based or velocity-based load autoregulation**, not AVT. AVT as described — benchmark set plus untargeted volume sets — is his own construct and is not what any cited study evaluated. To his credit he says so: "the evidence behind this method being truly superior physiologically speaking is not very strong."
- He notes the term "autoregulation" has been diluted to mean any subjective decision-making, and that the original term was cybernetic periodization, then declines to litigate the semantics.
