# Calling Obesity a Disease: The Hidden Cost of the Label

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Should we call obesity a disease?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hrD6li833js)) · 2022-12-16 · 6 min
**Topic:** Psychology & behaviour

## Bottom line
- The two arguments for the disease label are **institutional** (unlocking insurance, medical and government attention) and **psychological** (reducing weight stigma). He engages only the second.
- A recent study supports the stigma argument: pathologising obesity reinforces **biogenetic causal beliefs**, and those beliefs are associated with **less weight stigma**.
- But earlier experimental work found the same mechanism has a cost. Across **three experiments**, a disease-framed message — presented as a real New York Times article — **undermined weight-loss self-regulation** relative to an information-framed message.
- Specifically: the disease message **weakened the importance placed on health-focused dieting**, **reduced concern about weight** among people with obesity, and that reduced concern **predicted higher-calorie food choices**.
- The awkward part: the disease message also **lowered body-image dissatisfaction** — and feeling better about your body *also* predicted higher-calorie choices. **The benefit and the harm run through the same channel.**
- His practitioner conclusion: in one-to-one coaching, **don't call it a disease**. Frame it as a number under the client's control.

## The trade-off he is pointing at
This is not a claim that the stigma research is wrong. It is a claim that both findings are true and they pull opposite ways. Telling people their obesity is biological and genetic makes others judge them less **and** makes them act on it less, because a condition that happened *to* you is not one you expect to change. The same is true of the body-image effect: reduced dissatisfaction is a genuine wellbeing gain that comes bundled with reduced behaviour change.

His framing of the resulting question is the combative bit: **who is the label actually for?** Are we calling it a disease so people with obesity feel better about themselves, or so everyone else feels virtuous for having said it? He argues that public health messaging has been optimised for tolerance and empathy **at the expense of goal-directed action**, and that this trade is made without acknowledging that a trade is happening.

## What he does instead
He never tells a client they have a disease or a disorder. The conversation is: you are at body fat percentage X, you want Y, here is how we get there. Words matter — he grants the studies show exactly that — but **actions matter more**, and any framing that gets a client to their goal is the right framing.

His justification is scale: more than two in three people are overweight, more than one in three obese, and projections have over half the population obese by 2030. Against that, most people with obesity want to be leaner and are actively trying, and those people are better served by help than by semantics. He leaves explicit room for those who don't want to change.

## Where the confidence outruns the evidence
He sets aside the institutional argument — insurance coverage, medical funding, government attention — with a wave, and that is genuinely the stronger half of the case for the label. He scopes his conclusion to one-to-one coaching, but the video's framing is broader than his argument supports.

The self-regulation finding also comes from **message manipulations with food-choice proxies**, not real-world weight outcomes. Reading it as showing the disease label makes populations fatter is a longer step than the data takes.
