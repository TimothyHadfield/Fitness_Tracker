# Knee Valgus in the Squat May Be Functional, Not a Fault

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Is knee valgus actually a GOOD thing? New research" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UOWQUNZRVtU)) · 2024-06-12 · 7 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Standard coaching treats knees caving in as a fault caused by weak glutes and tight adductors. Menno argues **all three parts of that are probably wrong**.
- A biomechanics study of trained squatters found valgus **reduces demand on the hip adductors and increases demand on the glutes** — the opposite of the usual explanation. If that's the mechanism, **strengthening the glutes could make valgus more likely, not less**.
- The authors concluded ACL strain from knee abduction moments **plateaus below injury threshold**; a large study in elite female athletes found **no link between inward knee motion and future non-contact ACL injury**.
- Not a free pass: **you should be able to squat to parallel without valgus**, and most people should push their knees out more. Involuntary valgus on heavy attempts, in someone with that motor control and no pain, is probably fine.

## The biomechanics
The study compared trained lifters (parallel squat, at least 1-1.5x bodyweight) doing three variations — deliberate knees-in, deliberate knees-way-out, and their normal technique — then modelled the demand on individual muscles.

Hip extension out of the bottom of a squat is driven primarily by the **adductor magnus and gluteus maximus**. The hamstrings are effectively just stabilisers, a biarticular conflict: contracting hard at the hip would also pull you into knee flexion and fight the quads at the knee.

Knee position changes the lengths and leverages of adductor magnus and glute max, redistributing load between them. **Valgus shifts demand toward the glutes** in exactly the portion where you're coming out of the hole. When one of those muscles can't produce enough force through the sticking point, that redistribution lets you complete the lift. On this reading, valgus is the body **solving a force problem**, not failing at one.

## Why the standard fix backfires
The conventional prescription — strengthen glutes, stretch adductors — inverts the mechanism. If valgus loads the glutes *more*, stronger glutes give the body more incentive to go there.

He argues this explains why valgus is so common: weightlifters, powerlifters and most gym-goers train glutes heavily and directly, while adductor magnus gets trained only as a by-product of hip extension. He thinks most people carry an **overdeveloped glute-to-adductor-magnus ratio**.

His second argument is observational: if valgus were a correctable imbalance, you wouldn't see it constantly in **elite weightlifters, powerlifters and athletes**, most visibly under maximal force. A pattern the body reverts to under maximal load and that you cannot train away is usually the biomechanically optimal one.

## Is it safe?
This is where the evidence is thinnest and he says so — research on valgus safety in squatting, drop jumping and athletic movement is **inconclusive**. He leans on the study authors' conclusion that ACL strain plateaus below injury level and hip internal rotation stayed within normal range, plus the elite-female-athlete cohort finding no link to future non-contact ACL injury ("Kiss goodbye to the 'kissing knees'").

## What he actually recommends
He teaches students to be able to squat to parallel **without** valgus, especially sub-maximally, and thinks most people don't push their knees out enough — the squat is "sitting in between your knees", not folding inward like a harmonica, and the knees-out version usually gives greater range of motion with better lumbopelvic position.

The permission is conditional: **if** you can squat without valgus sub-maximally, **and** it appears involuntarily on heavy attempts, **and** it helps you lift more, **and** it causes no pain — then it's functional, not a problem to fix. Good technique is whatever produces the desired adaptation, not whatever looks tidy.

## Note on the evidence
The central mechanistic claim rests on **one biomechanics study** of a modelled, volitional knees-in/knees-out comparison — not on any training or injury outcome. The "strengthening glutes makes it worse" inference is his own extrapolation from that model, not a study finding, and is presented more confidently than the evidence supports. The ACL safety case is a genuine null result but from a single cohort in elite female athletes; he is right that the wider literature is ambiguous.
