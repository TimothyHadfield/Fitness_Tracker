# The Triceps Long Head Needs Its Own Volume Count

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Avoid this common triceps training mistake" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xK4HPxijOaE)) · 2024-09-03 · 11 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- The **long head is by far the biggest part of the triceps** — and therefore the biggest part of the arm. It crosses the shoulder as well as the elbow, which changes everything about how you train it.
- **Pressing exercises don't build the long head.** A biarticular conflict during pressing means the body under-recruits it. Skull crushers produced roughly **double the total triceps growth** of barbell bench press, driven specifically by long-head gains ([Brandão 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32149887/)).
- **Overhead extensions beat pushdowns by ~40%** in triceps growth ([Maeo 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/)) — and grew *all three heads* more, not just the long head.
- Practical consequence: **count long-head volume separately** from medial/lateral volume. Five sets of barbell bench = five sets for medial and lateral, roughly zero for the long head.
- Two fixes: make pressing work involve the triceps less (dumbbells, cables, convergent machines), and use **straight-arm pulldowns / lat prayers**, which load the long head without loading the other heads.

## The anatomy and the biarticular conflict
Three heads: the medial (tiny, buried), the lateral (the visible chunk when you flex), and the long head on the back — far and away the largest.

Uniquely, the long head originates on the **scapula**, not the humerus, making it **biarticular**: it crosses the shoulder as well as the elbow, and at the shoulder it acts in **extension**, assisting the lats in pulling the elbow to your side. It's also weakly active in adduction, as in a wide-grip pulldown with the shoulder externally rotated.

During a press you want elbow extension, so the body would like to fire the long head. But firing the long head also creates a shoulder-extension moment, pulling the elbow back down — fighting what the delts and pecs are doing. A muscle can only contract or not; the triceps cannot act at the elbow and abstain at the shoulder. So the body simply doesn't recruit the long head heavily during a bench press the way it does during a skull crusher.

The direct evidence: comparing barbell bench press against barbell skull crushers, the **skull crusher group got about double the total triceps growth**, specifically attributable to the long head.

## The volume-counting problem
Count the heads separately and an awkward structural problem appears: **it is hard to design a program that doesn't overtrain the medial and lateral heads while still adequately training the long head.**

Most programs do exactly that by accident — lots of pressing (medial and lateral only), plus lots of pushdowns. Overtrained small heads, understimulated large one.

His accounting rule: five sets of barbell bench count as five sets for the medial and lateral heads and **zero towards long-head volume**.

## Fix 1 — stop letting your pressing train your triceps
Do triceps work separately and let chest and shoulder work be chest and shoulder work: more lateral raises instead of overhead presses, more flyes, cable presses, and above all **dumbbells**.

The physics he gives: with a **barbell**, the hands are fixed, so triceps force — which acts to straighten the arm outward — produces lateral force into an immovable bar, which helps drive the weight up. With a **dumbbell**, that same force would throw the weight sideways. So dumbbell pressing recruits the triceps very little: a poor triceps builder, slight growth in lateral and medial heads ("you can probably even just not count the volume"), **almost zero effect on the long head**.

He frames this as a feature: less incidental triceps work leaves room for isolation work that trains all three heads, and dumbbells give more ROM for the pecs and delts anyway.

## Fix 2 — lat prayers
The long head both stabilises the straight arm and actively assists the lats in shoulder extension. Most people treat triceps involvement in straight-arm pulldowns as an unwanted side effect; he argues it's a targeted muscle, and that this is exactly the exercise you want — **long head loaded, other heads not** — to offset all the pressing volume landing on the medial and lateral heads.

## Why overhead beats pushdowns
Pushdowns produce about **40% less triceps growth** than overhead extensions. The obvious explanation is that shoulder flexion stretches the long head, giving more stretch-mediated hypertrophy.

But in that study **all the heads grew more overhead**, which stretch alone doesn't explain. His theory is a **resistance-curve** argument: in a pushdown the stretched top position carries very little tension, and the bottom lockout is so disproportionately hard that people intuitively skip it. In an overhead extension the forearm is literally the moment arm, so tension peaks at 90 degrees and even the bottom stays loaded. Overhead wins on both stretch and tension in the lengthened position. He flags this as **his own theory**, not a finding.

He also acknowledges the live debate over the term "stretch-mediated hypertrophy" — it isn't properly defined in research, and growth from literal stretching may be a different mechanism from growth from training at long lengths. His position: use it for both until there's evidence they differ, since we otherwise have no term for the second thing.

## Practical application
- Do **a lot of triceps isolation work, specifically overhead**: skull crushers, skullovers, rope or dumbbell overhead extensions.
- **Pushdowns aren't bad** — sometimes easier on the elbows — but aren't ideal. Kickbacks likewise.
- Use **dumbbells, cables and convergent machines** for chest and shoulder work.
- Add **lat prayers** to isolate the long head and rebalance against pressing volume.

**Overreach flag:** both central numbers — "double the growth" and "40% more" — come from single studies. He calls the Maeo trial the best we have, which is fair, but counting long-head volume at zero for all presses is a strong prescription built on thin outcome data plus a biomechanical argument.
