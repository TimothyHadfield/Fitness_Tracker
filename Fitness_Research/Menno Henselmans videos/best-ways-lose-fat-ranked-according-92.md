# Fat Loss Methods Ranked: A 92-Study Meta-Analysis

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The BEST ways to lose fat ranked according to 92 studies" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DrRP0VfmjwE)) · 2023-08-10 · 14 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Energy restriction is by far the dominant lever.** Everything else modifies it.
- The top-ranked intervention for body fat percentage loss was **energy restriction + high protein + exercise**. He calls this the holy trinity.
- **Resistance training outranked aerobic training** for fat loss, with and without energy restriction. Consistent across all outcomes, though he is explicit that the margin is not large.
- Three fundamentals: restrict energy intake; eat **1.6 g/kg/day protein minimum, 1.8 g/kg (0.82 g/lb) recommended**; do resistance training first and add cardio only once that's maxed out.
- This is a direct contradiction of the popular assumption that cardio is the fat-loss tool and lifting is the muscle tool.

## The ranking

Henselmans reports the meta's ordering rather than reading out effect sizes. The rankings for body fat percentage, total fat mass in kilograms, and all outcomes combined were similar.

The earlier 2015 meta-analysis (Clark) supports the direction and partly corrects the mixed-training artefact: it controlled for intervention duration, covered a broader age range with fewer studies, and found resistance training more effective than aerobic training **and** more effective than a combination of the two.

## Why resistance training beats cardio — six mechanisms

**1. Acute energy expenditure isn't that different.** Cardio sessions typically burn 200–600 kcal, strength sessions 300–500 kcal. Treadmill running, cycling and weight training came out broadly similar in the data he shows. Aerobic work is more *practical* for sustaining a high expenditure over an hour, but you can't hold genuinely high intensity that long. Circuit training (labelled "HRS" in the source figure) beat every other modality on calories per minute — his point being that **how you do the exercise matters more than the modality**.

**2. Higher EPOC.** Elevated oxygen consumption after training and between sets, plus multi-day tissue remodelling and repair. He is honest that this is usually under 100 kcal total — but against a 300–500 kcal session, 100 kcal is not nothing.

**3. Muscle retention is a zero-sum game against fat.** A 500 kcal deficit has to be fuelled from tissue. If the body burns muscle it isn't burning fat. So muscle loss directly reduces fat loss, and muscle *gain* stores energy, increasing the fat the body must burn.

**4. Muscle raises long-term expenditure.** He cites ~5% increases in resting expenditure when people take up lifting, and ~20% differences between bodybuilders and sedentary people. His framing: cardio is an acute payment, lifting is an investment that pays dividends plus long-term returns. He also argues total daily expenditure rises more than resting measurements suggest, because you carry the extra tissue around all day. **This is where he overreaches** — the claim that gaining a lot of muscle at unchanged intake puts you "in a 20% deficit" and will get you shredded treats a cross-sectional bodybuilder-vs-sedentary comparison as if it were a within-person prediction.

**5. Appetite.** He flags this as more contentious: some evidence for greater appetite-hormone changes and reduced ad libitum intake after resistance training than cardio, but "research on this is not super clear." Separately, most literature finds exercise doesn't raise total energy intake — which is why exercise creates a comparatively "free" deficit while dieting creates a hungrier one.

**6. Constrained energy expenditure.** Energy expenditure is not additive. Beyond a point, extra physical activity stops producing linear increases in total daily expenditure, compensated through reduced NEAT/spontaneous activity and improved metabolic efficiency — largely subconscious. He cites roughly **23% average offset** from the latest meta, i.e. burning 100 extra calories nets about 80, with values ranging up to 50–80% offset at very high activity levels. This plausibly explains both the mixed-training result above and why bodybuilders find that piling on prep cardio stops working.

## The three fundamentals
1. **Restrict energy intake** — by tracking, or via an ad libitum approach that shifts food choices toward satiating, less energy-dense foods.
2. **High protein** — the meta he co-authored puts the breakpoint at **1.6 g/kg/day**; he recommends **1.8 g/kg/day (0.82 g/lb)** of total body weight. Beyond that there's no further gain in muscle or strength.
3. **Cardio last** — still beneficial, but you get more return per minute from strength training, so add cardio once lifting is already maxed out.
