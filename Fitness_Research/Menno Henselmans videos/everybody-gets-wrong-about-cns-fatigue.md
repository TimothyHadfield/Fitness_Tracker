# CNS Fatigue Is Real, But It Is Local — Not Systemic

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "What everybody gets wrong about CNS fatigue" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fIcJhKnwp50)) · 2024-05-20 · 8 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- **Against the popular model:** heavy lifting and 1RM attempts do not "fry your CNS." CNS fatigue is driven mainly by **exercise duration, not intensity**, and endurance work produces far more of it than strength training.
- **Muscle mass involved is not a strong predictor.** Deadlifts are no more neuromuscularly fatiguing than squats or bench presses; leg extensions can fatigue as much as leg presses.
- **Contraction type matters more.** Eccentric biceps curls — one of the smallest muscles in the body — can produce days of measurable fatigue. CNS fatigue correlates strongly with **muscle damage**.
- Most so-called CNS fatigue is **local**, originating in the motor neuron. The motor neuron sits in the spinal cord (hence "central"), but its axon reaches a single motor unit, so the effect is confined to a few fibres of one muscle.
- A meta-analysis of **52 studies** on non-local muscle fatigue found no support for a general non-local effect on strength or power — only a possible effect on endurance outcomes.
- The whole-body tiredness at the end of a session is **mental fatigue**, not neuromuscular fatigue, and is not evidence of overtraining or a need to deload.

## The naming problem
"CNS fatigue" means reduced central drive to the muscles — weaker activation signals, so force output drops even when the muscle is fine. His analogy: brain is the driver, muscle is the car. The confusion is that the motor neuron is technically part of the central nervous system while its effects are anatomically regional. Central *origin* does not make the consequences systemic. He treats the label as the source of most mythology around deloads and deadlift recovery.

## Does true central fatigue exist at all?
He goes further and questions whether it's a needed construct, quoting a paper concluding that the force behaviour commonly attributed to central fatigue could be explained solely by peripheral factors, which "questions the very need for the existence of central fatigue."

His mechanistic argument: the brain is more like software than muscle. Muscle tears, accumulates damage and suffers metabolic stress; the brain doesn't run out of energy or wear down within a session the way tissue does.

The practical test he offers: if you're motivated, squats don't impair your curls afterwards, and neither does triceps work. Strength training is local — one muscle group is trained, fatigues and grows without affecting others. Endurance training is genuinely more systemic because it loads the cardiorespiratory system.

## What you actually feel in the gym
He grants you feel wiped out late in a long session, and performance drops even in untrained muscle groups — and attributes this to **mental fatigue**. Humans are effort-averse; the sensation comes from doing hard things you don't want to do. Visiting relatives you dislike or driving for hours is exhausting without producing any physical fatigue. His generalisation: feelings in the gym are just feelings, and don't necessarily map to physical reality.

**Overreach flag:** the video moves from "central fatigue may not be a necessary construct in the studies examined" to the near-absolute claim that strength training produces essentially no systemic fatigue. The meta-analysis he cites explicitly leaves a non-local effect on endurance outcomes open, and the closing framing — that pushing through fatigue "separates the winners from the losers" — is coaching rhetoric, not a finding.
