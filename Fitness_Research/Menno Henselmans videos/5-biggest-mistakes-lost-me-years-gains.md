# Five Mistakes That Cost Menno Years of Progress

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "My 5 biggest mistakes that lost me YEARS of gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uxFqo3FtIYQ)) · 2024-08-19 · 15 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Short-term strength gains are mostly neural. Long-term progress needs **body-composition tracking**, **benchmark lifts kept in the program for years**, and **"witness lifts"** you almost never train.
- **Planned cheat meals are a behavioural trap.** One cheat meal at contest-lean bodyfat can erase a whole week's fat loss. He argues the practice looks like disordered eating to a psychologist — a deliberately combative position.
- **Stretching is close to a waste of time** for non-athletes. Full-ROM strength training already delivers the mobility.
- **Count sets per muscle per week**, not per push/pull/legs category — those categories are anatomically incoherent (dips, upright rows).
- Target a **bulk-to-cut time ratio of 4:1**, ideally 6:1. Below 3:1 means the bulk is putting on too much fat.

## Mistake 1 — no long-term measure of progress
For years he program-hopped, gained strength on each new program, and only discovered from year-on-year holiday photos that he looked identical. Three fixes:

**Track body composition, not weight.** Weight alone hides recomposition — trained lifters who lose fat and gain muscle at stable weight often read that as failure and cut calories further, which is how muscle gets lost. He is explicitly unimpressed by DEXA and multi-frequency BIA for *tracking* (fine for a one-off ballpark, not sensitive enough over time). Waist circumference and skinfold calipers are cheaper and more practical. Track the *level*, not an estimated body fat percentage.

**Keep benchmark exercises.** The longer a lift stays in the program, the more its strength progress reflects muscle rather than skill. Adding 10–20% to a new exercise is mostly the nervous system learning it; adding 100 lb to a bench press you have trained for years is muscle.

**Use a "witness lift."** An exercise you essentially never train — his example is the Zercher squat. Because strength is highly movement-specific, progress on an untrained lift is a cleaner proxy for real muscle gain. Test a 5RM every six to twelve months.

## Mistake 2 — cheat meals
His most combative claim. The standard bodybuilding pattern — near-crash dieting all week to fund a planned weekend cheat meal — is, he says, a trait a psychologist would associate with an eating disorder. Planning a cheat meal makes Monday to Saturday "thinking about pancake day" days, glorifies the meal, and installs a **restrained-eating mindset**, which he calls one of the single biggest predictors of unsustainable dieting.

The damage is not trivial. The leaner you get, the larger the appetite and the easier the all-or-nothing switch flips; a single cheat meal can negate the week's deficit entirely, producing *negative* recomposition despite six days of willpower.

His fix: if you want the meal, have it, unplanned, as a rational indulgence — and don't drop every restraint. Eating fiber first before the junk food substantially cuts intake without reducing satisfaction, because satisfaction comes from relieving hunger and feeling full, not from the quantity or type of food. That last claim is asserted more confidently than the three cited papers support; the citations here cover cheat-meal social media content, weekend eating patterns, and long-term weight-loss maintenance, not the satiety mechanism.

## Mistake 3 — stretching, foam rolling and activation drills
During a "functional training" phase over a decade ago he spent large amounts of time on dynamic stretching, foam rolling, cooldowns and activation work. He dropped all of it from one day to the next and noticed nothing — no change in squat depth, range of motion, lifts, or daily life. His conclusion: stretching mostly makes you better at stretching, and full-ROM strength training is effectively weighted dynamic stretching that improves mobility about as well. The exception is a sport-specific need — he stretches now for kickboxing high kicks. Note this rests on personal experience plus one position paper (Nuzzo's argument for retiring flexibility as a fitness component), not on an intervention trial.

## Mistake 4 — categorising volume by movement pattern
Counting "back day" and "leg day" volume, or push/pull/legs, is too coarse. Dips are nominally a vertical push but train the horizontal-push musculature; upright rows are a vertical pull that train something closer to a lateral raise. Squats do not meaningfully train the rectus femoris, calves or hamstrings. His result from the coarse system was real imbalance: underdeveloped chest, overdeveloped delts and back. Count sets **per muscle per week**.

## Mistake 5 — crash dieting and dreamer bulking
Beyond a small surplus, extra energy is stored as fat, because the actual energy cost of building muscle is small: roughly 2,500–3,000 kcal per kilogram of muscle, i.e. about **100 kcal/day** of net storage. Crash cuts at low bodyfat cost muscle and block recovery. The cycle ends a year later at the same weight and composition.

His diagnostic is the **ratio of time spent bulking to time spent cutting**. His best-adhering clients run about **6:1** (consistent with a ~5% surplus against a ~30% deficit), mostly using mini-cuts. **4:1** is a good target for most. **Below 3:1** means substantial room for optimisation. These ratios are coaching observation, not a study finding.
