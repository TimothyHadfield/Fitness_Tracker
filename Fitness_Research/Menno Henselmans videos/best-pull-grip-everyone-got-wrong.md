# Grip Width Does Not Divide Work Between Back and Biceps

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The best pull-up grip: everyone got it WRONG [study]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QxtqcE2GqoI)) · 2023-10-19 · 15 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **"Wide for back, narrow for biceps" is wrong.** The best-controlled study found no significant activation differences across three pulldown grip widths — not for lats, biceps, traps or infraspinatus.
- Forearm orientation does not matter much either. Pronated pulldowns build biceps as well as supinated barbell curls ([Gentil 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4592763/)) — and under stretch-mediated hypertrophy logic, **pronation lengthens the biceps**, so a hammer or pronated grip might even have an edge.
- The biceps and back are **synergists, not competitors**. More biceps force makes the pull easier for the lats. Feeling your arms more does not mean your back is doing less.
- **Vertical pulls are real biceps volume; horizontal pulls mostly are not** — especially with dumbbells or other loose implements. Supinated dumbbell curls produced roughly double the biceps growth of supinated dumbbell rows ([Mannarino 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31268995/)).
- **Very wide grips are the worst of all worlds**: more shoulder problems, less lat stretch, and eventually less biceps involvement too.

## What the studies show
[00:30] The Andersen pulldown study is his anchor: three grip widths, 6RM at each, EMG across many muscles, no significant differences anywhere. Other comparisons — neutral vs supinated vs wide pull-ups and pulldowns — converge on the same null.

He is careful about the growth evidence rather than overselling it. The Gentil study showing pronated pulldowns match supinated curls for biceps growth is led by **Paulo Gentil**, whom he describes as having a poor reputation in the exercise science community, associated with (though not implicated in) Barbalho, whose papers were retracted over implausible data. He tells you to take it with a grain of salt. The convergence argument does the work instead: EMG nulls, plus a muscle-damage study finding shoulder-width supinated pulldowns and EZ-bar preacher curls cause similar biceps damage *(lead author's name is not recoverable from the auto-captions and is not in the description)*, plus the biomechanics all point the same way.

## The biomechanics, and why they are the interesting part
[04:54] This is the section that earns the video. If you only consider vertical forces during a pull-up, the biceps looks useless: with the elbow directly under the hand there is no elbow flexion moment arm at all, and with the elbow inside the hand, biceps force would seem to push the bar *up*.

The resolution is that the bar is fixed. The biceps produces a **horizontal, inward force** on it. Add that to the downward pull and the resultant force vector sits at an angle — which does create a positive elbow flexion moment arm, explaining why the biceps is involved.

The second consequence is the one nobody states: that same horizontal force **shortens the shoulder adduction moment arm for the lats**. Biceps involvement makes the movement easier for the back. The two muscles are cooperating on one task, not splitting a fixed pie. This is a specific mechanical account of the general claim — which he cites elsewhere in the literature — that compound and isolation exercises produce equal growth on average.

It also explains the dumbbell case cleanly. Horizontal force production only helps when the implement cannot move. With a dumbbell, single cable handle or rings, that force just displaces the weight, so the mechanism vanishes and biceps involvement collapses.

## Rows, elbow height, and where the lats drop out
[09:10] The real difference in rows is usually not grip width but **elbow height**. Elbows below the shoulders means shoulder extension, which the lats drive. Raise the elbows to shoulder height and shoulder extension disappears; the lats have very weak leverage for pure horizontal shoulder flexion and are effectively out. So face pulls, high rows and shoulder-height pulls isolate **rear delts and mid/lower traps**, with low biceps demand. Conventional rows train the whole back; high rows train the upper back.

## Functional differentiation
[10:43] Total activation being equal does not mean every fibre is loaded equally. The lats show genuine functional differentiation: **lower fibres** run outward and favour shoulder adduction (wide pulldowns), **upper fibres** wrap around the torso and favour shoulder extension (chin-up-style narrow pulls). He immediately hedges — these differences show mainly at submaximal contractions, and near failure the body recruits everything regardless of leverage. His practical use of it is modest: keep both chins and pull-ups in a program, "theoretically at least."

## Where the confidence outruns the evidence
The title claim is a null result: the studies found no significant difference, which is not the same as establishing equivalence, and EMG nulls in small samples are weak evidence. He also spends real time discrediting the lead author of his own best growth study and then keeps the conclusion anyway — defensible given the convergence, but worth marking. The specific advice against very wide grips (shoulder issues, reduced lat stretch) is presented as reasoning, not as a finding, and no injury data is cited.
