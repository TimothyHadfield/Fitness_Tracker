# Rest Intervals Are A Volume Knob, Not A Prescription

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is how long to rest between sets for max muscle" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sxWGSGQgTXg)) · 2025-11-25 · 13 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- A new within-subject study rested one leg **2 minutes** (3-4 sets to failure) and the other **20 seconds** (as many sets as needed to match reps). After 10 weeks: **no significant difference in any quad head by MRI**, and near-identical strength gains of **42%**.
- His own trained-lifter study found **3 minutes beat 1 minute** for growth and strength — but that one equated *sets*, not reps. That is the whole difference.
- The operative variable is **total rep volume at a given load**, i.e. tension × time under tension. Rest length matters only through its effect on how many reps you get.
- Short rests are far more **time-efficient**: the 20-second leg did roughly 6 sets versus 3, but finished in about **3 minutes versus 7**.
- Caveat he adds himself: after a hard set, high-threshold motor units drop out and the ATP-PC system takes **3-5 minutes** to fully resynthesise, so very short rests probably do have a ceiling for strong lifters — and for **strength** specifically he sides with longer rests.
- Practical default: **autoregulate.** Rest until you feel ready to perform again.

## The new study, and why it contradicts his own
[00:00] Untrained men, leg extensions at 10RM loads, one leg per condition. Long-rest leg: 3-4 sets to failure with 2-minute rests. Short-rest leg: 20-second rests, adding sets until it matched the other leg's total reps. Ten weeks, MRI, no differences anywhere in the quads; strength up 42% in both.

[00:30] He pre-empts the "untrained subjects" dismissal, which he thinks has become a lazy reflex: base physiology is mostly the same, and rest intervals are not among the established exceptions.

[01:30] The important contrast is with **his own 2016 study** on trained men, which found 3-minute rests produced more growth and strength than 1-minute rests. That study **equated sets**; the new one equates reps. Set-equated, longer rests win, because you get more reps per set. Rep-equated, rest length washes out. [04:32] He points to the four-group Longo study as the cleanest demonstration: both groups that ended with high total rep volume grew more, whether they got there through long rests or through extra short-rest sets.

## Against the old consensus, and against the new one
[02:00] The old bodybuilding-and-science consensus — short rests for hypertrophy, long rests for strength — rested on metabolic stress and the acute hormonal response. He says flatly that those hormonal changes do not correlate with hypertrophy, are metabolic rather than anabolic, and are too small and transient to matter. He wrote the 2014 review that made this argument.

[05:34] His replacement model is mechanical tension multiplied by time under tension. Load fixes the tension; anything that adds reps at that load adds stimulus. Rest intervals are one such lever, exactly like adding sets or training closer to failure.

[09:07] The consequence he stresses most is a program-design one: **you cannot set rest intervals and training volume independently.** He compares it to diet — you cannot rule on a single food in isolation, only the total. He also uses this to defuse the very-high-volume studies: labs typically use 90-second to 2-minute rests for throughput, so a 45-sets-per-muscle protocol may only be necessary *because* of the short rests. Autoregulating lifters resting 3+ minutes get more reps in later sets and should need fewer.

## How this compares to the library's position
Our library holds **3-5 minutes on compounds, 1-2 on isolation, never under a minute**, with the old one-minute-for-hypertrophy prescription abandoned. Menno matches on the last point emphatically — he is one of the people who dismantled it. He also supplies numbers consistent with the compound figure, but as observation and mechanism rather than prescription: ATP-PC resynthesis takes **3-5 minutes**, and autoregulating lifters rest **at least 3 minutes after serious sets, especially compounds**. He also sides with longer rests for strength.

Where he **does not match** is the floor. He does not accept "never under a minute" as a rule: the new study's **20-second** rests produced equal hypertrophy and equal strength gains once reps were equated, and he treats this as validation of myo-reps and rest-pause work. His position is that sub-minute rests cost you nothing *provided* you pay the volume back in extra sets — with a mechanistic caution, not a data-backed one, that strong lifters training near failure may still lose high-threshold motor unit recruitment. He gives no isolation-specific number at all.

## Practical takeaways he gives
- **Simple version:** autoregulate. Rest until you feel able to perform the next set. Session RPE is lower this way and most people prefer it.
- **Time-saving version:** count the total reps you currently get across your sets at a given weight (e.g. 12+10+8 = 30), then get those reps in the fewest sets possible. That is myo-reps.
- **For strength:** rest longer regardless, to preserve force output and muscle activity.

## Where the claim runs ahead of the evidence
The headline study is untrained, single-joint, machine-based and 10 weeks long. He defends extrapolation to trained lifters on general grounds rather than evidence specific to rest intervals, and his own trained-subject study points the other way when sets are held constant. The motor-unit ceiling argument against very short rests is mechanistic speculation, stated as such.
