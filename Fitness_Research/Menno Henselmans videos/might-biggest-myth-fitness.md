# You Cannot Feel Which Muscles Are Working

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This might be the biggest myth in fitness" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EeXpfa4_fMQ)) · 2025-04-14 · 15 min
**Topic:** Evidence & methodology

## Bottom line
- Henselmans co-authored the first study to test whether the "mind-muscle connection" tracks reality. **There were no statistically significant correlations at all** between what trained lifters said they felt and what EMG measured.
- Some correlations were near-zero, some were **negative**. At the individual level he calls the subjective data "essentially useless" for exercise selection.
- Group averages were roughly right — pool enough people and the crowd knows a row trains the lats — but that reflects shared knowledge of biomechanics, not introspection.
- His practical rule: **select exercises from biomechanics, EMG, ultrasound, MRI and long-term growth data. Treat how an exercise feels as strictly secondary.**
- He also pushes back hard on the fashionable claim that EMG is "debunked" — it is noisy and often badly used, but it is validated, and it has repeatedly beaten intuition.

## The study
[00:31] Strength-trained athletes did a pull day — pulldowns, barbell and dumbbell rows, pullovers, reverse flyes, curls — at standardised intensity. After each exercise they rated how strongly they felt it in specific muscles; EMG recorded actual activation at the same time. Correlating the two answers the question directly.

[03:04] He expected moderate correlations. He got none. Beyond the flat correlations, athletes **systematically underestimated the gap between an exercise's primary and secondary targets**, and the variance in reported feeling was enormous without any matching variance in EMG. During pulldowns, self-rated rear delt activation spanned roughly 3/10 to 9/10 across subjects while measured rear delt activity barely moved. During barbell rows the athletes reported feeling their rear delts less than during dumbbell rows — a difference absent from the EMG and, he notes, biomechanically unmotivated.

A methodological detail that strengthens the study: they used **4–6RM loads**, where most EMG work uses submaximal loads like 3 reps at 50% 1RM. He argues submaximal protocols make much EMG research close to worthless.

## The load objection, and why it does not rescue the idea
[08:09] The obvious counter is that people feel muscles better in the 6–12 rep range than at 4–6RM. He grants it might be true and then turns it around: heavier loads *necessarily* mean higher motor unit recruitment and higher mechanical tension. So if feeling improves as load drops, whatever you are feeling is **not** tension or activation. His candidate is metabolic stress — pump and burn — which varies with the resistance curve and whether tension is constant, and does not track mechanical tension.

## Defending EMG
[09:41] He treats the social-media consensus that EMG is discredited as sloppy. His position: EMG is noisy, cannot directly predict hypertrophy, and is frequently used badly (submaximal loads, non-equated exercise difficulty across conditions). But it is validated against effort, proximity to failure, training intensity, ultrasound and MRI, and dismissing a well-run EMG study is unscientific.

He gives three cases where EMG called it right and intuition did not:
- **Rectus femoris** — poorly activated in squats, well activated in leg extensions. Growth data later agreed.
- **Hamstrings in squats** — EMG said no; the Starting Strength camp insisted otherwise for years; growth data sided with EMG.
- **Standing vs seated calf raises** — the gastrocnemius is biarticular and too shortened at a bent knee to contribute, so a seated raise is essentially a soleus exercise. EMG showed it and the hypertrophy trial confirmed it ([Kinoshita 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2023.1272106)).

The glute case runs the other way: "glute amnesia" is a feeling, not a fact — EMG shows high glute activity in squats, glutes grow from squats, and glute activation drills raise neither activation nor gains. He offers himself as an anecdote: he never feels his glutes or delts, and both are among his best-developed muscles.

## Where the claim outruns the evidence
He flags his own bias and asks for replication, which is the right instinct. Still, the headline — that the mind-muscle connection is *the biggest myth in fitness* — rests on **one study, from his own group**, on one training day, in one population. Null correlations in a modest sample are also the result an underpowered study produces by default. The supporting arguments (the load logic, the EMG-beats-intuition cases) are stronger than the single dataset, and the practical advice would survive even if the study did not.

This pairs with his companion video on attentional focus ([perform-reps-max-muscle-growth](perform-reps-max-muscle-growth.md)), where he handles the obvious counter-evidence — Schoenfeld's finding that an internal focus grew biceps more over 10 weeks — by arguing it was a technique effect and possibly a false positive. The two claims are separable in principle: deliberately focusing on a muscle could help even if you cannot accurately sense what is active. He rejects both.
