# Two Fatigue-Management Moves: Go Heavier, and Train More Often

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "2 Tips for MORE gains with LESS effort" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tYoKCv_90kg)) · 2025-09-16 · 10 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- The limiting factor for most lifters is **training effort**, not genetics or supplements. A meta-analysis of self-selected loads found the average lifter leaves **6–7 reps in reserve** on a typical set.
- **Tip 1: go heavier.** Lower-rep, higher-intensity sets produce *less* subjective and objective fatigue per set, not more. Bro wisdom on "frying the CNS" is exactly backwards.
- The intuition pump: at the end of a 20RM you can't lift your 20RM. At the end of a 5RM you could still do a 20RM. Total work performed is far higher in the 20-rep set, and RPE/RIR ratings are consistently lower for low-rep sets.
- **Caveat he supplies himself:** heavy + high volume + high frequency raises injury risk. If joints and connective tissue are your limiter, higher reps are the compromise — and you pay for it in effort.
- **Tip 2: spread volume across more sessions**, ideally by training more often, otherwise by moving toward full-body workouts. Same weekly volume, less fatigue per session.
- Splitting 8 bench sets into 4+4 across the week gives **more than twice as fast** recovery, and one study found splitting into two same-day sessions allowed a **10% higher training intensity** *and* faster recovery.

## Why effort is the bottleneck
[00:00] The framing claim: anything that makes you train harder increases results, so fatigue management is undervalued — it isn't just for the already-maximal, it's what lets everyone else do more. He grounds the volume side in recent meta-analytic work finding no clear cutoff above which more volume becomes detrimental, including studies at volumes as high as **45 sets/week/muscle**.

## Tip 1: heavier is less fatiguing
[01:30] He calls this the area where conventional powerlifting and bodybuilding wisdom is "flat-out completely wrong in the literal opposite direction of the truth" — a direct swipe at consensus gym lore rather than at the research literature.

[02:01] Three lines of argument:
- **Logic.** Fatigue at the end of a set is defined by what you can no longer lift. After a 5RM you retain more capacity than after a 20RM.
- **Total work.** 20RM × 20 reps is substantially more total workload than 5RM × 5.
- **Perception.** The last five reps of a true 20RM resemble a 5RM in bar velocity; everything before them is added work on top. He notes 20-rep squats are called widowmakers for a reason.

[03:03] Practical consequence: if you struggle to reach genuine failure, **go heavier**. High-rep sets start feeling like cardio and strength work simultaneously, making it very hard to push through the point where it merely feels finished. He also cites reduced recovery time from lower-rep work, meaning you can tolerate more weekly sets.

[04:04] The caveat is the honest part. Combining high volume, high intensity and high frequency drives injury risk up. His decision rule: **if you gas out and can't reach failure, go heavier; if you keep getting injured when you push volume, add high-rep work.**

## Tip 2: more sessions, more evenly spread
[05:36] Sessions become disproportionately grueling past about an hour — part duration, part accumulated volume. Spreading volume more evenly lowers per-session effort.

[06:07] A time-efficiency mechanism he adds: as fatigue accumulates, people spontaneously take longer rest intervals, so **sets per minute falls** late in long sessions. Never reaching that point keeps the whole session efficient.

[06:37] **Full-body workouts are the most even possible distribution**, since every muscle gets attention every session. You get most of the benefit even without increasing frequency.

His evidence, with his own hedges attached:
- Full-body work improves the **testosterone-to-cortisol ratio** — which he immediately says is "definitely not definitive".
- Less **soreness** — and he says soreness ratings "aren't that informative".
- The direct evidence: 8 bench sets in one session versus 4+4 across the week gives more than twice as fast recovery, even though the split condition accumulates *more* total repetition volume because each session is less fatigued. Splitting 8 sets into two same-day sessions of 4 allowed **10% higher training intensity** with faster recovery.

[08:08] He closes with the broader claim that in the full-body versus body-part-split literature, "in virtually every instance" — especially in trained lifters doing higher volumes — higher-frequency, more evenly spread training gets equal or better results.

## Flags
- **Transcript error worth recording.** At [03:34] the captions read "higher rep sets cause objectively less fatigue as well". From the surrounding argument he clearly means *lower*-rep/heavier sets. Don't quote that line.
- "**In virtually every instance** full body gets equal or better results" overstates the frequency literature, which is mostly null once volume is equated — and **Gomes et al. 2019, in his own reference list, found high-frequency training was *not* more effective than low-frequency**. His actual mechanism (higher frequency lets you accumulate more volume) doesn't need the stronger claim, and the stronger claim contradicts one of his own citations.
- The stacked evidence for Tip 2 is a mix he grades himself: hormone ratios and soreness he explicitly discounts, leaving the recovery and same-day-split studies doing the real work. Those are small acute studies.
- Note the internal tension with the volume framing: he opens by citing 45 sets/week as non-detrimental, then spends the video on how to manage the fatigue such volumes create. Coherent, but the two halves pull in opposite directions rhetorically.
