# A Push-Up Progression, From Smith Machine To Weighted Suspended

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Push-up variations: beginner to highly advanced" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zEiE8lzEd3c)) · 2024-02-12 · 13 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Push-ups are primarily a **chest** exercise in his programming — front delts and the lateral and medial triceps heads come along, but other exercises serve those better.
- Too weak for a floor push-up? Use the **Smith machine bar** set high, lowering it as you get stronger. He prefers this to knee push-ups, which make full chest-to-floor range awkward.
- Too strong? Add reps first — research supports growth all the way to a **30RM** — then add a **deficit** (handles or hex dumbbells), then **load** (a plate on the back), then **feet elevation**.
- Feet elevated to roughly **knee height** turns a floor push-up from a slight decline press into a true horizontal press, which he argues recruits all the fan-shaped pec fibres.
- His favourite variation is the **weighted, feet-elevated suspended push-up**, which he considers a strictly better dip for most purposes.
- **One-arm push-ups are a gimmick.** They are more about technique and balance than strength, are a poor chest exercise given the tucked elbow, and are hard to progress.

## The easy end
[00:30] Knee push-ups get a lukewarm review. People who need them tend to carry more body fat or have larger breasts, so with proper plank form the hips or legs hit the floor before the chest does. He insists on **chest to the floor every rep** — not the face, not the stomach.

[01:32] Smith machine push-ups are his preferred entry point because the difficulty is continuously adjustable: the higher the bar, the easier, and you walk it down over months until you are on the floor.

## Progressing the standard push-up
[01:56] Step one is just reps. He is blunt that people who dismiss push-ups as "too easy" at 12 reps should go to 20 or 30 — the load literature over the past decade supports maximum growth up to about a **30RM**.

[02:22] Step two is a deficit, for range of motion and the stretch on the pecs and front delts, which he ties to stretch-mediated hypertrophy. Push-up handles are cleanest; hex dumbbells work; knuckles work if you have nothing. One technique note he repeats: **keep the elbows relatively out**, not tucked to the sides, unless your shoulders demand the tuck. People intuitively switch to a tucked grip the moment they hold handles, which shifts the exercise toward triceps.

[04:23] Step three is load. Bands and chains are "okay" — he mentions research finding banded push-ups roughly as effective as bench presses — but he is not sold, because heavy band tension makes the top hard and the bottom easy, costing you the stretched position. A plate on the back is his default, up to the largest plate in the gym.

[05:02] Step four is feet elevation, which he calls underrated. A floor push-up is really a slight decline press: you are horizontal only at the very bottom and press up at an angle. Elevating the feet to about knee height gives the most horizontal path, and roughly 90 degrees between upper arm and torso, where the fan-shaped pec fibres all converge on horizontal adduction.

## Suspended push-ups
[07:48] His favourite. Harder than floor push-ups, no deficit needed since the rings or straps already allow a maximum stretch, and easy on the joints. Elevate the feet again — ideally above hand height — and load with a dip belt (tightened so the plates ride high and clear the floor) or a dumbbell or kettlebell.

His claim: weighted, feet-elevated suspended push-ups are **a superior substitute for dips** — better for the chest because dips force a heavily declined press angle, equivalent for the shoulders but easier on them, easier on the sternum, and roughly equivalent for triceps. He labels this as mostly anecdotal.

[06:39] He also notes two structural benefits of push-ups generally: they are a **closed-chain** exercise and the scapulae are free to move, which no bench-supported press allows. He concedes the physiotherapy research on closed-chain superiority is weak and says he finds the anecdotal evidence more convincing here — worth reading as opinion.

## Variations he doesn't rate
[09:59] **Hand rotation** (diamond grip, or rotating the hands outward) does make the exercise harder by putting you at a biomechanical disadvantage, but it is uncomfortable on the wrists and shoulders and most people abandon it. The exception is a niche one he used himself during a shoulder injury: elbows fully tucked, hands rotated out, which stayed pain-free when every other chest press hurt.

[11:12] **One-arm push-ups** get the harshest verdict. The correct technique — legs spread wide, torso rotated, elbow tucked — is largely balance and skill rather than strength, so most serious lifters could already do them. The tucked elbow makes it a poor chest exercise, and there is nowhere to progress. "More of a status thing or a gimmick."

## Where the claim runs ahead of the evidence
This video is mostly coaching judgement, and Menno is reasonably upfront about that — the dips comparison, the closed-chain claim and the elbow-flare cue are all flagged as anecdotal or biomechanical reasoning rather than tested. The two research claims he does make in passing (growth up to a 30RM; banded push-ups equivalent to bench presses) are stated without citation, and the description's reference block lists only his own other videos.
