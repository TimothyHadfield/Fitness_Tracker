# Rep Tempo: Control the Eccentric, Don't Count It

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is the best rep tempo for MAXIMUM gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kMB3nW2Esag)) · 2025-02-20 · 12 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- A new squat trial comparing **1-second vs 4-second eccentrics**, load and frequency matched, both to failure, found **no significant difference in total quadriceps growth** over 7 weeks.
- The one positive regional finding — more vastus lateralis growth in the slow group — is most likely a **type I error**. Four of five measured regions showed nothing, and total quad growth was near-identical.
- **The same study found greater 1RM squat gains in the 4-second group**, contradicting both the prior review and an earlier squat trial that found the opposite.
- His practical position: **control the descent, don't count it.** No specific tempo. Explosive concentric if the goal is strength; whatever lets you do the most reps if the goal is size.
- A 4-second eccentric squat is longer than it sounds — outside studies with someone timing you, effectively nobody does it.

## The new study, and how he handles the regional finding
The design is clean: one group squatted with a 1-second eccentric, the other with a 4-second eccentric, same load, same frequency, both to failure — so one group did more fast reps and the other fewer slow ones, isolating eccentric speed ([Kojic 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2024.1531926)). He flags the sample oddity: participants were **endurance trained but not strength trained**, doing 6-8 hours of low-to-high intensity exercise a week as part of their academic curriculum.

The headline result was null on total quad growth, consistent with a 2022 narrative review concluding there is no clear difference between eccentric tempos of 2-6 seconds for hypertrophy or strength ([Handford 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01686-z)). The mechanistic account he offers: glycogen depletion in type I and type II fibres reaches the same level regardless of tempo provided you reach the same endpoint — more fast reps or fewer slow ones, same total work, same growth.

The section on the vastus lateralis result is the most useful part of the note, because it's a general lesson in reading studies rather than a tempo argument:
- Five regions were measured; one differed. That is what a false positive looks like in a short study with a small sample.
- Ultrasound is operator-dependent, so per-site measurement error compounds; **summing sites is far more reliable than any single site**.
- His analogy: clients obsessing over left-arm vs right-arm changes on a DEXA scan. Whole-body body-fat estimates are only reasonably accurate — good at group level, mediocre individually — and single-site readings are not accurate even at group level.
- The rebuttal that settles it: if there was more growth, you have to explain why **total** quad growth was nearly identical.

He gives the regional-hypertrophy interpretation a fair hearing and rejects it. Regional differentiation within the quadriceps is poorly evidenced; the vasti simply extend the knee, unlike the hamstrings or deltoids where heads have genuinely different functions. He notes the VMO-targeting and narrow-stance-squat myths have already been debunked. And if you *do* argue from regional data, the evidence cuts both ways: [Pearson 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34351728/) compared 1-second and 3-second eccentrics on leg extensions and found greater **distal** quad growth in the *faster* group, with total size and strength similar.

## The strength result, and the contradiction
The 4-second group gained significantly more squat 1RM. He does not accept this at face value, because it directly contradicts an earlier squat study finding greater strength gains with a **2-second** eccentric versus 4-second ([Shibata 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30325791/)), and because the mechanism runs the other way: strength gains are velocity-specific, and a somewhat faster controlled descent triggers a stretch reflex — not just elastic recoil but a neural signal raising muscle activation during lengthening.

His proposed reconciliation is a training-intensity artefact. Shibata used 75% of 1RM; the new study used 60-70%, which put the fast-tempo group at roughly a **15-rep max on squats**. Sets of 15 squats run into cardiovascular limits and lose explosiveness, which he thinks explains why the fast group underperformed on strength here. That is a plausible post-hoc explanation, not a tested one.

He uses the disagreement to make a broader point about study-by-study reading: it is normal and expected for individual findings to differ, and the social-media habit of treating each new study as overturning the last loses the picture. Taking the wider view, most research finds no significant effect of eccentric speed on either strength or hypertrophy.

## What he actually recommends
Control the eccentric — a dive-bombed descent means essentially no eccentric contraction, discarding the phase where you are strongest and can generate the highest mechanical tension, which he treats as the primary driver of growth. But do not actively slow reps down and do not count tempo. On the concentric, which the new study didn't test: explosive for strength, and for hypertrophy a tempo that lets you complete the most reps.

**Overreach to note:** "as long as you are controlling the descent then you're probably fine" is a reasonable summary of a null literature, but the mechanical-tension rationale for why an eccentric must be controlled is asserted theoretically — none of the cited studies compared controlled descent to no descent control.
