# Protein Only Suppresses Appetite Until You've Had Enough Of It

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Protein is NOT more satiating than carbs or fats" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YHLXank1PPk)) · 2024-08-09 · 8 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Against the consensus:** protein is not *inherently* more satiating than carbs or fats. It is more satiating only while you are still short of the intake your body wants — past that point the effect stops.
- His own crossover study in strength-trained lifters in a 20% deficit compared **1.8 vs 2.9 g/kg/day** for one week and found **no difference in fullness, desire to eat, cravings or hunger** — nor in energy, mood, training enjoyment or motivation.
- Proposed mechanism is **protein leverage**: appetite tracks protein status, and once protein is being oxidised for energy rather than used for protein synthesis, extra protein does nothing for satiety.
- A meta-analysis of **38 studies** found energy intake fell with rising protein only up to about **20% of energy intake (~1.2 g/kg/day)** — matching optimal intake for sedentary people. His own meta-analysis puts the muscle-growth breakpoint at **1.6 g/kg/day** (1.8 with a safety margin, 0.82 g/lb).
- **Practical:** if you already hit ~1.8 g/kg, adding chicken breast, white fish or whey to blunt hunger wastes calories. Spend them on fibrous carbs, vegetables, potatoes, beans and legumes.

## The study, and why the dosages are the argument
He chose 1.8 g/kg because that is his recommendation for maximising muscle growth, and 2.9 g/kg — over 50% higher — because it was a large but still realistic contrast. If a satiety benefit existed above the muscle-growth threshold, this gap should have exposed it. It didn't, on any measure.

They also tested for **habituation** — whether the appetite or hormonal response to a given intake drifts over time. Prior work found high-protein diets more satiating at first, with people desensitising later. He found no evidence of habituation, which he takes as reason to expect the null to persist. He is honest that there was no satiety benefit to habituate *to* in the first place.

## Reconciling this with the studies that disagree
He accepts that plenty of studies find higher protein reduces hunger, and his answer is dosage: nearly all that evidence comes from **relatively low protein intakes by bodybuilding standards, in people who don't lift**. Those studies move people from deficient to adequate. His moved people from adequate to surplus.

Protein leverage theory formalises this — a "protein stat" analogous to thirst or salt appetite drives eating until protein needs are met. Mechanistically, anabolic signalling (mTOR activation, AMPK downregulation) appears to trigger the appetite-suppressing response, because it signals enough protein has arrived. Once protein starts being oxidised for energy, further intake has no effect.

Evolutionary rationale: the body can't store protein efficiently, so a drive to seek it out is useful — but it must not be strong enough to stop you eating carbs and fats when plentiful. Supporting evidence: protein leverage is *stronger* in other species, with birds, rats and pigs self-selecting diets with complementary amino acid profiles matching their requirements.

## Satiety and satisfaction
He tells clients to optimise both. Satiating but unsatisfying is hard to adhere to even without hunger; satisfying but hungry is equally unsustainable. Low-energy-density, high-volume, high-fibre foods are the tool for satiety once protein is covered.

**Overreach flag:** the key study is a one-week crossover in a small trained sample, and is his own. The null on every single measure is presented as fairly settled. The protein-leverage breakpoint evidence is stronger than the direct study evidence here.
