# Sex-Based Performance Differences and the Effect of Testosterone Suppression

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The science of why transgenders are setting so many sports records" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H_J3cMQzyJE)) · 2023-06-07 · 21 min
**Topic:** Physiology

*This note records what the cited evidence establishes about measurable performance parameters, and separately records Henselmans's argument and policy position as his. The video's framing and title rhetoric are his, not this note's. Where possible, people are referred to with the terms they use for themselves: trans women, trans men, cisgender women.*

## Bottom line
- The two systematic reviews he cites (both 2021, neither named in the video and no references given in the description) report that **testosterone suppression produces only small reductions in strength, lean body mass, muscle size and bone density relative to the size of the baseline male–female difference in those parameters**, and that values in trans women **remain above cisgender female values at 36 months** of hormone therapy.
- The single quantified figure he gives from those reviews: after years of hormone therapy, the **average trans woman remains in roughly the top 10% of cisgender women for lean body mass and grip strength**.
- **No numeric effect sizes appear anywhere in the video.** "Trivially affected", "very small" and "top 10%" are the only magnitude language given, and all come from the reviews' own summaries rather than from data he presents.
- Eligibility thresholds of **10 nmol/L, more recently 5 nmol/L**, sit well above the **~2 nmol/L** upper reference limit he gives for cisgender women.
- The remainder of his case — anatomical parameters, prenatal androgen exposure, personality and visuospatial cognition — is **asserted without citations**, and the link from those parameters to sporting outcomes is inference rather than measured performance data.
- His policy section is explicitly his own opinion and is separated below.

## What the cited reviews report

**Review A (2021, systematic review).** Strength, lean body mass, muscle size and bone density are "only trivially affected by testosterone suppression"; the reductions observed are small compared with the baseline male–female difference in those same variables. Reported outcome: the average trans woman after years of hormone therapy sits in the top decile of cisgender women for lean body mass and for grip strength.

**Review B (2021, systematic literature review).** Hormone therapy does decrease strength, lean body mass and muscle area, but values remain above those observed in cisgender women **even after 36 months**. The review's own conclusion, as he quotes it, is that strength "may be well preserved" in trans women during the first three years of hormone therapy.

**Limitations that apply to both, and which the video does not state:** neither review is identified, so nothing here is independently verifiable from this note; both synthesise a small literature, largely on non-athletes, much of it observational and with short follow-up; and 36 months is the longest interval reported, so nothing in the cited evidence speaks to longer timeframes. Henselmans extends the 36-month finding himself, reasoning that if strength is preserved at three years it is "preserved pretty much indefinitely" — **that is an extrapolation, not a finding of the review.**

## Parameters he discusses without citation

**Pre-pubertal differences.** He cites a study of pre-pubertal children finding boys scored higher on all selected tests except **balance and flexibility**, where girls scored better. No effect sizes, sample size or reference given.

**Prenatal androgen exposure.** He treats the **2D:4D digit ratio** as a marker of prenatal testosterone exposure and says it correlates with performance across a range of sports. The proxy is contested in the literature and the reported correlations are typically small; he presents neither caveat.

**Anatomical parameters.** He lists proportionally longer limbs, a higher centre of gravity, longer forearms combined with a **lower carrying angle** (favouring grip strength), and a **Q angle roughly double in women** (reducing knee extension torque and raising knee injury risk). These are real, well-documented anatomical sex differences; the video gives no figures for them beyond "approximately double" for Q angle, and no study linking them quantitatively to performance. He additionally asserts that the average untrained man exceeds most female athletes on grip strength, including athletes in strength sports — a strong claim presented with no source.

**Personality and cognition.** He cites higher trait neuroticism in women on Big Five measures, and a male advantage in visuospatial cognition including object tracking and throwing accuracy. **The chain from these traits to sporting outcomes is his inference** — he cites no study measuring sport performance as a function of either trait, and offers an evolutionary account (hunting and fighting selection) that is speculation rather than evidence.

**Bone density in the face** as a factor reducing injury risk in combat sports is asserted in the policy section with no source.

## Anecdotes, which he presents alongside the evidence
He opens with the Avi Silverberg bench press entry in the Canadian Powerlifting Union and Anne Andres's competition record, describes a 2017 scrimmage in which an FC Dallas under-15 boys' team beat the US women's national team 5–2, and plays a montage of competition footage. **These are individual cases and an unofficial match, not data**, and he does not label them as such — a reader should treat the systematic reviews and the anecdotes as carrying very different weight.

## Where the argument generalises beyond its sources
He moves from group-average differences measured largely in non-athlete trans women to a conclusion about competitive fairness in "basically every sport", and treats the reviews' aggregate findings as settling individual-level cases; neither review he cites was designed to support that step.

## His position on policy (his opinion, stated as such in the video)
- He concludes that on fairness grounds trans women "cannot fairly compete with biological women", and that trans men competing with men raises no comparable concern.
- He then argues fairness is not the only consideration and is partly illusory anyway, because sport is already dominated by uncontrollable genetic variation between individuals — he uses Michael Jordan and Ronnie Coleman as examples.
- He proposes a utilitarian balancing: at recreational level inclusivity may reasonably win; at elite level he argues the interests of the larger group should prevail, and notes that trans women retain the option of competing in the men's category while women have no equivalent option.
- He opposes **both** a legal mandate for inclusion (his objection to the USA Powerlifting ruling) **and** a law prohibiting it; his stated preference is that individual sporting organisations set their own rules.
- On a separate trans category: he thinks it would fail for lack of entrants and viewership, drawing an analogy to women's bodybuilding and masters categories, and would not feel inclusive. He suggests an **open category** as a possible alternative.
- He states support for pronouns, name and document changes outside of sport.
