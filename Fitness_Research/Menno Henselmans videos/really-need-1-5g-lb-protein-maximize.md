# Why Henselmans Rejects the 3.2 g/kg Protein Recommendation

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Do you really need 1.5g/lb of protein to maximize gains?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=thfy9VdHzyo)) · 2025-02-06 · 25 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- A 2025 meta-analysis of **29 studies** in non-obese, energy-restricted trainees found a **linear** relationship between protein intake and fat-free mass change across **0.8–3.2 g/kg/day**, and concluded that serious trainees should eat toward the top of that range (**3.2 g/kg ≈ 1.5 g/lb**). Henselmans rejects the recommendation while accepting the analysis was competently done.
- His core objection: **no breakpoint or asymptotic model was fitted**. The authors compared linear, quadratic and cubic models only. With a linear fit, the recommendation is set by whatever the highest intake in the dataset happens to be — add one study at 5 g/kg and the recommendation would become 5 g/kg.
- The authors' own effect size: moving from **0.8 all the way to 3.2 g/kg produces only a small effect size difference** in fat-free mass. Going from ~1.8 g/kg to higher is therefore a very small increment.
- His position, unchanged for over a decade: **1.8 g/kg/day (0.8 g/lb)** maximises gains, with a defensible range of **1.6–2.0 g/kg/day**. **No well-controlled RCT has yet found a significant benefit above 1.6 g/kg/day.**
- Two findings he says were overlooked: **basing protein on fat-free mass is no more accurate than using total body weight**, and **larger energy deficits did not increase the protein requirement** — if anything the effect of protein was smaller in bigger deficits.

## Compared with what is already in this library
Our library's bulking target is **1.6–2.2 g/kg (0.7–1.0 g/lb)**. Henselmans's 1.6–2.0 g/kg sits inside that band, so his argument here **supports rather than contradicts** the existing library position — the disagreement is with the new meta-analysis, not with the established consensus. One caveat when comparing: this meta-analysis was on people in an **energy deficit** (cutting), whereas the library figure is a bulking target.

## What the meta-analysis did
29 studies, non-obese participants, all in energy deficit, with a minimum of only **3 months of resistance training experience** — so many participants were near-novices. Outcome was fat-free mass as a proxy for muscle. Protein intakes spanned 0.8–3.2 g/kg. Best-fitting model was linear, hence the recommendation to go high. Henselmans names Eric Trexler and Eric Helms as co-authors and says his disagreement is with the analysis, not the researchers.

## His five objections

**1. A positive association across a range does not license the top of the range.** The range starts at 0.8 g/kg, which is a sedentary intake. Of course higher intakes beat that. All the analysis strictly establishes is that intakes above 0.8 g/kg are beneficial — which he says teaches nothing new. What matters is the breakpoint, and the earlier meta-analysis he co-authored put it at **1.6 g/kg/day** ([Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)).

**2. The model set was theoretically wrong.** Nobody actually believes protein-to-growth is linear (unbounded), quadratic (gains eventually fall back to baseline) or cubic (multiple inflection points). The expected shape is diminishing returns, consistent with the **muscle full effect** — there is a ceiling on how much protein synthesis can be stimulated. A logarithmic or asymptotic fit, or a breakpoint analysis, would have addressed the actual question; none was run. Choosing the model that best fits the data over the model that makes theoretical sense is, in his view, the error.

**3. The dataset is heavily confounded.** Only **5 studies** met the original strict criteria — too few, so criteria were widened to reach 29. The resulting set includes studies designed to test protein quality, nutrient timing, energy intake, ketogenic dieting and intermittent fasting. Even within the comparisons that were about protein dose, groups often also differed in meal frequency, timing, energy intake, protein quality and in the training itself (habitual rather than standardised programs). He credits the authors for labelling the meta-regressions **exploratory** and noting the intervention heterogeneity, but says that caveat should limit how literally the numbers are read.

**4. Dropping energy deficit from the model.** Protein alone fitted better statistically than protein plus moderators (biological sex, deficit size and others), so deficit was not retained. He argues prior meta-analytic and RCT evidence establishes that deficit size affects fat-free mass change, and that a variable with that much support should not be dropped purely on model fit.

**5. Analytic choices that all lean the same way.** Implausibly large reported energy deficits were replaced with the median because that improved model fit; outliers were removed because removing them strengthened the protein–FFM relationship. Outliers should be excluded on pre-specified criteria (e.g. beyond two standard deviations from the median), he argues, not because their removal supports the hypothesis. He sees no intentional cherry-picking and expects the bias to be small — but it runs in one direction.

## Finding 1: fat-free mass is not a better basis than body weight
Model fit was essentially identical whether protein was scaled to body weight or to fat-free mass: **R² of 54% vs 55%**, and an **identical standard error of 0.09**. The authors nonetheless concluded FFM was better, on a 2% difference in the probability that extra protein benefited FFM change — which Henselmans says is fitting the hypothesis, not the data.

His mechanistic explanation: a kilo of muscle does require more protein than a kilo of fat, but both are dwarfed by **organ mass**, which is the strongest predictor of protein requirement and which scales fairly stably with body weight and height. Practically, since most people cannot measure body fat accurately and most consumer methods are unreliable, using fat-free mass may actually be *less* accurate. **If you are not overweight, use total body weight.**

One related result: the effect of protein on FFM change was larger the leaner the participants — but this held in both the body-weight and fat-free-mass models, so it likely reflects the higher risk of muscle loss at low body fat rather than anything about FFM scaling.

## Finding 2: bigger deficits do not need more protein
A subgroup analysis split deficits at **300 kcal**. The effect of protein, if anything, **decreased** in the larger deficits. Henselmans says he previously held the standard view that steeper deficits warrant higher protein, and now regards it as unsupported: protein availability and energy availability are separate constraints, and you cannot eat past an energy deficit with protein. He also notes the meta cannot support "higher protein while cutting" at all, because it never compared deficit against non-deficit conditions.

## Where he lands
- His original article (over a decade old, based on 20+ studies plus nitrogen balance data) argued **1.8 g/kg** is the highest intake likely needed. He acknowledges Milo Wolf and Greg Nuckols have criticised the reliance on nitrogen balance, and concedes better methods now exist — but did not when he wrote it.
- Roughly **3 of ~90 relevant studies** have found benefits from high protein, and all were confounded by nutrient timing and usually by energy surplus.
- The three **indicator amino acid oxidation (IAAO)** studies give: **1.7 g/kg** (male bodybuilders, non-training days, 2017), **1.5 g/kg** (female lifters, training days, 2019) and **2.0 g/kg** (male bodybuilders, training days, 2020). He argues the training-day figure overstates the daily average because it extrapolates from the post-workout window; 2.0 on training days and ~1.6 on rest days averages to 1.8.
- He rejects the practice of taking the **upper bound of the 95% CI** as the target on individual-variation grounds: that interval also absorbs measurement error, sleep, training effort, prior intake and diet phase, not just physiological variation between people.
- Final answer: **1.6–2.0 g/kg/day**, or the old 1 g/lb heuristic if you want margin. Anything above that buys, at most, a very small amount of extra gain. He says he is running his own meta-analysis with what he considers better methods.

**Where the argument outruns the evidence:** his claim that organ mass rather than lean mass drives protein requirements is presented as settled, but no study is cited for it, and it is doing real work in his recommendation to scale protein to total body weight.
