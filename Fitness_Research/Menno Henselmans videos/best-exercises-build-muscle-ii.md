# Seven Exercise Face-Offs: Where Exercise Choice Actually Matters

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "These are the best exercises to build muscle (science-based) II" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=55X7k0Cvojw)) · 2025-09-23 · 9 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Six of the seven head-to-head comparisons came out **roughly tied**. Where biomechanics match, the implement — barbell, machine, bodyweight, kettlebell — doesn't matter.
- **Push-ups equal bench presses** for chest and triceps when loaded to the same rep max. The only real problem with push-ups is loading them heavily enough.
- **Preacher and incline curls tie overall** but grow different regions: preacher favours the lower elbow flexors (brachialis), incline the upper.
- **Standing calf raises are the staple**; bent-knee (seated) calf raises can only grow the soleus, because the gastrocnemius is too shortened to contribute.
- **Leg extensions are the more complete quad exercise** than squats on paper — they grow the rectus femoris more — but squats win on usable range of motion in practice.
- **Incline bench does not increase upper pec activity.** It reduces lower pec involvement. This is his most contrarian position in the video.
- The one lopsided result: **dumbbell rows built only about half the biceps growth of curls**.

## The framing
These are equated comparisons — same volume, same relative intensity — so the exercise itself is the only variable. His rule going in: if two exercises have similar biomechanics, they produce similar outcomes regardless of the tool.

## 1. Bench press vs push-ups (chest and triceps)
Exactly equal, per multiple studies, when the push-ups are loaded (bands, harder variations) to the same rep max. Biomechanically they are near-interchangeable, so open vs closed kinetic chain and "naturalness" buy you nothing. The practical constraint is loading.

## 2. Preacher curls vs incline curls
The competing logics: incline curls put the biceps at a longer muscle length because it also crosses the shoulder — but as most people perform them, with the upper arm vertical at the bottom, gravity supplies almost no resistance in that stretched position. Preacher curls shorten the biceps at the shoulder but load the bottom hardest.

Result: approximately equal, with a regional split — preacher for the lower elbow flexors (brachialis), incline for the upper, likely the biceps proper. He describes a "holy trinity" of Bayesian, preacher and incline curls competing for the top spot, and recommends combining them.

## 3. Hip thrusts vs barbell back squats (glutes)
His own group's MRI study: no significant differences in lower, middle or upper gluteus maximus, nor in medius or minimus. His mechanistic reading is that hip thrusts generate more active tension (highest force potential at the top, corroborated by EMG and subjective activation), while squats offer more range of motion, longer muscle lengths and more passive tension at the bottom — and these roughly cancel.

Two side findings: neither medius nor minimus grew significantly from either exercise, so the "side booty" needs dedicated abduction work; and since the two likely stimulate the glutes differently, do both.

## 4. Standing vs seated calf raises
Standing wins for the calves as a whole. The distinction is not seated vs standing but **bent vs straight knee**: with the knee bent the gastrocnemius is too shortened to contribute meaningfully — active insufficiency — so a bent-knee calf raise only grows the soleus. Straight-leg work grows soleus and both gastrocnemius heads. He positions bent-knee calf raises as an optional soleus add-on, pairing well with lying leg curls.

## 5. Leg extensions vs squats (quads)
Approximately equal overall, but leg extensions grow the **rectus femoris** more — the middle portion often neglected because it isn't the sweep or the teardrop, and which adds noticeably to definition at low body fat. So leg extensions are paradoxically the more complete quad exercise. The counterweight is practical: most leg extension machines don't permit full range of motion, whereas a trained lifter can squat to parallel or below. Do both.

## 6. Incline vs flat bench for the upper chest
This is the one where he breaks hard with common belief. Multiple EMG and ultrasound studies find that inclining the bench does **not** increase upper pec activity, and does not increase muscle damage or swelling there either. What inclining does is *reduce* lower pec activity. Flat pressing and fly movements grow upper and lower pec regions about equally in the research he's drawing on.

He is honest about the weak point: there is a lack of long-term studies measuring actual growth, and the single one that exists found incline pressing better for both pec heads. He dismisses it on methodological grounds and refers the controversy to another video. So the position rests mostly on activity and damage proxies rather than on measured hypertrophy — worth holding loosely.

## 7. Biceps curls vs dumbbell rows
The one clear loser. With grip equated (both supinated), dumbbell rows grew the biceps only about **half** as well as curls, and pull-downs appear substantially better than dumbbell rows.

His explanation: pull-downs demand more elbow flexion over a greater range, and holding a fixed implement like a bar produces horizontal force from the elbow flexors — a non-trivial contribution. During a dumbbell row the biceps can't fully contract without pulling you out of position. Same parallel for triceps: dumbbell bench presses are a poor triceps exercise, while barbell bench presses are respectable for the medial and lateral heads.
