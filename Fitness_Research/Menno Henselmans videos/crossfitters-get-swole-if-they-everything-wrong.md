# Why CrossFitters Look Big Despite Suboptimal Bodybuilding Programming

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "How do CrossFitters get so swole if they do everything wrong?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MWKXkXC7Y0I)) · 2023-01-13 · 6 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- A meta-analysis he had shared shortly before found that **HIIT produces a worse interference effect on muscle growth than low-intensity steady-state cardio** — and CrossFit is built largely on high-intensity interval work, done in the same session as the strength work, which is the worst case.
- CrossFit is **not optimised for hypertrophy**: high-intensity intervals in-session, a lot of power work, and very little that emphasises the eccentric, where mechanical tension is highest. But CrossFit's goal is CrossFit performance, so "wrong" here means wrong for bodybuilding only.
- The impressive physiques people cite are the **top 10 at the Games** compared against the average gym-goer — an unfair comparison twice over: elite genetics, and, in his view, near-universal PED use at that level with unimpressive drug testing.
- Run those athletes' stats through an **FFMI/muscular potential calculator**: if it returns 1%, it means 99% of the population couldn't reach that physique naturally. That is an indication, not proof, for any individual — but across a top 10 it becomes a pattern.
- CrossFit is nonetheless **far from bad at building muscle** — better than running, cycling or tennis, because it is still a strength sport with a lot of resistance training in it.

## What CrossFitters get right
This is the substantive half of the video, and his point is that fundamentals beat optimisation:

- **They train genuinely hard.** The culture rewards effort to the point of injury — "no pain, no gain," suck it up. He contrasts this with the average commercial gym, where people do leg extensions on their phone. Most people's results are limited by effort, not programming.
- **They do heavy strength training.** Not always the ideal exercise selection, but performed with maximum effort.
- **Whole-food, high-protein diets.** The paleo-adjacent CrossFit diet culture makes eating part of the lifestyle rather than a summer chore.
- **Relatedness.** He cites his own book on why CrossFit works: the community turns diet and training into group identity, which drives adherence far better than individual willpower.

## The general lesson
The exceptional-looking members of any sport are exceptions. The same pattern shows up in baseball, rugby, powerlifting, strongman, even some tennis players: a handful of outliers with good genetics and pharmacological help look like they could step on a bodybuilding stage, while the average participant does not. Selecting on the outliers and attributing their physique to the sport's programming is the error.

His closing rule is blunt: **if you want to get better at CrossFit, do CrossFit; if you want to get better at bodybuilding, do bodybuilding.** Choosing a sport as a hypertrophy method is choosing the wrong tool.

## Where the claim outruns the evidence
The PED accusation is the strongest claim in the video and rests on no cited evidence beyond incentives, the general state of drug testing, and calculator outputs. He hedges at the individual level ("we'll never know") but not at the group level. The muscular-potential calculator is his own, and the population percentiles it returns depend on assumptions about the natural FFMI ceiling that are themselves contested.

Note also that no reference for the HIIT-versus-steady-state interference meta-analysis is given anywhere — it opens the video and carries a lot of its argument.
