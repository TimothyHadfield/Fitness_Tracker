# Warming Up Does Almost Nothing For Lifting Performance

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Is warming up BS like cooling down? [New study]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_soBOW_NIrQ)) · 2025-07-29 · 9 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- A new preprint had trained lifters do **four sets of leg press and bench press to failure at 10RM** under three conditions — **no warm-up at all**, **one warm-up set** (3–4 reps at 75% of 10RM), or **two** (3–4 reps at 55% then 75%). Total reps were **near-identical** across all three. No effect on mental readiness either.
- This is not an outlier: past research finds warm-ups do little for **higher-rep** lifting performance. Effects show up mainly for **1RM, maximal force, rate of force development and power**.
- Henselmans's position against mainstream wisdom and most PT certifications: **warming up is overrated, and there is zero hard data that it reduces injury rates.** He still recommends warming up, on theory and feel, not on evidence.
- Practical rule: **one warm-up set per exercise** to check range of motion and pain, then start. Beyond that, go by feel.
- **Dynamic stretching is redundant** — warm-up sets are weighted dynamic stretching. **Static stretching of the muscles you're about to train should be avoided**; it reduces motor neuron excitability and force output.

## Why the theory predicted an effect and the data don't
[01:41] The mechanisms are real: warmer muscle is more elastic, nerve conduction velocity rises, chemical reaction rates and blood flow increase. Henselmans's point is that these are **literally temperature effects** — "warming up" is not a metaphor — and that they matter for maximal force, rate of force development and endurance work, none of which describes a set of ten.

[03:04] His resolution: for a 10-rep set, **the first reps of the working set are themselves the warm-up**, sufficient to improve the later reps when it actually gets hard. He invites the reader to test it — do a 20RM bench press cold and count the reps.

## The two reasons he still says warm up
[05:25] **Muscle damage and recovery.** Evidence that pre-workout **vibration training** reduces muscle damage markers suggests a warmer, more elastic muscle takes less damage — which would raise recoverable volume and indirectly improve gains. Note this is an argument from a vibration-training meta-analysis being generalised to warm-up sets in general.

**Injury prevention.** He calls this the strongest remaining argument and then says plainly that **there is no hard evidence — "zero hard data" — that warming up lowers injury rates.** He backs it with theory (stiff tissue tears more easily) and anecdote (pain is likelier when cold; nobody sinks into a max squat or an all-out sprint cold, and people have been injured doing exactly that). He also notes that he personally needs one or two warm-up sets before he can comfortably hit squat depth, which makes the question partly moot in practice.

## Stretching
[07:41] A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis (Warneke et al.) found **dynamic stretching and foam rolling are no better than any other warm-up** for acute flexibility and stiffness — consistent with the effects being temperature-mediated. The best dynamic stretch before squatting is squatting.

Static stretching is the one thing he tells you to actively avoid: it prepares a muscle for relaxation, not contraction, cutting force output, with research suggesting it can blunt strength and hypertrophy. Stretching a muscle you are **not** about to train — shoulders before back squats, wrists before front squats — is fine. Light stretching is fine. Heavy static stretching of quads, hamstrings and glutes before squatting is not.

## Where this sits
This is a genuinely contrarian position, and the honest version of it is narrower than the title: the claim is that warm-ups don't improve **rep performance on moderate-to-high-rep sets in trained lifters**, not that they do nothing. He accepts the maximal-effort exception, and his final recommendation — always at least one warm-up set, err on the side of more if you can't judge by feel — is not far from conventional practice. What he strips out is the elaborate ritual: activation drills, dynamic stretching routines, timed protocols. Walking or cycling to the gym, or wearing a hoodie, does most of the job.

One flag: he uses the absence of injury-prevention evidence as grounds for calling warm-ups overrated, while simultaneously recommending warm-ups *because* of injury prevention. The absence of hard data cuts both ways here, and he doesn't note any trial that looked and found nothing.
