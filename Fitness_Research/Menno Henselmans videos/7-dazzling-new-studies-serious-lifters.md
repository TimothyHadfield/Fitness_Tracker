# Eight New Studies: Hardgainers, Lengthened Bias, Eccentrics, Food Tracking, Priming, Stretching, Supersets

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "7 Dazzling New Studies For Serious Lifters [2025]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qbfwmSLy58c)) · 2026-01-06 · 13 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Non-responders are largely an artefact.** Repeat the same 10-week programme and the people who "didn't respond" the first time do respond the second. Nobody failed to gain in both phases.
- **Lengthened-bias training via cam machines produced no advantage** over shortened-bias for delts, pecs or glutes. Increasing range of motion is a more reliable lever than shifting where the resistance peaks.
- **Eccentrics may not be inherently more damaging than concentrics** once unfamiliarity is controlled for.
- **MyFitnessPal is unreliable**: 226 kcal/day average disagreement between users logging the same food, plus ~94 kcal/day database error. Cronometer performed much better.
- **AI photo calorie estimation was off by 36%** on daily intake — enough to turn a planned 20% deficit into a surplus.
- **Post-workout stretching does nothing** for soreness, strength, performance or injury risk. **Supersets** save large amounts of time without impairing gains.

## 1. Hardgainers
[00:00] Participants ran a 10-week programme, were classified as responders or non-responders, then repeated it. Phase-to-phase correlations existed, but **nobody was a complete non-responder** — some gained no measurable muscle in one phase, none in both, and this held for both biceps and quads. Menno's reading: the failure was training, effort, volume prescription or lifestyle, not genetics. He cross-references earlier work showing individuals who don't respond to one training volume responding to another. The coaching anecdote that follows is rhetoric, not evidence.

## 2. Lengthened-bias training
[02:33] Unilateral reverse flyes, chest flyes, lateral raises and multi-hip kickbacks to failure, one limb on a cam that loads the lengthened position and one on a cam that loads the shortened position. After 10 weeks, **no significant difference** in strength or MRI-measured growth. This lines up with prior lateral raise and curl work: if you use full range of motion, emphasising one portion makes at best a small difference.

[03:34] His conclusion is deliberately contrarian to a current trend: **extending range of motion into longer muscle lengths is the reliable lever**, and going all-out to maximise tension only at long lengths is probably unnecessary. He also offers a mechanism-based caveat — glutes, delts and pecs may benefit less because of their length-tension curves, with glutes seemingly producing most tension at relatively short lengths. And he notes leg presses to full depth do not out-grow a merely decent depth, so squatting past parallel to ass-to-grass is not clearly better.

## 3. Eccentric vs concentric damage
[04:35] Untrained women, equally unfamiliar with both, trained one biceps concentrically and one eccentrically — 75 maximal contractions each. Both got very sore, but over four days there were **no significant differences** in damage markers, soreness or strength recovery. Menno's interpretation: much of the classic eccentric-damage finding is an unfamiliarity effect, since nobody trains eccentric-only. He still expects eccentrics to cause somewhat more damage, since they permit more force.

## 4. MyFitnessPal
[06:06] Canadian athletes' food logs were entered into MyFitnessPal, Cronometer and the Canadian Nutrient File. Two failures: different users pick different database entries for the same food, producing an average **226 kcal/day** difference (95% CI −326 to +776); and entries flagged as verified still disagreed with the official database by about **94 kcal/day**. His workaround: MyFitnessPal is fine if you only enter your own food labels — at which point it is a spreadsheet.

## 5. AI calorie estimation from photos
[08:03] Mean error of **36%** on daily calorie intake. The main cause is hidden calories — restaurant oil, butter, sugar, sauces. The photos even included cutlery as a size reference, so performance without that would be worse. Fair caveat: the models tested (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5 Pro) are dated.

## 6. Priming
[09:37] Trained men sprinted faster and jumped higher **6 hours after** a heavy squat session (4x5 at 85% 1RM). Fits an existing potentiation literature: post-activation potentiation within about a minute, post-activation performance enhancement over 3-5 minutes, and apparently longer-horizon effects across a day. He discounts the study's trend favouring cluster sets as statistically too weak to act on.

## 7. Post-workout stretching
[11:08] A meta-analysis of 15 studies found no significant effect on soreness, strength or performance; other work finds stretching does not change injury susceptibility. His mechanistic account: stretching adaptations are largely **neural** — reduced pain sensitivity, nervous system permission to relax further — not increases in actual muscle length. So they are highly specific to the stretch trained. Stretch if you want the splits; do not stretch for recovery.

## 8 (bonus). Supersets
[12:19] A review of agonist-antagonist supersets (bench and row) and upper-lower supersets (squats and chin-ups) concluded they do not on average impair muscle activity, strength development or growth, while saving substantial time — which lets many people raise volume. The exception is running them truly back-to-back so the second exercise suffers. His preferred solution is **combo sets**, and he names this as his main reason for not recommending push-pull-legs or upper-lower splits.

## Where the claims run ahead of the evidence
The "absolutely everybody can get lean, that is physical law" section is coaching conviction rather than a study finding, and the non-responder study only shows that response is inconsistent across phases, not that everyone can reach any given outcome. The lengthened-bias conclusion also generalises from one 10-week study on three muscles that he himself says may be the muscles least likely to benefit.
