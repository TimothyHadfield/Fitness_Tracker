# Seven Basic Nutrition Questions, Answered Bluntly

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Nutrition scientist answers the web's 7 most searched questions" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R-P_Oggr1Bc)) · 2024-08-26 · 9 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The best fat-loss exercise is **strength training, not cardio** — it protects and builds the muscle that keeps energy expenditure up. At least ~2 hours a week.
- Protein: **1.2 g/kg/day if you don't train; at least 1.6 g/kg if you do**, his working recommendation being **1.8 g/kg** (~0.82 g/lb). Use total body weight, not lean mass.
- Dieting without training costs roughly **25% of the weight lost as lean mass**, lowering metabolic rate and making the rest of the cut harder.
- **No food is a fat-loss food.** The useful properties are high protein, high fibre, high volume, low energy density.
- **Body fat distribution is largely genetic.** Stubborn regions just take longer.
- Pre-workout meal: **≥15 g net carbs and ≥0.3 g/kg protein** (~20 g for most). Nothing above that reliably helps unless the session is long or highly glycolytic.
- **Caffeine is the only active ingredient in pre-workouts** — and even caffeine, he argues, doesn't improve long-term adaptations.

## Fat loss, not weight loss
He reframes the first question immediately: you want to lose fat, not weight, and that changes the answer. Strength training beats cardio for fat loss on average, he says, because it retains muscle — and retaining or building muscle maintains metabolic rate going forward and raises expenditure through carrying more mass.

Prescription: at least two hours of strength training per week, more with time and inclination, cardio added on top. Cardio's value he places mostly in health plus calories burned — worth exactly as much as the same deficit created by eating less, and no more. The core is a high-protein diet plus strength training plus a consistent deficit.

The claim that strength training beats cardio "in research, at least in the long term" comes without a citation and is stronger than most reviews would put it — expenditure per session favours cardio, and muscle retention is what he's actually leaning on.

## Protein
- Not training: ~1.2 g/kg/day — already above official recommendations, justified by satiety and health.
- Training: at least 1.6 g/kg (~0.7 g/lb) as *total* intake from all sources, assuming at least half from high-quality sources.
- His own default: 1.8 g/kg (~0.82 g/lb).

Use **total body weight**, not lean body mass — a practical rather than physiological argument: whatever accuracy lean mass adds is lost to the fact that almost nobody knows their body fat percentage accurately.

## Losing weight without exercise
You can, but you lose more than you should. Around 25% of the weight lost will be lean mass, whether dieting conventionally or on a GLP-1 agonist. That drops metabolic rate, so you have to eat progressively less to keep losing — which is why strength training during a cut matters.

## Best foods, and belly fat
There is no such thing as a fat-loss food, and no magic blueberry. What helps is anything that lets you create a deficit without hunger or under-eating protein — high protein, high fibre, high volume, low energy density. Vegetables, fruit, lean meat, fish, egg whites with some whole eggs.

Belly fat: distribution is essentially a fixed genetic blueprint, pear-shaped versus apple-shaped. Better health, diet and exercise shift it somewhat toward a more decentralised (and healthier, less visceral) pattern, but beyond that where you lose fat is not under your control. Stubborn regions — lower abs for most men — simply come off last. Getting from 15% to 10% is the same process as getting to 15%, just longer.

## What to eat before training
From a systematic review he says he ran himself: at least **15 g of net carbohydrate** (excluding fibre) and at least **0.3 g/kg protein**, around 20 g for most people. Beyond those thresholds, no reliable further performance gains.

The exception is high glycolytic demand — sessions over about 90 minutes, or high-intensity interval work — where the body is forced onto carbohydrate. Below roughly 60–90 minutes you run on liver and muscle glycogen, not on what you just ate; and a large meal eaten immediately pre-training isn't absorbed in time to be used during the session anyway.

He recommends against training fasted, and the interesting part is the ranking of reasons. Performance is the smaller effect. The larger one is anabolism: mTOR integrates signals including circulating amino acids and available energy as an indicator of how much adaptation to permit — for endurance training as well as strength.

## Pre-workout supplements
His most combative answer. Pre-workouts do "not much." Caffeine is the only active ingredient; citrulline, beta-alanine and similar have not been shown in long-term research to improve strength development or muscle growth. He also raises possible negative interactions between caffeine and creatine, suggesting they may not belong in the same product.

Then he goes after caffeine too: its effect is **psychological**, not physical — more motivation to train rather than meaningfully more force production. And even caffeine fails to improve long-term adaptations, because the mental effect is modest and may fade while tolerance erodes any physical effect. Conclusion: the best pre-workout is whichever one you enjoy taking.

This goes further than the evidence-based mainstream. The ISSN position stands endorse caffeine as a genuine ergogenic aid for strength and power, and beta-alanine for efforts of roughly 1–4 minutes. Henselmans isn't disputing the acute performance data so much as arguing acute effects don't translate into long-term gains — defensible but distinctly minority, and asserted here rather than cited.
