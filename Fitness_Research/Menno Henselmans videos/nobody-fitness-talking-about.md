# Low Fat Intake and Injury Risk, and Oxidised Fish Oil

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Why is nobody in fitness talking about this?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uudSoRSny1U)) · 2026-06-09 · 7 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- A meta-analysis of nine prospective studies in female distance runners found **lower total energy and fat intake associated with higher rates of running-related and bone stress injuries**. Carbohydrate and protein intakes showed no such relationship.
- The mechanism he proposes: intakes around **20% of calories from fat** are associated with reduced testosterone, oestrogen and growth hormone, all of which support connective tissue recovery.
- His practical floor: **never below 20% of calories from fat**, and 30-40% is preferable when calories allow. Protein is the last macro to compromise; carbs versus fat is the trade-off to make in a deep deficit.
- Separately: **lower training volume when cutting.** An energy deficit is a recovery deficit, and relative energy deficiency in sport reliably raises injury rates.
- Omega-3 fats oxidise readily during manufacture and storage, and **oxidised omega-3 is toxic rather than merely inert**. Independent surveys repeatedly find most supplements exceed safe oxidation limits or miss their label content ([Albert 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep07928), [Mason 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28011269/)).

## The injury data and their limits
Henselmans is unusually careful here for a video framed as an alarm. He flags four problems himself:

1. The studies are **associative**. Prospective design suggests low fat intake preceded injury, but the design compared injured against uninjured runners and then looked back at macros, rather than assigning fat intakes and watching injuries accrue.
2. The relationship reached significance **only in women**. He argues the male analysis had roughly half the data points and was therefore underpowered — a plausible reading, but an interpretation rather than a finding.
3. **Low fat intake and low energy intake cannot be disentangled.** Cutting fat cuts calories. That fat and not carbs or protein tracked injury is suggestive, but not proof that fat is the operative variable rather than total energy.
4. Running carries roughly **ten times the injury rate of resistance training**, because of repeated uncontrolled impact — so the transfer to lifters is an inference, not a demonstrated result.

His conclusion is correspondingly hedged: low energy availability probably raises injury risk, and low fat intake *might* add to it.

## Oxidised omega-3
The claim that you "might be paying for poison" is the more combative half of the video, and it rests on thinner ground than the injury section. Human data on oxidised fish oil are, by his own account, very limited and short-term, showing maybe some harm and maybe not; the alarming data are from animals. Human trials of omega-3 supplements — oxidised ones included, since nobody screens them — still mostly show net positive health outcomes. His argument is essentially about accumulated small risk over decades of daily use, and about paying for a benefit you may not be getting.

The actionable part: buy only a product with **independent lab work confirming label content and oxidation levels within safe limits**. He named specific brands in the video (unaffiliated, compiled for his students); those are not recoverable from the description.

## Where he overreaches
"Paying for poison" is stronger than the evidence he then presents, and he walks it back within two minutes. He also asserts supplements "might have heavy metals in them" without citing the survey that found this.
