# Heat Therapy: The Mirror Image of Ice Baths, Only Much Weaker

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Ice baths kill gains. Does the sauna do the opposite? [New research]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B6ZzQRNcBE4)) · 2026-08-25 · 5 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- The logic is symmetrical but the payoff is not. Cold blunts the inflammatory signalling that drives repair; heat is itself a **hormetic stressor** that should add stimulus. A preprint meta-analysis of six studies gives a **90% Bayesian probability that heat favours hypertrophy — with a very small effect and a credible interval extending below zero**. For strength the effect size was **almost exactly zero**.
- The cardiovascular case is stronger than the muscle case: a 2018 systematic review on dry sauna bathing found vascular benefits, and a 2021 meta-analysis found a **~4 mmHg reduction in blood pressure**.
- **The heat has to be genuinely unpleasant to do anything.** A 2026 meta-analysis of hot-water immersion up to 41 °C found no significant vascular improvement — the only meaningful effect was a lower heart rate. A relaxing sauna is not the intervention that was studied.
- Heat stress is **not recovery-neutral**. In one of the few elite-athlete trials with a proper placebo control, swim performance the day after a sauna was **impaired**.
- Sauna use **reduces sperm count and motility** at as little as two weekly Finnish sauna sessions. The effect reverses once the heat stops.
- His verdict: enjoy it if you enjoy it. If you don't, **the time, discomfort and money buy more if you spend them training**.

## The mechanism he leans on
Heat speeds chemical reactions and blood flow, but the piece he treats as load-bearing is **heat shock protein activation** under mild hyperthermia. His framing is explicitly hormetic — a stressor the body adapts to, beneficial in small doses only insofar as you can recover from it. That is the same logic our library applies to cold water immersion and to high-dose antioxidants around training, run in the opposite direction: cold and antioxidants *remove* a signal, heat *adds* one.

Note the asymmetry in effect size, though. Cold water immersion's cost to hypertrophy is a reliably measured, practically meaningful loss (over 12 weeks it lost to a ten-minute bike cooldown on every measure). Heat's benefit is a small effect whose interval crosses zero. **Symmetrical mechanism, very unsymmetrical evidence** — and he says so himself rather than overselling it.

## Where the trained/untrained distinction actually bites
He usually argues that untrained-subject findings transfer to trained lifters with a change of degree, not direction. He makes an explicit exception here: if an athlete's vascular system is already well adapted, sauna bathing may **no longer be a sufficient stimulus at all**. That is a direction-of-effect exception, not a magnitude one.

## The placebo problem he can't solve
You cannot blind someone to sitting in a sauna, and most people arrive with positive expectations. He concedes this is a genuine limitation, then argues the mechanistic chain from heat stress to heat shock proteins to vascular adaptation makes a pure placebo account unlikely. That is a reasonable argument but it is not evidence — and it is the one place in the video where he leans on plausibility rather than data.
