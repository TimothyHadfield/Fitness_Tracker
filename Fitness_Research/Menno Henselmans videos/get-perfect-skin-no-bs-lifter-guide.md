# Skin for Lifters: A Dermatologist on Sun, Acne, Hair Loss and Ageing

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "How to get perfect skin: a no-BS lifter's guide" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i7-XaHuD-fg)) · 2026-03-03 · 46 min
**Topic:** Physiology

Interview with **Dr Natalia Spierings**, consultant dermatologist and author of *Skin Intelligence*. Henselmans asks the questions; almost all substantive claims are hers. Settled dermatological position versus personal clinical preference is flagged throughout — the distinction matters here because several recommendations involve prescription drugs.

## Bottom line
- **There is no healthy tan.** A tan is the damage response. Mainstream dermatological consensus, not her personal view — and time of day, tanning bed vs sun, and sunscreen-plus-longer-exposure create no exception.
- **Daily SPF 50 is the whole routine for most people.** Indoors most of the day, a morning application is still ~84% active by evening; reapplying every 2–3 hours is a beach rule, not an office rule.
- **Beyond sunscreen there is no essential skin care.** Healthy skin needs nothing. Moisturiser only blocks water leaving the skin.
- **Acne is genetic and androgen-driven, not caused by hygiene, diet, pillowcases, whey protein or creatine.** OTC options that do something: 2% salicylic acid, benzoyl peroxide 2.5–5% (better for back than face), true retinoids where available.
- **Comedogenicity is largely a myth** for products on sale today; Vaseline is the negative control in comedogenicity testing.
- **Hair loss:** topical minoxidil to start; oral minoxidil plus oral finasteride is what she prescribes for prevention, including to anabolic users.
- **Anti-ageing:** don't smoke, don't tan, skip collagen, and botulinum toxin is the only intervention she rates for lines. **Eye bags and cellulite have no non-surgical fix.**

## Sun and tanning: the decision is a trade, not a technique

Henselmans asked repeatedly whether there's a sweet spot — timing of day, UVA versus UVB, tanning bed versus midday sun, longer with sunscreen versus shorter without. Spierings rejected the framing each time: if you're tanning, your cells are already signalling damage, and the harm is cumulative. She attributes "no such thing as a healthy tan" explicitly to the mainstream dermatological position, which is accurate — it's the standard message from dermatology associations, not an idiosyncratic take.

Two consequences she emphasises. First, cosmetic: photoageing shows up decades later, and most of the damage producing it accumulates in childhood and adolescence — before age twelve especially. Anyone in their 30s is carrying damage they can no longer prevent, only avoid adding to. Second, the one she thinks lifters underrate: non-melanoma skin cancers on the head and neck. Basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma are usually not life-threatening, but excising one from a face can be disfiguring. That reframing, from "just cut it out" to "closure on a small nose is difficult," is the useful part.

On vitamin D she relays the British Association of Dermatologists' guidance directly: don't use sun exposure as a reason to skip sunscreen — supplement instead.

Two questions she couldn't answer and said so: whether you meaningfully tan through aircraft windows at altitude, and how much window tinting changes UV transmission. Glass does pass some UV; the magnitude is unclear to her.

## How much sunscreen effort is actually required

Her practical answer for an average office worker: SPF 50 every morning, and that's it. The load-bearing claim — sunscreen applied in the morning is still about 84% active after a day spent indoors with under an hour outside — comes from her without a citation. She uses it to dismantle the commonest reason people abandon sunscreen: that reapplying every few hours is impossible at work. Beach-day reapplication rules still stand; office reapplication doesn't. She notes formulations have moved on far enough that a mattifying SPF 50 without a white cast is no longer a barrier for men.

Note the honest boundary she draws: sunscreen is a prevention recommendation, not a medical necessity. If you don't care about ageing and accept the skin-cancer risk, "it's not essential either."

## Building a routine: mostly, don't

Spierings is scathing about the industry — she calls it "big skin care" and rates it as far less regulated than pharma. Concrete regulatory points: ingredients present below 2% don't have to be listed, concentrations aren't disclosed, and a product can name retinol among fifty ingredients while containing an amount she describes as effectively homeopathic. Her flat answer to whether an "anti-aging" claim on a label can be trusted: "100% no."

The recommendation that follows is unusual from a dermatologist: **if your skin is healthy, you're better off using nothing.** She says the products themselves generate a substantial share of the problems she sees in clinic, and that she'd prefer her patients use only what she prescribes, because everything else interferes with treating the actual condition.

**Dry skin.** Moisturiser is for people who need it — genuinely dry skin, often genetically driven (she names filaggrin deficiency and ichthyosis vulgaris, both of which also predispose to eczema). Mechanism matters here: moisturiser doesn't deposit hydration, it prevents transepidermal water loss. The prototype is an occlusive like petroleum jelly, blocking about 98% of that loss. Sheet masks work by the same trapping mechanism, which is why the plumping is temporary. Many current users could probably stop and let their skin adjust.

**Oily skin.** Overactive sebaceous glands, driven by follicle sensitivity to circulating androgens rather than by high androgen levels. Mostly inherited. The key relationship for lifters: you can have oily skin without acne, but you cannot have acne without oily skin. Remove the oil and the acne resolves.

**Red skin.** Her answer on self-treatment is a clean no — "red skin" is too vague to act on. It could be acute flushing, chronic capillary dilation from years of flushing, or an inflammatory condition (she names rosacea and acne). Each needs a diagnosis first. **Clinician territory.**

## Acne: what to buy, and what isn't causing it

**Causes.** She is emphatic that acne is not caused by hygiene, pillowcases, hair on the face, insufficient skin-care products, or dietary self-discipline. Her clinical frustration is that young patients blame themselves daily. Blame genetics, puberty, pregnancy, menopause — not behaviour.

On specific foods, her position is that there's no evidential basis for any of it; she doesn't argue with patients who exclude something harmless. Her mechanistic argument is the strongest part: **a pimple cannot form overnight.** It starts as a comedone and takes weeks. So a spot the morning after chocolate, a facial or a new product is not that trigger's doing — it's an *acneiform eruption*, an irritant reaction producing acne-like lesions through a different process. Worth flagging that the broader diet-and-acne literature (dairy, high-glycaemic load) is contested rather than settled; her "no evidence" is stronger than the field's current position, and Henselmans doesn't push on it.

**Whey and creatine.** Directly asked, she says protein shakes don't cause acne and creatine is definitely not acnegenic; people who get acne "from protein shakes" are typically taking other things too. Useful for this library, since the whey-acne belief is common in lifting circles.

**Comedogenicity.** She calls it one of the worst terms in her field — a research term from testing done roughly fifty years ago that doesn't map onto real products. Almost nothing on sale today is genuinely comedogenic: not Vaseline, not olive-oil-based products, not facial oils. Vaseline is the *negative control* in comedogenicity testing. This contradicts a lot of mainstream skin-care advice, so treat it as her considered position rather than uncontested consensus — though the negative-control point is checkable fact.

**Over-the-counter options that do something,** for mild-to-moderate acne with no underlying disorder:

- **2% salicylic acid** — her first choice, especially for younger people. Unsticks the cells plugging the follicle, plus a mild antibacterial effect. Widely available, no particular precautions.
- **Benzoyl peroxide 2.5–5%** — antibacterial but drying, and drying acne isn't how you treat it. She reserves it mostly for back acne; facial skin reacts badly. It bleaches fabric while wet, so let it dry fully.
- **True retinoids** (adapalene/Differin, tretinoin, tazarotene) where available without prescription — the distinction she stresses is **retinoids, not retinols**; OTC retinols she dismisses as effectively homeopathic. They reduce oil output, far less dramatically than oral isotretinoin. Sun sensitivity rises temporarily and reverses after 6–12 months of consistent use; long term they're protective against sun damage.

Her preference for **tretinoin over adapalene for essentially everyone** is explicitly personal practice, not guideline: tretinoin hits all retinoic acid receptors while adapalene targets the acne-specific subset, and she knows tretinoin's behaviour well enough to pick strengths confidently. She wasn't sure which generation adapalene belongs to and told Henselmans to check. Standard guidelines generally position adapalene as first-line for acne specifically; her preference is defensible but not the textbook answer.

## Pimples and scars

**Popping.** The official answer is no. Her practical concession, which she admits sounds like bad advice: for a visible pus-filled head, use a sterile needle, puncture gently, and press with cotton buds — never fingers. Don't make it bleed.

**Deep, headless, painful pimples: leave them entirely alone.** These are already micro-abscesses; digging spreads the contents into surrounding skin, drives more immune response, and worsens both inflammation and eventual marking. Her figure: a pimple resolves on its own in about 13 days, and interfering prolongs that.

**Scars.** Three things, three answers:
- Flat red marks — resolve on their own, nothing to do.
- Dark marks — post-inflammatory pigmentation, treatable. Over the counter, **hydroquinone** where still available (in most places it isn't). She corrects the "bleaching" framing: it's a tyrosinase inhibitor blocking tyrosine-to-melanin conversion, so it returns skin to its natural tone rather than whitening it.
- **Pitted scars — no cream exists.** These need resurfacing, e.g. laser. **Clinician territory.**

**Blackheads vs sebaceous filaments.** A genuine blackhead is a comedone — a plug at the top of the follicle — and tretinoin can clear it. But the dotted pattern most people have on the nose is not acne: those are **sebaceous filaments**, the physiological oil lining of the follicle, black only because the exposed tip oxidises. Squeezing produces a small yellow tube of oil. **Don't treat them.** Prominent ones just mean more oil production. Enlarged-looking pores follow the same logic — chronic high oil output dilates the follicle, so the only lever is reducing oil.

## Hair loss

Relevant to this library because Henselmans steers it explicitly toward anabolic steroid users, whose hair loss is accelerated.

She was honest about a gap: she cannot explain why DHT drives hair growth almost everywhere on the body but causes scalp loss, doesn't think anyone really understands it, and committed to reading the biopsy literature. She rejected a gravity-based mechanism Henselmans floated as "crap." This is common in the field — the treatments work without established mechanisms.

**Topical minoxidil**, over the counter, is her recommended entry point; she rates it as working consistently, the main obstacle being that daily scalp application is a nuisance. She raises and then walks back a figure: about a third of people lack enough of the enzyme converting minoxidil to its active form and need above 5% — then immediately calls a third "probably an exaggeration" and says true non-responders are rare in her practice. **Treat that number as unreliable; she disowned it in the same breath.** Her fallback after 3–6 months of no response is a compounded 10% preparation. Mechanism is uncertain — increased blood flow is the assumption, not established. It grows hair wherever applied, including beards.

**Oral minoxidil and oral finasteride**, compounded into one tablet, are what she gives men wanting prevention — including men in their twenties and including anabolic users, who she says prefer orals. She reports patients doing well over her ten years as a consultant, and notes it isn't licensed for prevention. Oral minoxidil grows hair everywhere, most visibly in people already predisposed to body hair.

Henselmans asked whether DHT blockade is anti-anabolic. Her answer is no, and Henselmans supplies the reasoning: less conversion of testosterone to DHT leaves more testosterone, so net anabolic effect is unchanged. **That is reasoning, not a cited finding.** More importantly, **the conversation never mentions finasteride's reported side-effect profile** — the sexual and mood effects that are the main reason it's a prescription decision. Don't read the silence as reassurance; take it to a doctor.

## Ageing, and the honest nos

**Prevention beats everything.** Don't smoke — she calls it the single worst thing for skin ageing, reasoning by analogy from emphysema that it destroys elastin in skin as it does in lung. Then sun protection. Then genetics, which you don't control. **Collagen supplements: don't bother** — and Henselmans notes the parallel argument in fitness, that with enough protein and vitamin C the body makes its own.

**Botulinum toxin** is the only intervention she endorses for lines, and she calls it evidence-based without reservation. Wrinkles come from repeated muscle movement creasing the skin. Her threshold for starting is when *dynamic* lines (visible only on movement) begin leaving *static* etchings visible at rest — treating then prevents deepening rather than fixing later. Nobody needs it and there's no correct age. The trade-off is expressive: you lose some forehead and frown movement, and she confirms this has been studied for its effect on social perception — children and pets read the absent frown differently — though she couldn't recall specifics.

She calls it "100% safe" with negligible systemic absorption. **This is the clearest overstatement in the video.** Injected botulinum toxin has a well-documented local adverse-effect profile — ptosis, brow asymmetry, spread to unintended muscles — and no regulator calls any injectable 100% safe. Read it as "she considers the risk low in competent hands," which is defensible; read it literally and it isn't.

**Eye bags: no.** No cream or roller does anything — she describes the topicals as pure irritants. The fix is oculoplastic surgery. **Clinician territory.**

**Cellulite: no.** Losing fat reduces its appearance without eliminating it. The problem is the fibrous netting holding fat in place, not the fat, so liposuction doesn't solve it either. The one promising agent she's seen — QWO, an injectable repurposed from Dupuytren's contracture treatment that dissolved the septae — was highly effective and withdrawn within about a year over bruising and post-inflammatory pigmentation; she expects a reformulated version eventually. Both stress cellulite is a normal presentation, not a pathology: most people have it, including lean ones.

## Where a clinician is genuinely required

Collected, because the video's tone is casual enough to obscure them: facial redness without a diagnosis; moderate-to-severe acne or anything needing oral isotretinoin; pitted acne scarring; eye bags; oral finasteride or minoxidil (prescription decisions whose side-effect profiles this video does not cover); and any new, changing or non-healing lesion, given her own emphasis on non-melanoma skin cancer.

## Consensus versus extrapolation

- **Relayed consensus:** no healthy tan; the BAD position on vitamin D; acne is not caused by hygiene; leave deep pimples alone; retinoids beat retinols; smoking and UV dominate photoageing.
- **Clinical preference presented as fact:** tretinoin over adapalene for everyone; "no such thing as essential skin care"; almost nothing sold today being comedogenic; no evidence linking any food to acne.
- **Overstated:** "Botox is 100% safe"; minoxidil "works consistently in everyone"; the one-third enzyme-deficiency figure she retracted mid-sentence.
- **Uncited:** the 84% sunscreen retention figure; the 13-day pimple duration; finasteride having no net anti-anabolic effect.
- **Openly unknown, and she says so:** the DHT scalp paradox; minoxidil's mechanism; UV through aircraft and tinted windows.
