# Can Excess Protein Make You Fat? Yes — Just Not Directly

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Can excess protein make you fat?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4mHjBqREy_4)) · 2023-05-12 · 5 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

A clip from his Q&A for Oxford Brookes University ([full session](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rh_9vb9TI-k)).

## Bottom line
- **Yes, excess protein contributes to fat gain** — but the protein itself is rarely what gets stored. Surplus energy from a high-protein diet still counts as surplus energy.
- The mechanism is **substrate displacement**: the body readily oxidises protein for energy, which frees up the dietary fat and carbohydrate you also ate to be stored instead. Fat is stored as fat because it's the cheapest pathway.
- **Direct conversion of protein to body fat is real but negligible in practice.** It requires a two-step route via gluconeogenesis then de novo lipogenesis, which is metabolically inefficient. It only becomes relevant if you eat essentially nothing but protein.
- He is **openly sceptical of the Antonio overfeeding studies** that found no fat gain on very high protein intakes: free-living conditions, very high dropout, poor dietary records, and subjects who told him personally they couldn't stomach the prescribed protein. He thinks **over-reporting of intake is the likely explanation**.
- **Controlled metabolic ward work (Bray et al.) does find higher fat gain** when protein is increased with everything else held equal.
- The thermic-effect argument **cannot get you to zero**. TEF is a *percentage* of intake, so even at the highest estimates of ~30% for protein, 100 calories still leaves ~70 to be stored. Reduced fat gain, yes. No fat gain, arithmetically impossible.

## Where this contradicts the popular reading
The "you can't get fat from protein" claim has real currency in evidence-based fitness circles, and it rests almost entirely on the Antonio free-living overfeeding trials. **He rejects that reading directly**, and does it on methodology rather than mechanism — the studies weren't controlled enough to detect what they claim to have ruled out, and he says he has taken this up with the author. That the person whose work he is criticising confirmed the compliance problems to him is his strongest card here, though it is also unpublished anecdote.

What survives from the pro-protein side is smaller than advertised:

1. **A genuine thermic advantage.** High-protein foods do cost more energy to digest and absorb.
2. **A muscle protein synthesis effect.** Below your optimal protein intake, increasing it raises MPS, which raises whole-body metabolic activity and energy expenditure. **This benefit caps out at the point of maximal muscle growth**; past that only the thermic advantage remains.

His summary of the net: most research does find reduced fat gain on higher protein, but the differences are **small**, and you cannot infer zero from marginal.

## The parallel with keto
He and his interviewer draw the same error out of ketogenic dieting: clients and athletes who conflated **fat oxidation** with **fat loss**. If you overfeed fat, the body preferentially burns the fat you just ate rather than dipping into adipose tissue. Being a "fat burning machine" by oxidation rate says nothing about adipose tissue loss. Protein produces the identical confusion — a substrate the body handles readily is not a substrate that escapes the energy balance.

His blunt version: **the body doesn't know what your surplus is made of. It registers energy.**
