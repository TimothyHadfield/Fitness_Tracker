# Changing Your Program Only When Progress Says To

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "How to keep building muscle after the newbie gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZvvTi78MtOM)) · 2024-12-12 · 12 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Muscle confusion is backwards.** Adaptation is the *point* of training. Studies find excessive variety produces no benefit and trends worse on a volume-equated basis — in one within-subject trial the "confused" leg trained with significantly more volume and still didn't grow more.
- **Progression is the only physiological reason to change a program.** Everything else (boredom, variety) is psychological. If it's working, don't fix it.
- **Diagnose the plateau before treating it.** Three types: one exercise, multiple exercises for one body part, or systemic. Match the fix to the level.
- **Short-term strength gains on new exercises are almost entirely neural** — motor cortex learning, not growth. Long term, strength and size correlate extremely strongly, which is what makes benchmark lifts a usable proxy.
- **Volume should trend upward over a training career**, not down.
- **Use the fatigue index as a volume diagnostic.** Reps holding steady across sets (12/12/12/12) suggests you're not near failure or could add volume; heavy rep drop-off suggests volume is too high.

## Muscle confusion, and the evidence against it
He calls it "muscle confusion confusion." The stress-recovery-adaptation curve, general adaptation syndrome and the specificity principle all say the same thing: you impose a systematic stress, the body adapts, and the adaptation *is* the muscle growth and strength gain. Trying to prevent adaptation is trying to prevent the outcome.

The strongest evidence he cites is a within-subject design: one leg did sets of 9-12 to failure with straightforward progressive overload; the other rotated a different workout each session — an eccentric-only version, an added-sets version, a higher-rep version — while also attempting to progress. The varied leg trained with **significantly more volume and did not gain more muscle** ([Damas 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31268828/)), a result he says was replicated in 2022 ([Angleri 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35092084/)). He notes this is doubly damning, because added volume normally *does* produce more growth when recovery isn't the constraint.

He also refers to a study where trained men doing the same exercises every session beat a group rotating exercises, non-significantly, on a volume-equated basis. The captions garble the author name (rendered "bz fatal"), it is **not** in the description's reference list, and it is not identifiable with confidence — so it is left unlinked here.

Program hopping is the same error in a different costume. Beyond losing the repeated-bout effect, the real cost is that switching destroys your progression benchmarks: because early strength gains on an unfamiliar movement come mainly from the motor cortex learning to coordinate it — balance, timing, antagonist coactivation — a lifter who changes program every four weeks never learns whether any of it built muscle.

The long-term case rests on the powerlifter correlation: rank competitive powerlifters by body composition scan and the ordering nearly matches their competition rankings ([Ferrari 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003570); [Maden-Wilkinson 2020](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00224.2019); [Reggiani & Schiaffino 2020](https://doi.org/10.4081/ejtm.2020.9311)). Ironically, he notes, the sport built on strength-over-size shows the tightest strength-size relationship.

## The three-level plateau diagnostic
This is the practical core of the video, and it's a triage tree rather than a prescription.

**One exercise stalling.** Fix that exercise only. Change its progression model — lower the rep target with intensification, apply daily undulating periodization to that lift, use a reactive deload or plateau breaker. Swapping the exercise is the last resort. He adds a realism caveat: some exercises aren't progression vehicles at all. Nobody progresses a lateral raise for years; lifetime progression there might be to 20 kg. Keep the big compounds as benchmarks and rotate accessories for variety.

**Multiple exercises, one body part.** Adjust training parameters for that body part, primarily volume. His examples are pattern-recognition from coaching: women often stall across lateral raises, overhead presses and other shoulder work simultaneously; men often stall on chest and biceps first, having trained mirror muscles disproportionately. The usual answer as you advance is *more* volume for that body part — though an over-trained mirror muscle may instead benefit from a reduction block.

**Systemic.** Multiple unrelated exercises stalling means something global: sleep, stress, or overall program design. His test for whether volume is the culprit: add a rest day. If you then progress, volume was too high. Add volume; if you progress faster, it was too low. He warns that this reads badly for strength specifically, because strength responds less to volume than size does — don't expect an immediate 50% faster rate.

The second diagnostic is the **fatigue index** — rep loss across sets. Very little rep drop-off means low fatigue, which he treats as a sign either that you can add volume or, bluntly, that you should question your effort, because for most men hitting identical reps across four sets after an all-out first set isn't plausible at normal rest intervals. Large rep loss points the other way. He notes some women genuinely do have very high work capacity here.

**Overreach to note:** the plateau taxonomy, the volume-should-rise-over-a-career claim, and the fatigue-index thresholds are all presented as teaching material from his certification course rather than as findings, and none of the five references support them. The muscle-confusion evidence, by contrast, is specific and cited.
