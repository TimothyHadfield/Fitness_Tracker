# Pre-Workout Carbs: 15 Grams Is Enough for Almost Everyone

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is how many carbs you need pre-workout for max strength" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YeuNkBdH9_U)) · 2025-02-13 · 15 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- His team's systematic review — the first on this question, all previous reviews being narrative — found **22 studies; 14 found no performance difference between higher and lower carb intakes**. Of the 8 that did, 6 favoured higher carbs and **2 favoured lower**.
- Every study showing a carb benefit **also gave that group more total energy**, usually against an overnight-fasted comparator receiving nothing. Carbohydrate per se is confounded with breakfast.
- Practical recommendation: within 3 hours of training, **at least 15 g carbohydrate and at least 0.3 g/kg protein** (~20 g). Do not train fully fasted. Beyond that, the dose barely matters.
- The mechanistic reason: **a hard session depletes at most ~40% of muscle glycogen**, and you need roughly 40%+ depletion before neuromuscular function suffers. The tank does not empty.
- Glycogen is a **local reservoir**. Biceps glycogen cannot fuel quads, so total session length is the wrong variable — sets per muscle per session is the right one.

## What the review actually found
[00:15] The headline is the count: majority null. The two studies favouring *lower* carbs are the detail that makes the distribution look like noise rather than a suppressed effect.

The confound is his main argument. In the positive studies the design is typically: fasted subjects arrive in the morning, one group gets a carbohydrate drink, the other gets nothing at all. That compares breakfast to no breakfast, not carbs to fewer carbs.

He then stacks up the evidence that a chunk of the remaining effect is non-metabolic:
- Where a **taste- and texture-matched placebo** was used, the carbohydrate supplement showed no benefit over it.
- In one study a **solid placebo breakfast** — a modified gel with no energy — outperformed a liquid one, correlating with hunger ratings (though later work has not confirmed hunger as the mediator).
- **Carbohydrate mouth rinsing** improves performance without swallowing anything, apparently via dopaminergic receptors in the mouth.

Dose-response studies close it off. One compared 15, 30 and 60 g/hour: all beat nothing, **15 g produced the numerically highest performance**, and there was not even a trend favouring more. (The lead author's name is not recoverable from the auto-captions.)

## Why carbs matter less than the "carbs are energy" story implies
[05:39] The arithmetic is the strongest part of the video. A good session burns roughly 300–500 kcal, and much of that is rest. Eccentric contractions are energetically cheap, which is why continuous concentric work like cycling burns far more per minute and depletes far more glycogen.

Of those calories, the aerobic system supplies 20–70% (mostly fat, because rest periods permit it) and the phosphocreatine system another 16–31%. Take the least favourable assumptions — 500 kcal, ~64% glycolytic — and you get about **80 g of carbohydrate to fuel a session**. Against ~500 g of stored glycogen in a trained athlete, that is roughly **16% depletion**.

The measured literature agrees. The largest depletion recorded from a single session, even in bodybuilders on a high-volume leg day, is **39% (41% including isokinetic work)**. The disruption threshold is around 40%, or glycogen down to ~250–300 mmol/kg dry weight. A representative study — Essén-Gustavsson and Tesch, as best the captions can be read — found only **28% quad depletion after 20 sets to failure at 12RM** across squats, leg press and leg extension.

The timing point follows: much of a pre-workout carb dose is not digested, absorbed and stored as glycogen in time to fuel that session anyway. **What matters is carb intake between the last workout and this one**, not the pre-workout meal specifically.

## The exception, and the tangent
[10:47] Above about **11 sets per muscle per session**, the trend does start favouring higher carbs — plausibly depletion in high-threshold type II fibres. He notes these studies still are not isocaloric.

He then dismisses his own exception: you should not be doing 10+ sets per muscle in one session, because returns diminish sharply past about six sets and the rest is junk volume — more damage and protein breakdown without more synthesis. That number matches this library's existing junk-volume material (Krieger's ~6 sets/session plateau) closely.

## Where he pushes past the source
A later meta-analysis ([King 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01716-w)) found ergogenic effects when **session duration exceeded 45 minutes and the fast exceeded 8 hours**. He describes this as replicating his review "almost to the letter" — then rejects its session-duration criterion outright, arguing glycogen is muscle-local so a long full-body session raises no muscle's requirement, and that rest interval length breaks the duration-to-depletion mapping entirely. He substitutes his own threshold of ~10 sets per muscle. The reasoning is sound, but this is a **substitution of variables, not a replication**, and it is presented as the latter.

The final recommendation is also explicitly precautionary rather than evidence-driven — his review's own wording is that given "inconclusive evidence and potential for benefits but not harm," lifters are *advised* to take 15 g carbs and 0.3 g/kg protein. That is a safety-margin recommendation, not a demonstrated requirement, and the video's title frames it more definitively than that.
