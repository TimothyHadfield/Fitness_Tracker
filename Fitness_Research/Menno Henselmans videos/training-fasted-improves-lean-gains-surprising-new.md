# Fasted vs Fed Training Over 8 Weeks: A Result He Didn't Want

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Training fasted improves lean gains? Surprising new study" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YNtKu5U5INg)) · 2025-04-29 · 8 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- His own 8-week study in trained lifters compared **16:8 fasted training** (8-hour eating window, first meal ≥1 h post-workout) against **four spread meals a day, trained fed** — 10% surplus, ~2.2 g/kg protein, 4 full-body sessions per week.
- **Squat 1RM gains were worse in the fasted group.** Leg extension, bench press and leg press strength showed no significant difference.
- **Fat-free mass gains were equal**, and the fasted group gained *less fat* — so body composition nominally favoured fasting. He does not accept this at face value.
- His explanation is **under-reporting via social desirability bias**: fasted subjects reported hitting the surplus but likely ate less, because a compressed window makes a surplus hard to hit.
- The finding he considers most important: **total repetition volume was lower in fasted workouts, and the gap widened over the 8 weeks.**
- Fasted training also produced **lower reported daily energy levels**; mood and sleep quality were unaffected.

## Why the body-composition result doesn't mean what it looks like
Read naively, fasted training won. His argument against that has two parts.

First, the intake discrepancy: both groups logged the same intake under dietitian supervision, but he expects the fasted group ate less. Under-reporting is common when an intervention restricts food choices or timing and subjects are asked to bulk — it's easier to say you're eating more than to do it.

Second, why the shortfall didn't cost them muscle: he argues the effect of energy intake on hypertrophy is **overrated**. Prior studies find surprisingly small effects, and in a natural trainee a surplus beyond a modest level mostly becomes fat, because there's a ceiling on how fast muscle can be built. On that reading, the fed group's extra calories bought fat, not muscle — which is what the data show.

## The repetition-volume finding
Fasted workouts produced lower total rep volume, and the fed group's advantage grew across the study — better strength-endurance compounding over time. He frames repetition volume as a primary driver of hypertrophy: it is why higher frequencies, longer rest intervals and training closer to failure can each improve growth, all via accumulated time under tension.

In 8 weeks this didn't reach significance for muscle growth, but he projects the widening performance gap would translate into worse gains over longer horizons — consistent with a literature he describes as trending clearly negative for fasted training on strength and size, though with small effect sizes.

## On fasted training and fat loss
Standard rebuttal to the fasted-cardio myth. Training fasted increases fat oxidation during the session, but acute fat burning is not long-term fat loss: burning more fat means burning fewer carbs, which means glycogen resynthesis later, which itself costs energy. Long-term fat loss is dictated by energy balance, and fasted training does not raise energy expenditure.

## Limitations
Pandemic lockdowns shut the lab before the planned ultrasound measures, losing a cohort and some data. Fat-free mass came from a **3-compartment model** (BodPod plus BIA), which he considers adequate. Small sample, 8 weeks, inconsistent strength results across lifts.

**His verdict:** if you strongly prefer fasted training, or the eating window helps you hold a deficit and fat loss is the priority, the cost is real but modest — somewhat worse performance and slightly worse long-term gains.
