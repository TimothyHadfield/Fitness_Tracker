# Six Reality Checks on Getting Lean: Environment Beats Willpower

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "How to get a sixpack: 6 brutal reality checks" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UqYQMc7Oybw)) · 2025-12-09 · 13 min
**Topic:** Psychology & behaviour

## Bottom line
- It isn't chemicals, hormones, metabolic damage or a set point. Processing barely changes energy density; thyroid — the most metabolically active hormone — moves daily expenditure by at most **5–10%** across the whole range. Metabolic adaptation is real, but transient.
- Fat loss doesn't stick because people return to the lifestyle that produced their old body fat percentage. **This includes GLP-1 agonists**: stop the drug without changing food choices and activity, and you revert.
- **You cannot train general self-control.** Improvements from practice are domain-specific. The response is to *rely on it less* — restructure your environment so fewer decisions are needed.
- **Outcome goals don't predict success**; loftier goals sometimes produce worse outcomes. Focus on today's actions: meal prep, daily weigh-ins, a scale you can't avoid.
- **Consistency is among the strongest predictors** of long-term leanness — which makes cheat meals and refeeds a popular idea with poor long-term evidence behind it.
- Below roughly **10% body fat for men and 20% for women**, anabolic hormone production falls; natural competitors often reach near-castrate testosterone at contest. Getting there once has value; living there does not.

## The causes people prefer aren't the cause
He clears the excuses first. Food processing and additives have marginal effects on macronutrient composition, so energy density is unchanged. Hormones are too weak a lever — even thyroid tops out at a 5–10% swing in expenditure. And metabolic damage doesn't exist: his group's own review found no evidence of it even in starved populations, women with anorexia, and people in absolute poverty. What exists is metabolic adaptation, which gets harder the leaner you get but bounces back with regained fat and is never large enough to make leanness impossible.

His conclusion is deliberately uncomfortable: you are the reason. He justifies it instrumentally — an **internal locus of control** predicts success across domains, and accepting responsibility is what makes action feel available.

## Sustainability, and why the drugs don't change the logic
Most people yo-yo because they revert to the food choices and activity level that produced their previous body fat percentage. His GLP-1 point follows directly: the drug reduces hunger, so people eat less of the same foods; when the drug stops and hunger returns, so does the old intake and the old body fat. Nothing about the mechanism is special.

The reframe: stop asking what to do *this cut*, start asking what permanent changes raise expenditure and lower intake. And prefer **addition to subtraction** — eating broccoli before the pizza costs less self-control than being told to eat less pizza, and people who feel deprived quit.

## Self-control, environment, and goals
This is where he is most combative with the coaching mainstream. Self-control is not a muscle and does not strengthen with use; you will keep making the same mistake in the same situation. So the interventions are all environmental: separate cupboards from housemates' food, keep unwanted food out of sight and out of reach, have a meal ready to microwave for when you come home tired and hungry, don't shop hungry.

He extends the argument to goals. Research finds no correlation between goal-setting and weight loss success, and ambitious targets can backfire when unrealistic. "I want to be 10% body fat" is an expression of desire that tells you nothing to do. What works is a process focus: today's session, today's macros, and mechanical habits — put the scale where you cannot miss it with a notepad beside it, so weighing requires no decision. Likewise "how long will the diet last?" is the wrong question; *diaita*, he notes, originally meant way of life.

## Advice people want versus advice that works
Cheat meals and refeeds are popular because people want them, and the physiological justification makes it sound respectable. His counter is that irregular eating patterns predict worse long-term maintenance, and **consistency** — in weigh-ins, diet and training — is one of the strongest predictors of staying lean. A varied, innovative diet is not required; many successful people eat close to the same thing daily, which removes both decisions and temptation.

Keto gets the same treatment: it works to the extent it raises protein and lowers calories, and its restrictions are usually unsustainable. So does IIFYM — technically correct, and in practice an invitation to spend 800 of 2,000 calories on ice cream and then wonder why you're always hungry.

## Being shredded
Below ~10% (men) / ~20% (women), anabolic hormone production declines gradually; libido, stress tolerance and energy go with it. For women, a menstrual cycle becoming irregular and stopping is the body's alarm and marks the floor.

He still thinks most people should get unsustainably lean **once**: as self-discovery — everything you enjoy narrows down to food — and because afterwards normal dieting feels easy for life. The long-term target is the leanest level you can sustain, chosen deliberately rather than accepted by default. His example: get to the point where you *could* hold 7%, and then choose 10% because the trade-offs aren't worth it.

## Overreach to note
This video is almost entirely unverifiable from the description: one link is given and all other sourcing is deferred to his paid book. Several of the strongest claims — that general self-control cannot be improved, that goals don't correlate with weight loss success, the 5–10% thyroid figure — are stated flatly with nothing to check them against. The self-control claim in particular is contested in the psychological literature, and he presents one side of it as settled. The contest-prep material is coaching anecdote, not data.
