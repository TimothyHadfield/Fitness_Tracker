# Arm Exercise Tweaks: Keep the Muscle Loaded at Long Lengths

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "These Optimized Exercises BLOW UP Your Arms" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=804N7_4FPDM)) · 2025-03-06 · 7 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Overhead triceps extensions beat pushdowns** for triceps growth. Do them with the arm held **fully overhead and fixed**, not swinging back and forth.
- The long head is a **shoulder extensor as well as an elbow extensor**, which is why pressing trains it poorly — bench press and push-ups stimulate roughly **half the growth in triceps that they do in pecs**.
- Standard dumbbell and incline curls put the arm **vertical at the bottom**, so the biceps is unloaded exactly where it's most stretched.
- General rule for free-weight arm work: **the arm should not be vertical in the bottom position.**
- This is optimisation, not correction — he says the conventional versions work fine.

## The biarticular conflict
The long head crosses the shoulder and assists the lats in pulling the elbow back toward the body. During a bench press you extend the elbow but *flex* the shoulder, so a maximally contracting long head would fight the movement at the shoulder. A muscle can only contract or not, so the body under-recruits it. The same conflict appears in cheated overhead extensions where the upper arm swings backward.

The practical consequence is to **lock the upper arm in place overhead** — hold it, or brace it against your head — and move only at the elbow. He adds an anecdotal coaching observation that fixing the upper arm reduces reported elbow pain.

Two smaller cues: use an **off-hand grip** (hand offset toward one end of a large dumbbell) so the weight doesn't collide with your body and limit range, and get the **hand behind the body** to close the elbow angle fully.

## The bottom-position problem in curls
At the bottom of a standing dumbbell curl the arm hangs vertical and gravity pulls straight down the line of the arm — there is essentially **no demand for elbow flexion**, so the biceps is unloaded.

Incline curls are chosen because putting the elbow behind the body stretches the biceps at the shoulder. True, but the standard incline curl still leaves the arm vertical at the bottom, so it's stretched *without tension*. That matters because, he argues, the biceps is unusual in its operating sarcomere lengths: it produces its **highest active force at long muscle lengths**, so the stretched position is worth loading even setting aside stretch-mediated hypertrophy arguments.

Three fixes he gives:
- A **very steep incline curl** — enough that at the bottom the shoulder is maximally stretched and the forearm is still off vertical.
- **Standing curls with elbows pressed forward** onto the hip bones, which tilts the arm out of vertical and loads the bottom.
- **Concentration curls with a backward lean**, using the leg as a fulcrum so the arm isn't vertical at the bottom.

## Mechanical drop set
The elbows-forward curl chains into a drop set with no equipment changes: go to (or near) failure elbows-forward, continue with regular standing curls to failure again, then continue **one arm at a time**. That last step exploits the **bilateral deficit** — the body coordinates one limb better than two, so you're stronger unilaterally. Test it by dropping one dumbbell after two-arm failure; you'll get more reps even with the weaker arm.

## Caveats
- Two studies for a video that is mostly biomechanical reasoning and coaching anecdote. The specific cues — off-hand grip, elbows-on-hips curls, the leaning concentration curl, the elbow-pain claim — are **reasoned from mechanics, not tested**.
- The overhead-vs-neutral finding supports the exercise choice, not the technique tweaks layered on top of it.
