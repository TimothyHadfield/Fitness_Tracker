# Sets Matter More Than Rep Ranges: What a 4x4 vs 3x12 Bench Study Shows

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "4x4 Builds bigger arms than 3x12, new study finds" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vEfL2Ms5eM)) · 2025-12-02 · 7 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Novices benched 3x/week for 8 weeks in three schemes: **4x4 heavy**, **3x8-12 to failure on set three**, and **4x6-7 at 40% 1RM explosive** (plus a no-training control). All rested 3+ minutes.
- The "strength" group did **not** out-gain the "hypertrophy" group on strength — and gained **more arm lean mass** on segmental DEXA.
- Menno's reading: the 4x4 group did **four sets instead of three**. Set count, not rep range, is doing the work.
- Rep range is close to irrelevant for hypertrophy across roughly **4-30 reps**; **volume in hard sets is the lever**.
- He dismisses power training (40% 1RM speed work) as **"largely a waste of time" for non-athletes** — a stronger claim than one novice study can carry.

## Why rep range barely matters for size
Heavier loads mean more mechanical tension per rep but less time under tension; lighter loads flip the trade. Across the middle of the range those two cancel out, which is why most studies find no hypertrophy difference from 4 to 30+ reps. High reps still recruit high-threshold motor units because their recruitment threshold drops as the set fatigues.

Both ends break down. Above ~30 reps the set becomes cardio — you are limited by energy depletion and oxygen rather than muscle recruitment (his example: sets of 50 on the squat). At the bottom there appears to be a **minimum time under tension per set of roughly four reps**; a single 1RM does not build as much muscle as a set of ten despite far higher tension. That threshold is why a 4x4 protocol sits right at the edge and still works.

He also flags the classic bodybuilding "6-12 rep hypertrophy zone" as debunked in both directions — the lower bound is much lower than the tradition claims.

## Strength: specificity, and when it starts to matter
Heavier work is better for 1RM strength in **trained** lifters, because 1RM strength is largely a coordination skill specific to load, velocity and tempo — you are training for the test. In novices that effect is weak, which is why he prefers starting beginners at 8-12 reps on the bench: the risk/reward of going heavy isn't there yet. He would keep squats moderate rather than very high-rep, since sets of 20 turn into cardio.

## Caveats
- Menno himself discounts the headline result. **Segmental DEXA is not accurate** for limb-level comparisons, and total and trunk lean mass did **not** differ between groups. The "bigger arms" finding is the weakest measure in the study.
- Novices only, 8 weeks, bench press only. It does not transfer cleanly to trained lifters.
- The blanket write-off of power training for recreational lifters is his opinion, not something a single 8-week novice study establishes.
