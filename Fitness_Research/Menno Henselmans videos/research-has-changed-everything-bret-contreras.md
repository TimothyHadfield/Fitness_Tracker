# Bret Contreras on the Squat vs Hip Thrust Study He Paid For, and What He Changed His Mind About

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This research has changed everything - Bret Contreras" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KoGDWboi46U)) · 2024-03-05 · 72 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- The squats-vs-hip-thrusts trial Menno and Contreras **co-funded out of their own pockets** (roughly $40k each, against an $80k quote) found **equal glute hypertrophy** in untrained subjects, by MRI. Contreras expected hip thrusts to win by a wide margin.
- Two findings in it cut against things he built a career on: **EMG did not predict hypertrophy**, and **every subject felt their glutes more during hip thrusts** while growing the same amount from squats.
- It also found **no regional difference** — upper, middle and lower glute max grew similarly on both, glute medius barely grew on either. This "blows my mind," and he won't drop regional programming on one study.
- His programming for 60 female clients: **three glute sessions a week, four or five exercises, two hard sets each — roughly 30–40 sets a week — no drop sets, no training to destruction.** High frequency bought by capping per-set punishment.
- He has genuinely shifted toward **eccentric overload and lengthened partials**, explicitly as hedging rather than conviction: "I don't want to take two years and be wrong."
- He defends several positions the consensus does not hold: **exercises differ substantially in injury risk**, **shortened-position work still earns its place**, **ultrasound-based glute hypertrophy data is untrustworthy**, and bands and sumo stance are fine.

## The study, and the critiques of it
Menno opens by putting the two main criticisms of their own study to its co-funder.

**Critique one: the subjects were selected to be people who couldn't squat.** Contreras rejects it on process and on fact. Neither he nor Menno had any role in recruitment, training, data collection or analysis — that sat with the researchers, whom he names only as Daniel and Mike (no surnames in the transcript, so I have not tried to identify them). Only around three subjects couldn't squat deep, and most went below parallel. He tells the story against himself: the worst-proportioned subject, a roughly 6'8" man effectively doing quarter squats, grew his glutes well anyway.

**Critique two: the duration favoured hip thrusts**, because squats take longer to coordinate and short-trial gains are largely neural. Contreras doesn't rebut the mechanism; he observes who raised it. The same commentators did not apply that standard to a squat-favouring study — he names Kassem (no surname given), who criticised one such study, later revealed to be fraudulent, without raising the objections he raised about theirs. He calls this human nature rather than bad faith, and includes himself.

He concedes one scope limit, which cuts *for* his own view: the result may hold only in beginners, and more advanced trainees might do better on hip thrusts, particularly since the study had to equate volume. And he makes a point about the industry rather than the data — he believes he is the only man in the field who publicly said "I was wrong," when roughly 90% of the people who had spent years calling hip thrusts useless should have.

## Short lengths, long lengths, and neuromechanical matching
Menno raises Chris Beardsley's argument that stretch-mediated hypertrophy should be *maximal in beginners* — if the mechanism is fascicle lengthening via added sarcomeres in series, and a muscle can only get so long, the effect should taper with training age. That would make most of the long-length literature, largely on trained subjects, a study of a phenomenon already partly exhausted.

Contreras won't adjudicate it. Physiology isn't his strength (he's "more of a physics biomechanics person") and he sends papers he can't parse to Brad Schoenfeld. He credits Menno and Beardsley with being early to the mechanical-tension-only position, and says Beardsley has since gone further out on a limb than he's comfortable following.

Menno's counter is the sharper contribution: the muscle demonstrably gets *thicker*, not just longer, and gets stronger at short lengths — neither of which you'd predict if the whole effect were sarcomeres in series. Contreras accepts this and calls stretch-mediated hypertrophy real and probably the biggest revolution in exercise science in a decade, while flagging that the terminology is contested — researchers mean sarcomerogenesis specifically, the industry means "growth is greater when you load the stretch."

His reason for still defending shortened-position work is where it gets interesting, because his stated evidence is **bodybuilding practice, not research**. Bodybuilders have always thrown in a pec deck, always finished biceps with a concentration curl. Menno puts the obvious question straight back: so were the bodybuilders doing it suboptimally all this time? Contreras's answer is genuinely open — "it is very possible."

For glutes specifically he offers a mechanistic reason to expect short lengths to matter more: **neuromechanical matching**. The idea — which he says originated in respiratory muscle research and which he independently proposed in his first-year PhD proposal, where his reviewer Justin Keogh pushed back — is that the nervous system allocates drive according to a muscle's leverage at a given joint angle. In deep hip flexion the adductor magnus out-levers the hamstrings; at full extension the glute max has the best internal moment arm of the hip extensors. He finds it plausible, then undercuts himself: the two OpenSim models he checked give completely different values, which taught him how many assumptions sit inside biomechanical modelling.

Menno offers a limitation Contreras accepts without argument: **at failure it may not apply at all.** Preferential recruitment of the best-levered fibres is a submaximal phenomenon; take a set to failure and it's all hands on deck.

Their synthesis is a fair reading of the data: hip thrusts win on EMG and subjective tension, yet the two tie on growth — so something compensates on the squat side, most likely stretch-mediated hypertrophy and passive tension. Use both rather than choose.

## What he trusts to measure, and what he doesn't
This is the most methodologically useful stretch of the interview.

**MRI** is why the study cost $80,000 — the gold standard, and near-mandatory for glutes.

**Ultrasound** he does not trust for glutes at all, on first-hand grounds: for his PhD he tried it on five subjects and could not reliably identify the fascia layer. He'd accept it for biceps, quads or hamstrings where there's bone to reference against; for glutes you're reading a muscle layer with fascia and no bony landmark. He knows studies have used it and says flatly he doesn't trust them. Menno reports hearing the same from specialists.

**EMG** is the awkward one, because he built much of his early reputation on it, and his own funded study found it didn't predict hypertrophy. He won't call it worthless, and gives two reasons it fails: it can't account for the stretch, and — citing Andrew Vigotsky — surface EMG ignores muscle architecture, so pennate muscles produce overestimates of neural drive.

He then describes an unpublished experiment he clearly still finds persuasive: twelve female clients held at the bottom of a squat, performing a five-second maximal isometric push *up* versus a five-second maximal push *out*, randomised order, same position. Every glute region including lower glute max showed roughly three times higher activation pushing out (10% vs 30% mean). His argument: when muscle length is held constant, EMG has to mean something. "How could it not?"

Be clear about that evidence's standing: n=12, unpublished, isometric, and an activation measure used to justify exercise selection immediately after he agreed activation didn't predict growth in the better-controlled study he paid for. Neither of them resolves it.

Underneath all of this is a structural complaint: longitudinal training studies are rare because they're expensive, and academics don't share bodybuilders' questions. Hence "it's always going to be the wild, wild West in our industry."

## How he actually trains sixty women
Contreras trains about 30 clients in Miami and 30 in San Diego in person, which is the basis for everything he says about practice.

The per-session template: a hip thrust variation; a squat pattern on one or two legs; a hinge or knee-flexion movement; then abduction (now in two planes) and optional kickbacks. Sessions run every other day, alternating the quad slot between something genuinely quad-biased (hack squat, pendulum squat, leg press) and something glute-biased (leaning step-up, walking lunge). The hinge slot rotates too — deadlift, 45° hyper, knee flexion — because three heavy hinge sessions a week is too much.

The load structure is the part that matters. **Two hard sets per exercise, four or five exercises, three times a week — roughly 30–40 weekly sets for glutes.** The trade-off is explicit: you cannot run that frequency if you destroy people. A week of soreness, he says, is a coach failure, not a coach flex — "if that happens to me, I go: no, I screwed you" — and he tells clients to stop going to trainers whose sessions leave them trembling on the floor. He autoregulates heavily, and uses "deload" to mean change the stimulus and lower effort, not stop training.

The illustrative case is a wellness competitor (Yarishna) with very developed legs who needed glutes. His prescription: **stop training legs entirely** for about a year, doing only hip thrusts, abduction and kickback machines three times a week. This constraint shapes his whole quarrel with the wider industry — bikini and wellness competitors are judged on proportions, so quad and hamstring growth is a cost, not a benefit.

He defends PRs against neighbouring gyms saying bikini competitors shouldn't chase them — how else would you add muscle? — with a real concession: after five years PRs get harder, and obsession with them produces stupid decisions and injuries.

## Where they disagreed
**Whether feel and EMG tell you anything.** His own study produced the cleanest possible refutation of "feel" as a growth proxy — every subject felt hip thrusts more, growth tied — and he reports it honestly and says it matters. Then keeps using both, on himself and his clients. Menno presses on how predictive EMG is; Contreras answers with the same-muscle-length argument; neither concedes. The central unresolved standoff in the episode.

**Practical observation against research.** Contreras states it as plainly as it can be stated: "I know there's the research on frequency, but I'm telling you, in practice, the girls grow their glutes the best hitting it three times a week." He frames it as "what the industry of scientific people say" versus "what I see in here," and Menno doesn't argue, only asks how much stock he puts in practical observation — "Absolutely." Worth noting the observation is uncontrolled, on self-selected clients, with no comparison condition; Contreras doesn't claim otherwise.

**Contreras against Mike Israetel, by name.** The most pointed disagreement is with a third party. Contreras describes an Israetel video presenting "the scientific way to grow the glutes" as all stretch-position movements — sumo squats, two deadlift or good-morning variations, lunges to failure, deep leg press — with no abduction, no kickbacks, no hip thrusts and nothing for the upper glutes, at an intensity that would leave his clients sore for a week. Two objections: it would blow up the quads and hamstrings of clients whose sport penalises exactly that ("Mike just showed that he does not work with any bikini competitors"), and training people that hard is what you do to impress a client, not what a working coach does. Israetel is a major source elsewhere in this library, and the companion Menno interview has him calling hip thrusts backwards on force-curve grounds while defending pump and soreness as stimulus proxies. A live, two-sided dispute over population, effort ceiling, and whether shortened-position glute work is worth programming — not to be flattened.

**Injury risk.** Menno puts up the position, now common online, that all exercises are effectively equally injurious and it's all volume management — swaying on squats is fine, knee valgus is fine, the body adapts to anything. Contreras "vehemently" disagrees, and his evidence is thirty years of living in a gym: injuries come from squats, deadlifts, bench, military press, bent rows, weighted dips — the loaded, popular, ego-attached lifts. Machines and single-joint work produce chronic elbow and shoulder irritation, not acute strains. He argued the reverse with Stu McGill in the 2000s over crunches and back extensions, having never seen anyone injure themselves on either. Menno partly agrees, adding that clients with back pain generally hurt less squatting with neutral pelvic alignment. The most portable insight: **people get injured on the exercises they care about** — nobody pushes through a warning sign on seated rows, everybody does on bench.

Note the shape of that argument: anecdotal, about exercises with wildly unequal exposure, load and self-selection. He's arguing against an over-correction and says so — called the worst trainer in the world in 2010 for showing back rounding, he now thinks the pendulum has swung too far toward "you can build up to anything."

**Menstrual cycle and strength.** Menno says he took a lot of flak for posting research showing the menstrual cycle does not meaningfully influence strength. Contreras cites a review by Lauren Colenso-Semple with Stuart Phillips and agrees the average effect is null — "if I took my whole glute squad and averaged out their strength over their menstrual cycle, you won't find any big differences." But he insists individual variation is large, reporting one client who reliably set PRs on the day she started bleeding while others are weakest then. Same data, different emphases: the population claim is settled, and it doesn't help him program an individual. Neither gives ground.

**Hip dip.** Menno reports seeing abduction work increase hip dip in female clients. Contreras says flatly he doesn't see it, and reframes it as a body-composition story — pronounced below roughly 11% body fat, far less noticeable at 15–20% — so reach your preferred leanness before deleting exercises. He concedes a conditional: an already-lean client who still dislikes hers should stop frontal-plane abduction. Anecdote against anecdote.

**Regional glute hypertrophy.** Here he disagrees with his own study. It "blows my mind," because he can palpate a squatting client and feel lower glute max working more than upper. His position — "I do think it's important, but I'm open to changing my mind, I'd like to see more research" — is a refusal to update on one study: defensible, and also exactly the move he criticised others for when the result went against them.

**What he's changed.** Menno says he wanted this conversation because Contreras funded a study that could have proved him wrong, and then said it did. His programming has moved toward eccentric overload (controlling into the stretch on plate-loaded machines, iso-holds at neutral on the abduction machine where tension is otherwise absent) and toward lengthened partials — explicitly as insurance rather than conviction. He doesn't know the answer and doesn't want clients short-changed for two years if long-length work turns out to be right. Programming under uncertainty for a population you can't re-run is a real and underrated stance.
