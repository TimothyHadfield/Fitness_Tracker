# Five Claims He Says the 2026 Research Kills

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "5 Fitness Myths Science Officially Debunked in 2026" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C1AmKusewKg)) · 2026-06-16 · 7 min
**Topic:** Evidence & methodology

## Bottom line
- **Caffeine works fine in the morning** — the adenosine-receptor argument was theoretical and is contradicted by the data. If anything caffeine is *extra* useful in the morning.
- **Caffeine as a muscle builder is itself probably a myth.** One small 6-week trial found more muscle thickness; his review counts **seven studies finding no effect** on strength or body composition.
- **Women build muscle and strength at the same relative rate as men** (meta-analysis of 27 studies); absolute gains differ only because starting muscle mass differs. **Menopause does not wreck trainability** — strength, hypertrophy and fat loss are the same pre- and post-menopause.
- **Citrulline is not a proven muscle builder.** Meta-analysis of nine studies: average lean mass effect size ≈ **0.0**.
- **Slow eccentrics do not add hypertrophy.** Two meta-analyses. His default: controlled negative, explosive concentric.

## Caffeine timing and caffeine as an anabolic
The morning-caffeine myth came from reasoning about adenosine receptor interactions, never from outcome data. His counter-mechanism runs the other way: caffeine raises core body temperature, already elevated by the afternoon. Since most sports records are set in the afternoon and early evening, morning caffeine **fast-tracks the circadian rise** and offsets training earlier than your body is adapted to.

He then undercuts the study that made the segment: the small Iranian 6-week trial is outnumbered. Most of caffeine's benefit is **psychological** — it helps you push, but when effort is already maximal there's little left to add, and tolerance blunts long-term effects. His conclusion is hedged: *if* caffeine helps growth at all, morning is when.

## Women, testosterone, and menopause
The "15x less testosterone means 15x less potential" reasoning fails because level isn't the whole story — what matters is also how an individual's body uses it, so no clean between-sex hormonal comparison exists. His stronger claim: testosterone mainly determines **baseline** muscle mass rather than the **rate** of growth, which is why men start bigger without gaining faster in relative terms.

On menopause he generalises to ageing: **being sedentary and eating badly for decades** wrecks a physique, not age itself, and people in their nineties still make significant changes when they start lifting.

## Citrulline and eccentric tempo
Citrulline had "sneaked into" the evidence-based supplement list. He's fair to it — most trials were **underpowered** to detect a small benefit — but argues the only plausible mechanism is performance enhancement, which is marginal (a few reps on high-rep sets). Then a twist: citrulline acts as a **reverse blood flow restriction**, lowering metabolic stress so performance rises. Since metabolic stress increases motor unit recruitment, more reps at less stress may need *more* volume for the same result. BFR runs the logic in reverse.

On tempo: same load, same fatigue endpoint. Slower reps mean fewer reps lasting longer, so total time under tension — and the area under the mechanical tension curve — comes out similar. He recommends controlled eccentric plus explosive concentric: it improves strength development, keeps rep performance high, and needs no metronome.

## Note on framing
"Officially debunked" is overstated for at least two of these. The caffeine-timing case rests on inference from acute performance studies; the eccentric-duration and citrulline meta-analyses are genuine null findings, but a null in underpowered trials — which he concedes for citrulline — is weaker than a debunking. He is more careful in the body of each segment than the title suggests.
