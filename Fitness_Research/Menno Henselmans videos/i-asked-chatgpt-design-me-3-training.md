# What ChatGPT Gets Wrong When It Writes a Hypertrophy Program

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "I asked ChatGPT to design me 3 training programs. Here's what it did" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T2L2foSt_gg)) · 2023-05-04 · 25 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Henselmans prompted ChatGPT three times for a hypertrophy program for an intermediate male lifter. All three outputs were, in his assessment, roughly **2010-era program design** — and the third and best one still failed on rest intervals, exercise selection and muscle balance.
- The recurring structural failure across all three: **hamstring knee-flexion work is absent entirely**, so the short head of biceps femoris is never trained. Rear delts, traps and calves are similarly neglected while arms and quads are over-served.
- ChatGPT prescribed **60–90 s rest between sets**. Henselmans co-authored the review work that dismantled that recommendation; his position is **~3 minutes minimum** for hypertrophy, on the same ATP–phosphocreatine logic that applies to strength work.
- It opened with a **4-week strength block before a hypertrophy block**, which he says the block-periodisation literature does not support, and it added **"muscle confusion"** variety, which he calls a straight myth.
- It could not follow the instruction "four days per week" — on the second attempt it produced a rotating split with a day appended after a rest day.
- This is a **May 2023 snapshot of an early ChatGPT version**, and should be read as such rather than as a standing claim about AI program design.

## The three attempts

**Attempt 1** — prompt: a program for maximum hypertrophy for an intermediate male lifter. Output: a 12-week plan opening with a 4-week strength phase, everything at 4 sets of 4, on what appears to be a two-days-on/one-off rotating split that the model never actually specifies. Rest was given as 2–3 minutes. Henselmans notes the model volunteered "incorporate cardiovascular exercise 2–3 times per week", which sits badly against a hypertrophy brief given the concurrent-training interference effect.

**Attempt 2** — same prompt plus "trains four days per week". The model reproduced essentially the same program and bolted a fourth day on after the rest day, producing a three-on/one-off structure described as four days. The added day was Romanian deadlift, barbell curl, skull crusher and seated dumbbell press, all at 4×4.

**Attempt 3** — the best of the three. The model returned principles plus a sample upper/lower split rather than a fixed program, which he liked. But the principles were dated: compound movements preferred for hypertrophy, 3–5 sets of 8–12 reps, 60–90 s rest, twice-weekly frequency per muscle, and "variation to keep your muscles guessing".

## What he takes issue with, and why

**Strength block before a hypertrophy block.** He acknowledges one Brazilian study finding that a preceding strength phase increased hypertrophy both within the later block and overall, but calls it an outlier, and says subsequent work found order did not matter and favoured within-session over block organisation. His summary of block periodisation in exercise science is that the record is "virtually entirely negative or at best neutral". No study is cited in the description for this, only his own blog and Instagram posts.

**Rest intervals.** He points to a 2014 review he wrote with Brad Schoenfeld concluding the then-standard 60–90 s recommendation rested on essentially no evidence, with direct contrary evidence available; and to a later paper he co-authored showing 3-minute rests beat 1-minute rests. Mechanistically, short rests leave ATP–phosphocreatine resynthesis incomplete, so energy availability rather than mechanical tension limits the set.

**Compound-only exercise selection.** He says the latest meta-analytic evidence finds compound and isolation exercises do not differ in hypertrophy per se — what matters is whether a given exercise trains the target muscle well. Bench press is good for pecs but poor for the long head of triceps, so an overhead extension is likely needed regardless of whether it is "isolation".

**Muscle confusion.** He calls this a myth outright: muscle is contractile tissue that registers tension and does not respond to novelty. He references work (caption gives the author name as "damas et al", which the auto-transcript garbles) showing excess exercise variety raises muscle damage and protein breakdown without raising protein synthesis or growth, while harming skill acquisition on the movements you actually want to improve.

**The 8–12 rep "hypertrophy zone".** He treats this as settled myth, and reads its presence in the output as evidence of what the model is doing: aggregating what is most frequent on the internet, not what is currently best supported. His stated model of why: paradigm shifts start with researchers, trickle to coaches, then to general population, and the written volume online lags the shift by years.

## The programming detail he keeps returning to

Across every output he audits volume per muscle rather than per exercise:

- **Hamstrings** get only hip-hinge work (deadlift, RDL). Nothing trains knee flexion, so the short head of biceps femoris is untrained. He raises this three separate times.
- **Lower body** is one exercise against six for upper body in attempt 1; attempt 3 swings the other way to squat + deadlift + leg press + lunge, which he calls quad-dominant with hamstrings still neglected.
- **Calves** get three sets tacked onto the end of the last session.
- **Triceps** volume is over-counted if you count pressing as full triceps volume. His own practice: count bench pressing fully for the lateral and medial heads and essentially not at all for the long head.
- **Squats and deadlifts in the same session**, and two deadlift variants in one session, both of which he advises against.

## On what ChatGPT is
He argues the model is a text processor that cannot reason, cannot produce anything genuinely novel, and functions as "Google's I'm Feeling Lucky" for people who don't use Google well — comparing it unfavourably with chess engines, which are superhuman precisely because the domain is narrowly defined. **This is asserted, not evidenced**, and it is the weakest part of the video: he offers no test of reasoning, only the observation that the model mishandled "four days per week" and produced dated recommendations. His conclusion is that ChatGPT cannot design effective training programs and will not for some time, which he explicitly time-stamps to the version available in May 2023.

## Practical takeaways
- Audit any program — AI-written or not — by **weekly sets per muscle group**, not by exercise count. That is what exposes missing knee-flexion work, absent rear delt work and end-of-session calf tokenism.
- Use **~3 minutes rest** on hypertrophy work, not 60–90 s.
- Do not add exercise variety for its own sake; consistency on a movement pattern is what drives skill and strength on it.
- Treat rep-range prescriptions as flexible within roughly 5–30 reps rather than locking to 8–12.
