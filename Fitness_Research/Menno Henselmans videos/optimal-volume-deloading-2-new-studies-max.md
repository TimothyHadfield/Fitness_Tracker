# 33 Sets a Week Didn't Break Anyone, and Deloads Didn't Help

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Optimal volume & deloading: 2 new studies for max gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sZm5cBtXQCg)) · 2026-03-24 · 10 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Study 1:** trained lifters already doing 12–20 sets/week/muscle had one leg raised by 20% (~18 sets/week) and the other by 120% (~**33 sets/week**), all to failure, for 8 weeks. The researchers expected overtraining. They found **no significant difference in muscle growth** and comparable anabolic, proteolytic, autophagy and ribosome-biogenesis signalling.
- In absolute terms the 33-set leg grew **10.3% vs 7.6%** — his own reading of hard-to-read preprint graphs. Statistically significant as a main effect, **not** as a pre-to-post interaction, and muddied by pre-existing size differences between legs. He presents both readings honestly.
- **Study 2:** untrained men on ~7 sets/week/muscle, one side deloading (2 sets) every 4 weeks and the other not. **No difference** in growth or strength after 8 weeks.
- A prior study in strength-trained lifters at 20 sets/week to failure found a mid-programme deload **trended worse**, significantly so for strength.
- His larger argument: **deload studies are just volume comparisons**, and a deload week barely moves average weekly volume. That's why they keep coming out null.
- **There is no universal optimal volume.** The latest meta-analysis shows gains continuing to ~45 sets/week/muscle, but individual studies are wildly inconsistent because optimal volume varies enormously between people — and with sleep and stress.

## The extreme volume study
[00:00] The within-subject design is the reason to take it seriously: comparing one leg to the other in the same person controls for diet and genetics and buys statistical power. The volume was leg extensions and leg presses, half from leg presses, **all sets to momentary failure**.

[02:04] The statistical wrinkle recurs in the literature and is worth recording. Testing pre, post and the pre-post difference lets you find a significant **main effect** — pooled values across both timepoints being higher — without a significant **time × condition interaction**, which is what actually tests differential change. In a within-subject design you shouldn't see a baseline difference at all, but here the legs did differ in starting size. His summary is fair: read it as a marginal advantage to the high-volume leg, or as no reliable difference, which is what the authors concluded. Either way, **not a detrimental effect**.

[03:05] He generalises: the meta-analysis shows continued gains to ~45 sets/week/muscle, individual studies disagree, and the occasional trend toward harm appears **almost exclusively for high volume within a single workout, not across the week**.

Flag that against material already in this library. It is the same claim as Nippard's junk-volume position — that per-session set counts have a ceiling — arrived at from the opposite direction. Menno's headline sounds like an argument for very high volume; his actual position is that weekly volume tolerates a lot while per-session volume does not.

## The deload study
[05:29] Around seven sets a week every week, versus seven sets a week with a two-set deload week every fourth week. Biceps and quads, one side each, 8 weeks, untrained men, no difference.

[06:10] He notes this shouldn't surprise anyone, since below 10 sets/week/muscle you shouldn't need a deload at all. The more interesting datapoint is the contrast: at 20 sets/week to failure in trained lifters, a mid-programme deload produced a trend toward **worse** outcomes, statistically significant for strength.

His two-sided conclusion is the honest one: most people don't need deload weeks, and taking one won't materially hurt you either.

## The framing argument
[06:40] The strongest section. A deload week every 4 or 8 weeks changes average weekly volume trivially — "like comparing a group doing 20 sets per week versus 21". These studies are underpowered to find anything, and reading them as verdicts on deloading is a category error. **Total accumulated volume matters far more than its distribution**, as energy balance over time matters more than any individual day.

[07:41] He then attacks **univariate optimisation** — asking separately for the optimal proximity to failure, frequency, rest interval, set count. He borrows *impulse* from endurance research (total accumulated training stress) and offers his own **tension integral** — tension × time under tension — as the hypertrophy equivalent.

[08:41] The trade-offs that follow are the practical payoff, and they're coherent:
- High volume → probably shouldn't train to failure.
- Low volume → training to failure becomes more valuable.
- A well-optimised program (good exercise selection, long rest intervals, volume spread across the week, higher frequency) needs **fewer sets** for the same total stimulus.

[09:12] He recommends **reactive deloads** — triggered by performance, not by the calendar — over scheduled deload weeks.

## Flags
- **"There is no research at all to say [deload weeks] are beneficial"** is stated moments after he has described two studies on deloads. Absence of a positive finding in a handful of small, underpowered trials is being presented as an established negative — the exact inferential move he criticises elsewhere.
- The **10.3% vs 7.6%** figures come from him eyeballing graphs in a **preprint** that has not been peer reviewed. He says so, which is to his credit, but the numbers should not be quoted as published results.
- Both new studies are 2026 and one is a preprint. Study 2 is in **untrained** men, which limits its bearing on the trained lifters the deload question is actually about.
- The "all factors together" model that resolves the video's tension is **his own PT course and app**, not a published method, and the video is structured to arrive there. The reasoning is sound; the destination is a product.
