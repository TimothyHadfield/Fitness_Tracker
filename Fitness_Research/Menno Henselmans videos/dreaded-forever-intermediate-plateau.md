# Six Reasons Lifters Get Stuck as Forever Intermediates

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The Dreaded Forever Intermediate Plateau" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-IFctbSz3MI)) · 2026-09-01 · 12 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Sleep belongs with training and nutrition as a pillar, not with supplements.** Cutting sleep from 7.5 h to 5 h dropped fat loss from **1.4 kg to 0.6 kg** and raised fat-free mass loss from **1.6 kg to 2.4 kg** — roughly a 50% worse result in both directions.
- **Don't cut too fast.** In elite athletes, a **0.7%/week** weight-loss rate produced slight recomposition and better strength gains than **1%/week**, which lost muscle — for a near-identical change in body-fat percentage.
- **Don't perma-cut either.** Advanced lifters can't add appreciable muscle without a surplus, and "maingaining" is inefficient: over 10 weeks a maintenance group gained 1 kg lean mass and lost 1.4 kg fat, while a **10% deficit** group gained the same 1 kg lean mass but lost **2.9 kg** fat, with no strength difference.
- **Ego lifting is the silent killer.** Reps that shorten range of motion or add momentum don't count. Zero-tolerance rule: if you can't do another rep with identical technique and range, that was failure.
- **Progressive overload is the measurement, not the mechanism.** It's the evidence your last workouts worked, and the thing that tells you what to change.
- **Stop program hopping.** Switching every four weeks destroys your benchmarks, and early strength gains on new exercises are mostly neural.

## Lifestyle and energy balance
He puts sleep first specifically because it is more controllable than stress and better evidenced. The framing is the interesting part: most lifters mentally file sleep alongside supplements, whereas he argues the effect size sits alongside training and nutrition ([Nedeltcheva 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20921542/)). He concedes the exact magnitudes are contested.

On cutting, the rule scales with body fat: the fatter you are, the more aggressive you can be; the leaner and more muscular, the more conservative ([Garthe 2011](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.21.2.97)). The obvious inference — spend your life at maintenance — is one he explicitly rejects. Energy deficiency impairs lean-mass gains ([Murphy & Koehler 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14075)), so you can't build there; and a recent trial found the maintenance group simply lost less fat for the same muscle, meaning you spend longer in the cut and have less time left to bulk ([Vargas-Molina 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-026-06209-6)).

He flags the limitation himself: that sample averaged ~25% body fat and included women, so the recomposition seen even in the maintenance group is not something advanced lean lifters should expect. Positive recomposition, in his account, is largely a novice and intermediate phenomenon.

## Technique as a hard constraint
The ego-lifting section is where he is most prescriptive. Shortened range of motion — especially losing the lengthened portion — is treated as settled: *"this is basically consensus in exercise science right now, so I'm not even going to bother referencing this."* That is a real gap; the claim is doing load-bearing work in the video and carries no citation.

His practical implementation is a zero-tolerance policy with clients: technique and range are a hard limit set before the rep target, and any rep where either degrades is not counted at all. Failure is defined as the point where you cannot maintain both.

## Progressive overload and program stability
The conceptual move worth keeping: progressive overload does not *cause* gains, it *measures* them. Adding sets or grinding sloppier reps is not overload — it's noise. If the same technique and the same volume now yields an extra rep or more load, that is proof the previous block worked.

Two consequences follow. First, without a reliable progression signal you cannot know which body parts or exercises need changing, so program updates become guesswork. Second, clear performance targets drive effort: without "get the ninth rep," most people stay inside their comfort zone, and for an intermediate that one extra rep is often the entire session's available progress.

He is scathing about muscle confusion ("muscle confusion confusion") — adaptation is the goal of training, so avoiding it is self-defeating. Switching programs also erases your benchmark history, and because early strength gains on unfamiliar exercises are mainly neural coordination, constant switching means you never distinguish learning from growth. His proposed proxy in the absence of regular DEXA: sustained long-term strength progress on *familiar* compound lifts, which cannot be faked at 30% increases.

Getting past intermediate, in his framing, is mostly a shift from doing roughly the right things to running a structured, measured program with auto-regulation (reactive deloads) and, later, some periodization such as daily undulating rep targets.

**Overreach to note:** he asserts that energy balance and weekly set volume are "the two variables most important to optimize," and that optimizing them makes or breaks a program. That's a coaching judgement, not a finding from any study he cites.
