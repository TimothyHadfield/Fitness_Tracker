# Volume for Size, Intensity for Strength: What 200+ Studies Agree On

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "These 2 fundamentals build the most muscle & strength" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UU2dpLFIOHU)) · 2023-09-18 · 20 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Two 2023 big-picture reviews converge: **set volume is the strongest and most consistent driver of hypertrophy; load (intensity) is the strongest driver of strength**, at least in the short term.
- **Total sets per muscle group per week** is the single best predictor of growth. Ballpark from the best meta-analysis he cites: **10–30 sets per muscle per week** ([Baz-Valle 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35291645/)) — closer to 10 if you train close to failure, closer to 30 if you don't and your recovery is good.
- **The "hypertrophy zone" of 6–12 reps is a myth.** Set-for-set, anything from roughly **5 to 30 reps** (above ~30% 1RM) taken to similar proximity to failure produces similar growth.
- **Training to failure was not a consistent predictor** of growth or strength in either review. It helps a little, and he attributes that mostly to the extra reps — i.e. extra volume — not to failure itself.
- **For maximal strength you need to spend a considerable portion of training in the 1–5 rep range**, and he thinks occasional 1RM attempts are not substitutable — but not for novices.

## The two reviews
- A **2023 meta-analysis of 178 studies**: highest-ranked prescriptions for strength involved higher loads; highest-ranked for hypertrophy involved multiple sets.
- A **2023 umbrella review of 44 systematic reviews** ([McLeod 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2023.06.005)): the only highly consistent predictor of both hypertrophy and strength was volume, with a much stronger effect for size than for strength; for strength the leading variable was intensity.

He is explicit about how to read these: don't mine the details. Reviews at this altitude are for identifying what works robustly across everyone, not for optimising an individual program.

## Why volume drives hypertrophy
Mechanical tension on the fibres is the primary growth stimulus, and **a set is essentially a unit of that stimulus**. More sets means the signal is delivered more times. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why sets per muscle per week — not reps, not tonnage — is the metric that predicts growth best.

## Why load matters for strength but not for size
The resolution is motor unit recruitment plus time under tension.

- With a **heavy set** (say ~80%+ 1RM), full motor unit recruitment occurs from the first rep. Tension is on everything for the whole set.
- With a **light set**, only low-threshold units are active initially. As neuromuscular fatigue accumulates, the **size principle** brings in type 2A, then 2AX, then 2X units. By the end of a set taken near failure, everything has been recruited anyway.

So for hypertrophy the two converge, which is why sets of 8, 18 and 28 grow similar amounts. The boundaries: **below ~30% 1RM / above ~30 reps** the load is too light for maximal tension; **above ~85% 1RM / below ~5 reps** each rep carries higher tension but there are too few of them to accumulate enough time under tension.

For **strength**, the difference matters. Light sets recruit everything eventually but not **simultaneously**, so peak muscle activity at any instant is low. Strength is substantially neural — his recurring analogy is engine size (muscle) versus driver skill (motor cortex) — and synchronising high-threshold units at maximum output is what the nervous system has to learn. You need high force production to train high force production.

## Volume still matters for strength, over a longer horizon
His main criticism of the reviews is that they are short-sighted about timeframe. Neural adaptation is fast but finite; once technique, coordination and antagonist co-activation are optimised, **further strength requires more muscle**. He cites powerlifting data showing the strength–size relationship becomes essentially linear at that point ([Brechue & Abe 2002](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-001-0543-7)), remarking that rankings in classic powerlifting track fat-free mass index closely enough that a DEXA scan reproduces much of the competition result. The practical consequence: taking a 100 kg bench to 120 kg means roughly 20% more pec, delt and triceps tissue, which takes a long time to build. **Volume is therefore a long-run strength variable even though intensity dominates in the short run.**

## Mentzer, Yates and high-intensity training
He addresses the obvious objection head-on. In both reviews, **set endpoint / volitional fatigue were not consistent predictors** of hypertrophy or strength. His read of the more granular literature: training to failure does help on average, especially for growth, but the effect is small and largely explained by the additional reps performed — i.e. by volume, not by failure per se.

On the individuals: he says reasoning from single outliers is invalid, that genetics, training age and drugs dominate at that level, and that high volume is in fact standard practice among successful bodybuilders. He notes he personally would *prefer* low-volume, to-failure training because it suits him, and that this is not what the data support — a useful marker of where his stated preference and his conclusion diverge.

## Reconciling "volume = sets" with "volume = total work"
He gives a clean rule for why researchers use both definitions:

- **When load is held constant** (e.g. training-to-failure studies), total tension scales with total repetitions, so **rep volume** is the meaningful measure. This is why failure and rest intervals and exercise order show effects on a rep basis.
- **When load varies** (intensity studies), higher load raises tension per rep but reduces reps, and the trade-off is roughly neutral across ~5–30 reps. So **set count** becomes the meaningful measure.

## Practical application
- Track **sets per muscle group per week**; target within **10–30**, adjusting for proximity to failure, recovery capacity, stress and sleep. If you train Yates-style to failure, sit near 10; if you leave reps in reserve with good recovery, you can approach 30.
- **More is not always better** — some studies find higher volumes produce *worse* growth and strength, which he attributes to overreaching and recovery limits.
- For **strength**: spend a considerable portion of training at 1–5 reps and do occasional 1RM attempts, accepting the joint and time costs of stacking heavy low-rep work with high volume.
- For **novices**: skip 1RM attempts. Higher reps first — needlessly injurious otherwise, poor for technique practice, and untrained people show little strength difference by load anyway.
- Strength and size are **synergistic, not mutually exclusive**; the practical divergence between strength- and size-focused training is about joint cost and time budget, not any inherent conflict.

**Where the argument outruns the evidence:** the claim that powerlifting rankings essentially reduce to fat-free mass index is a rhetorical extension of a correlation from a small body of powerlifting research, and the 10–30 sets range is wide enough that it functions as a boundary condition rather than a prescription — he says as much, but the number is often quoted as if it were more precise.
