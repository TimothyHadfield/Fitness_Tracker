# Five Things Lifters Would Rather Not Hear

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "5 Brutal fitness reality checks: you'll wish you never saw this" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ME8ChKIqVfw)) · 2025-06-24 · 5 min
**Topic:** Psychology & behaviour

## Bottom line
- **Drugs are far more common than viewers assume** — among actors doing rapid transformations, Olympic athletes, and fitness influencers. His argument is incentives, not evidence: the reward for a lean, muscular on-camera physique is large and detection is near-zero.
- **If you are fatter than you want, you are eating more than you expend.** Not your metabolism, not your hormones. Untracked intake is systematically underestimated — he says even trained dietitians do this unless they weigh food and use labels or a reliable database.
- **No legal supplement is a shortcut.** Creatine is as good as legal supplementation gets, and most people quit it because they do not notice anything.
- **Most people simply do not train hard enough.** A systematic review found the average gym-goer leaves roughly **6–7 reps in reserve** on their sets. No amount of programme optimisation rescues that.
- **Measure, then change one thing.** Compare photos year over year; track strength and body-composition trend lines. Programme hopping is not a fix.

## On drug prevalence
He makes the audience part of the mechanism: genuinely natural lifters get hostility even with respectable physiques, so viewer expectations create the incentive to enhance. He invokes Charlie Munger's "show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome," and names Jeff Nippard as a physique people wrongly dismiss as unimpressive. For a natural ceiling estimate he points to his own FFMI calculator.

## On the effort problem
This is the one check with a citation behind it. Steele 2022 pooled self-selected load data and found trainees habitually work well short of failure. His framing: evidence-based lifting has produced a population of over-analysers optimising rest intervals and weekly set counts while training too easy for any of it to matter. The two levers that drive growth are **training hard** and **training enough** — and if you do not train hard, you need far more volume to compensate.

This is consistent with the junk-volume material already in this library — the "easy sets" problem, from a different dataset.

## Overreach
The energy-balance check is delivered as an absolute: it is not your hormones, and you need no diet break or maintenance phase. Thermodynamics guarantees that energy balance determines stored energy; it does not establish that hormonal or metabolic conditions never alter expenditure or intake regulation, which is what a listener will take away.

The drug-prevalence check is an argument from incentives with no prevalence data attached, delivered with the same confidence as the cited finding.
