# What the Incline Bench Press Actually Does to Your Chest

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is the perfect press angle for complete chest gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BqpExp-mLu0)) · 2025-04-07 · 19 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Inclining the bench does not add stimulus to the upper pecs. It subtracts stimulus from the lower pecs.** The upper fibres should already be maximally recruited during a flat press, so inclining isolates rather than intensifies.
- **Default to a slight incline of roughly 15-30°.** Not because incline is better, but because arching a flat bench press effectively creates a decline, and a small incline cancels that out — putting you back near 90° of shoulder flexion, where all pec fibres contribute.
- **Decline pressing has almost no use.** Reduced range of motion, worse stretch, worse stability, and no gain for the lower pecs it supposedly targets.
- Biomechanics, EMG and acute ultrasound swelling all agree with each other. **The single long-term growth study disagrees with all three**, finding incline superior everywhere — and he argues that study is unreliable, citing a **35% dropout rate**, 8 weeks, untrained subjects, one session per week.
- There is no "middle pec" and no "inner pec". The pec major has a clavicular head and a sternal head; the rest is sensation, not function.

## The biomechanical argument

This is where his confidence lives, and it drives everything else.

A flat bench press or fly is horizontal shoulder flexion/adduction — bringing the elbows toward the midline. The pec major is a fan-shaped muscle whose every fibre pulls the arm toward the midline, so during that movement **all** fibres should be maximally recruited. A muscle only fails to contribute when something actively prevents it — his example is the hamstrings during a squat, where hip extension helps but the simultaneous knee flexion would sabotage the quads, so the net contribution isn't beneficial and the muscle drops out. Nothing like that applies to either pec head during a flat press.

Now incline the bench. The limiting demand shifts from horizontal shoulder flexion toward vertical shoulder flexion, as in an overhead press. The clavicular fibres still contribute, but they were already at maximum — there is no upgrade available. The sternal fibres, which can only assist with the horizontal component, progressively drop out as that component stops being the limiting factor, and they also pull the elbow downward against the movement.

The decline case is the mirror image: at extreme decline you approach pure adduction, the lower fibres stay fully engaged, and the upper fibres stop contributing. So decline changes the ratio too — it just doesn't add anything to the lower pecs either.

**The conclusion he draws: press angle changes which fibres you exclude, not which fibres you emphasise upward.**

## What EMG shows

Across studies, upper pec activity is not much enhanced by inclining, while lower pec activity falls off sharply as the incline increases. Front delt activity rises only slightly, which fits — the front delts already assist with horizontal shoulder flexion during a flat press. Triceps findings are mixed and he treats press angle as largely irrelevant to them.

There is a modest upward trend in upper pec activity from 0° to 30°, and he offers an explanation that becomes the video's practical recommendation: **most people don't actually bench flat.** Benching with an arch reduces the angle between the torso and the pressing arm, which is functionally a decline. A 15-30° bench incline offsets the arch and returns you to approximately 90° of shoulder flexion.

He also mounts a defence of EMG research generally, which is a departure from the usual evidence-based line. His position: EMG does not translate linearly to hypertrophy and has real limitations, but within a narrow set of comparable movements, with proper normalisation and electrode placement, it has decent validity — it agrees with ultrasound, agrees with MRI, tracks subjective effort, and rises with load. Discarding it entirely because it isn't a growth measure is, in his word, imprudent, especially when it agrees with the biomechanics.

## What ultrasound shows

One study measured both acute muscle swelling and EMG for flat versus incline pressing ([Albarello 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2022.102722)). Upper pec swelling with incline was no greater than with flat; lower pec swelling was significantly less. The ultrasound and EMG patterns matched each other.

He is careful about what swelling means: it's a blended measure of muscle activity and muscle damage, there is some evidence that acute swelling corresponds to long-term growth, and it is **not** the same as the pump you feel — subjective pump doesn't track measured cross-sectional area well.

This gives him three independent lines pointing the same way, which he calls consilience.

## The one long-term study, and why he rejects it

A 2020 Brazilian study (the transcript renders the lead author as "Chavez"; the paper is *Effects of Horizontal and Incline Bench Press on Neuromuscular Adaptations in Untrained Young Men*) ran untrained but physically active subjects for 8 weeks on flat, 44° incline, or a half-and-half combination ([Int J Exerc Sci 2020](https://doi.org/10.70252/FDNB1158)).

The result contradicts his whole model. Incline produced more growth than flat and than the combination — statistically significant at the upper pecs, and the same pattern non-significantly at the middle and lower measurement sites, including about **33% more lower pec growth with incline than with flat**.

He calls this incredulous, and gives his reasoning honestly:
- He could have accepted "more upper, less lower". What he can't accept is incline being straight-up better everywhere, including at the fibres biomechanics says it disengages.
- The strength data is internally inconsistent. Incline was best for incline strength, but flat was *not* best for flat strength — adding incline to flat helped flat strength. If exercise variety improves motor learning in untrained people, that should have worked symmetrically. It didn't.
- **35% dropout.** For a study asking physically active people already training three times a week to add one low-volume gym session weekly for eight weeks, that is very high, and dropout that heavy undoes randomisation and raises type one error risk.
- Small, short, untrained, low volume.

He names his own bias in passing — "maybe I'm just completely biased and I want to stick with the theory" — and then sticks with the theory anyway. That is the honest position stated, not resolved.

**Flag:** he is discounting the only direct hypertrophy evidence in favour of mechanistic and acute-measure evidence. His reasons are specific and reasonable, but the resulting recommendation is a theory-led one, not an outcome-led one, and he doesn't quite say so. There is also a pattern here worth noting across his videos: he has separately flagged suspected data problems in Brazilian training studies, and "it's a Brazilian study" appears in his list of reasons to doubt this one. That is a heuristic, not an analysis.

## Practical recommendation

- **Most of your pressing at roughly 90° of shoulder flexion between torso and arm** — which for most people, given a normal arch, means a **slight incline of about 15°, up to 30° if you arch heavily.**
- If you specifically want to emphasise upper pecs, use a **larger incline** — but understand you are reducing lower pec growth, not increasing upper pec growth.
- **Skip decline pressing.** Shortened range of motion, less stretch, less stability without feet planted and scapulae retracted, and no benefit to the lower pecs.
- Ignore inner/outer and upper/middle/lower pec targeting beyond the two actual heads. Feeling the squeeze in the middle and the stretch at the outside is sensation, not differential function.
