# Mike Israetel on Supplements, Exercise Selection, and What Technique Is Actually For

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "How To Build Muscle Superhumanly Fast - Mike Israetel" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FMIgzYlp7lQ)) · 2025-03-10 · 75 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Israetel's whole supplement list is **creatine, a multivitamin/multimineral, and a protein powder as needed** — and he is emphatic that even those don't matter much. Everything else, including citrulline malate and beta-alanine, he treats as unproven for hypertrophy.
- The three unlocks he names for a stalled early intermediate, in order: **prove you're actually training hard** by beating a written log every week; **raise frequency** (three sessions a week is not enough — four to five); and run **specialisation blocks** where two or three muscles go from a normal 5–15 sets/week up to 25–35 while everything else is maintained.
- On the preprint showing strict vs. cheated reps produced **equal growth over 8 weeks in untrained people**, he concedes nothing. His reading: the cheat group did far more mechanical work for the same result, so it is evidence *for* strict technique on stimulus-to-fatigue and stimulus-to-risk grounds.
- On lengthened bias after a run of null results: he's stepped back to **"don't skip the deep bottom stretch, and pick exercises that load it"**, while explicitly declining to endorse lengthened partials for everything.
- He states outright that he is **"pretty much by myself in the evidence-based community"** in using pump and soreness as stimulus proxies. Worth flagging as a position he holds against the field, including against his host.
- The recurring methodological point: most exercise-science findings are **statistically significant but small**. Seated vs. lying leg curls are the rank-one and rank-three boxer — genuinely different, both effective.

## The supplement cupboard is nearly empty
Israetel's top three are creatine, a multivitamin/multimineral, and a protein powder. He rates creatine on three converging lines of support: it works in trials, it works visibly in the gym, and the mechanism is coherent. He is much softer on the multivitamin — "the literature on that is not super clear" — and frames it as health insurance, not a hypertrophy aid. Protein powder he half-jokes is just food; the argument is cost and convenience, not a supplement effect.

The blunter point is what happens when a lifter decides they've earned the right to buy supplements. Israetel's image: you open the door expecting a stocked room, and a dustbin falls over with creatine sitting there alone. He is explicit that telling an intermediate "get creatine and protein and you'll be jacked" is not true — the effect sizes are too small for those to be things you *need*. Beta-alanine he'd take for sport performance but not for muscle growth; citrulline malate he followed closely in the 2010s and now considers a supplement that failed to hold up.

The interesting move is that Israetel refuses to let this slide into blanket cynicism, and names the fallacy he says he used to commit: *keep it simple, stupid*. There are hundreds of compounds on the market and almost certainly some that work and simply lack the research volume to prove it. The reason not to take them is economic, not epistemic — $20–60 a month each, and you cannot buy them all.

Menno's addition is the sharper version of the argument: with supplements the evidence bar should be *higher* than usual, because publication bias and false positives are rampant in that literature. And the specific trap with citrulline is that people get excited about a **proxy** for the outcome rather than the outcome. Citrulline lets you do more reps; nobody has shown it produces significantly more muscle. Israetel accepts this without argument — notable, because he defends proxies elsewhere in the same conversation (see below).

## What actually moves an intermediate
**First, verify you train hard.** The instrument is a written log and an objective standard, not a feeling. Set an easy target in week one of a mesocycle — roughly 3 RIR — write down every set and rep, then make it your job to match or beat every single set every week until the deload. He contrasts this with independent RIR estimation (organically hitting a proximity to failure each session), which he calls a fine and liberating way to train *if* you have a strong training drive, but useless for diagnosing underworking. The point is that "failure" is far more psychological than physiological, and chasing a number you don't think you can hit is what exposes the gap. It also catches false deloads: lifters call a reactive deload when they were really at 3 RIR and having a bad week.

**Second, frequency.** Nobody on the IFBB pro stage trains three times a week; five is about average. A stalled three-day lifter should go to four, then five.

**Third, specialisation blocks.** He calls the volume literature "crystal clear that cranking your volume as long as you can recover is probably a good idea" — a stronger claim than the evidence supports, and worth flagging, since dose-response work generally shows benefits flattening with wide individual variance. His practical version is at least bounded: keep most muscles at 5–15 sets a week, pick two or three per block, start around 10 sets and push up until they're too fatigued to continue — landing at 25 sets for pecs, 30 for biceps, 35 for forearms. The justification is a margins argument: an intermediate's baseline gains are small enough that a small absolute improvement is a large relative one, which is why the same advice would be wrong for a beginner.

**Fourth, for later intermediates, exploration** of exercises, technique variants, rep ranges and splits — a hit-rate game with as many failures as revelations. The individual differences he describes are large: leg extensions do almost nothing for him but wipe out his colleague Dr. James Hoffmann.

## Exercise picks, and the reasoning behind them
The specific picks matter less than the criteria, which are consistent: load the stretch, use a favourable resistance profile, keep systemic and axial fatigue low, make it progressable. (A shorter Menno–Israetel video covers the same ground in more detail — see `best-exercises-each-muscle-mike-israetel.md`.)

- **Standard picks:** belt squats and belt-squat calf raises (axial fatigue near zero because the load hangs from the hips); stiff-legged deadlifts, properly executed, which he says is rare; pull-ups; cambered bar bench and much deeper incline dumbbell presses; deep skull crushers plus overhead work; deep incline, lying and behind-the-back cable curls. He dislikes rope push-downs for loading the contraction, "the least useful part of the movement," and he and Menno agree standing barbell curls have a backwards resistance profile.
- **Abs:** he questions whether most people should train them at all. If they do: a selectorised crunch machine, weighted 45° inverted bench crunches, ab wheel rollouts. Planks he dismisses outright.
- **Erectors:** no bodybuilding case for isolating them, but a health case for **loaded spinal flexion** — cable and dumbbell flexion rows, Jefferson curls, starting very light and high-rep. Being strong only in a locked neutral position, he argues, is what leaves you vulnerable when something unlocks you; he reports his own back problems resolving after adding flexion work. This runs against decades of conventional back-safety advice and rests entirely on his own experience.
- **Rear delts and traps** are the other departure. He rarely programs rear delts separately, since any horizontal pull loads them heavily ("if you had an avulsion in your rear delt you could not do pull-ups"); Menno adds that research shows compound pulling trains them as effectively as standard external-rotation rehab work. Traps he never isolates and, more importantly, never tries to *de*-emphasise. The argument is bandwidth: telling someone to focus on side delts while also not using traps splits attention, costs neural drive, and ends with them using 5 kg instead of 10. He generalises it — the idea that one muscle "steals" from another is backwards, and strong triceps can push the pecs past where they'd otherwise stop.

## Technique, and a null result he refuses to concede
Menno puts the preprint to him directly: untrained subjects, unilateral, one arm strict push-downs and curls, the other using heavy body English, roughly equal growth over eight weeks.

Israetel first lists the standard limitations — eight weeks, untrained subjects, and the general point that a single study shows almost nothing — then does something more useful and grants the finding for the sake of argument. His conclusion is that cheating produces roughly equal *direct stimulus*, and that this was never the claim technique was making. "I have never heard anyone say that strict technique will have a very high effect of maximising your direct stimulus to the muscle."

What he says the study actually shows is a cost difference. The cheat group used more weight or more reps and therefore did substantially more mechanical work for the same result — spending energy through the whole body, adding injury risk, and eating into later sessions. Cheating grows muscle; the stimulus-to-fatigue and stimulus-to-risk ratios are terrible. He adds that he'd have been *more* confused by the opposite result, since less growth in the cheat arm would imply mind-muscle connection matters more than he believes.

His prescription is uncompromising: technique is never traded away. If you can't get the target rep with decent technique, the set is over — take five seconds and do a myorep, or two minutes and do another set. He guards against the obvious failure mode by defining the tolerance loosely: not rep-perfect form, but "are you still doing the range of motion you set out to do, is your body still under control."

## Lengthened bias after a bad run of data
Menno lays out the reversal: an avalanche of research favouring long-length training and lengthened partials, then a meta-analysis showing a trivial effect size for lengthened over shortened exercises, two trained-subject studies showing lengthened partials equal to full ROM, and a study showing cable lateral raises not significantly better than dumbbell.

Israetel's pooled position: the lengthened component is still "extra special," but by a margin, not double or triple growth — a claim he says he never made. His practical rule is deliberately narrower than the one the field held a year earlier: **don't skip the deep bottom stretch, and choose exercises that load it heavily.** Whether you add partials, squeeze at the top, or go full ROM he treats as open, and he declines to endorse lengthened partials universally because "the recent literature has not been kind to it."

His offered mechanism for why partials might still work is his own framework — local fatigue is massive, systemic fatigue similar or lower, so stimulus-to-fatigue is favourable — and he immediately answers Menno's "does that make sense?" with "no, not a ton." He labels it as speculation.

Both then converge on the framing that does most of the work in the episode. Menno's version: for high frequency, for long-muscle-length training, and for most of what they recommend, the research is either positive or neutral, and the chance of it being harmful is very small — so a purely statistical decision rule endorses them even if the mechanisms are unknown and individual studies are unreliable. Israetel adds the missing-out cost on the other side.

Israetel then spends several minutes on **significance versus effect size**, the most transferable content in the video. Significance answers "are these two things different at all"; effect size answers "by how much." His illustration: the rank-one and rank-three boxers in the world are significantly different and both will destroy you. Lying vs. seated leg curls sit in that relationship. Worse, the ranking can invert per individual, and novelty alone makes the neglected exercise better after six months of the other. Night-and-day effect sizes exist, but at the level of "leg extensions vs. biceps curls for quads."

## Where they disagreed
This is a friendlier conversation than the Contreras one, and Menno spends most of it interviewing rather than arguing. The real fault lines are these:

**Pump and soreness as stimulus proxies.** The sharpest one, and Israetel raises it himself, unprompted, while defending hip thrusts: "this is where I'm pretty much by myself in the evidence-based community with my proxies like pump and soreness." He predicts he'll win the argument eventually and says the pump literature has been good to him lately. Menno lets it pass here, but this is a direct conflict with his own position — Menno's whole framing of citrulline moments earlier was that proxies for growth are not growth. Israetel uses proxies to *justify* exercise selection (five sets of front-foot-elevated Smith lunges produce a stimulus magnitude "incomparable" to hip thrusts) while agreeing that citrulline's rep-count proxy proves nothing. Neither of them reconciles this, and it is left standing.

**Hip thrusts and the force curve.** Israetel's objection is that hip thrusts are hardest at the top and trivial at the bottom, which is the wrong way round, plus the loading hassle and the fact that it's an isolation exercise. Menno immediately raises Bret Contreras's counter — peak active mechanical tension in the glutes occurs at the top, so a resistance profile matching that may be a feature. Israetel half-concedes ("you are correct, probably... if the force curve was half as extreme that might be the thing") then pivots: lengthened tension is now dominant enough to outweigh top-end active tension, and passive tension grows muscle too. He ends up endorsing hip thrusts as the best near-isolation glute exercise while wanting a cam-levered machine that inverts the resistance curve. Nobody concedes; the dispute is how to weigh active against passive tension, and nobody knows. *(Contreras's own view, including his direct criticism of Israetel's glute programming, is in the Contreras note in this library.)*

**Volume ceilings.** Israetel says the volume literature is "crystal clear" that more is better within recovery, and describes 25–35 sets per muscle per week in specialisation blocks. Menno doesn't challenge it on camera, but it sits well above the volumes he generally advocates, and "crystal clear" overstates a literature that mostly shows flattening returns.

**Cheat reps.** Menno presents the null preprint as a finding that needs accounting for; Israetel accounts for it by reframing the outcome variable rather than revising his prescription. Defensible — the study measured growth, not cost — but the reframe is unfalsifiable within the study design, and the stimulus-to-fatigue advantage he claims for strict technique has not itself been measured.

**Where they agree against the field:** lengthened bias is real but small, single studies are near-worthless, the "one muscle takes over from another" framing is wrong, and the significance/effect-size confusion is the biggest driver of bad conclusions from fitness research. Menno explicitly frames the episode as two people who "disagree on some things" recommending nearly the same practices — which is itself the point he wants made.
