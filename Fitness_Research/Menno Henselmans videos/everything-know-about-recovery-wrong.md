# Fatigue Is Loss of Force: Two Counterintuitive Ways to Reduce It

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Everything You Know About Recovery Is Wrong" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1oBE9ZHuc7E)) · 2026-08-11 · 12 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- **Define fatigue properly or you can't program around it.** In exercise science neuromuscular fatigue is simply **loss of force production**. The fatigue index is just rep drop-off across sets — eight reps falling to four is 50% fatigue. It is not the same thing as mental fatigue.
- **Strategy 1 — train each muscle more frequently.** At matched weekly set volume, spreading sets across the week improves recovery and reduces fatigue. One trial: 8 bench sets in one session vs 2 sessions of 4 — the split group recovered faster *and* did **10% more work**.
- **Strategy 2 — train heavier.** Higher intensities cause *less* neuromuscular fatigue than light, high-rep work. A 20RM is far more fatiguing than a 1RM. The "1RMs fry your CNS" idea is backwards.
- **Frequency is just a longer rest interval.** Rest between sets, hours, or days — it's all the distribution of volume over time. A bro split is therefore the worst possible fatigue management, because every set sits behind the maximum number of preceding sets.
- Practical inversion: for unmotivated, older or beginner clients, **sets of 5-8 are gentler than sets of 15-20**, not harsher.

## Why higher frequency reduces fatigue
The mechanism is almost trivially simple once he reframes it: set eight of eight has seven sets of accumulated fatigue in front of it. Move three of those sets to Wednesday and they are performed fresh. That is why the split group in the bench-press study recovered faster despite doing more total work ([Bartolomei 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.2021.1939848)). A second study found switching from a four-day upper/lower split to daily full-body training improved recovery capacity while increasing volume — with full recovery reached 22 hours after a test workout following the heavy training period, but not before it ([Raastad 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12753488/)).

Three supporting lines, each of which he explicitly downgrades:
- **Soreness** falls with higher frequency. He immediately caveats that soreness is not a reliable marker of muscle damage at all — but argues it matters psychologically, creating a self-fulfilling prophecy where bro-split soreness convinces people they can't handle more volume, when the soreness is itself an artefact of concentrating that volume.
- **Testosterone-to-cortisol ratio** improves with higher frequency. Called "a very rough marker," used only directionally.
- **Powerlifters with higher training frequencies have lower injury rates** ([Strömbäck 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29785405/)). He calls this associative and weak, and his considered position is that frequency and injury risk are simply unrelated.

That degree of self-caveating is unusual and worth noting — the argument is carried by the mechanical logic, not the mechanistic markers.

## What frequency does and doesn't do for growth
He is careful to keep the fatigue claim separate from the hypertrophy claim. When volume is equated between conditions, **training frequency does not improve muscle growth in the majority of research**. His argument is that frequency lets you *accumulate more volume* — more reps at the same load because you're fresher — and that is where the gains come from.

His best illustration is a within-subject design: one leg trained with nine sets once weekly, the other with three sets three times weekly, either volume-equated or free to run its natural (higher) rep count ([Neves 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36228016/)). Volume-equated, growth was the same. Volume-free, the higher-frequency leg gained roughly **double** — about 4% vs 2%. He states plainly that this was **not statistically significant**, and rests only on effect-size analysis. He also points to a course document tallying trained-lifter frequency studies, claiming that where a significant difference exists it always favours higher frequency — that tally is not a published analysis and can't be checked here.

## Why heavier is less fatiguing
The self-test he offers: after a 2RM you're too fatigued to lift your 2RM again; after a 20RM widow-maker you're too fatigued to lift your 20RM — let alone the much heavier 2RM. Total tonnage (weight x reps) is consistently far higher in high-rep sets, so total mechanical work output, and therefore neuromuscular fatigue, is higher. He extends this to central nervous system fatigue, which research finds is **consistently higher after low-intensity exercise**, and dismisses CNS-frying from maximal singles as nonsense ([Farrow 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.2020.1734521); [Yoon 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17626289/); [Behm 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9134916/)).

The coaching implication reverses standard practice: prescribing very high reps to unmotivated, older or novice clients may be defensible physiologically but is poor fatigue management. Most people also find it easier to push near failure at roughly 6-12 reps — which he offers as the one piece of the old "hypertrophy zone" that survives scrutiny, on effort grounds rather than growth grounds.

He closes with the limit on his own advice: most lifters aren't actually limited by neuromuscular recovery in the first place, and for many the real constraint is injury. Going heavy all the time trades neuromuscular fatigue for connective-tissue risk, so the question is which kind of fatigue is actually limiting you.
