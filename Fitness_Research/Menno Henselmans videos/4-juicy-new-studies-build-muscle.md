# Four New Studies: Squat Load, Leg Extensions vs Leg Press, Cold Plunges, and Starting Muscle Mass

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "4 Juicy new studies on how to build muscle" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uE55QhQX5hY)) · 2026-04-21 · 8 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- In national-level powerlifters squatting at **70-90% 1RM**, glute force production rose far more steeply with load than quad force did. Quads are trained reasonably well even at lighter loads or further from failure; the glutes only come fully online as load and effort rise.
- **Squats do not train "legs."** Force production in the hamstrings and rectus femoris was very low — both are biarticular and can't contribute productively.
- Over 12 weeks with within-subject MRI, **leg extensions beat leg press for the rectus femoris**, with no difference in the vasti. That makes leg extension arguably the more *complete* quad exercise.
- Cold water immersion **reduced soreness but did not improve strength or power recovery**. He considers it net negative post-workout because it blunts muscle protein synthesis and growth.
- **Starting muscle mass barely predicts gains** — correlations of 0.1 to 0.2. Being skinny does not make you a hard gainer.

## 1. Squat load shifts which muscles work hardest
Muscular forces were estimated at each joint across 70-90% 1RM in 5% increments. Heavier loads meant more force everywhere, but the *magnitude of increase* differed sharply — glutes gained far more than quads, alongside a more forward torso lean. He links this to bench press analyses where pecs are stimulated well at lower intensities while triceps only become heavily involved near failure. The pattern he proposes: **a compound lift's primary mover is trained well even when you don't train hard; the secondary musculature requires heavy load or high effort.**

His own caveat is substantial: this was submaximal loading, and where lifters go close to failure the differences may be too marginal to matter. A long-term training study is needed.

## 2. Leg extensions vs leg press, same person
One leg did leg extensions, the other leg presses, 12 weeks, gold-standard MRI. Leg extensions were superior for the **rectus femoris**; no difference in the vasti. Since rectus femoris can't productively activate during a squat or leg press but is targeted well by leg extension, the compound exercise is the less complete quad option here.

Two refinements:
- Subjects did not **recline** during leg extensions. You should — leaning back lengthens the rectus femoris, allowing higher force and more mechanical tension, and prior research shows more growth as a result.
- Other research finds squats or leg presses grow the vasti better. He attributes this to machine resistance curves and range of motion, not anything inherent — at equal resistance curve and ROM he expects the three to be equivalent for the quads.

The study also replicated that leg presses barely train the hamstrings, and found MRI growth patterns **matched EMG activity patterns well** — which he uses to push back on the claim that EMG has been "debunked". It has real limits and can't be extrapolated directly to hypertrophy, but properly conducted it does show which muscles an exercise involves.

## 3. Cold water immersion
A 30-study meta-analysis plus another new study both found cold plunges reduce **perceived soreness** without objectively improving strength or power recovery. He notes sharply that the authors nonetheless concluded CWI was beneficial — a case for reading results rather than abstracts.

His stronger position: post-workout cold plunges are a **negative**, because multiple studies show they blunt muscle protein synthesis and growth. Inflammation is part of repair; suppressing it past a point impairs recovery — the same pattern seen with high-dose vitamin C and E and with NSAIDs.

## 4. Baseline muscle mass doesn't predict gains
A meta-analysis of four studies found starting bone-free fat-free mass only very weakly related to fat-free mass gained (r = 0.1-0.2). Bone mass didn't predict response either. He flags **low statistical power** but says the wider literature agrees. Nuance: some earlier research found **frame size** does predict response, which he interprets as frame size influencing final muscular *potential* more than the *rate* of approach to it.

**Overreach flag:** study 1's practical claim — that you can selectively bias glutes with load — is a biomechanical model at submaximal loads, which he says himself, yet the video leads with it as a training implication.
