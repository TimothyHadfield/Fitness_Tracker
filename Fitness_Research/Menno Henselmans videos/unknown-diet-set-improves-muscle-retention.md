# Nutrient Timing Isn't Dead: Distributing Calories Across the Day

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This unknown diet set-up improves muscle retention" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7z2om0eO2Xk)) · 2025-05-21 · 11 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **He is arguing against the evidence-based consensus here.** "Nutrient timing is dead" is the current default position; he says the field over-corrected from the six-meals-a-day era and the truth sits in the middle.
- **Practical rule: sandwich your workout inside a 5-hour inter-meal window.** No more than five hours between the pre- and post-workout meal; train anywhere in between. A long fast after training is the thing to avoid.
- A forgotten 1997 metabolic-ward crossover ([Keim 1997](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/127.1.75)) found **significantly better muscle retention when most calories came later in the day** (35/35/15/15 vs 15/15/35/35), with similar fat loss — so better partitioning overall.
- **The anabolic window shrinks as you get more trained**, which is why timing matters more for advanced lifters and is probably irrelevant for beginners.
- **Eating the same calories every day likely means bulking on rest days and cutting on training days.** Consider matching intake to expenditure across days too.
- He repeatedly flags that **all of this evidence is weak** and that the effects are marginal relative to total intake.

## The pendulum argument

His framing: views swing between extremes. Fifteen years ago you had to eat six meals a day and sprint to the locker room for a post-workout shake. That was false, and the correction has overshot into "nutrient timing doesn't matter at all."

His reductio: if timing genuinely didn't matter, one meal a month would work. One a week. One a day. Obviously not — so the question was never *whether* timing matters but **at what granularity it stops mattering.** What happened, he argues, is that findings about immediate pre/post-workout protein being unnecessary were generalised to nutrient timing as a whole.

His favourite counter-evidence is a study design: trials where a post-workout shake group did **not** end up with higher total daily protein — sometimes slightly lower — and still made better gains. If total intake didn't differ, the timing did the work. Hence a methodological complaint: he distrusts protein-intake studies that didn't control protein *timing*.

He is not reviving the locker-room shake. The mechanism is **hyperaminoacidemia** — amino acids in the blood — not ingestion time.

## The 5-hour window

His actual prescription: **no more than five hours between the pre- and post-workout meal**, workout somewhere inside. Within that, exact timing probably doesn't change results. The failure mode is a long post-workout fast with no protein available to support the synthesis you just stimulated.

## The energy-distribution review ([Murlasits 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/17479541241308970))

A systematic review extending the argument from protein to total energy: athletes should have intake that **dynamically matches requirements**, with higher-expenditure parts of the day matched by higher intake, avoiding prolonged periods of restriction.

He doesn't oversell it. The review **leans heavily on observational data** — athletes who match intake to expenditure have better body composition — with no randomised interventions showing that adjusting distribution improves outcomes. His verdict: weak, but plausible and consistent with other findings.

## The 1997 study he thinks nobody talks about

[Keim 1997](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/127.1.75) is the strongest single piece of evidence here. Female lifters, full-body training 3x/week, six weeks on each of two conditions with identical macronutrient totals: **AM-loaded** four meals at 35/35/15/15% of energy, versus **PM-loaded** at 15/15/35/35%.

Crucially it ran in a **metabolic ward**, so intake was fully controlled. He spends real time on why that matters: diet adherence is the standard failure mode of nutrition research and coaching alike, and a study where subjects don't follow the assigned diet is undermined at the root.

The women trained after breakfast, so the conditions amounted to most energy *before* the workout versus most in the **post-workout to bedtime** window.

Result: **significantly better muscle retention on the PM pattern.** Fat loss was similar, but because the PM group lost no significant muscle their body fat percentage change was slightly better. Limitations he names himself: **small sample** (partly offset by the crossover design) and body composition by **bioelectrical impedance** — good equipment, not the gold standard.

## The anabolic window, properly stated

The one-hour post-workout version is conclusively false. The real anabolic window is the whole growth process, which in **untrained** people can run for multiple days — which is why he thinks timing is essentially irrelevant for beginners.

The interesting move is training status: research shows **the window shortens as you get more trained.** You gain less per workout so the process completes faster, and trained lifters incur less damage and recover quicker. Higher training volumes may extend it again.

So for a trained lifter training earlier in the day, much of the post-workout MPS peak falls between the workout and bedtime, and a meaningful share of daily energy and protein should land there.

## Across days, not just within them

Most people eat the same calories every day, which means their **rest days are a surplus and their training days are a deficit** — backwards.

His worked example: 2,500 kcal expenditure on rest days, 3,000 on training days, on a 10% deficit. Averaged across the week that's roughly 2,786 kcal expenditure against about 2,507 kcal intake — genuinely 10% down on average. But on a rest day you eat 2,507 against 2,500 expended, a slight *surplus*, while every training day is a deficit.

Good caveat: if you train very late in the day the recovery period mostly falls on the *following* day, so the extra calories might belong on the rest day instead.

## Where the evidence stops

He states his own limits repeatedly, which is worth recording: very little research; dietary adherence makes firm conclusions hard; **timing effects are generally smaller than total intake effects**; and for anyone eating three roughly even meals with the workout inside a five-hour window, further optimisation is marginal and only relevant to well-trained lifters.

The one place he outruns his evidence is the "timing mattered even when total intake didn't change" claim, which carries much of the argument's weight but is tied to no named study. Hulmi, Mori and Sharp are all post-workout supplementation trials in the list, but none is identified as the source, so treat the claim as uncited.
