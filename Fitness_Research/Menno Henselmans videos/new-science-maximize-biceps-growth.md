# Incline vs Preacher Curls, and Whether to Go All-In on Lengthened Training

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The new science of how to maximize biceps growth" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FnOzyr_NE_8)) · 2025-03-17 · 16 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Incline curls grow the biceps, especially the upper portion. Preacher curls grow the brachialis and brachioradialis.** Roughly five new studies now point the same way. Total elbow flexor growth is usually not different between them.
- The mechanism is not stretch alone: it's **high tension at long muscle length**. An incline curl lengthens the biceps at the shoulder but has little tension at the bottom. A preacher curl has high tension at the bottom, and the brachialis and brachioradialis don't cross the shoulder so they aren't shortened by it.
- **Bayesian curls** — elbow behind the body, cable resistance — are his pick because they combine both: long length *and* high tension in the stretched position.
- **You can go essentially all-in on lengthened training for the biceps.** A 2025 preprint comparing 100% long-length work against a 50/50 split found no meaningful difference overall, but the trend favoured the long-length group: **89% greater cross-sectional area increase (81% probability of superiority)**, more arm circumference, marginally more isometric strength — despite that group getting no exercise variety.
- Soreness and perceived effort were the same, so there is no stimulus-to-fatigue penalty for training only at long lengths.
- **These are fine-tuning effects.** He says so repeatedly: none of it matters as much as training hard and getting the volume in.

## The two-exercise question

[Kassiano 2025](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2517-0509) had women train biceps with either incline or preacher curls. The answer depends on where you measure: **significantly more growth near the upper biceps with incline curls, significantly more near the lower biceps with preacher curls.**

His explanation turns on the biceps being biarticular — it crosses the shoulder as well as the elbow, so raising the arm forward shortens it and drawing the arm back lengthens it. An incline curl therefore stretches the biceps at the shoulder, which is why it grows the upper region more. But at the bottom of an incline curl the arm hangs vertically and tension is mostly passive — not much active loading.

The preacher curl does the opposite. The biceps is slightly shortened, but tension in the bottom position is high, and the **brachialis and brachioradialis don't cross the shoulder**, so they aren't shortened at all. They get long length *and* high tension.

His general principle, and the one worth taking away: **length alone is not the point. Tension is the driver of hypertrophy, so you want tension at long lengths.** He treats mechanical tension as principally, possibly exclusively, the growth mechanism.

Ultrasound can't cleanly separate biceps from brachialis, so the "lower biceps" result in that study is a hypothesis about the brachialis rather than a measurement of it.

## The confirming studies

- **An unpublished Japanese study** (conference proceedings only, no full text, not peer reviewed) compared incline and preacher curls within the same men and used **MRI**, which resolves the individual muscles. Result: no difference in total elbow flexors, significantly more **biceps** growth with incline — most pronounced at the top — and significantly more **brachialis and brachioradialis** growth with preacher curls. That is the direct confirmation the ultrasound study couldn't provide, and it's preliminary.
- **Trained women, incline vs preacher** ([Zabaleta-Korta 2023](https://doi.org/10.5114/jhk/163561)): no significant difference in total elbow flexor volume; significant growth near the elbow with preacher curls but not with incline. The between-condition difference was not itself significant.
- **An unpublished Iranian study**, Bayesian vs preacher curls, 10 weeks, 15 subjects: no significant differences, but Bayesian trended better for biceps and preacher better for brachialis.
- **A three-week blood flow restriction study** ([da Silva Vendruscolo 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/19417381241287522)) found nothing. He dismisses it — three weeks, BFR, and you shouldn't expect fine-tuning to move the needle in that window anyway.

## His methodological argument, which is the real content

The middle of the video is a lecture on how to read a research literature, and it's the most transferable part.

The complaint he addresses: research seems to flip-flop, preacher curls better, then not better, then better again. His answer: **every new study leans toward the null.** Short duration, few subjects, and — with trained lifters — genuinely tiny effects to detect. A null result is the default expectation, not evidence of contradiction.

So the correct read is the **trend across the whole body of work**, not the significance stars on individual studies. Here the trend is one-sided: some studies find benefits for lengthened-biased training, **none find the opposite**. That asymmetry is what you bet on. Real flip-flopping would look like a study finding Bayesian curls *worse* than dumbbell curls, and no such study exists.

He also inverts the usual "trained subjects are better" preference. Trained subjects make extrapolation easier but make effects nearly undetectable — he says he's surprised when a trained-subject study finds significant growth at all. Untrained subjects are often the better design for demonstrating that a difference exists, and in most cases it extrapolates. This runs against the standard evidence-based-fitness instinct and is worth flagging as a genuine position of his.

**Flag:** this framework is defensible and he applies it consistently. But it also lets him convert a run of non-significant results into a directional conclusion, which is a move that can be made to work in either direction. Two of his five supporting studies are unpublished and not peer reviewed. He labels both, to his credit.

## Should you only train at long lengths?

[Hinson 2025](https://doi.org/10.51224/SRXIV.486), a preprint he says has all data published and no glaring issues, is the strongest design in the video: trained lifters completed two 8-week blocks, one doing 12 sets of seated Bayesian-style incline cable curls, the other 6 sets of those plus 6 sets of shortened-position cable curls.

The authors concluded the difference was not meaningful — so it's fine to do half your volume at short lengths.

Henselmans reads the same data differently and says which side he'd take: **cross-sectional area increased 89% more in the long-length group with an 81% probability of superiority**, arm circumference increased reliably more, and isometric biceps strength — a crude, technique-independent measure that tracks size well — increased marginally more. He weights this further because the long-length group got no exercise variety at all and still won on trend.

His supporting reasoning: the biceps has an unusual length-tension relationship — it is **strongest near anatomical position with the arm extended at the side**, so lengthening it adds passive tension without costing much active tension. Best of both. Shortening it, by contrast, reduces both. And it's a short muscle with most fibres spanning its full length, so regional hypertrophy shouldn't be a large concern and you shouldn't need much variety.

**Practical version:** get your variety within the lengthened category rather than by adding short-length work. Incline curls and preacher curls are variety enough; a Scott curl with elbows high is not needed. Bayesian curls with the elbow behind the body for the biceps; preacher curls for the brachialis, which is what fills the gap between biceps and triceps.

## Flags

- Two of the five studies are unpublished and unreviewed. He labels both clearly, but the MRI study is the one doing the most work in his mechanism argument and it's the one we can't read.
- "89% more cross-sectional area increase" is a relative comparison between two very small absolute changes over eight weeks — a number that sounds much larger than the underlying effect. He does frame it as fine-tuning, but the figure is easy to quote out of context.
- The conclusion that there is "no theoretical or empirical rationale to train the biceps at short lengths" is stronger than the Hinson preprint, whose own authors concluded the difference was not meaningful. He is disagreeing with the study authors on their own data and says so, which is honest, but the reader should know it's his read, not theirs.
- Six studies are discussed; five references are listed. The Iranian study has no corresponding link, so it is not cited below.
