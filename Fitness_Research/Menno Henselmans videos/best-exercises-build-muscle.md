# Six Exercise Head-to-Heads, Each Decided by One Study

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "These are the best exercises to build muscle (science-based)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iEXEyHyBmbU)) · 2025-08-05 · 10 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Skull crushers beat bench press for triceps**, and specifically for the **long head**. Medial and lateral head growth was similar. Pressing under-stimulates the long head because of a biarticular conflict at the shoulder.
- **Front vs back squat: no significant difference** in quad growth over 12 weeks (5.1% vs 4.4%), despite the back-squat group doing more volume (22 vs 19 sets/week). The "upper back is the limiting factor" objection to front squats is not supported.
- **Overhead triceps extensions grew the triceps ~40% more than pushdowns** — and in *all* heads, not only the stretched long head.
- **Stiff-legged deadlifts vs Nordic curls: equal total hamstring growth, different regional pattern.** Nordics favoured semitendinosus, SLDLs favoured semimembranosus. His conclusion: you need both a hip-hinge and a knee-flexion movement.
- **Seated leg curls beat lying leg curls** for the biarticular hamstring heads (hip flexed = hamstrings stretched). Short head of biceps femoris grew equally, as biomechanics predicts.
- **Wide-grip pulldowns vs barbell curls: similar biceps growth.** Underpowered (n=10), but consistent with the broader finding that compounds and isolations grow an agonist about equally.

## Triceps: pressing under-stimulates the long head
[01:02] The long head crosses the shoulder as well as the elbow, so during a press it faces a conflict — contracting at the shoulder pulls the elbow back down, the opposite of what the lift needs. It therefore cannot maximally contract during a bench press. This is a clean biomechanical explanation for a clean result.

[03:04] The overhead extension vs pushdown comparison is more interesting because growth appeared in every head. He argues the resistance curve explains it: overhead extensions load the stretched position, whereas a pushdown puts most of its resistance where the triceps is shortest. So even heads that aren't lengthened by the overhead position still see more tension at long muscle lengths.

## Front squats and the gap between feel and force
[02:04] He reads the front-squat study as leaning slightly in their favour — nominally higher growth on lower volume, and most people get a bit more knee range of motion in a front squat than the equated protocol allowed.

[02:34] The more portable point: many lifters *feel* the core or upper back as the limiting factor in a front squat, and recent work finds **subjective and objective muscle activity barely correlate**. What you feel most is not necessarily what is working most.

## Hamstrings need variety, not a winner
[05:08] The regional-differentiation finding is the strongest practical takeaway in the video. The hamstrings have fibres with different leverages at different lengths, and regional activation within a single movement is well established. He recommends **at least one hip-hinge and one knee-flexion movement in almost every program**.

[05:38] He is explicit about a limit: the ultrasound couldn't separate the short and long heads of biceps femoris, so the expected short-head advantage for Nordics couldn't be tested. He says he is "quite confident based on the biomechanics" that it exists — that is inference, not the study's finding.

[07:09] The seated leg curl result he rates as some of the most compelling evidence for training at long muscle lengths, then immediately notes it is **one study in untrained individuals**. Good self-policing.

## Compounds already train your arms
[08:10] The pulldown vs curl result fits a general pattern: when a muscle is a clear agonist or synergist in a movement, it tends to grow similarly regardless of the specific exercise, provided the muscle lengths trained are comparable. A supinated-grip pulldown study finding similar muscle damage to curls points the same way.

[09:10] His practical conclusion is a coaching one: lifters — men especially — undercount the arm stimulus from their compound work, count only direct isolation volume, and end up with elbow injuries from overtraining biceps and triceps.

## The Gentil aside
[08:40] He interrupts the pulldown discussion to say the study's lead author "does not have the best reputation for integrity as a scientist" and is "known to find things in research that kind of confirm his prior beliefs", and links him to the Barbalho data-fabrication affair via a student. This is a serious public accusation made in passing, and worth logging as such. Note that he then accepts the study's result anyway because it agrees with the wider literature — which is a somewhat awkward pairing.

## Flags
- Every one of the six verdicts rests on **a single study**. He acknowledges the weaknesses (n=10 and 10 weeks for the biceps comparison; untrained subjects for the leg curl study; ultrasound limits for the hamstring study) but the framing as a "test" with right answers presents them more definitively than that.
- The **~40% triceps advantage** for overhead extensions is a single-study effect size, and one that large from one trial should be expected to shrink on replication.
- The stated caption title for the biceps comparison is "wide grip pulldowns"; the supporting damage study he mentions used a **supinated** grip. Different exercises, treated as mutually supporting.
