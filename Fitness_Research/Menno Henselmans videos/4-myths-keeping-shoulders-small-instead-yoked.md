# Four Trap Myths, and How to Actually Train Them

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "4 Myths keeping your shoulders small instead of YOKED" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BEAYmxOrdcw)) · 2024-09-10 · 14 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Traps matter more to the "yoked" look than most lifters think — a position he says he **changed his own mind on**.
- The upper trap fibres run nearly horizontal, so they are poor direct scapular elevators. **Shrug with the shoulders abducted** — wide-grip barbell shrugs, wide cable shrugs between two far-apart pulleys, wide-grip overhead/snatch-grip shrugs.
- **Deadlifts do train the traps**, roughly on par with standard shrugs, because the contraction is a long-length isometric with an eccentric overload component. Count the volume.
- Non-shrugging shoulder work (overhead press, lateral raises) barely trains the upper traps — **count it as zero, or at most 50%**.
- Shrug with the **head slightly flexed forward**, not pulled back. You are stronger there and the muscle is longer. Compressive load goes through the sternoclavicular joint, not the cervical spine.

## Myth 1 — you don't need to train traps
His position is that for most men, direct trap work has a large effect on apparent total shoulder size. The exceptions are enhanced bodybuilders and people whose trap shape already reads as large. He notes you can approximate the look temporarily by simply rolling the traps — an aesthetic observation, not a training claim.

## Myth 2 — barbell and dumbbell shrugs are the best trap exercise
This is the substantive section, and he stacks three lines of evidence.

**Anatomy.** Johnson et al. concluded that the essentially transverse orientation of the upper and middle trapezius fibres "precludes any action as elevators of the scapula as commonly depicted." Most upper trap fibres run near-horizontal, only slightly diagonally upward — a poor line of pull for elevation.

**A training study.** Conley et al. ran a four-times-per-week full-body program for 12 weeks in recreationally trained subjects and found **no significant upper trap hypertrophy** by MRI, despite the program containing deadlifts, mid-thigh pulls, barbell rows, conventional shrugs and overhead presses. Intensity wasn't the problem — the quads grew — and a subgroup adding direct neck extensions did grow the neck (separate take-home: neck size needs neck-specific work).

He then **argues against his own evidence**, which is worth noting: he calls the null result likely a type I error, points out that the traps are clearly involved in shoulder elevation even without a direct elevation moment (they pull medially on the clavicle, lifting the whole shoulder complex — which is why a shrug rotates inward rather than travelling straight up), and notes plenty of people have built big traps on conventional exercises. The study is suggestive, not decisive.

**EMG on abduction.** Two studies looked at shrugs at varying degrees of shoulder abduction, which aligns the near-horizontal upper fibres with the required force. One found **30° beat 0°** (0° being a standard dumbbell or barbell shrug); the other found **90° or even 150° beat 30°**. He flags real limitations: no training to failure, and imperfect EMG normalisation.

His conclusion pools all three: shrug with the shoulders abducted — **wide-grip barbell shrugs** or **cable shrugs between two widely separated pulleys**. Overhead shrugs work, though one study suggested fully vertical ones are not ideal; a wide-grip/snatch-grip version is better. Monkey shrugs qualify but nobody likes them. The movement should feel like the elbows coming inward as the shoulders rise.

He also argues that shoulder work without scapular elevation — overhead presses, lateral raises where you aren't actively shrugging — doesn't meaningfully train the upper traps, citing EMG showing even standard dumbbell shrugs beat lateral raises and rehab exercises substantially. Don't count that volume toward traps, or count it at 50%.

## Myth 3 — deadlifts don't count because the contraction is isometric
Romanian deadlifts were in the Conley program that found nothing, yet he ranks deadlifts roughly equal to shrugs for trap hypertrophy. The general claim that isometrics are ineffective rests on old, sparse research. Newer work on **long-muscle-length isometrics** shows robust hypertrophy, far more than short-length isometrics — and isometric contractions generate very high force, hence high mechanical tension.

In a deadlift the shoulders are dragged down by the load, so the upper traps sit at a relatively long length. For most people it's more than isometric: the scapulae progressively depress through the set, making it an **eccentric overload** at maximal load.

His resulting tier list for upper traps:
- **S tier:** shrugs with shoulders abducted — wide-grip barbell shrugs, wide cable shrugs, wide-grip overhead/snatch-grip shrugs.
- **Average:** standard shrugs, deadlifts.
- **Below that:** most other shoulder exercises, including overhead presses. Upright rows fall somewhere between, depending on whether you actively shrug.
- **Garbage:** everything else.

## Myth 4 — keep the head back while shrugging
The "military posture" cue is probably wrong. Strongmen doing farmer's walks put the head forward because it makes them stronger. The upper trap is biarticular, acting on both the neck and the scapular complex; extending the neck shortens the upper fibres and can push them toward active insufficiency (try shrugging with the head fully back — it feels weak). A slightly flexed neck means more force, more tension, and a longer muscle length.

On safety: because the traps act indirectly via the clavicle rather than producing a direct elevation moment on the scapula, most compressive force is borne by the **sternoclavicular joint**, which is very sturdy — consistent with how rarely people injure their necks shrugging.

**Where this outruns the evidence:** the S-tier recommendation for abducted shrugs is built on two acute EMG studies he himself calls limited, plus biomechanical reasoning and a null hypertrophy trial he argues is underpowered. No training study compares abducted to standard shrugs. The head-forward recommendation is reasoning plus strongman observation with no cited study at all.
