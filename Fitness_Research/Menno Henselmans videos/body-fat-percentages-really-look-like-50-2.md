# What Male Body Fat Percentages Actually Look Like: 50 Lab-Grade Scans

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "What body fat percentages REALLY look like: 50 actual men's DEXA scans" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bZavPkacBhM)) · 2025-08-26 · 9 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Most body-fat percentage image charts online are **stock photos with invented numbers**. This video is his own decade-long collection of client and public photos, each with a laboratory-grade scan attached.
- **Calipers and bioelectrical impedance scales** — especially single-frequency ones — are "hilariously inaccurate" for lifters, and typically **underestimate** body fat in muscular people because they're calibrated on sedentary populations.
- **Muscle mass changes the picture at every level.** More muscle means you can carry more absolute fat at the same percentage — so two men at 15% can look completely different.
- The key visual landmark: **losing all abdominal definition, even flexed, means you're above 15% and probably nearer 20%** — not "almost at 10%" as many lifters assume.
- **His own figure: 10% body fat**, by InBody scan, replicated later. Note this is InBody, not DEXA — the video title overstates the uniformity of the methods.

## The scale, as he presents it

| Body fat | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **2–5%** | Essential body fat for men. Anything claimed below this is almost certainly wrong. |
| **4.1%** | About as lean as it gets. (Wesley Vissers, from his own publicly posted DEXA.) |
| **~6%** | Full Christmas tree, striations, heavy vascularity. Roughly the floor for a natural lifter. |
| **8%** | Shredded, chiselled. |
| **9.6–10.6%** | Visually indistinguishable from each other. Full six-pack, especially flexed, if you're muscular. His stated **target body fat for most people**, and his own level. |
| **11–13%** | Still good ab definition even relaxed, given decent muscle mass. |
| **15–17%** | The point where many muscular men finally lose all abdominal definition. |
| **~19%** | Often the "skinny fat" look. |
| **20%** | Some semblance of ab definition still visible in favourable lighting. |
| **21%** | Commonly the **overweight** threshold for men. |
| **23–24%** | Clearly overweight. |
| **25%+** | Technically **obese**. |
| **27–31%** | Still no huge gut if fat distribution is favourable and there's some muscle — "skinny fat" appears again at 31%. |
| **33–34%** | Substantially obese; visible change from 40% down to 34% is small in photos. |
| **43%** | Morbid obesity in medical terms. |

His blunt corollary at [04:36]: if you're a man with a serious gut and you aren't unusually unlucky with fat distribution, **you are probably obese**, not merely overweight.

## Why the low end requires muscle
[02:03] Sub-8% is essentially only achievable-looking with high muscle mass; without it you look malnourished rather than lean. The same arithmetic runs upward — [03:05] a man at 12.6% with a lot of muscle is carrying more absolute fat than a man at 12.5% with little, because muscle mass sits in the denominator. He frames this as a standing advantage of being muscular.

## Within-individual comparisons
The second half is the more useful part, because it removes the confound of different builds. Examples he shows:
- One client across **6% → 11% → 17%**: glute striations at 6%, full six-pack at 11%, still some definition at 17%.
- **16% → 11% → 8%** with muscle gained along the way, ending up shredded.
- **18% → 8%**: a slight belly at 18% but still some ab definition, because of underlying muscle.
- **10% vs 16%**: full six-pack at 10%, significant definition still at 16%.
- **12% vs 19%**, shot in poor lighting — which he uses to make the point that lighting alone changes apparent leanness dramatically. Changing rooms, he notes, have some of the worst lighting there is.
- **14% → 10%** across four DEXA scans with concurrent muscle gain, showing recomposition as a gradual but real visual shift.

## Caveats he gives
[08:42] Two, both worth keeping. **DEXA itself can be off by a couple of percentage points.** And **fat distribution varies a lot between people** — someone storing fat mainly on the legs can look lean at 15% in an upper-body photo. He concedes that visual estimation is a hard skill that took him years, and that people often come away from these photos more disillusioned than confident.

## Where the framing outruns the data
The title says "50 actual men's DEXA scans," but the transcript describes a mixture of DEXA and InBody results — including his own 10% figure. InBody is bioimpedance, the category of method he spends the opening minute dismissing. He also opens by saying you need a three- or four-compartment lab model for a decent estimate, which DEXA is not. The set is still far better sourced than a stock-photo chart, but the methods are not uniform and the headline implies otherwise.

Several names in the auto-captions are mangled and are not reproduced here where the real person could not be confirmed.
