# Reactive Deloads: Stop Scheduling Recovery You Can't Predict

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This will improve your gains and reduce fatigue" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wG7HgHhhoeA)) · 2025-03-31 · 12 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Deload means reducing load, not taking a week off.** The standard "week off every fourth week" is the wrong unit, the wrong duration, and the wrong scope.
- **A trained lifter doing a habituated workout recovers within 72 hours at the very most**, sometimes within a day. The week-plus recovery times in the literature come from untrained people doing unfamiliar, damaging protocols.
- **Fatigue is local.** Fatigued quads say nothing about your biceps, so deloading everything because one body part is beaten up makes no sense.
- **Scheduled deloading can cost you.** One trial had a group take a week off after four weeks; it reduced strength gains and did nothing for growth, with **8 of 10 effect sizes** favouring the group that never deloaded — despite 20 sets per muscle group to failure with verbal encouragement.
- **The replacement: reactive deloads.** If your first (benchmark) set on an exercise stalls or regresses, skip the remaining sets of *that exercise only* and carry on with the rest of the workout.
- **Speed work instead of skipping:** 60-70% of 1RM, sets of 1-5, explosive. Any rep slowdown means it's too heavy or too many reps.

## Three reasons the calendar deload is wrong
**The lunar cycle doesn't fatigue you.** He calls it "Gregorian calendar bias" — assuming training must run on days, weeks and months because our calendar does. Fatigue does not arrive on a four-week schedule, and he considers monthly deloading very frequent. More importantly, proactive deloading requires predicting the unpredictable: bad sleep, a stressful period, a niggling injury. That argues for auto-regulation on principle.

**A week is far too long.** His recovery timeline is aggressive: trained individuals doing habituated work recover within 72 hours even after extreme sessions, and within a day at moderate loads. The literature suggesting a week or ten days comes from untrained people given unfamiliar high-damage protocols. The repeated bout effect makes trained lifters a different population. He adds an aside worth keeping: people dramatically underestimate recovery capacity, which he thinks is why the very high volume studies keep surprising everyone.

**Fatigue is local.** He treats systemic fatigue and the CNS-fatigue boogeyman as already debunked elsewhere, and asserts neuromuscular fatigue is almost exclusively local — stimulus, recovery and adaptation all regulated at the muscle. So deloading should be body-part specific, and tolerances vary wildly by body part (his own: elbows injure easily, hips essentially never).

Two extra costs of a week off: it **disrupts habits**, which he rates as heavily underrated for long-term success, and it **slows recovery**, because being sedentary reduces blood flow, immune activity and metabolic rate — particularly bad for poorly vascularised connective tissue.

The direct evidence he gives is a single trial he attributes to **Coleman et al.** — one group took a week off in the middle of nine weeks, the other trained through, and the deload group came out worse on strength with no growth benefit. The name is from the audio only; no matching study appears in the description's reference list, so it is left unlinked here.

## How reactive deloading works
The simplest implementation, which is all he teaches in this video:

1. Pick a **benchmark set** — the first working set of an exercise.
2. If you plateau or regress on it (you did 100 kg x 6 last time and wanted 7, but got 4), that is evidence you are still in the recovery phase of the stimulus-recovery-adaptation curve rather than the adaptation phase.
3. **Skip the remaining sets of that exercise**, or replace them with speed work.
4. **Keep doing the rest of the workout as planned** — unrecovered quads don't stop you doing chin-ups or overhead presses.

He attaches an important precondition: this inference only holds if your volume is on the high end. On a low-volume program the same stall could mean undertraining, in which case backing off is exactly wrong.

**Speed work** is his preferred substitute for simply stopping, especially for strength-focused lifters: 60-70% of 1RM for sets of one to five, moved explosively. The weight should feel like a real work weight, not trivial, but the volume should be low enough to accumulate no fatigue at all — any rep slowdown means you've gone too heavy or too long. The claimed payoff is neural adaptation for rate of force development and technique practice, plus active recovery, at negligible fatigue cost.

**Where not to use it.** Reactive deloads need a reproducible benchmark. Squat, bench and deadlift qualify — you know whether you got the rep. A cable lateral raise doesn't: different pulleys, different friction, standing a step further from the stack, and performance moves for reasons unrelated to recovery. Apply it to barbell and dumbbell compounds; on inherently variable exercises you'd deload half your workouts for no reason.

**Overreach to note:** three claims outrun their citations here. The 72-hour recovery ceiling is stated as a general rule but rests on a small set of studies in trained men on specific lifts. "Fatigue is by and large almost exclusively local" is asserted with reference to his own prior videos rather than to evidence in this one. And the central deload trial is named but not linked, so the video's strongest empirical claim cannot be checked from the description.
