# Electrolyte Supplements Are Just Salt, and Most People Don't Need More Salt

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "I Read Every Electrolyte Study. The Industry Is Lying." ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-yypmdC5Ykc)) · 2026-04-28 · 10 min
**Topic:** Supplements

## Bottom line
- **Electrolyte supplements are multi-mineral supplements that are mostly sodium** — biologically close to eating extra table salt.
- The 2025 review of sodium for athletes concluded that **most manufacturer claims are clearly not evidence-based**, and there is **no evidence athletes need daily sodium supplementation** ([McCubbin 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s44410-025-00011-9)).
- Sodium supplementation starts to help at **4+ hours of exercise, especially in heat** — ultramarathons, Ironmans. Not lifting.
- Typical intake is **3–5 g sodium/day** across populations; official recommendations are **2–2.4 g**. Exercise costs roughly **1 g per hour**. Most people already have the buffer covered.
- One **LMNT packet is ~1 g sodium** — about 50% of the recommended daily maximum on top of an already-excessive intake.
- A 2020 dose-response meta-analysis: **each extra 1 g/day of sodium ≈ 6% higher cardiovascular disease risk** ([Wang 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu12102934)).
- He discloses turning down a **lucrative affiliate deal** from a major electrolyte producer, and that friends in the industry make money from these products.

## What he says is unsupported
Worth being precise, because the video's title is broader than its argument. The specific claims he says lack evidence:

1. **That athletes need daily sodium supplementation.** This is the review's own conclusion, not his inference — McCubbin's review states most supplement-manufacturer claims are not evidence-based and there's no evidence for routine daily sodium supplementation in athletes.
2. **That electrolyte supplements improve performance for normal trainees.** The review's decision tree puts the benefit specifically at **4+ hours of exercise**, usually in heat, at which point supplementing sodium alongside water becomes beneficial or potentially beneficial. Below that threshold, nothing.
3. **That hyponatremia is a sodium-intake problem.** He calls this ironic and it's the strongest point in the video: hyponatremia is real and has serious consequences, but it is **mostly caused by excessive water intake**, not insufficient sodium. The industry's fear-marketing about low sodium drives the behaviour (drinking huge volumes of water) that actually produces the condition.

He does *not* claim electrolytes never work. He names three legitimate use cases up front and repeats them at the end: **ultra-endurance athletes (4+ hours), ketogenic diets, and medical conditions predisposing to hyponatremia.**

## The arithmetic
**Losses.** Roughly 1 g of sodium per hour of exercise. Specifics: **0.7 g** over a 40-minute indoor strength session; **1.4 g** over 75 minutes of cardio in the sun; **1.2–2.7 g** for an outdoor tennis or soccer match. He flags the wide range honestly — sweat rate and sweat sodium concentration vary enormously, which is why professional endurance athletes quantify their own requirements.

**Intake.** Most people, athletes included, consume **3–5 g/day** — remarkably consistent across populations and cultures, set by taste rather than need. Official recommendations are **2–2.4 g**. So even at the extreme end of losses, a conventional intake almost certainly covers requirement plus exercise buffer.

**Evolutionary context.** Hunter-gatherer sodium intake is estimated below 1 g/day, and many contemporary hunter-gatherer populations still live below that. Getting large amounts of sodium from unsalted food is very difficult; table salt is what raised intakes dramatically.

## The cardiovascular case
Sodium is highly osmotic, so more sodium in blood draws in water, raising blood volume and therefore pressure through a fixed circulatory system — his garden-hose analogy. The body upregulates excretion, but that capacity is limited, so higher intakes proportionally raise blood pressure. Hypertension is the "silent killer": no symptoms, elevated risk of stroke, vessel damage and essentially all cardiovascular complications.

Where he is careful: he **concedes the J-curve** — the observational finding that very low intakes also carry risk — then gives two reasons to distrust it.

- **Spot urine analysis.** Most J-curve research estimated daily sodium from a single urine sample. Errors of up to **85%** have been found versus more accurate methods.
- **Reverse causality.** People on very low sodium with high cardiovascular risk are often low-sodium *because* they were diagnosed and told to cut salt. The arrow points the other way.

Research using detailed urine collection and controlling for potassium finds the monotonic relationship: more sodium, more risk. Hence the 6%-per-gram figure.

He also volunteers, against his own position, that **many researchers including himself think the 2–2.4 g recommendations are unrealistically low** — but argues that isn't a licence to add another gram.

## Where his rhetoric outruns his evidence
Worth flagging, because this is a strongly-worded video:

- **"The industry is lying"** is not what the cited review says. McCubbin says manufacturer claims are not evidence-based, which is a claim about evidence, not intent.
- **"A significant health risk"** and **"all risk, zero reward"** are considerably stronger than a 6% relative CVD risk increase per gram of sodium, derived from observational meta-analysis. That is a real signal, but it is population-level relative risk on a common exposure, and he presents no trial evidence that adding one packet a day to a healthy lifter's diet produces measurable harm.
- The **~1 g per hour** loss figure and the **3–5 g** intake figure are both averages spanning wide individual variation — variation he explicitly acknowledges when arguing that pro endurance athletes need individual testing, then sets aside when arguing everyone else is covered.
- He concedes the official sodium ceiling is probably too low, which weakens the "one packet is 50% of your maximum" framing he uses two sentences earlier.

None of that overturns the central point, which is well supported: for a lifter training an hour a day, electrolyte supplementation has no demonstrated performance benefit, and the sodium it adds sits on top of an intake already above recommendations.
