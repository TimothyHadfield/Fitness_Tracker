# Bench Press Grip Width: What It Actually Changes

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "How does your grip width during the bench press affect which muscles you train?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xd0JSkmM__U)) · 2022-12-03 · 15 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- The conventional "close grip = triceps, wide grip = pecs" rule is **half wrong**. Triceps demand is roughly **constant across grip widths**.
- The reason is **horizontal force production**. A wide grip generates around **40%** of total force horizontally (medium ~20%, narrow ~0%), and because the bar can't move sideways that force converts into upward force — so the triceps stay loaded even without an elbow-flexion moment to resist.
- Elbow net joint moments are similar at all grip widths; **shoulder** moments are clearly lower with narrow grips, especially elbows tucked.
- The pec half of the conventional wisdom holds, but **only for the lower (abdominal-head) pecs**. Upper and mid pec activity are essentially grip-width-independent.
- Henselmans' broader position: **the bench press is a poor triceps exercise regardless of grip**, and especially poor for the long head.

## The biomechanical problem the study solves
Simple moment-arm analysis says the triceps should be idle or worse during a wide-grip bench press. If the hand sits directly over the elbow there is no elbow-flexion moment to resist; if the hand sits *outside* the elbow the moment reverses, which on paper would make it a biceps exercise. That is obviously not what happens.

The resolution is that the bar is a fixed object. Pushing outward on it — the powerlifting "spread the bar apart" cue — is a triceps-driven lateral force that cannot move the bar sideways, so it resolves into upward force. Formally, horizontal and vertical force combine into a diagonal resultant vector, changing the moment arms at both elbow and shoulder. Previous analyses assumed the resultant force pointed straight down, which is why they got this wrong.

## What the study measured
Four conditions: medium grip, wide grip, narrow grip with elbows tucked, and narrow grip with elbows flared. Henselmans singles out the tucked/flared distinction as a genuine strength — most "close grip bench" studies don't specify elbow position, and the difference is substantial.

**Force data.** Horizontal-to-vertical force ratio was near zero for both narrow grips, ~20% at medium, ~40% at wide. Elbow net joint moments came out similar across all four conditions. Shoulder moments were noticeably lower with the narrow grips, lowest with elbows tucked. Relative to force-production capacity, the triceps sat at roughly **60% capacity in every condition**.

He flags a methodological caveat himself: the study normalised against a standing/seated force-capacity measurement, which changes joint angle, length-tension relationship and internal moment arms. He assumes the bias is equal across conditions, which is reasonable but not demonstrated.

**EMG data.** Triceps lateral head showed no major difference except a drop in the wide-grip condition — a partial disagreement with the kinetic data — and was substantially more active than the long head throughout. Deltoid activity was similar across conditions (he warns against reading much into the %MVIC values here for technical reasons).

The pec result is the interesting one because the study split the pecs three ways:
- **Clavicular (upper) pecs** — similar at all grip widths. There was a slight decrease with the wide grip, which he dismisses as unlikely to replicate.
- **Sternocostal (mid) pecs** — similar, even more so than the upper.
- **Abdominal (lower) pecs** — **substantially less active with narrow grips.**

## Why the lower pecs are the exception
Both regions contribute to horizontal shoulder flexion (bringing the elbow toward the midline). But the clavicular head is *also* a shoulder flexor — it pulls the elbow up as well as in — so it stays highly active regardless of grip. The abdominal head has the opposite line of pull, and can even assist a straight-arm pulldown; it needs horizontal shoulder flexion specifically. Tuck the elbows or narrow the grip and that demand largely disappears, taking the lower pecs with it.

## Range of motion, as a modifier
EMG and joint moments describe an instant; hypertrophy also depends on range of motion. A close grip closes the elbow angle further, giving more triceps length change and potentially some stretch-mediated hypertrophy. A wide grip probably gives a better pec stretch at the bottom, assuming the elbows travel all the way down. Both nudge in the conventional direction; he expects neither to be large.

## Practical summary
- **Anterior delts** are highly active at any grip width. (Only the anterior head is meaningfully trained by any bench press.)
- **Triceps** are near-constant across grips. Only a very wide grip — hands outside the elbows — reduces activity, mostly in the lateral head, and even that is largely offset by horizontal force production.
- **Long head of the triceps** is never well trained by benching. It's biarticular and also assists shoulder extension, so high long-head activity would work against pressing the elbow up. He cites his own research overview showing pecs grow considerably more than triceps from benching, plus a study he attributes to a researcher the captions render as "Brandow" — **not verifiable, since the description lists no references; treat the attribution as uncertain.**
- **Pecs:** wide grip trains all heads well; narrowing the grip costs the lower pecs specifically, not the upper.

The whole video rests on one study, and the conclusions are stated more confidently than a single kinetic/EMG paper warrants — particularly the near-constant triceps finding, which the study's own EMG data partly contradicts.
