# Protein Requirements Do Not Rise in a Deficit

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Understanding this makes fat loss easier" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=__hRCUDVJx0)) · 2024-07-11 · 11 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **He is directly attacking an evidence-based-fitness consensus position.** "You need more protein when cutting" is treated as settled; he says there is **no direct research supporting it** and several lines refuting it.
- **1.6 g/kg/day is the optimum whether you're cutting, maintaining or bulking.** That is the breakpoint from the meta-analysis he co-authored ([Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608)) and it does not shift in a deficit.
- His recommendation: **at least 1.6 g/kg/day, and 1.8 g/kg/day to be safe** (0.73 and 0.82 g/lb), with **at least 50% from high-quality animal sources**, spread over **at least three meals**.
- The theory fails on its own logic: **muscle protein breakdown barely changes in a deficit** — it's a remodelling function, not a discretionary one — while **protein synthesis falls a lot**. Less synthesis plus roughly flat breakdown means *lower* total protein demand, not higher.
- Practical kicker: **protein quality goes up when you cut**, because a smaller share of intake comes from grains, which would offset any theoretical increase anyway.

## Where the belief came from

He traces it to Eric Helms' 2014 systematic review ([Helms 2014](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2013-0054)), part of his PhD, which found six studies and observed that the higher-protein ones seemed to do better. He is fair about it: **no formal statistical analysis**, no direct study, no indirect evidence of benefit beyond what's seen at maintenance — and that was fine, because the review introduced Helms' actual RCT.

That RCT ([Helms 2015](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2014-0056)) compared **1.6 vs 2.8 g/kg/day** in weightlifters at a **40% energy deficit** and found **no difference in body composition or strength**. Its real limitation is that it ran only **two weeks**.

His complaint about the field: people discounted the direct null on duration grounds and kept the theory anyway. The hypothesis survived on plausibility rather than data.

## Why the mechanism doesn't hold

The theory says a deficit raises protein breakdown, so you eat more protein to offset it. Two rebuttals.

**Breakdown barely moves.** In most research it's unchanged in a deficit, rising only at very large deficits you shouldn't be running. The reason is functional — breakdown is mostly **remodelling and repair of damaged proteins**, not the body discarding tissue. His analogy: adding a balcony to a house means knocking out a wall first. That function exists identically at maintenance and in surplus. Where a very large deficit does drive excess breakdown, that's the deficit's doing and it will cost you muscle regardless of protein intake.

**Synthesis moves a lot.** Because breakdown is essential and near-static, the body regulates the other side: **synthesis is the variable one**, swinging hugely with feeding and energy status, and it drops significantly in a deficit.

So a large synthesis decrease plus at most a small breakdown increase means **total protein demand falls**.

## The data in untrained people

- Raising protein above the level that maximises body composition at maintenance improves nothing once you enter a deficit; studies comparing intakes at maintenance and in deficit find the same effects in both.
- Even at severe restriction, more protein doesn't protect against the muscle loss the deficit causes. Deficit and protein intake act **independently**. Same pattern in bed rest and hospitalisation studies of rapid muscle loss.
- [Hudson 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/advances/nmz106) confirms it: no significant effect of protein above the RDA (0.8 g/kg/day) on lean mass, **with or without energy restriction**. Not enough data to run the same analysis in trained people.

## The triple interaction argument

The sharpest reasoning in the video, and it's methodological rather than physiological. If you claim requirements rise in a deficit *but only in trained lifters*, you are asserting a **triple interaction effect**: protein affects growth, that effect differs by training status, and it differs again only in trained people only in a deficit. Triple interactions are very rare biologically. He concedes it's possible and even plausible here rather than dismissing it.

## The data in trained people

**Per-meal dose response:**
- [Larsen 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tjnut.2023.08.011) gave 15 g, 35 g and 60 g of whey post-workout to the same women at maintenance and in a deficit. **No difference in the dose response.**
- [Gwin 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2020.07.019): mixed muscle protein synthesis is optimally stimulated by **8-10 g essential amino acids (20-30 g high-quality protein)** in young subjects "regardless of whether an individual is in a state of energy balance or moderate energy deficit."

He notes we lack equivalent studies on a **total daily** basis.

**Total daily intake:**
- [Walberg 1988](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2007-1025018): cutting weightlifters held **positive nitrogen balance at 1.6 g/kg/day**.
- [Antonio 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-11-19): 1.9 vs **4.4 g/kg/day** in strength trainees losing fat, 8 weeks. No difference. [Antonio 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-015-0100-0): 2.3 vs 3.4 g/kg/day. No difference.
- [Pasiakos 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2013.197): military personnel, 1.6 vs 2.4 g/kg/day. No difference in body composition, nitrogen balance or resting metabolic rate. He flags the strength training was light, though overall activity was high.
- Four studies in middle-aged adults training ~3x/week find **1.2 g/kg/day** already sufficient — best of them [Campbell 2015](https://doi.org/10.3945/jn.115.213595), 117 subjects over nine months, no benefit above 1.1-1.2 g/kg/day. He says plainly he **doesn't recommend intakes that low** to his own clients. Two further studies in military personnel and female athletes show similar intakes ward off muscle loss over one- to two-week diets.

## The practical argument nobody makes

Bulking on 4,000 calories, a substantial share of protein comes from grains and other lower-quality sources. Cutting, a **larger proportion comes from chicken breast, white fish, egg whites**. Average protein quality rises when you cut, which would offset a modest requirement increase even if one existed.

## Where the note has to push back

The evidence is genuinely one-sided in his favour on the *narrow* claim — no study shows a deficit-specific requirement increase. But most of it is null results in short or small studies, and he concedes the key gap himself: **no total-daily-intake study in trained lifters in a sustained deficit**. Absence of a demonstrated effect is not demonstrated absence, and the triple interaction he raises is exactly the scenario his data can't rule out.

He also frames the Helms review as the origin of the belief while acknowledging it was never presented as conclusive — the field's overreading of it isn't Helms' error.
