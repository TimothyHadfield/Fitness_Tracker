# Lean Back On The Leg Extension To Actually Train The Rectus Femoris

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "A simple trick to get bigger quads" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d5rzL-J1zA8)) · 2023-07-08 · 13 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Set the leg extension seat back so you are reclined roughly **40-45 degrees**, not sitting upright. Upright (80-90 degrees) is the worst of the tested positions for the rectus femoris.
- The reason is **active insufficiency**: the rectus femoris crosses both hip and knee, so flexing the hip already shortens it, and it cannot then generate much tension at the knee.
- 1RM was **highest at 40 degrees**, lower both fully upright and fully lying flat — and here strength is a reasonable proxy for how much muscle the position lets you involve.
- Second, larger take-home: **trained bodybuilders were poor at feeling which part of a muscle was working.** Group-average impressions roughly tracked the objective data; individual judgements were scattered.
- Do not use the pump, the stretch or the "feel" as your read on activation. Use biomechanics.

## Why the upright position wastes the rectus femoris
[00:31] Menno's long-running complaint is that lifters treat "military posture" as automatically good technique — upright on the overhead press, upright on the leg extension. On the leg extension it is actively counterproductive. The rectus femoris is biarticulate: it crosses the hip and the knee. Sitting at 90 degrees flexes the hip, which shortens the muscle before you have done anything. Ask it to shorten at the knee as well and the actin-myosin overlap is so bunched that it cannot form effective cross-bridges.

[01:33] You can test this on yourself: with the hip fully flexed you cannot fully straighten the leg. That is active insufficiency in its extreme form. Short of that extreme, the muscle can still move the weight but cannot produce much tension — and since mechanical tension is the growth stimulus, that is a wasted rectus femoris. (The description adds a correction Menno did not say on camera: passive insufficiency of the hamstrings also contributes to the leg-straightening limitation.)

## The study
[02:34] Japanese researchers tested **bodybuilders**, not untrained subjects, on three leg extension positions across three visits: fully reclined (0 degrees, lying), leaning back (40 degrees), and near-upright (80 degrees). Activation was measured by **MRI T2 shift** rather than EMG — exercise changes intramuscular fluid balance, and the scanner picks that up, which handles deep muscle far better than surface EMG.

[04:41] Findings: 1RM was higher leaning back than upright, but slightly lower fully reclined, so 40 degrees won. T2 activation followed the same shape — the 40-degree condition beat both 80 and 0 degrees across proximal, middle and distal rectus femoris. Menno's guess for why fully flat loses: the rectus femoris may be over-stretched, and you have nothing to brace against, so it is hard to pull yourself into the machine.

[08:15] A detail the authors underplayed: vastus lateralis and vastus medialis showed a non-significant trend of *increasing* activation the more upright you sat. His reading is that upright doesn't recruit the vasti harder, it just **isolates** them, because the rectus femoris has dropped out.

## Feel is a bad instrument
[06:13] The second half asked the bodybuilders to rate how strongly each region contracted, then compared that to the T2 data. For the proximal rectus femoris the subjective ratings matched significantly. For the middle portion the trend matched but missed significance, with individual data points scattered. For the distal portion they could not detect a real difference that existed.

[09:16] Menno's interpretation is that lifters **confuse isolation with activation** — an upright leg extension "feels" more like a vastus exercise because less else is contributing, and people generalise this into believing isolation exercises are inherently better for growth. He notes this is not what the training literature shows. Activating the biceps hard in a chin-up does not mean the lats are activating less; activation is not a zero-sum split.

## Where the claim runs ahead of the evidence
This is one acute study, on a small group, measuring an activation proxy rather than muscle growth — no hypertrophy was measured, and no long-term outcome. Menno is careful about the mechanism but presents the 45-degree prescription with more certainty than a single acute study supports. His claim that the optimum "seems to be between 45 and 0 degrees" also goes past the data, which tested only 0, 40 and 80 and found 40 best.
