# Protein for Max Gains: Three 2025 Studies on Cutting, Plant Protein and Satiety

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "How much protein for max gains? 3 New studies [2025]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yHLW04lDY64)) · 2025-05-15 · 14 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- A new RCT in strength-trained athletes cutting at a ~12% deficit compared **1.3, 1.7 and 2.3 g/kg/day** (0.6, 0.7, 1.0 g/lb) and found **no significant difference** in body composition, strength or performance. Effect size for the fat-free-mass difference was η² = 0.00002 — no trend at all.
- His recommendation is unchanged from his November 2024 video: **1.8 g/kg (0.8 g/lb) of total protein on total body weight**, a floor he would not go below "in almost any scenario".
- He **declines to follow the ACSM's 1.2–1.7 g/kg** even though the study authors endorse it, on the grounds that it isn't aimed at lifters maximising hypertrophy — while conceding the ACSM is not obviously wrong about the size of the effect.
- A meta-analysis of 12 studies found **animal protein stimulates slightly more muscle protein synthesis than plant protein**. On a plant-based diet, add roughly **20%**, or use an 80/20 pea-rice blend, in which case 1 g/lb is ample.
- Muscle, thermic effect and satiety all show **sharp diminishing returns above ~1.8 g/kg**. Doubling protein buys roughly **70 kcal/day** of extra expenditure in normal conditions.

## The cutting study, and what it settles
Canadian researchers gave strength-trained athletes from mixed sporting backgrounds an identical training programme and matched energy intakes, varying only protein. Body composition came from DEXA, cross-checked against protein mass and resting energy expenditure; performance from chest press, leg press, leg curl, chin-ups and broad jump. Nothing separated the groups.

He is careful about what this does and doesn't show. Six weeks and 21 completers cannot detect the small effects plausibly there. His reading is that protein's effect on fat-free mass is **gradual**, not a cliff — results do improve as intake rises, but increments become tiny approaching ~1.8 g/kg, so nulls in tightly controlled trials are expected. Recent meta-analyses trend toward higher intakes; he discounts them for not controlling energy intake or nutrient timing.

The secondary point matters for this library: it is further evidence **against needing more protein while cutting**. He states flatly that the bodybuilding belief in elevated protein in a deficit "is not based on any solid evidence."

## Plant versus animal protein
Two mechanisms: lower digestibility, and an amino acid profile less similar to human tissue, so a nominally adequate total intake can still under-deliver specific essential amino acids. He notes this would have been uncontroversial a decade ago and only became contested with the rise of vegan dieting — a characteristic swipe at where he thinks consensus has bent to fashion.

The caveat is his own: the effect was small and dragged down by one study whose vegan supplement supplied more protein *and* far more calories. Excluding that, the direction is near-unanimous but the effect sizes stay small. A separate controlled study found animal protein produced a higher thermic effect of food and resting energy expenditure — consistent with the MPS difference, too small to move fat loss.

## Thermic effect and satiety: real, and smaller than advertised
The third study fed healthy young adults ultra-processed diets at 30% versus 13% of calories from protein for 54 hours in a lab. The high-protein group burned **128 kcal/day more** while eating **196 kcal less** — a ~300 kcal swing. He deflates it immediately: the contrast was 3.3 vs 1.5 g/kg at *massive* overfeeding (32% surplus in the low group, 18% in the high), which he calls "dreamer bulk versus ultra dreamer bulk." Under normal whole-food conditions a meta-analysis finds the expenditure difference usually non-significant — roughly 70 kcal from doubling protein, not worth chasing given the cost.

Satiety behaves the same way. Above the intake needed for maximal muscle protein synthesis the advantage becomes trivial; in the new study the high-protein group reported **higher subjective appetite** despite eating less, and his own 1.8 vs 2.7 g/kg trial found no satiety difference. He explains this via protein leverage: appetite drives you to eat until protein needs are met, after which protein is just food. Lean protein also doesn't reliably beat high-fibre foods, vegetables or potatoes per calorie — in most research it does worse.

## What moved since his November 2024 note
**The headline number did not move.** Both videos land on 1.8 g/kg (0.8 g/lb) as the practical recommendation, with ~1.6 g/kg as the underlying evidence-based plateau. What changed:

- **The direction of the fight reversed.** In November 2024 he defended 1.6–1.8 g/kg against a meta-analysis claiming benefits to 3.1 g/kg. Here he defends the same figure against the ACSM's *lower* 1.2–1.7 g/kg — and is unusually explicit that his number is a risk/reward compromise, since the effects below it are genuinely marginal.
- **The cutting position hardened.** In 2024 he said there was "no strong support" for more protein in a deficit and flagged newer research he might cover. This video supplies it, and he now calls the bodybuilding belief unfounded. Still against our library's 1.8–2.7 g/kg cutting figure.
- **The vegan exception became a number:** a passing caveat in 2024, now a concrete **+20%** or a pea/rice blend, with 1 g/lb sufficient in that case.
- He also now names **1.2 g/kg** as his recommendation for *sedentary* people — a floor the earlier video didn't state.

## Overreach to note
The 20% plant-protein adjustment is a rule of thumb, not a figure the cited meta-analysis produced. And the null result from a six-week, 21-participant trial does more work in his conclusion on cutting than in his caveats, where he correctly says such a study cannot detect the effects he elsewhere maintains are real.
