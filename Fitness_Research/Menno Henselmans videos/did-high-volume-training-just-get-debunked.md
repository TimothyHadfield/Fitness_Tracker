# Two New Volume Studies, and Why He Says They Don't Debunk High Volume

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Did high-volume training just get debunked? [2 New studies]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3-yluleePK4)) · 2026-05-12 · 11 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **His position: volume still wins, but the margin is small.** In an 87-person, 12-week trial of **4.1 vs 18 fractional sets per muscle per week**, growth was statistically equivalent — but he back-calculated the absolute means and found roughly **15% faster growth** favouring high volume.
- 15% extra growth for **4x the training volume** is a terrible trade if you are already progressing. It is a decisive difference if you are not — the difference between gaining and not gaining, which compounds.
- He **rejects the second study's headline** that fatigue rather than volume drives growth. His counterexamples: training to failure massively raises fatigue for almost no extra growth; short rest intervals raise fatigue while *reducing* growth.
- He still treats the [Pelland 2025 meta-regression](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w) as the best current answer: **more growth at 18 than 4 sets/week, with strong diminishing returns past about 10.**
- Prescribe volume from **recovery capacity and preference**, not from a universal number — training status, sleep, stress, nutrition.

## Study 1: 18 vs 4 sets per week, to true failure

87 strength-trained subjects, 12 weeks, three sessions a week, 7-10 rep range with reverse pyramids, taken to **true momentary failure** — Yates/Mentzer style, from a research group known for actually getting subjects to fail a rep. Effectively HIT versus science-based high-volume training.

He opens with a **volume-counting correction** that matters for reading any of this literature. Other creators reported this as 12 vs 36 sets per week; that is the total across the *arms* and the *thighs*, not per muscle. Counted properly — per muscle, summing fractional contributions, with indirect work like rows and presses credited 50% to the arms — it is **4.1 vs 18 sets per muscle per week**. He notes the 50% rule is crude but defensible here.

Result: no statistically significant difference in cross-sectional area growth.

His limitations, which he takes seriously:
- **Measurement.** Skinfold calipers and circumference tape, not MRI, and collected across **22 different gyms** by at least 22 different assessors. Large error, only partly recoverable by statistics.
- **22% dropout**, high for a pure training intervention.
- **Still a preprint**, unreviewed — though he expects it to hold given the team.
- **Trained subjects are a liability here, not an asset.** Trained lifters don't grow much in 12 weeks, so you end up comparing one tiny effect to another. The authors' own power analysis concluded most exercise science studies are "woefully underpowered" to detect small differences in hypertrophy.

Then the part worth keeping: effect sizes for both arms and thighs **favoured the high-volume group**, and his own back-calculation of absolute means puts it at about **15% more growth**. He is careful that this depends on how missing data and skinfold conversions are handled.

## Study 2: fatigue-matched squat volumes

Strength-trained men squatting at least 1.4x bodyweight, randomised to low, moderate or high volume on a squat specialisation program, **matched for total fatigue** by having the higher-volume groups take progressively longer rests between individual reps. He calls the design "kind of Elon Musk vibes where you're not sure if genius or just weird" — in practice every group performed one extended cluster set: a rep, 10+ seconds rest, a rep.

After 8 weeks: no significant differences in estimated squat 1RM, muscle volume or cross-sectional area. **All groups grew 7-10%**, across a 6.5-fold difference in volume.

Two readings:

1. **The bigger surprise is that the low-volume group grew at all.** Roughly two submaximal sets per week, most of which likely never came within five reps of failure. That makes the loose version of the **effective reps model** — only the last five reps count — most likely false.
2. **The fatigue conclusion is a reach.** By serious-lifter standards none of these groups experienced much fatigue in the first place, so the study cannot support "fatigue drives growth."

## Why he thinks fatigue is a byproduct

A dissociation argument, and a clean one: there are well-documented ways to raise fatigue without raising growth.

- **Training to failure** adds essentially nothing to hypertrophy — that last partial rep is trivial extra volume — while substantially raising fatigue markers and delaying recovery.
- **Short rest intervals** *increase* fatigue while *reducing* growth and strength, because you get less total volume from the session.

Mechanistic work also points to mechanical tension, time under tension and total tension as the drivers, with fatigue a side effect. So his conclusion inverts the study's: volume matters much more than fatigue, and the useful concept is **stimulus-to-fatigue ratio** — what goes wrong in high-volume programs is junk volume, sets that buy fatigue without stimulus.

## How he prescribes volume

- Set total volume from **recovery capacity and preference**, not a universal target. Recovery capacity is driven mostly by **training status** — advanced lifters tolerate more — plus sleep, stress and nutrition.
- **Decision rule:** making good gains on low volume and happy? 15% more growth isn't worth 4x the work. Look at last year's photos and see no change? Volume is one of the levers.

## Where the note has to push back

The 15% figure is **his own back-calculation from a preprint's absolute means**, on skinfold-and-tape data collected across 22 sites, in a study that found no significant difference. He is transparent about all of that, but the number will get quoted without those qualifiers, and it should not be treated as a study finding. It is a plausibility estimate.

He is also arguing that a null result still favours his prior — the effect sizes trended his way — which is exactly the mechanism-over-data move he criticised himself for in his body-fat self-correction. Here it is defensible because the meta-regression backs him, but it's the same shape of reasoning.
