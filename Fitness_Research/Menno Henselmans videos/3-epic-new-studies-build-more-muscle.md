# Three New Studies: Myo-Reps, 30 Sets In A Deficit, And How Close To Failure

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "3 Epic New Studies to Build More Muscle [2026]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jfUXXKTMqNw)) · 2026-06-23 · 13 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Myo-reps matched traditional straight sets for chest growth and bench 1RM with **29% fewer reps in 62% less time** — but the study had 22 men over 8 weeks and never ran an equivalence test, so "no difference found" is not "identical."
- Do myo-reps as Børge Fagerli intended: **activation set not to failure**, then mini-sets. Going to failure on the activation set turns them into rest-pause and burns work capacity.
- **30 sets/week/muscle in a 40% energy deficit** for 4 weeks beat 12 fractional sets on strength (15-27% vs 7-14% gains); lean mass was unchanged in both, but 4 weeks is far too short to read body composition.
- In a 158-man bench specialisation study, **higher intensities won for strength**, and proximity to failure showed an **inverted-U with an optimum around 30% velocity loss** — going to failure did not help strength and trended worse. For hypertrophy, closer to failure was straightforwardly better.
- Practical simplification: for most people, just do as many reps as possible. RIR and RPE tracking is over-complication unless you are a powerlifter.

## Study 1: myo-reps vs straight sets
[00:00] Strength-trained men benched, incline benched and did cable flyes twice a week for 8 weeks. Traditional group: 3 sets of 6-12 to failure, 2-minute rests. Myo-reps group: same first set to failure, then 40 seconds rest, then mini-sets of 2-3 reps with 20-second rests up to five sets. No significant differences in pec thickness, bench 1RM, RPE or perceived recovery — with 29% lower volume load and 62% less time.

[02:03] Menno's methodological objection is the sharpest content in the video. Failure to reject the null is not equivalence; there is a formal equivalence test and the authors did not run one. He is scathing about their claimed 82% power, which came from a **post-hoc power analysis** that conveniently landed on exactly their sample size of 22. With 11 per group over 8 weeks, a null result is what you would expect regardless of the truth.

[03:33] His considered position: the wider rest-redistribution literature suggests you have to **equate total rep volume or tonnage** to get equal gains. So myo-reps are a strong time-efficiency win, but he doubts you get 100% of the gains from 70% of the volume.

## Study 2: 30 sets a week in a 40% deficit
[05:35] Trained lifters did 30 sets/week/muscle in a 40% energy deficit against a control group prescribed 12 fractional sets, for four weeks only. Both groups held lean mass and lost fat, and nitrogen balance did not differ; he discounts the body composition results as uninformative over four weeks in trained lifters. The signal is strength: the high-volume group gained 15-27% on presses, rows and leg presses versus 7-14%, with three of four comparisons significant.

[07:07] Context he adds himself: the study is **still in peer review**; rests were only 90 seconds at 1-2 RIR, so training closer to failure with longer rests would need fewer sets for the same result; average body fat was in the 20s, so leaner lifters would tolerate a 40% deficit far worse. And the real constraint on high volume is usually connective tissue, not muscle — "many people that try something like in this study would get injured when they try this for more than 4 weeks."

[08:08] His broader claim: with one exception, every training-volume study so far has found higher volume produces significantly greater or equal results. That is a strong statement stated without a citation.

## Study 3: intensity and proximity to failure
[08:39] 158 strength-trained men split across **12 groups** — average intensities of 48%, 63% and 78% crossed with velocity losses of 0%, 15%, 25% and 50% — on a bench press specialisation programme. Menno's complaint is that researchers with that many subjects should have done one thing well; twelve arms guarantees most comparisons miss significance, so he reads trends only.

Trends: higher intensity was better for strength and power. Proximity to failure produced an inverted U, with roughly **30% velocity loss** giving the biggest 1RM increases and closer-to-failure trending worse. His explanation is lost work capacity, degraded technique, and no additional neural adaptation from the grindy last rep. For hypertrophy the pattern reversed — closer to failure meant more growth, which he attributes not to failure per se but to it delivering **more volume**, i.e. more time under tension at the same load.

[11:11] He then generalises to a recommendation he clearly likes: for most people, "simply perform as many reps as possible," because nobody estimates RIR accurately, it introduces tracking noise, and it maximises reps per set. This is a real departure from the mainstream evidence-based emphasis on RIR-based autoregulation.

## Bonus: swearing
[12:12] Three high-quality studies found swearing improved push-up performance, apparently mediated by state disinhibition.

## Where the claim runs ahead of the evidence
Menno flags the statistical weakness of studies 1 and 3 himself and does it well. The unflagged overreach is his own: "with one exception, every single study so far" on volume is an absolute claim with no citation, and the recommendation to abandon RIR in favour of always going to failure sits awkwardly beside study 3, whose own finding was that going to failure is *worse* for strength.
