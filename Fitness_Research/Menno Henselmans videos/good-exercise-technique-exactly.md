# There Is No Universal Good Technique — Only Biomechanics That Match Your Goal

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "What is 'good exercise technique' exactly?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MA_-yJfXFAI)) · 2024-05-04 · 5 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- His definition: **good technique is whatever biomechanics stimulate the training adaptation you actually want**. Nothing else. There is no one correct way to perform an exercise independent of a goal.
- Three common proxies for "good technique" are all wrong: that it looks tidy, that it is slow, and that it is the safest option.
- Making a lift look *worse* can make it work better — leaning back away from the dumbbell on a concentration curl, or deliberately using body English on a pulldown.
- Tempo is a **separate variable** from technique. Textbook demos are slow because they use light weights and want the movement to read clearly on camera, not because slow is inherently correct.

## "It looks good"
Serious training does not look pretty — you get red-faced and you shake. More importantly, ugly is sometimes better. In a demonstration on the RP channel, leaning slightly back on a concentration curl increases active and passive tension in the stretched bottom position, which he argues raises mechanical tension and stretch-mediated hypertrophy. It looks awkward and feels worse. He rates it more effective anyway.

## "It's slow"
Tempo is a separate variable: you can have good fast technique and good slow technique. Momentum is a legitimate tool in some lifts.

His main example is a **pulldown**. Jerking the weight down using hip extension and a backward lean lets you move more load on the concentric — but that momentum does not help you control it on the way up. The result is **eccentric overload**, which he says research links to higher muscle activity, more tension on the fibres, and in some cases more growth and strength. Lateral raises and calf raises are further cases; momentum is most useful on lifts with a pronounced sticking point.

## "It's the safest"
Athletes disprove this routinely. A powerlifter's priority is moving the bar, and many deadlift with some spinal flexion. He points to his own article on whether flexion is genuinely more injurious than a neutral lumbopelvic position, but the argument does not depend on the answer: athletes knowingly trade a safety margin for performance, so safety cannot be the definition.

## Overreach
Both recommendations are argued from mechanism rather than from any trial comparing the two executions for growth. The video cites no studies, and the eccentric-overload claim ("in some research") is left unsourced.

This cuts against the standard coaching line that beginners should default to strict, slow execution. He is not saying strict form is wrong — only that it is right when it matches a goal, not because it is *the* correct form.
