# What Makes Muscle Grow: Tension Survives, Damage and Metabolic Stress Do Not

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "What makes muscle grow?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=voMnQr7sfmk)) · 2017-07-25 · 43 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- Henselmans takes Schoenfeld's three-mechanism model — mechanical tension, muscle damage, metabolic stress — and argues that after a decade of follow-up evidence, **only mechanical tension survives as a direct cause of growth**.
- **Muscle damage is rejected outright.** He goes further than "unproven": he argues damage is a *byproduct* that lengthens recovery and raises protein breakdown, i.e. something to minimise, not chase.
- **Metabolic stress is demoted to an indirect route.** Where it appears to work (blood flow restriction), his explanation is that occlusion makes the exercise harder, which raises muscle activation, which raises mechanical tension. Tension is still doing the work.
- Practical upshot: **progressive overload and total work are the whole game.** You do not need to design a programme to "hit all three pathways."
- Concrete consequences he draws: longer rest intervals beat short ones, full range of motion is the default, low reps work as well as the "hypertrophy range" when work is equated, and pump/soreness are useless feedback signals.

This is a conference lecture, not a scripted video — it runs long, includes a Q&A, and has **no reference list in the description**, which matters for how much weight to put on the studies he cites; see References below.

## Where this sits relative to the rest of the library

The account already in this library — mechanical tension as the surviving driver, with damage and metabolic stress having failed to hold up — is one Henselmans **agrees with, and states in a stronger form than the library does**. He is not departing from that framing; he is pushing past it. The library's existing treatment leaves the question open, recording it as unresolved with no clean design available to separate the three. Henselmans, in 2017, calls it: "muscle damage: rejected," and metabolic stress with no direct line to growth at all.

He also does something the library's other sources mostly do not: he draws a **causal graph**. The final model is not "tension matters most." It is that damage is a *consequence* of tension rather than a cause of anything, and metabolic stress feeds into growth only by increasing muscle activation, which is itself just a route to more tension. Everything routes through one node.

Worth cross-referencing: in his 2024 interview with Schoenfeld (also in this library), **Schoenfeld is markedly less willing to close the question** than Henselmans was seven years earlier — the jury is still out on damage, damage demonstrably initiates satellite cell activation, and manipulating one mechanism inevitably manipulates another. The man whose paper Henselmans is revising has not followed him to the same conclusion.

## The mechanism, in brief

He opens with the uncontroversial chain any review paper will give you: **mechanotransduction** (tension on the fibres translated into a chemical signal) → **myogenic signalling** (IGF-1, IL-6 and similar released as the "start growing" message) → **mTOR** integrating those signals plus amino acid availability and triggering translation initiation → **muscle protein synthesis** adding myofibrillar protein → **myonuclear addition** raising the ceiling. His analogy for the last: a fibre is a city and nuclei are command centres, and you can only run so much area per command centre.

That is why mechanical tension is not in dispute — it sits at the head of the only well-described causal chain we have. The rest of the lecture asks whether the other two contenders have any chain of their own.

## The case against muscle damage

He builds this from five independent angles rather than one study, which is the right structure for the claim.

**Correlation.** His opening logic: causation without correlation is possible but rare, so if damage causes growth we should at least see them move together. A study by Damas and colleagues measured myofibrillar protein synthesis and damage markers in trained young men doing lower-body work. Raw protein synthesis looked elevated when damage was high — but corrected for damage, the growth signal was flat over time. His reading: when muscle is damaged there is more breakdown and more synthesis, but the synthesis is repair, not net accrual. Protein synthesis correlated with growth; damage correlated with nothing.

**Pre-training out the damage.** A second study ran a pre-trained group (three weeks working up, so the repeated bout effect had already blunted soreness) against a naive group thrown straight in, at matched total volume. The naive group hit up to a **fivefold higher creatine kinase increase** and was still significantly sorer at week four. Growth and strength gains were equal. The gym translation: ease into split squats and you will not be crippled; jump straight in and you will be, and you will not have earned anything for it.

**Eccentrics.** The strongest-looking case for damage, since eccentric work both damages more and is widely believed to grow more. He grants the second half only weakly: a Schoenfeld meta-analysis found a trend but no statistically significant effect, with a small effect size. And most of that literature does not equate total work — which matters, because you are much stronger eccentrically. When work *is* equated, the eccentric advantage disappears. He then picks apart the one volume-equated study that did find an eccentric advantage: it controlled for work *a priori*, assuming a 20% eccentric-concentric strength difference taken from data in men, and then ran the study in women, where the gap is far larger.

**Sex differences.** Women experience considerably less muscle damage from training but achieve the same relative growth. He points to his own article on the natural muscular potential of women rather than to a study here.

**Blood flow restriction.** Low-load occlusion training produces growth comparable to heavy training with nowhere near the damage — when KAATSU was first studied, damage was so hard to detect that researchers initially thought there was none. And adding occlusion at a matched 30% load raises growth without raising damage.

**Supplements that reduce damage.** Creatine generally reduces or at least does not increase damage markers — plausibly via reduced oxidative stress, suppressed inflammation, calcium pump upregulation and satellite cell activation — yet it is the best-supported legal muscle-building supplement there is. Damage down, growth up. Citrulline looks similar on the damage side without growth data to match, and he makes a dry aside about an HMB reference of his sourcing back to Jacob Wilson, questioning whether that work still counts.

His verdict: **damage rejected**, and probably actively unhelpful, since it extends recovery and increases protein breakdown. He adds a personal reversal — he used to favour damaging techniques and low-frequency, high-overload sessions, and got better client results once he cut damage and per-session volume and raised frequency.

## The case against metabolic stress

He is fairer to this one, and sets out the theoretical rationale properly: cell swelling, reactive oxygen species, inflammation and myokine production, hormonal elevation, and increased fibre recruitment. Then he lands the methodological objection that shapes the whole section — **metabolic stress is not quantified.** It is a bag of things (lactate, phosphate, hydrogen ions, hypoxia) with no agreed measure, so there is no way to run the Damas-style correlation study on it. Everything below is indirect.

**HIIT.** Enormous metabolic stress, very little growth beyond novices — and adding it to lifting often reduces growth via the interference effect. He pre-empts the "but sprinters are jacked" objection with an anecdote from a sprinter he coached: a 20-second effort is *cardio* to an actual sprinter, who considers five seconds a sprint and needs five minutes between reps, not ten seconds. Tabata work is not sprinting, and sprinters lift heavily besides.

**Rest intervals.** The flagship case. Short rests were prescribed to bodybuilders precisely to accumulate metabolic stress. Work-equated studies show no difference; but studies that fix the *number of sets* and vary rest length find **more growth with longer rests**, because you complete more reps and thus more total work. He co-authored the review and a follow-up randomised trial on this with Schoenfeld. His framing is unusually blunt: this was not merely a harmless bodybuilding ritual like six meals a day — it was one where lifters were flatly limiting their own gains.

**The "hypertrophy range."** 6–12 reps was supposed to be magic because it combines tension with metabolic stress. He points to work from as far back as 2002 (captions render the name as "Campell"; most likely Campos et al., but the description gives no reference list to confirm) showing low reps are equally effective when volume is equated, and notes that only one study has ever found more growth in a higher-intensity group. If the rep range does not matter once work is matched, the extra metabolic stress in the middle of the range is not buying anything.

**Constant tension.** The practice of avoiding lockout to keep the burn going is, he says, thoroughly debunked; full range of motion is the default for hypertrophy, strength and essentially every other adaptation, with partials and isometrics as strategic additions. He credits Bret Contreras, who was billed for the same event but could not attend.

**Training to failure.** Metabolic stress rises *exponentially* over the last few reps — ammonia especially — while growth does not. Studies comparing matched protocols with and without failure mostly find no difference. He is careful here: he writes "no/little" on the slide specifically to avoid the takeaway that you should never train to failure, saying there is strategic value in it, but declines to open that argument.

**Cluster sets.** Resting within the set kills the pump and the burn almost entirely while permitting a lot of high-quality work. Growth is not compromised. Another dissociation between metabolic stress and outcome.

**Buffering agents.** Creatine and beta-alanine reduce hydrogen ion accumulation — literally less metabolic stress — and improve growth or at least have the potential to, because they let you do more work.

**Blood flow restriction, the strongest case for the defence.** He concedes this is the one place metabolic stress looks real, then narrows it hard. Above roughly 40% of 1RM, adding a cuff does nothing extra as long as you go to failure; at around 20% of 1RM, occluded training still underperforms heavy training. That leaves roughly a 30% of 1RM band where the cuff genuinely adds growth — a conditional effect, not a dose-response one.

Two findings then finish the section. First, a study asking directly whether metabolic stress is an independent anabolic stimulus applied occlusion to one arm *after* sets of curls to failure rather than during. Growth was the same or slightly worse in the occluded arm; women in particular lost muscle. His reading is not "they did it wrong" but "**metabolic stress by itself is not a stimulus** — it needs the contraction alongside it," and possibly the restricted venous flow impaired repair, in line with what is known about icing, NSAIDs and compression in injury management.

Second, the finding he calls the kicker: **BFR grows non-occluded muscle too.** Cuffs on the arms during bench pressing grow the pecs, not just the triceps; cuffs during squats grow the glutes as well as the thighs. Metabolic stress downstream of the cuff cannot explain that. What can is that occlusion simply makes the exercise harder, muscle activation rises everywhere to compensate, and higher activation means higher tension.

## The model he ends with, and what it implies

One driver: **mechanical tension**. Muscle damage becomes a byproduct of tension with no arrow back to growth. Metabolic stress gets one arrow, and it points to muscle activation, which points to mechanical tension, which points to growth — indirect, and only under specific conditions.

Practically: progressive overload and total work are what get you bigger, and programmes built to "stimulate all three pathways" are not wrong so much as accidentally right — they usually amount to undulating periodization or useful variety, and the pathway rationale is doing no work.

He closes on the psychology, which is really the point of the lecture. Lifters chase sensation — pump, burn, next-day soreness — because those are tangible and growth is not. His analogy is the foam in shampoo: added because non-foaming shampoo feels like it is not working, though the foam cleans nothing. Same with fizzing pre-workouts. His prescription is to ignore pump and soreness entirely as signals of a productive session.

The Q&A adds two positions worth recording. On super-slow reps as an alternative to BFR for rehab: he cites a Schoenfeld tempo meta-analysis suggesting very slow tempos stop working, and says they only seem effective when the load is so light that slowing down is the only way to make it challenging — BFR is well-evidenced as safe and he would rather use it, though the two are not exclusive. On the mind-muscle connection: **not a fan for compound work**, since internal focus can raise activation in an isolated muscle but impairs performance, and above ~80–85% of 1RM you need everything firing regardless. He accepts it for isolation work, mainly as a technique aid. This is a 2017 position stated before the long-term attentional-focus study covered elsewhere in this library, which found a real biceps-growth advantage for internal focus — but his practical split matches where that literature landed.

## Where the confidence outruns the evidence

- **"Muscle damage: rejected"** is a stronger verdict than the evidence he presents supports. Five converging indirect lines is a genuinely good argument for *demoting* damage, but the direct evidence is essentially one correlational study plus one damage-manipulation study. He does not flag any of it as provisional, and the researcher whose model he is revising did not draw the same conclusion.
- **The claim of "161% greater strength in women during eccentric contractions"** is presented in passing as an established figure. It is doing real work in his takedown of the one eccentric study that disagreed with him, and a number that extreme deserves a citation the lecture does not give.
- **The BFR load thresholds** (nothing above 40%, insufficient at 20%, useful around 30%) are stated as fairly settled boundaries drawn from a small conditional literature. That is a lot of precision for the underlying data.
- **The failure section** uses "no or little increase in growth" as evidence against metabolic stress while simultaneously reserving a strategic role for training to failure. Both can be true, but he does not reconcile them.
