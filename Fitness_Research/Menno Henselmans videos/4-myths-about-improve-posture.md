# Posture: Four Myths, and Why "Corrective Exercise" Has Almost Nothing Behind It

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "4 Myths about how to improve your posture" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n7h8H4nGeMw)) · 2025-01-23 · 10 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- There is **no universal correct posture**. The military-upright ideal fails the scientific definition of optimal posture — the position requiring the *least* neuromuscular effort.
- **Anterior pelvic tilt is the norm, not a defect.** In the prevalence data he cites, only **9% of men and 18% of women** had a neutral hip position; the rest were mostly anteriorly tilted, and none had symptoms.
- Posture is **not a readout of muscle length or strength**. Across studies of hamstring extensibility, hip stiffness and abdominal strength, correlations with spinal curvature and pelvic tilt are absent or too sparse to generalise from. "Upper cross" and "lower cross syndrome" are not syndromes.
- **Systematic reviews find no reliable causal link between posture and back, neck or shoulder pain.** What causes trouble is holding *any* posture for very long periods without moving.
- Most exercise programmes **do not change posture**, and where exercise helps, the *type* doesn't matter — pointing at general deconditioning, not a corrective effect.
- His strongest positive claim: posture tracks **mood and confidence** more tightly than biomechanics, so the insecurity the corrective-exercise industry sells you is likelier to be making you slump than any tight pec.

## Myth 1: There is a perfect posture
[00:30] The formal definition he quotes — optimal static standing posture is the position needing the **least neuromuscular activity** and minimising gravitational stress, so you move efficiently and free of impairment — immediately disqualifies the ramrod-straight ideal, which is effortful for most people.

[01:33] Anatomical variation is uncontroversial: up to **2 cm** of asymmetry between limbs is normal, and pelvis and spine shape vary widely. A single template posture for everyone is incoherent.

[02:04] The within-person point is stronger: **your own posture varies across the day** — after waking, after a shower, after the gym — and with mood. It is not only wrong to call a posture defective for differing from a textbook; it is wrong to say you *have* one posture.

## Myth 2: Posture reflects muscular balance
[03:06] The model says the joint sits wherever opposing muscles pull it: anterior pelvic tilt means weak glutes and abs, slouching means tight pecs and a weak back.

[04:07] His anatomical objection is the good one: **the muscles aren't contracting**. Optimal posture is by definition the position your muscles hold *without* expending much energy. Nothing is pulling on anything.

[04:37] The evidence: multiple studies of posture against muscle strength, length and tightness show essentially no significant correlations, outside people genuinely detrained who simply need to exercise. He quotes one group directly — some correlations between hip stiffness and postural measures existed, but "these were too few to permit generalized assumptions", and "the textbook assumption of stiff muscles opposing compliant ones was not supported."

## Myth 3: Posture causes pain
[05:37] The best-supported claim in the video, and the one this library most needs recorded. **Multiple large systematic reviews** — including a systematic review *of* systematic reviews on spine posture and low back pain — conclude there is essentially no established causal relationship between posture and the likelihood of back, neck or shoulder pain. This holds for how you stand, sit and walk.

[06:08] The nuance he gets right: **being sedentary is bad for you, but the posture you're sedentary in isn't the problem.** You can develop trouble from hours slumped, hours bolt upright, or hours standing. The culprit is prolonged static loading.

[06:39] He then goes further, claiming some "postural abnormalities" are performance-*advantageous*: anterior pelvic tilt is prevalent in runners and increases with experience, and forward-rounded posture and kyphosis may help boxers and swimmers. He hedges this ("some research suggests"), correctly — it is cross-sectional observation, and the adaptive interpretation is his.

## Myth 4: Corrective exercise works
[07:41] Two specific negative findings. A study of the standard prescription — stretch the pecs, train the upper back to retract the scapulae — found **no effect on shoulder blade position**. (The auto-captions mangle the author's name beyond recovery; the closest match in his reference list is Lin & Karduna 2016, which found a four-week programme changed neither rotator cuff activation nor scapular kinematics. Attribution unconfirmed.) And **Levine's abdominal strengthening study found no effect on anterior pelvic tilt**. His list also contains a systematic review of non-surgical interventions for excessive anterior pelvic tilt, which likewise found no reliable fix.

[08:11] Where exercise does help — significantly deconditioned or elderly people — the **type of exercise doesn't seem to matter**, which he reads, reasonably, as a general-fitness effect rather than a corrective one.

[08:41] The final move is the least supported: he argues exercise improves posture chiefly through **confidence and well-being** rather than biomechanics, on the grounds that mood–posture correlations are stronger than muscle-length–posture correlations and that exercise reduces depression effectively. The mood–posture studies are small-scale embodiment work; **the causal chain from exercise → confidence → improved posture is his construction, not a finding.** This is the video's largest overreach.

## Practice-based, not evidence-based
- The 500 lb deadlifter anecdote (clients told by a physiotherapist their back was too weak to hold their ribcage up) and the "money-making scam" framing.
- Anterior tilt as a *functional* running adaptation.
- Insecurity as the dominant cause of slouching.
- "Most research has failed to find any effect of exercise, regardless of type, on posture" sits awkwardly with **Lee et al. 2017**, in his own reference list, which reported changes in rounded shoulder and forward head posture with exercise. He cites it but doesn't address it.

## When to see a clinician
*Not from the video.* His conclusion, "if you don't have pain or dysfunction you probably don't have a postural problem", is sound. The corollary matters: **if you do have pain or dysfunction, this video is not your answer.** See a clinician for numbness, tingling or weakness in a limb; pain at rest or waking you at night; pain after trauma; loss of function; a spinal curve that is progressing or markedly asymmetric (structural scoliosis is a medical condition, not a posture habit); or pain not improving over several weeks. Note too that the reviews showing exercise helps back and neck pain (Rainville 2004; Smith 2014; the Cochrane neck update) are about treating pain, not correcting posture — a distinction the video moves past quickly.
