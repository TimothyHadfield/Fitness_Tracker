# Five 2025 Studies: Pre-Workout Carbs, Fasted Training, Cold Plunges, Partials and Muscle Memory

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "5 Surprising new studies on how to build muscle in 2025" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qpi7pWmM35A)) · 2025-05-28 · 13 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Pre-workout carbs did nothing** in a properly blinded trial: no carbs vs 0.3 vs 1.2 g/kg two hours before, taste- and texture-matched, gave identical rep totals. Much of the apparent benefit in older work is placebo plus fasted control groups.
- **Fasted training is viable but not free.** A 12-week trial in untrained overweight people found no meaningful difference; his own trial in trained lifters found equal muscle growth but **worse squat strength gains, lower performance and lower energy**, with the gap widening over time.
- **Don't cold plunge after training.** Cooling cut microvascular blood volume and blunted amino acid incorporation into muscle protein. A broader review also found cold exposure doesn't improve most health outcomes.
- If you do **lengthened partials**, do the whole set that way rather than full ROM to failure then partials to failure — 10% vs 7% calf growth over 8 weeks. The evidence is very weak (Bayes factor 1.2) and he says so.
- **Muscle memory** gains a third mechanism alongside myonuclei and neural adaptation: proteins built during 10 weeks of training persisted at least 2.5 months after stopping.

## Pre-workout carbohydrate is largely placebo
The design is the point: with meals matched for taste and texture, participants couldn't tell how many carbs they were getting. Across three all-out sets of squat, overhead press, bench and row at 80% 1RM, carbohydrate intake made no difference to reps. His own 2022 systematic review found the same, with two exceptions — training genuinely fasted, and extremely high volumes. He argues that where pre-workout carbs "worked", the control group was fasted with no proper placebo; supply a placebo and the effect often vanishes.

His mechanistic case: you fuel a session from stored muscle glycogen, not from carbs eaten two hours earlier, so pre-workout carbs are already too late. The body resynthesises glycogen well even on low carb intakes, partly via the Cori cycle. Much of the energy in lifting comes from the creatine phosphate and aerobic systems rather than glycolysis, and total carbohydrate use across even a punishing session is under ~100 g.

## Fasted training
The new trial compared overnight-fasted training with training 1–2 hours after a high-carb breakfast for 12 weeks, daily macros matched. No differences in quadriceps thickness, 1RM, power, session workloads or fat mass. The fasted group did gain significantly *more* fat-free mass by DEXA — and he refuses to take that at face value: it contradicts prior research and mechanism, the effect size was trivial, and what actually happened is that the *fed* group failed to gain fat-free mass significantly at all. He also distrusts the sample: untrained overweight participants, several reporting light-headedness and nausea, and 25% dropout despite only two sessions a week.

His own study in well-trained lifters is the more useful comparison. No difference in muscle growth, but impaired squat strength gains, worse in-session performance and lower reported energy in the fasted group — and the performance gap **widened over time**, which he argues could eventually cost muscle via reduced training volume, since growth is downstream of accumulated mechanical tension. His verdict is permissive rather than enthusiastic: fine if you prefer it and you're not a high-volume competitive athlete.

## Cold plunges
Cooling after exercise greatly reduced microvascular blood volume, which tracked with lower incorporation of dietary amino acids into muscle protein; the authors recommend avoiding post-exercise cold water immersion. He frames this as the mechanism behind earlier findings that post-exercise cold reduces muscle protein synthesis and long-term growth.

The framing he offers is genuinely useful: cold suppresses swelling and inflammation, which are *part of* recovery, not obstacles to it. That's a net negative if your next session is tomorrow, and a net positive only if you have to perform again in a few hours — an Olympic athlete competing twice a day.

A second review found cold exposure doesn't improve most health outcomes: acute inflammation without changes in overall immune function, no significant effect on most mood and stress measures, and the famous cold-shower study's headline — fewer sick days called in — came with **no objective difference in illness days**, which he reads as resilience or simply less lying about being ill. He still endorses cold showers as a shot of adrenaline when you're drowsy, and nothing more.

## Post-failure lengthened partials
Trained lifters did calf raises either as lengthened partials only, or full range to failure followed by partials to failure. Partials-only produced 10% growth versus 7%. He is unusually careful here: a 96% Bayesian probability of superiority sounds strong, but the Bayes factor of 1.2 makes this very weak evidence. His recommendation is correspondingly soft — if you do lengthened partials, do the whole set that way; if you prefer the other, do that.

## Muscle memory
Three mechanisms: myonuclei added during training persist, possibly permanently, and support later growth; neural coordination gains persist; and now a proteomic signature — proteins built during 10 weeks of training were still present 2.5 months later, some linked to established epigenetic changes.

## Overreach to note
The closing speculation that muscle-building epigenetic changes might be **passed to your children** is explicitly flagged by him as "a bit of a leap", but it is far outside what the cited proteomics study examined and shouldn't be recorded as a finding. His claim that "mechanistically there's just no way cold is beneficial for recovery" is stronger than one acute perfusion study supports.
