# Which Steroid "Tells" Actually Work

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "5 clues that give away fake natties" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7jIIX1DkKKE)) · 2024-09-26 · 16 min
**Topic:** Evidence & methodology

## Bottom line
- **Capped delts and big traps are a bad tell.** The whole "upper body has more androgen receptors" idea rests on a single study with **eight** steroid-using powerlifters and no measurements of muscle size.
- A recent study found **no relationship between androgen receptor markers and hypertrophy** in men or women, acute or chronic — so even if receptor density did differ by region, it may not matter.
- A direct comparison of enhanced vs natural powerlifters found the size difference was **slightly larger in the quads than the traps** (61% vs 58%, and 33% vs 44%) — the opposite of what the receptor-density model predicts.
- **Gynecomastia is a bad tell.** It's more prevalent in obese men than in steroid users, most people online are diagnosing stiff nipples rather than gyno, and androgens actually reduce gyno risk unless drugs are abused or body fat is high.
- **Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is a weak tell at best** — it says more about skincare and acne genetics than drug use, though acne-scarred back plus exceptional physique is suggestive.
- **The two tells that work:** large, sudden physique changes over short periods (natural growth is slow and natural size is stable), and **fat-free mass index** — a crude number that beats narrative reasoning.

## Clue 1: Capped delts (doesn't work)

The bro-science model: traps and delts have higher androgen receptor density, so anabolic androgenic steroids act more strongly there, producing a disproportionately large upper body.

The whole model traces to one study comparing androgen receptor density in traps versus quads across steroid-using powerlifters, natural powerlifters, and untrained controls ([Kadi 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10664066/)). What it actually found: traps do have higher receptor density than quads, but in the steroid-using powerlifters receptor density was *higher in the quads*, and there were **no between-group differences** at all. No muscle size was measured. n=8 for the enhanced group, and they were powerlifters rather than bodybuilders.

His three objections stack:
1. **It doesn't match observation.** Bodybuilders have huge shoulders and traps, but their quads are usually a standout body part too.
2. **Receptor density might not matter.** A 2024 study found no association between acute or chronic androgen receptor markers and hypertrophy in either sex ([Bergamasco 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38934511/)).
3. **A direct size comparison points the other way.** Enhanced versus natural powerlifters showed size differences slightly favouring the quads over the traps — 61% vs 58% and 33% vs 44% ([Eriksson 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16059740/)). The numbers are close enough that his own reading is that receptor density may simply not matter.

He also notes receptor density varies by fibre type, region, individual and possibly training style, so extrapolating a universal upper/lower difference from one study isn't warranted regardless.

His concession: many steroid users genuinely do have disproportionate upper bodies, and steroids do exaggerate whatever imbalance exists — but that's a training-practice effect, not a pharmacological one. Capped delts are mostly **origin and insertion genetics, plus muscle mass, plus leanness.**

## Clue 2: Gyno (doesn't work)

Gynecomastia results from a poor ratio of testosterone activity to oestrogen, progesterone and prolactin activity on breast tissue. That makes it **most prevalent among obese men**, who aromatise a large share of their testosterone to oestrogen — more so, he says, than among steroid users.

Steroids are, by name and by action, strongly androgenic, so they push the ratio the other way and **reduce** gyno risk. Gyno in a user typically means abused compounds, or high testosterone at high body fat with no aromatase inhibitor. Plenty of users never get it.

He also thinks most online gyno diagnoses aren't gyno at all. Stiff nipples aren't gynecomastia, and from his own modelling and competing, cold, posing and arousal all produce them.

## Clue 3: Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (weak)

The most defensible of the three popular tells. Steroids do increase sebum production and the proliferation of acne-associated bacteria, so acne is a genuine side effect — and what onlookers see is not the acne but the dark spots left afterwards.

The problem is base rates and confounds. Back acne is common in men generally, some people get post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and some don't, and acne treatments prevent the pigmentation entirely. So it says more about **skincare, hygiene and genetic acne propensity** than drug use. His net position: acne scarring *plus* an extremely impressive physique is suggestive, but the sign alone carries little.

## Clue 4: Massive, sudden physique changes (works)

**Growth is slow.** There is no six-week transformation adding 20 lb of muscle. Even newbie gains cap out around 1-2 kg of muscle per month. Someone showing that kind of change is on drugs nine times out of ten, with muscle-memory regain the main honest exception.

**Natural size is stable.** Maintaining muscle is very easy — he claims a single hard full-body session per week suffices for most lifters outside a deficit — and atrophy takes over a week to begin. So when someone alternates between looking amazing and not, and explains it as "I didn't train as hard that period", he calls that BS: training hard or not moves a natural lifter by at most a few kilos. His own anchor is 84-95 kg over a decade at 185 cm, where 84 is dehydrated contest-prep and 95 is a fat bulk peak.

**The water-retention refinement.** Aromatising bulking compounds cause substantial water retention — the "moon face". Someone on gear during a bulk looks like they got fatter, but not fat and flabby: everything stays full and hard, definition disappears, the look is puffy rather than soft. He admits distinguishing this from body fat takes skill.

## Clue 5: Fat-free mass index (works best)

The single best tell is simply **how muscular someone is**, quantified as fat-free mass index — BMI's equivalent for lean mass rather than total weight.

The methodological point underneath is the reason this note is filed under evidence and methodology. **In many domains a simple prediction from hard quantifiable data beats expert narrative reasoning.** People are drawn to stories and clues; a single number outperforms them. That is a real and well-replicated finding about clinical versus actuarial prediction, though he asserts it rather than citing it.

Why FFMI rather than strength: strength is far more variable between individuals, because it depends on the nervous system (his analogy: muscle is the engine, coordination is the driver) and on mechanical leverages — insertion points and internal moment arms can roughly **double** someone's lifted numbers without their muscles producing more force.

His caveat on his own tool: the [FFMI calculator](https://mennohenselmans.com/ffmi-calculator/) does **not** apply a hard cutoff at 25, which he calls nonsense. It reports the proportion of the population that could achieve a given FFMI naturally. A very low percentage is likely but not definitive evidence of drug use — one-in-ten-million outliers exist. It is always probabilistic, and you will never actually know, since even a confession could be false. He notes nobody using the calculator has exceeded the estimated natural ceiling at a low body fat level; those who exceeded it were all at higher body fat.

## The behavioural point at the end

He argues natty-or-not is usually a proxy question. Asking whether someone is enhanced is like asking a classmate their exam score — you want to locate your own. So the calculator's second mode reports how far along you are toward your own estimated ceiling, by body part, and he redirects the reader to that: **your progression against your own past and potential** is both the more useful and the more liveable comparison.

## Flags

- The reasoning is asymmetric. He is rigorous in dismantling the three popular tells and much looser on his own two — the FFMI ceiling estimates rest on data he describes but doesn't cite here, and the "nobody has exceeded it" claim is self-reported from his own tool's users.
- "A very simple prediction based on hard objective quantifiable data is better than a lot of speculation and narratives" is a real research literature, but no source is given for it.
- The claim that gyno is *more* prominent in obese men than in steroid users is stated flatly with no citation. Directionally plausible given aromatisation, but presented more confidently than the video supports.
- Three references for a 16-minute video, all concerning clue 1. Clues 2 through 5 are unsourced.
