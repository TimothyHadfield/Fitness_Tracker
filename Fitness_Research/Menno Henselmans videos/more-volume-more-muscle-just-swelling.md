# Is Volume-Driven Muscle Growth Real, Or Just Oedema?

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "More volume = more muscle… or just swelling? [New study]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0QrJ-JPEDHU)) · 2026-02-03 · 7 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- He is **defending** high-volume research against the oedema objection, not endorsing it. His conclusion: higher volumes cause **real contractile hypertrophy**, not swelling.
- New study: trained men (10RM squat ≈ three plates) did **7, 14 or 21 quad sets** of squats, leg press and leg extension at ~10RM to maximum effort. Swelling — muscle thickness plus echo intensity as an oedema proxy — **returned to baseline by 24 hours, with no effect of volume at all**.
- Second study: muscle size measured **70-120 h** after the last workout of a 6-week program, then again a day later. If oedema were inflating it, size should have dropped. It didn't.
- Since most training studies measure size **48-72 h** post-workout, both results say those measurements are robust.
- **Perceived recovery was badly decoupled from objective recovery.** Lifters still didn't feel recovered 72 h after even the 7-set workout, while 10RM squat performance was already back to or above baseline.

## Directly relevant to the volume-ceiling dispute
This is exactly the critique that bears on the high-volume literature in this library: several studies measure muscle thickness by ultrasound a couple of days after the last session, and higher volumes cause more muscle damage, so the higher-volume arms could be showing swelling rather than growth. Menno takes the argument seriously and answers it with a study that measured swelling directly rather than inferring it.

His resolution of why the older oedema literature disagrees is the **repeated bout effect**. Studies showing swelling persisting past three days overwhelmingly used **untrained** subjects doing, by definition, unfamiliar workouts. Trained lifters become dramatically more damage-tolerant on familiar exercises — the same reason many well-trained lifters never get sore from their normal sessions but get wrecked by a first day of skiing. Both new studies used trained lifters doing familiar exercises, which is the population every volume study actually samples.

## The measurement-site caveat he raises himself
Hypertrophy was measured mid-thigh, so effectively rectus femoris and vastus intermedius. He calls this a poor choice: rectus femoris is biarticular and can't contribute effectively during squats and leg presses, since extending the knee would simultaneously pull it back into hip flexion. His counter is that even granting that squats and leg presses do nothing for rectus femoris, they still train vastus intermedius, and the leg extensions — which do train rectus femoris — also scaled with volume across groups.

## Perceived vs objective recovery
Perceived recovery status essentially didn't correlate with objective strength recovery, defined as restored force production. Lifters felt unrecovered 72 h after every volume condition; their squat 10RM said otherwise. A learning effect (10RM rising at 24 and 72 h) confounds this, and he flags it, but the finding matches prior studies. **Most lifters can recover from more volume than they think.**

## His position on low-volume training
Blunt, and worth recording because it cuts against the current trend: you can make excellent gains on low volume, and that may be satisfactory — but "you don't get more with less. You just get good gains." For maximum growth, the work is required.

**Overreach flag:** the Alvarez study measured acute swelling after single workouts, not the swelling state at the end of a multi-week block, and the Varovic re-measurement is a one-day check in a single study. Two studies is thin ground for treating this as settled, and describing the meta-analysis as finding no volume endpoint at all is stated more confidently than a dose-response meta-regression with sparse data at high volumes supports.
