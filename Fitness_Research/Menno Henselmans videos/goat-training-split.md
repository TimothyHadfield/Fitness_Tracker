# Why He Thinks Full Body Is the Only Rational Split

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is the GOAT training split" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nSIQ0vVXRBw)) · 2025-07-22 · 19 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **He does name a best split: full body, at whatever frequency you train.** Four days a week means four full-body sessions; seven days, seven. He does this himself.
- The argument is that **split architecture is downstream of one variable — frequency per muscle group** — and the frequency literature is better-or-neutral, never worse. A 2024 meta-analysis put the probability that higher frequency produces more hypertrophy at **91%**, effect small and inconsistent; the strength effect was more consistent.
- The mechanism he proposes is **not** frequency itself but the volume it buys: spreading sets across days means each is performed fresher, so you complete more reps or more load for the same number of sets. Frequency is "just a very long rest interval".
- Within-session volume has **strong diminishing returns past roughly six sets per muscle**, with muscle protein synthesis plateauing around **eight to ten sets** — so the seventh, eighth, ninth chest set is the volume worth relocating.
- **Time efficiency is the practical case.** Rotating exercises for different muscle groups instead of resting between sets can cut a session to a third of the time. His own sessions run 45 minutes.
- Recovery is not a counter-argument: the limited evidence trends towards **better** recovery markers with higher frequency, and two studies found equal gains training Mon/Tue/Wed versus Mon/Wed/Fri.

## Where this contradicts our library

This directly contradicts the position already recorded from [Bro Splits vs Higher Frequency](../Jeff%20Nippard%20videos/bro-splits-vs-higher-frequency.md) — that no split architecture is physiologically superior, that volume is the driver, and that three practical questions decide the split (does it let you hit the volume, does it allow recovery, does it fit your life).

Henselmans accepts the second half and rejects the first. His route around it: splits look equivalent in the research precisely *because* researchers equate volume, which is not a situation any lifter is ever in. Once volume is free to move, the higher-frequency arrangement produces more of it, and volume is king. The splits are equal only under an artificial constraint.

His verdict on the "subjective fit" criterion is dismissive: "there's really no objective reason not to do full body workouts", and every argument he's heard for splits reduces to an irrelevant or subjective indicator, usually the pump.

His own prescription softens at the end, though. The program does not have to be a full-body workout by name — "it just needs to have a high training frequency per body part." That is a much smaller claim than the title, and closer to the library's position than the rest of the video.

## The empirical argument

Across roughly 50 frequency studies, he says, the pattern is better gains or no difference. He finds exactly one exception, where a lower-frequency group won in one of four muscles, and dismisses it as internally inconsistent. The pooled result he leans on is a 2024 meta-analysis — the auto-captions render the author as "palentatal", and the description's first reference is an unresolved SportRxiv preprint, so treat the name as uncertain — reporting a 91% probability of more hypertrophy with higher frequency, effect small and inconsistent, plus a more consistent positive relationship with strength.

He draws the asymmetry explicitly: **you might get more growth, you probably get somewhat more strength, and there is essentially no probability of doing worse.** Maximising frequency is then the rational bet even if the expected effect is small.

Two boundary conditions he states himself:
- **Untrained lifters:** frequency doesn't matter, volume does. About four studies find benefits, none find harm.
- **Where the benefit concentrates:** trained lifters, training to failure, doing a lot of volume per session. If you only do two sets for a muscle, moving one to another day changes nothing.

He also argues body-part splits are a niche practice of one segment of bodybuilding, and that sprinters, gymnasts, throwers, strongmen and pre-1950s bodybuilders all trained close to full body. Rhetorical rather than evidential.

## The volume mechanism

Take a Monday chest day with incline, flat and decline pressing. Move the incline to Wednesday and the decline to Friday. Total volume rises, because you now perform those presses fresh instead of after six sets of other pressing. Hence the rest-interval analogy: a longer rest between sets raises volume and therefore growth; frequency is the same intervention on a longer timescale.

This also explains why volume-equated studies find nothing. If the high-frequency group must match total reps, they are simply training less hard — and in several such studies they reported *lower* perceived exertion. Equal gains for less effort, which he treats as a finding in its own right.

[Neves 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36228016/) tests this directly, with three arms: nine sets on one day, three sets three times a week volume-equated, and three sets three times a week with as much volume as possible. The equated arm matched the single-day arm. The unequated arm doubled the hypertrophy, 2% to 4%. This did not reach statistical significance, and he explains why he wouldn't expect it to: an 8-week study in trained lifters, a modest volume increase on the diminishing-returns part of the curve, and an effect size well inside ultrasound measurement error. He is arguing from effect direction and confidence intervals, not significance, and says so.

## Diminishing returns within a session

Krieger's Weightology meta-analysis showed a near-plateau after about six sets per muscle per session — the same figure the junk volume material already in the library arrives at, from the same analysis. Henselmans says his own analysis found a similar result but was confounded by suspected data fraud in some Brazilian studies, and calls for a proper meta-analysis of the within-session data. That is an unusually frank caveat.

The mechanistic support is the muscle protein synthesis dose-response, which he says rises with per-session volume and flattens around eight to ten sets. In rats the picture is cleaner: MPS plateaus while protein breakdown keeps climbing, so past about ten sets the net effect trends negative ([Ogasawara 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28729395/)). He acknowledges the rodent data and missing human breakdown data, arguing basic exercise physiology usually transfers.

## Recovery and stimulus-to-fatigue

His claimed advantages of higher frequency: better testosterone-to-cortisol ratios, less soreness, lower perceived exertion, higher muscle activity. He concedes most individual studies find no significant difference and rests on the direction of the trend. The active-recovery argument is that training raises blood flow systemically, and some studies find very light work — a warm-up-intensity bench session — improves bench strength recovery over following days.

[Raastad 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12753488/) is his best single piece of evidence: lifters moved from a four-day upper/lower split to daily full-body quad work at six sets per session, and after two weeks their recovery speed *improved* — complete recovery 22 hours post-workout, which had not been true before the block. They adapted rather than overtrained.

He then questions whether recovery days are needed at all, citing two studies where Mon/Tue/Wed produced the same gains as Mon/Wed/Fri ([Yang 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29967584/); [da Silva Carvalho 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27682004/)). He half-retracts it immediately — he thinks recovery matters and you should stay under your maximum adaptive volume — but the general claim stands: most people underestimate what they can tolerate, and the binding constraint is mental, not muscular.

## Time efficiency

The argument least dependent on contested evidence. A session of bench, five minutes rest, bench, five minutes rest is mostly spent not exercising, and monopolises equipment. Full body lets you fill the rest interval with a different muscle group — squats, chin-ups, overhead press in rotation — cutting session time by roughly two thirds while the rest interval for any given exercise stays long. In a busy gym, stay flexible about exercise order and take whatever is free.

## Flags

- The core empirical claim rests on a preprint meta-analysis whose author name the transcript mangles and whose URL doesn't resolve. The 91% figure and its caveat both come from it.
- "Research decisively shows" higher frequency leads to superior or equal gains is stronger than his own description of the effect as small, inconsistent and mostly non-significant. The direction is one-sided; "decisively" is not the word for a body of mostly null results.
- The MPS-plateau-and-negative-net-effect claim is rat data extrapolated to humans, by his own admission.
- The active-recovery and stimulus-to-fatigue benefits are a trend across studies that mostly found nothing, not a result.
- 23 studies listed flat with no per-claim mapping. Only unambiguous mappings are linked above.
