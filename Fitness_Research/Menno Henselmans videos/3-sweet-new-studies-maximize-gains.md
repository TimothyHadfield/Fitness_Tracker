# Three New Studies: Splits, Quad Exercises, and the Thermic Effect of Protein

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "3 Sweet new studies to maximize your gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7uwT05Uy4jU)) · 2026-02-10 · 11 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Your training split barely matters.** Total accumulated mechanical tension is the stimulus; the split only matters insofar as it changes how much volume you can produce per set. Spread volume across the week — full body is probably the most efficient structure.
- He **doesn't trust the split study he's reporting**: it found no tonnage difference between conditions, which shouldn't happen in serious lifters and makes the null result questionable.
- **Squats and leg extensions are complementary, not interchangeable.** Leg extensions grew the **rectus femoris** more; squats grew the **vastus lateralis** more.
- Squats beat leg extensions for *leg extension* strength — but only in **untrained** subjects, where motor learning outweighs specificity. He expects specificity to win in trained lifters.
- **The thermic effect of protein is overrated.** Across 28 studies, the advantage is real in single-meal work but **not significant in studies over a month**. Eating more protein purely for the thermic bonus buys maybe **50 calories** and costs hundreds.

## Study 1: does the split matter?

Trained men, upper body only, split two ways. Group one did a conventional push/pull arrangement. Group two took the isolation exercises off the push and pull days and swapped them onto the opposite day — so biceps work landed on the pressing day and triceps work on the pulling day. He is blunt that this split has no name and is "just an abomination," which is the point: it's an ugly-on-paper structure to test against a tidy one.

After 8 weeks: **no significant differences** in biceps, triceps or pec growth, and none in bench press or lat pulldown 1RM.

His caveat is more interesting than the result. **Total tonnage (weight x reps x sets) didn't differ either**, and he argues that makes no sense: move biceps isolation off a pull day, where the biceps is pre-fatigued by rowing, onto a press day where it's fresh, and a serious lifter should produce more reps or more load. It didn't happen, which makes him doubt the data.

That matters because tonnage *is* his model of why any of these manipulations work. Higher frequencies, redistributed volume, longer rest intervals — all help for one reason: **less fatigue per set means more tonnage means more accumulated mechanical tension.** "Muscle is just a slab of meat. It experiences tension and it grows," and it doesn't care when.

He offers a supporting study where arm volume moved from one day to three: **volume-equated, no difference; not volume-equated, a trend toward more growth** on the spread-out schedule, because fresher arms produced more reps at the same load.

Practical answer: pick the split that distributes volume most evenly and so maximises volume per set. Neither PPL nor upper/lower — **full body is probably most efficient.**

## Study 2: Smith machine squats vs leg extensions

Untrained women, 8 weeks, everything else equated to isolate exercise selection.

- **Rectus femoris grew more from leg extensions.** Clean mechanism: the rectus femoris is biarticular, so in a squat it can't contribute much force without pulling you into hip flexion — back down into the squat. No such conflict in a leg extension. Multiple studies agree.
- **Vastus lateralis grew more from squats** — which surprised him. Same pattern in an earlier leg press study, with growth in both concentrated near the knee, though that regional finding was statistically weak. His tentative explanation, offered as speculation, is longer muscle length in the squat.

So they're complementary: different heads, different biomechanics, do both.

**On strength:** squats produced more *leg extension* strength than leg extensions did — also seen with leg press. But both studies used **untrained** subjects, and he thinks that's the whole explanation: in beginners the motor learning from a compound movement outweighs specificity. In trained lifters he expects specificity to reassert itself — "I have a very hard time seeing how anything could make you better at leg extensions than leg extensions." He adds that people underestimate **specificity** because our language treats strength as a trade rather than a skill; it is extremely movement-specific, increasingly so at higher levels.

## Study 3: the thermic effect of protein ([Guarneiri 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100332))

A meta-analysis of 28 studies on diet-induced thermogenesis across protein amounts and types.

- **Single-meal studies:** high-protein meals show a clearly higher thermic effect.
- **Long-term studies:** the thermic advantage is **no longer significant**. Restricted to studies over a month, it disappears entirely.
- Resting and total daily energy expenditure still favoured higher protein.

His reconciliation: the durable expenditure advantage isn't thermic at all, it's **fat-free mass**. Protein drives muscle protein synthesis, more muscle raises daily expenditure — and that mechanism runs out, because most of the short-term thermic effect reflects people moving from inadequate to adequate protein. Once you're at an optimised intake, extra protein doesn't raise MPS further and is largely oxidised.

The arithmetic makes the argument stick. Even at best the thermic effect is around **25%**, so at least 75% of those calories count. Eating far more chicken breast to buy a **~50 calorie** advantage means consuming hundreds to get it — and the comparison isn't flattering next to high-fibre whole foods like lentils or vegetables. For a lifter already at optimal protein this is a bonus if you like high-protein food, not a reason to restructure a diet. "There's no such thing as free energy."

## Where the note has to push back

Two of the three studies are unresolved preprint-stage JSCR entries, so neither the split study nor the squat/leg extension study can be checked against a published record here. The quad study is **untrained women, 8 weeks, Smith machine** — a narrow base for exercise-selection advice to trained lifters, and he only flags the untrained limitation for the strength finding, not the hypertrophy finding.

His treatment of study 1 is also worth watching: he rejects a null result on the grounds that a mediating variable didn't move the way his model predicts. That's a legitimate criticism, but it means the study is being discounted for disagreeing with the theory rather than the theory being updated.
