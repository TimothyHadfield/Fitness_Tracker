# Mentzer Was Right About the Method, Wrong About the Prescription

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Everyone Misunderstands Why Mike Mentzer Was Right About Bodybuilding" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dwnXEuTIdbY)) · 2026-07-16 · 14 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Mentzer was right about **the scientific approach** — treating bodybuilding like medicine, with an optimal dose — and mostly wrong about the doses he prescribed.
- **The training that built his prime physique is not the training he is famous for.** His prime was 1978–79; Heavy Duty II was published in 1996. His early routines were full-body 3×/week and 4–6 sets per muscle to failure every 4–7 days — **10+ sets per muscle per week**, broadly in line with modern meta-analyses.
- The famous **one set to failure every 7–21 days** is maintenance volume, not growth volume.
- **Training to failure is not required.** It works via the extra volume it produces, not via intensity — volume-equated designs show equivalence.
- **6–10 reps is not a magic zone.** Roughly **4–30 reps** produce similar growth per set when taken close to failure.
- **Super-slow tempo (10 s/rep) does not help** and trends toward reducing growth. The eccentric matters, but slowing it further doesn't.

## The historical argument
[02:17] The video's core move. Mentzer peaked competitively in **1979** (heavyweight Mr Olympia) after **1978** (Mr Universe, allegedly a perfect score), and retired after a disputed fifth place in **1980**. Heavy Duty II arrived **sixteen years after retirement** — so virtually every prime-physique photograph predates the methods those photographs are used to endorse.

[03:25] What he actually did: full-body training three times a week in college — the standard of the era. His early Heavy Duty routines ran **four to six sets per muscle group to failure every four to seven days**. Only later did he gravitate to extreme minimalism.

Henselmans also raises Mentzer's late-80s/90s troubles — drug addiction, financial problems, a psychiatric admission — as context for weighing the later published positions, while saying it shouldn't detract from the achievements. **Flag:** biographical instability is not evidence about a training protocol, and this edges toward ad hominem; the argument about *when* the books were written stands on its own without it.

## Volume and frequency
[03:25] Modern meta-analyses show more volume tends to be better with no clear cut-off, but sharp diminishing returns past ~10 sets per week ([Pelland 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)). Mentzer's prime training landed in that zone. His later prescription — one set to failure once a week or less — corresponds to **maintenance**, which research confirms is very low. Henselmans' own teaching is a process rather than a number: estimate recovery capacity from energy balance, sleep, stress and training advancement.

## Training to failure
[05:17] He credits Mentzer for being ahead of the curve in defining **momentary muscular failure** — genuine inability to complete another rep — against **volitional failure**, which just means you gave up. Many exercise scientists still don't distinguish the two, and the gap can be several reps.

But Mentzer's position that training to momentary failure is "an absolute objective requirement for inducing growth stimulation" Henselmans calls **indisputably wrong**. Failure increases growth, but ironically it does so by adding volume: more reps completed. Equate total volume — more sets short of failure versus fewer to failure — and the stimulus is equivalent ([Robinson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)).

He goes further: the insistence on absolute failure probably **prevented Mentzer from finding better settings for everything else**. Failure has a poor stimulus-to-fatigue ratio and greatly extends recovery ([Morán-Navarro 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/)), plausibly why Mentzer concluded he needed enormous rest and few sets. His own default: **do as many reps as you can, but don't intentionally fail a rep** — a failed rep isn't a complete rep, so it adds little tension and a lot of fatigue.

## Rep range and tempo
[08:42] Mentzer's 6–10 reps reflects the old 6–12 hypertrophy zone. Fine, but not special — growth is similar per set from roughly **4 to 30 reps** when sets go to or near failure ([Schoenfeld 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000002200)). Practical payoff: for injury-sensitive exercises, go higher in reps.

[09:25] Mentzer prescribed 4 s up, 2 s pause, 4 s down — ten seconds per rep. Research finds no benefit and **a trend toward reduced growth** at 10+ seconds ([Schoenfeld 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0304-0)). It's easy on joints and terrible for strength, which Mentzer wouldn't have cared about. His practical objection: almost nobody actually does it — put a metronome next to them and the reps are much faster.

## Eccentrics
[11:02] Mentzer was early and correct that the eccentric matters especially for growth, siding with Arthur Jones and Nautilus. Lacking eccentric-specific machines, he used a **one-rep yielding isometric** — hold the weight and resist as it drags you down.

That has now been tested. Over 8.5 weeks, traditional training produced **6.7% muscle growth versus 4%** for the yielding isometric, with better 1RM curl gains too ([Henderson 2025](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2025-0039)). His preferred implementation instead is **eccentric overloading** — exercises where momentum or biomechanics naturally load the lowering phase harder, such as calf jumps — while conceding that "research is not entirely clear that this improves muscle growth, but theoretically it should."

**Flag:** two thin spots. The yielding-isometric verdict rests on a single trial in untrained individuals, presented as settling the question. And eccentric overloading is programmed into his app on theoretical grounds while he concedes the evidence isn't there — the same reasoning-from-mechanism he criticises elsewhere.

## The conclusion
Mentzer's methods were viable but not optimal, and probably overly restrictive. Combined with consistency, effort, genes and heavy anabolic use they produced a world-class physique — which is not the same as being the reason for it. Henselmans' warmest point is that Mentzer was arguably the first self-professed evidence-based lifter, and born today would have reached very different conclusions.
