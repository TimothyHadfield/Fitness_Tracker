# Carbs and Hypertrophy: His Own Meta-Analysis Found Nothing

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "You need this many carbs to maximize muscle growth" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XlDGB3o8g8g)) · 2026-02-20 · 10 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- Henselmans is first author on the **first systematic review and meta-analysis of carbohydrate intake and muscle growth**. Screening >1,000 papers left **11 RCTs**. Result: **no effect of carbohydrate intake on hypertrophy**, consistent across every single included study.
- The result survived leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, showed no publication bias, and held in subgroups that equated energy intake and that measured muscle directly rather than inferring it from fat-free mass.
- It matches his earlier systematic review on strength: **16 of 17 studies found no benefit** of higher carbs. The one that did was confounded by higher energy intake in the high-carb group.
- Mechanism: lifting depletes at most **~40% of muscle glycogen** (often **<30%** even in high-volume bodybuilders). ~400 kcal above basal is ~100 g of carbohydrate against ~**500 g** of stored glycogen. You never get near critical depletion.
- Exceptions he names: **10+ sets per muscle group in a single session**, **<24 h between sessions for the same muscle group**, **fasted training**, and **glycolytic team sports** (50–90% depletion), to which none of this applies.
- Practically, high-carb diets may still be preferable when **bulking** — not for any physiological reason, but because they make a high energy intake easier to eat.

## The study
[01:00] Inclusion criteria: randomised controlled trials only; at least two different carbohydrate intakes compared; muscle measured by a two-compartment model (DEXA) or ultrasound, with skinfolds and circumference excluded; ≥6 weeks of strength training; healthy adults. Critically, they **excluded studies where protein intake differed between groups** — his stated reason being that keto advocates routinely cite low-carb studies whose low-carb arm also ate more protein.

## Why carbs don't matter as much as the fuel-source logic suggests
[03:33] The superficial argument — carbs are the dominant fuel for high-intensity work, therefore more carbs means more performance — fails because the muscle runs on **glycogen it has already stored inside itself**, and lifting barely dents it. Strength training relies heavily on energy-efficient eccentric actions and has long rest periods, so total glycolytic expenditure is low.

[04:34] Much of the actual energy comes from the creatine phosphate system, plus a substantial aerobic (fat-fuelled) contribution both between sets and as a baseline during them.

[05:05] Recovery of glycogen doesn't require dietary carbohydrate either, given ~24 hours: the **Cori cycle**, the **glycerol backbone of triglycerides** (fat literally fuelling glycogen resynthesis), plus whatever carbohydrate and excess protein you do eat.

## The non-performance mechanisms
[06:36] The strongest remaining argument is that carbs raise insulin and insulin suppresses muscle protein breakdown. He accepts the premise but argues **protein makes it redundant** — protein is itself insulinogenic, and a lifter's high-protein diet already produces enough insulin. Beyond that you'd need injected insulin, which is not a natural-lifter consideration. Same reasoning applies to IGF-1.

[07:06] He then goes further and claims research is "very very clear" that **low-carb (therefore higher-fat) diets are better for testosterone production** than high-carb. This is the most contestable statement in the video and it carries no reference (see below).

## Where the practical advice diverges from the physiology
[07:37] Growth is driven by mechanical tension, which is a function of load and time under tension — i.e. training volume. If carbs don't change volume, they don't change growth.

[08:07] But at a granular level there is a weak trend for high-carb diets producing more growth **in the studies where the high-carb group overate**. Cutting carbs removes most calorie-dense foods, so people struggle to sustain a surplus — and several studies report unintentional fat loss. Hence the pattern coaches see: low-carb dieters get lean easily and gain muscle poorly; high-carb dieters do the reverse. He attributes this entirely to energy intake, not carbohydrate.

## Where this cuts against the mainstream
This is a direct challenge to standard sports-nutrition guidance, which prescribes high carbohydrate intakes for resistance-trained athletes. His position is that this guidance was imported wholesale from endurance and team sports, where glycogen depletion genuinely is 50–90%, and never tested in lifters. That is a fair criticism, and the meta-analysis is the strongest evidence available on the question.

## Flags
- **Self-citation.** Both references are his own papers, and the video is essentially a promotion of his own meta-analysis. That doesn't make the finding wrong, but the note should record that no independent synthesis is offered as a check.
- The conclusion that lifters "don't need pretty much any carbs" is stronger than 11 RCTs can support. A meta-analysis of 11 trials showing no *average* effect is not the same as evidence that the full range down to near-zero carbohydrate is safe for performance, and the included trials are unlikely to span that range.
- The testosterone claim is asserted with high confidence and **no citation at all**. The literature on macronutrient ratio and testosterone shows small, inconsistent effects of unclear practical relevance; "very very clear" overstates it.
- The 10-sets-per-session exception is smuggled in alongside an unrelated argument that such sessions are a bad idea anyway — so the one condition where his own reasoning says carbs might matter is waved away rather than examined.
