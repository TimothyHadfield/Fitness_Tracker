# Peptides: One That Works, and a Category With No Human Data

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Do peptides live up to the hype? [Science review]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TfTJIOakPOU)) · 2026-04-07 · 7 min
**Topic:** Supplements

*Medical topic. This note records his review, not a recommendation. Nothing here endorses use.*

## Bottom line
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists** (Ozempic and similar) are the exception: effective and safe weight-loss aids, working by suppressing appetite and so cutting energy intake.
- For the fitness peptides — **BPC-157, ipamorelin and other GH boosters, CJC-1295** — his review found **zero high-quality human data**. No RCTs for any purported benefit: recovery, sleep, body composition, none. **The safety data are rodent-only**, and over 92% of drugs fail translation from animal testing to human treatment.
- Some trials were **cancelled or never published**, which he reads as a bad sign rather than a neutral one.
- None are FDA approved, so **production is unregulated** — most are sold for research or animal use, and adulteration is the majority case, not the exception.
- He will not endorse use, but explicitly leaves the choice to the individual.

## Why absence of evidence is the argument
He concedes the logic up front: absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. His counter is about how the research market behaves. When a compound works, **trials multiply fast** and manufacturers race to patent and commercialise it.

He pre-empts the standard defence — that peptides are too cheap to be patentable. His answer: you can always patent a **novel delivery system or proprietary blend** and brand it. These trials are also unusually cheap to run, since the compound exists and willing subjects are plentiful; even absent commercial interest, research institutes would be on it. So years of silence plus cancelled or unpublished trials reads to him as evidence the human results were not there.

On **BPC-157** specifically, even the rodent evidence is thin: almost all of it comes from a **single Croatian lab** (the transcript's "Securiche et al." is an auto-caption mangling; the real name isn't confirmable from the description, so it's left as-is). The scant human data are things like two-person uncontrolled observations of growth hormone rising.

## Production and contamination
Without FDA approval these carry none of the associated manufacturing standards. He is careful not to treat approval as a general quality guarantee — his point is narrower, that for **production standards** it helps. Peptides bought online are labelled for research or animal use, and across supplements and underground-lab drugs the documented rate of adulteration, mislabelling and underdosing means the **majority** of products are not what the label says.

## Why the anecdotes don't move him
The only evidence in favour is the volume of enthusiastic user reports, some from well-informed evidence-based people. Two reasons he discounts them.

First, 15 years of pattern recognition: many hyped compounds generated exactly this testimony and then vanished. He compares supplement fads to fashion.

Second, and more specific: the main claimed benefit is **recovery and pain relief**, the domain with the strongest documented placebo effect. Massage guns, cupping and cold plunges all had people swearing by them and all failed once placebo was controlled for. That makes anecdote structurally unreliable for exactly this class of claim.

He closes honestly — he'd like BPC-157 to work, since it would let him train kickboxing without constant injury — but won't publicly endorse injecting compounds with **no human safety or efficacy data**, while acknowledging it's the individual's choice.

## Note on the evidence
This is a negative review, mostly an argument from missing evidence plus the general translation-failure and adulteration literature. The cited sources support the **background rates**, not any specific finding about BPC-157 or the GH secretagogues. Fair use of them, and he says as much — but nothing cited here tests these peptides directly.
