# Why Copying Bodybuilders Fails: Five Practices That Backfire

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "5 Popular bodybuilding tips that are HURTING your gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-bDRL2U3rKc)) · 2024-08-02 · 12 min
**Topic:** Evidence & methodology

## Bottom line
- **There is no bodybuilding consensus to copy.** What looks like consensus is the echo-chamber effect; the actual practices have swung dramatically — full-body training was universal until the 1950s, and low-carb and ketogenic diets have repeatedly been the norm.
- **The biggest lifters are almost invariably enhanced,** and enhanced bodybuilders have measurably different training, supplement and diet practices — more intensity techniques, more cardio.
- **Five practices that research finds counterproductive:** sloppy/partial-range technique; very short rest intervals (especially pre-contest); training only in the "6-12 hypertrophy zone"; constant failure training and post-failure techniques; and very high cardio volumes.
- **The reason experience fails here is feedback.** Chess has Elo, investing has return on investment; bodybuilding has months-delayed, confounded, visually-estimated feedback. You can see what works, but not what works *best*.
- Practitioners *did* beat scientists twice: on **high protein intake** and on **anabolic steroids**.

## The epistemics: why "the big guys know" fails
This is the part of the video doing the real work, and it's a methodological argument rather than a training one. He grants that in many domains copying elite practitioners is efficient and sensible. The disqualifier for bodybuilding is feedback quality. Chess gives you a rating and now an engine that scores individual moves; investing gives you a percentage return that is agnostic to starting capital. Bodybuilding gives you a visual estimate over months, on an outcome confounded by starting muscle mass, genetics, and drug use — and even in research, circumference measurements are unreliable.

He also attacks the premise directly: there is no consensus to copy. He cites Jamie Lewis's surveys of the strongest and biggest lifters as finding far more differences than similarities — some elite powerlifters barely benched, many barely deadlifted outside the final weeks before competition. If practitioners can't agree that you must train the competition lift to be good at the competition lift, there is no stable practitioner wisdom to defer to.

## The five practices
**1. Technique and range of motion.** He argues many pros ego-lift and cut range, and that full range — particularly the deep-stretch, long-muscle-length portion — is strongly productive. The direct evidence he offers is the attentional-focus trial: lifters told to focus on the biceps and use strict technique gained more than those told simply to get the weight up ([Schoenfeld 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29533715/)). He names Arnold's curls as the example, while conceding Arnold's status.

**2. Very short rest intervals, especially pre-contest.** The pre-contest ritual of dropping rest and raising reps to "burn fat" is, in his words, conclusively false; what it does do is cut growth, because longer rest lets you do more reps at the same load and therefore accumulate more tension. His actual recommendation inverts the practice: take *longer* rest during a contest diet, because you're exhausted and need it to hold performance. (No rest-interval study appears in this video's reference list — the claim is carried over from his own prior work.)

**3. The 6-12 "hypertrophy zone."** He puts the real range at roughly **5-30 reps** taken close to failure, and argues that sticking to one narrow band may be actively worse than varying rep ranges — different growth pathways, different fatigue sources allowing more total volume, and possible neural advantages ([Schoenfeld 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27042999/); [Dos Santos 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29945272/)). He is careful here: the evidence is a *trend*, not a clear finding.

**4. Training to failure and post-failure work.** He accepts that harder training on average produces better results, but rejects the extrapolation that more is always better. Constant failure training and intensity techniques cut total work capacity and have a poor stimulus-to-fatigue ratio — a particular problem for naturals, and notably a practice research finds concentrated among enhanced lifters ([Li 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36165879/); [Hackett 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22990567/)).

**5. High-volume cardio.** One to two hours a day pre-contest triggers the interference (concurrent training) effect, reducing growth and strength, and eats recovery capacity that a natural lifter cannot spare. For naturals, cardio should be a last resort rather than a first instinct.

## Where he lands
Not "science only." He credits practitioners with establishing that strength training builds muscle, high protein augments it, steroids augment it further, and hard training pays — and notes scientists were slow on protein and steroids, though he adds the fair caveat that serious exercise and nutrition science barely existed before the 1990s. His position is that the balance has tipped: for *directional* questions, experience is fine; for *optimisation* questions, only research can settle them.

**Overreach to note:** "this has been conclusively shown to be false" (short rest intervals burning fat) and the interference-effect claim are both stated more strongly than the reference list in this video supports — the interference material rests on one 2004 combined-training study plus two unresolved abstracts.
