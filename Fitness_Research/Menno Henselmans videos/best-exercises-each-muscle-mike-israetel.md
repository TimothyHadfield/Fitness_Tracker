# Mike Israetel's Best Exercise for Every Muscle

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The BEST Exercises for Each Muscle – Mike Israetel" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vgZbaVhIMAs)) · 2025-02-27 · 34 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- One rubric throughout: **full range of motion with a long lengthened component, loadable, easy to progress, repeatable**, and hardest where the muscle is longest.
- Headline picks: **belt squat** (quads and calves), **stiff-leg deadlift** (hamstrings), **loadable crunch machine** (abs), **front-foot-elevated Smith lunge** (glutes), **pull-ups, any grip** (lats), **Smith inverted row** (rear delts), **cambered bar bench** (pecs), **deep skull crushers** (triceps), **lying/incline and behind-the-back cable curls** (biceps).
- He **doesn't isolate traps, rear delts or front delts on purpose**, puts almost all shoulder volume into side delts, and says erectors need no direct work for bodybuilding.
- He states outright that he is **"pretty much by myself in the evidence-based community"** on using pump and soreness as proxies for stimulus — and most of his picks rest on exactly those proxies. Nippard's tier lists use a near-identical rubric but treat feel as a tiebreaker, not evidence; that single difference explains most of the divergences below.
- **The video cites no studies.** Every claim is mechanism plus coaching experience.

## Muscle by muscle, against the Nippard rankings

**Calves.** Any **straight-leg calf raise that unloads the spine** — specifically a belt squat calf raise off a ledge, very deep, two-second hold at the bottom, up only halfway, short rests, myo-rep style. Anyone who has only done seated bent-leg raises gets "the first calf gains of your life."
→ *Agrees.* Nippard's 2026 position is that standing straight-leg beats seated bent-leg for the gastrocnemius (Kinoshita 2023), and his non-negotiable cue is a **1–2 second pause in the stretched bottom position**. The belt squat version appears nowhere in our material; "belt squat" has zero hits across the library.

**Hamstrings.** A **properly executed stiff-leg deadlift** or good morning (one movement to him), which almost nobody does right: people round the back, unlocking the hips and taking tension off the hamstrings, and don't go low enough. Done right, **two or three sets per session** is enough. Leg curls are unnecessary for beginners and intermediates, worth adding for complete development or stage prep; his split is hinges one day, curls the other. Time-constrained and aesthetics-only, **curls beat hinges** on stimulus-to-fatigue ratio, especially lengthened.
→ *Partial clash.* Nippard makes knee flexion structurally required because the **biceps femoris short head only crosses the knee** and hip extension never trains it. His seated leg curl is a top-10 exercise overall, on a 12-week study finding ~50% more growth from seated versus lying curls. Telling beginners they can skip curls is the weakest-supported thing he says here.

**Quads.** A **good belt squat**: all the way down, sit two seconds, up two-thirds, back down slowly, myo-rep style — close to failure, lock out, breathe 5–10 seconds, repeat, three rounds. Many people can get a very good quad workout from **one set like that**. Rationale: axial fatigue is non-existent because the load hangs at the hips, systemic fatigue is low, and with the knees well over the toes it's almost purely quads. Best pound-for-pound stimulus-to-fatigue quad exercise he's found.
→ *Same logic, different exercise.* Nippard crowns the **hack squat**, and his reason for preferring it over the barbell squat is exactly Israetel's: less stabilisation and axial cost for the same quad tension. The belt squat is the missing rung. Two gaps: Israetel never mentions the **leg extension**, which Nippard treats as necessary for the rectus femoris (it shortens at the hip on every squat pattern), and "one set" sits far below Nippard's 12–18 weekly sets.

**Abs.** He isn't sure most people should train abs at all. When he does: a **crunch machine with a selector stack**, the rare ab exercise you can genuinely load and progress. Also weighted 45° inverted bench, **ab wheel rollouts (phenomenal**, though they tax the triceps long head and lats and are hard to progress), and a cable rope over the shoulders crunching over a ball. On planks: *"such a goddamn waste of time for physique and strength people in most cases."*
→ *Agrees on the principle, clashes on planks.* Nippard's pick is the **weighted cable crunch** for the identical reason, and he ranks the ab wheel top for rectus abdominis activation (Youdas 2008). But he programs three patterns — weighted flexion, **anti-rotation** (Pallof press) and **anti-extension** (plank) — with a specific rescue for the plank's ease (elbows to eye level, squeeze into posterior pelvic tilt, per Schoenfeld 2014). Israetel discards the category.

**Glutes.** **Front-foot-elevated Smith machine lunges**, justified "from an experiential perspective": instantaneous-onset soreness, glutes plus quads plus adductors, a raw stimulus magnitude nothing else matches. Hip thrusts are the best near-isolation glute movement, with three problems: you must load an absurd amount of weight, it's isolation when you could be training more, and **the force curve is backwards**. He wants a **cam-leveraged hip thrust machine that's hard at the bottom and easy at the top**.
→ *Strong convergence.* Nippard's glute S-tier includes the **front-foot-elevated Smith lunge** by name (his overall pick is the walking lunge). The barbell hip thrust sits at high B with almost the same charge sheet — hip pinch, setup time, no deep stretch because even a deep thrust reaches only ~90° of hip flexion against the ~130° available — while the **machine version is high A**, exactly where Israetel's imagined machine points. Gap: Nippard has **machine hip abduction in S tier** for upper glutes; Israetel never mentions abduction.

**Erector spinae.** *"There's probably no reason to train your erectors for bodybuilding"* — a line he contradicts a minute later, admitting he has "gnarly gigantic" erectors and sometimes wants them bigger, "for vibes." For robustness he likes **dynamic spinal flexion** — cable, dumbbell and barbell **flexion rows** where you deliberately bend at the spine, plus **Jefferson curls**, light and high-rep to start. He credits this with fixing back problems he had as a powerlifter: being strong only in a locked position leaves you fragile the moment something unlocks you. **Back extensions he dismisses** — not heavily loadable, force curve wrong (easy at the bottom, hard at the top), and they don't train the rest of the back.
→ *Direct clash.* The library has the **barbell 45° hyperextension as "the most underrated exercise"** and the **45° back extension in glute S tier**, framed as hip extension rather than a low-back movement — the exercise Israetel writes off. Nippard also instructs balancing ab work with an equal amount of erector work. On deliberate loaded spinal flexion they are furthest apart: Nippard endorses lumbar flexion for abs only, at moderate-to-light load; Israetel programs it as a health intervention.

**Lats.** **Pull-ups, any grip.** Chin to the bar, milk the eccentric especially through the bottom two-thirds, accept few reps. His entire justification is DOMS and pump: "I've tried them all, nothing compares." He refuses the word "chin-up" — supination is one grip among several, there is no wrong answer, and **variation is part of the right answer**.
→ *Real disagreement.* Nippard crowns the **chest-supported row**, and his S tier is six pulldown and row variations. **Pull-ups sit at A**, docked for a resistance profile that's easy at the bottom and hard at the top; the **chin-up drops to B** for the back because the supinated grip shifts tension to the biceps. On grips Israetel gets support — Massey 2016 found no significant difference between four pull-up variations — but Nippard's advice is the opposite of variation: pick one grip and hold it eight weeks. And the criterion Israetel uses to elevate pull-ups, soreness magnitude, is precisely the proxy Nippard's rubric excludes.

**Rear delts.** Isolate only if lagging. His favourite is a **Smith machine or racked barbell inverted row**, body planked, pulling to between chest and throat height with the arms sweeping back — the only thing that gives him a rear delt pump, with deep tension at the stretch and easier leverage at the contraction. He **hates the reverse pec deck**: "it almost does nothing for me at all, it ends up being a back exercise." He treats rear delts like front delts and rarely trains either deliberately, because full-range back work already gives him ~18 sets of rear delts a week. **Almost all shoulder volume goes to side delts**, which get little from pressing and pulling and carry the most visual payoff.
→ *The sharpest clash in the video.* Nippard's rear delt #1 is the **reverse cable crossover**, with the **reverse pec deck sitting sideways, one arm at a time, also in S tier** — the machine Israetel says does nothing. More pointedly, Nippard's most-repeated rear delt error is *"turning the movement into a row by over-retracting"*, because rear delts need transverse abduction rather than a straight pull back; Israetel's favourite rear delt exercise is a row. On isolation Nippard is emphatic the other way: EMG has the reverse pec deck beating the seated row, and his baseline is 8+ weekly isolation sets. On side delts they agree completely — Nippard's notes cite Israetel's own 70–90% figure.

**Traps.** He almost never isolates them; he tried for eighteen months, they got bigger, he stopped. Low-angle rowing covers mid and lower traps; upper traps get plenty from **side delt work**. His strongest point is procedural: **never try to de-emphasise your traps during lateral raises.** Neural drive is bandwidth-limited, and a lifter juggling "focus on side delts, don't use traps, control the eccentric" ends up using 5 kg instead of 10 kg. The myth that activity in one muscle comes at another's expense is wrong and often reversed: strong triceps let you drive the concentric harder so the pecs eat a heavier eccentric. "Taking over" usually means you need more focus on the target, or better technique.
→ *Clash on isolation.* Nippard argues traps do need direct work: deadlift trap involvement is **isometric**, eccentric actions appear necessary to maximise hypertrophy, so **6–10 extra weekly isolation sets**, trap bar shrug as the pick. His parallel case is Conley 1997 on the neck — a compound-only program produced **zero** change in neck cross-sectional area while nine weekly sets of direct work produced 13%.

**Pecs.** **Cambered bar bench presses** — "goated beyond recognition" — for roughly three extra inches of range on a movement that's stable, loadable and technically repeatable. Then **properly executed incline dumbbell presses**: the proximal edge of the dumbbell touches the proximal edge of the bicep, about three inches deeper than most people go. Also deficit push-ups, forward-leaning dips, and a press machine that travels deep. He defends the bench hard — almost everyone who dislikes it isn't arching, retracting, controlling the eccentric or pausing.
→ *Agrees, and fills a gap.* Nippard's crowned best is the **machine chest press** (matching Israetel's praise for a deep machine), with **bench and incline dumbbell both at A**. Nippard's specific deduction against the bench is that the bar stops on the ribcage so the stretch isn't as deep as dumbbells — precisely the problem the cambered bar solves. **The cambered bar bench appears nowhere in our Nippard material.**

**Triceps.** Isolation is required for the biggest arms. **Skull crushers** across the spectrum — barbell, Smith, inverted, incline — because the force curve is near perfect, especially inverted (easier at the top, harder at the bottom); his most extreme version pauses the bar **at the throat**. **Pushdowns are awesome**, but he dislikes rope pushdowns for emphasising the contraction, the least useful part. **He no longer does overhead work himself** — his arms are too big to get into position, which he says is why you rarely see pro-level guys doing it — though his prescription for others still rotates pushdowns, skulls, incline variations and overhead. Dips are excellent but roughly 50/50 with chest unless the torso is planked vertical and the elbows slide back.
→ *Clash with the library's strongest finding, but softer than it looks.* Nippard's #1 is the **overhead cable extension**, resting on Maeo 2023 finding roughly **40% more triceps growth than pushdowns** over 12 weeks — the strongest single exercise-selection result in the library. Israetel dropping overhead work is personal, not prescriptive; his advice to others still includes it. They agree exactly on the EZ-bar skull crusher (S tier for Nippard) and on rope pushdowns being the weaker pushdown.

**Biceps.** Barbell curls work, but: **backwards force curve**, incomplete range, and heavy standing sets fatigue the low back. Pushing the elbows forward improves the force curve at the cost of the stretch. His picks are **deep incline curls**, **lying dumbbell curls on a bench**, and **free-motion behind-the-back cable curls done with both arms** — step forward so the cable pulls back at the bottom, go far behind the back, finish easy at the top. Low systemic fatigue, easy myo-reps. He hedges honestly: "probably my favourite all-time bicep exercise — I'm not saying it's the best."
→ *Close agreement.* His two-arm behind-the-back cable curl is a two-armed **Bayesian curl**, Nippard's 2024 #1 and his must-include for the long head. Barbell curls are B tier for Nippard ("technically C+, bumped for accessibility") on the identical complaint — tension drops to zero at the bottom where the biceps are most stretched. One divergence: Nippard puts the **45° preacher curl** above incline curls, citing Zabaleta-Korta 2023 finding significantly more distal biceps growth from preachers, and by 2026 makes the preacher his single pick. Israetel's hedge pre-empts it.

## Where they disagreed

Menno interviews far more than he argues — this is Israetel talking for most of 34 minutes. Three moments are genuine pushback:

- **The hip thrust force curve.** Menno counters "the force curve is backwards" with the Contreras position that you get the **highest active mechanical tension in the glutes at the top**, so peak difficulty there may be a feature. Israetel half-concedes — "if the force curve was half as extreme that might be the thing" — then argues passive tension also grows muscle and lengthened tension is now so dominant it's hard to say how much top-end active tension offsets it. Menno pushes once more ("is it not a balance thing"), and rather than answering, Israetel volunteers that this is where he stands **"pretty much by myself in the evidence-based community"** with his pump and soreness proxies. That is him naming the epistemic gap between his rankings and this library's.
- **Soreness as evidence.** When Israetel attributes glute/ham-tie-in soreness to lengthened-position training, Menno offers the alternative — it's a **repeated bout effect**, people don't do those exercises often enough to adapt — and separately notes there is no "glute-ham tie-in" muscle, it's just glute. Israetel concedes the anatomy and keeps the lengthened-tension explanation.
- **The word "chin-up."** Trivial, but Menno uses it and Israetel objects: the movement is a pull-up and supination is just a grip.

Menno's one other contribution is supporting rather than adversarial: research finds compound exercises that pull the elbow back train the rear delts as effectively as standard external-rotation rehab work. He names no study.

## Where he overreaches

- **No references at all**, for a video this dense with ranking claims. The picks most driven by his proxies — **belt squat calves and quads, front-foot-elevated lunges, pull-ups** — are justified with a variant of "the soreness is insane" or "nothing else gives me that pump." That proxy has a well-known confound, novelty; he raises the repeated bout effect himself when Menno prompts him, then doesn't apply it to his own rankings.
- **"One set like that is a very good workout for many people"** (belt squat myo-reps for quads) sits far below any volume prescription in this library, and is given without qualification.
- Naming caution: the transcript is auto-captioned and mangles equipment brands. One manufacturer he criticises could not be identified with confidence and has been left out.
