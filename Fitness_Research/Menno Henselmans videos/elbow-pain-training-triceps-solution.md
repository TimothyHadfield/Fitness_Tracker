# Elbow Pain from Triceps and Pressing Work: Six Modifications

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Elbow pain when training triceps? This is the solution" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zlGEliRQBsc)) · 2024-07-04 · 12 min
**Topic:** Training

> **Read this first.** This is injury content and it is thin on evidence. Henselmans states his sources up front: his own elbow injuries, over a decade of coaching, and the injury-management literature. Only **one substantive study** is cited, and it supports a single background claim. Everything else here is coaching practice. Persistent, worsening, or nerve-type elbow pain — numbness, tingling, weakness, pain at rest or at night, pain following a specific traumatic event — needs a physiotherapist or physician, not a video. These modifications are for managing a nagging, load-dependent ache, and they are not a diagnosis.

## Bottom line
- **Go higher in reps.** Connective tissue needs roughly **70% of 1RM** (≈12RM) to be robustly stressed ([Bohm 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-015-0009-9)); staying at **sets of 20-30** loads the muscle while sparing tendon and ligament. Muscle growth holds down to about **30% of 1RM** if you take sets close to failure.
- **Don't train in the middle.** Either train hard on a pain-free variation, or don't train that movement at all. Medium-effort, 5-10 RIR training aggravates the injury *and* builds nothing.
- **Keep forearms vertical** and **widen your bench grip** — both cut elbow torque at little cost to triceps stimulus.
- **Swap the exercises with bad reputations.** Skull crushers out, skull-overs in. Rotating implements (cables, dumbbells) over fixed bars.
- The governing rule: **train the maximum amount that is genuinely pain-free**, and lift the *least* weight that still stimulates the muscle.

## 1. Higher reps (the one evidence-backed claim)

Connective tissue has lower plasticity than muscle — tendons and ligaments adapt more slowly. The threshold for robust tendon adaptation is around **70% of 1RM**, so training below it doesn't stress connective tissue much. That is [Bohm 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-015-0009-9), and it is the only place in this video where a claim rests on cited research.

Note what he does with it: the paper is about how to *build* tendon, and he inverts it to find a load range that *avoids* stressing tendon. Reasonable, but his inference rather than the paper's finding.

He uses **sets of 30** as his ceiling — partly tedium, partly because it isn't clear maximum hypertrophy survives above 30 reps. Blood flow restriction lets you drop the load further still. The cost is strength, which he argues most lifters are irrationally attached to on isolation exercises where it transfers to nothing.

## 2. Hard or not at all

Any pain beyond ordinary muscular exertion is a stop signal by default; ignoring pain is something only knowing long-term injury management permits.

The consequence: if you can't do sets of 30 close to failure on a movement even after every modification here, **don't do that movement** — train other muscles. His coaching observation is that people who compromise (moderate load, 5-10 reps from failure) get the worst of both worlds: still aggravating the injury, still not growing.

## 3. Controlled tempo

The more uncontrolled the movement, the more load lands on connective tissue. On the eccentric the muscle should be the brake; if it isn't, the tendon is. He also suggests avoiding full elbow lockout while injured — the joint handles lockout fine in general, but it can irritate an already-sore elbow and adds nothing to hypertrophy.

## 4. Vertical forearms and wider grips

A forearm angled away from vertical creates a moment arm at the elbow; a vertical forearm makes that torque nearly zero. On dumbbell bench people naturally flare into a triceps-assisting position to move more weight — but dumbbell bench is a poor triceps exercise anyway, so vertical forearms cost almost nothing and help the elbow a lot.

Same logic on barbell bench: **go wider**, even with hands outside the elbows. He argues (from a previous video) that grip width costs much less triceps activation on a fixed implement than on dumbbells, because horizontal force production still recruits the triceps. So the trade is favourable.

## 5. Grip and wrist

Rotating implements — cables, dumbbells — are easier on the elbow than a barbell or a machine that locks wrist and elbow into a fixed path. Start from the most natural shoulder-elbow-wrist alignment, usually a **neutral / hammer grip**, then rotate slightly to find what feels clean.

He also recommends **direct wrist flexion work** for weak forearms: when the wrist collapses back under a bench press, the forearm leaves vertical and elbow torque rises.

His closing caveat is the most honest line in the video: **individual pain signals trump everything else he has said.** He notes he personally finds fully supinated pushdowns easier than regular ones, which is counterintuitive, and goes with it anyway.

## 6. Exercise swaps

Presented explicitly as trends worth trying first, not rules.

**Chest — easier:** cable chest presses, dumbbell presses with vertical elbows, flyes, convergent chest press machines. **Rougher:** barbell bench (better wide), push-ups. He likes suspended ring/TRX push-ups — harder, which serious lifters need, and easy on wrist and elbow because the handles rotate.

**Triceps — be wary:** skull crushers, which he calls "elbow crushers." Overhead extensions can also bite unless taken into a genuine full overhead stretch rather than a half-press, with no lockout. **Try:** skull-overs; pushdowns with a rope or single handle rather than a straight bar; and for severe pain **kickbacks** — which he dislikes generally (no stretch, cramped) but concedes are often the last exercise that doesn't hurt.

He notes a strict overhead extension creates near-automatic blood flow restriction, so very light loads give a large pump — useful when the goal is the lowest load that still trains the muscle hard.

## Where the evidence stops

Only tip 1 has a citation. The vertical-forearm and wide-grip torque arguments are biomechanically plausible but uncited. The exercise rankings, the skull crusher warning, the "hard or nothing" rule and the wrist flexion advice are all coaching experience presented as trends. That is a legitimate way to give advice, and he says as much; it just shouldn't be recorded as evidence. Nothing here establishes that any of it *treats* an injury, only that it may let you keep training around one.
