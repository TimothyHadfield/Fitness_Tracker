# Do Women Recover Faster Than Men?

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Do women recover faster than men?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IC_-wHBft-8)) · 2023-01-01 · 6 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- His position: **women probably do recover somewhat faster, but the difference is small** — worth a few extra sets per week, not an extra training day or double the volume.
- **Acute fatigue resistance in women is well established** and he treats it as settled: women complete more reps at a given percentage of 1RM (his example: 60%) and show a smaller drop-off in reps across sets.
- **Recovery over hours and days is not settled.** He can recall multiple studies showing women recovering faster and none showing the reverse — but says explicitly this is from memory, not a systematic search.
- He **disagrees with the authors** of the study he reviews, which concluded there is no sex difference in recovery. He argues it was underpowered and that the raw pattern points the other way.
- Programming implication: assess a **female client's actual work capacity** against what he'd expect for a woman, and if it holds up, add a small amount of volume relative to a comparable man.

## The study and his reading of it
Men and women performed four sets to failure at the same RM load, with performance retested at 4, 24 and 48 hours — each time point on a separate occasion so one test wouldn't contaminate the next. Its title, as he quotes it, is that sex differences may exist for *performance fatigue* but not *recovery*.

The first half he accepts: women fatigued less within the session, consistent with the broader literature.

The second half he rejects. On **biceps curls**, men showed roughly -7% at 4 hours, -6% at 24 hours, and +0.7% at 48 hours — a textbook curve implying men needed about 48 hours to recover from four sets to failure. Women showed no meaningful decrement even at 4 hours and were *above* baseline at 24 hours, which he reads as supercompensation rather than mere recovery.

On **leg extensions** the picture is far weaker, and he says so. Men were down 3.4% at 4 hours, women 3.8% — effectively identical. At 24 hours women were +8% against men's +3%, so both had recovered, with a slightly larger rebound in women. Then at 48 hours the men held steady at +2.7% while the women fell from +7.9% to -2.4%, appearing to re-fatigue with no intervening stimulus. He calls this nonsense, and evidence that the data are extremely noisy.

## The statistical objection
Standard deviations in reps performed were close to 10, while the recovery differences under discussion are one or two reps. On that basis he argues the study was **woefully underpowered** to detect a recovery difference, and that a null result here is not evidence of absence.

## Where his reasoning is vulnerable
He uses the underpowering argument in one direction only. Having established that the point estimates are too noisy to trust — and having shown a women's 48-hour value that is biologically incoherent — he then reads the biceps curl point estimates as supporting faster female recovery. If the noise invalidates the authors' null, it also weakens his positive inference. He concedes the literature "is not really clear," but the conclusion he lands on rests substantially on unaided recall rather than the data he just examined.

One limitation of scope worth holding: both exercises here were **moderate-load sets to failure** on single-joint movements. Nothing in this video speaks to recovery from heavy loads, and the acute rep-endurance advantage he cites is itself a moderate-load phenomenon.
