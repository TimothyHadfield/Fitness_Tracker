# Training Around an Injury: Seven Strategies, Ranked by Risk-Reward

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "7 Essential Injury Management Tips for Lifters" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r74D5KjIrFk)) · 2025-08-19 · 10 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- The organising principle for all seven: **cut the mechanical stress on the damaged tissue while preserving the stimulus to the muscle.** Train *around* the injury, don't stop training.
- **Best risk-reward: change the exercise** so the movement is pain-free. Losing some optimality for the target muscle costs little; training through pain turns a one-week nuisance into a months-long injury.
- **Second best: go higher-rep.** Connective tissue reportedly needs ~**70% of 1RM** to be robustly loaded, while muscle grows equally well from ~**30% of 1RM** near failure. Sets of ~30 give the same per-set hypertrophy at a fraction of the tendon and joint stress.
- **Third: slow the tempo and pause at both ends.** Tempo barely affects hypertrophy, so slowing the eccentric cuts load further — combined with high reps, often **half** the weight of a normal set of 15.
- **Worst risk-reward: cutting volume or backing away from failure.** These reduce injury stress and gains roughly proportionally. His view: 5 RIR is the worst of both worlds — still aggravating the tissue, no longer stimulating the muscle.
- Warm-ups get a "might help, won't hurt" recommendation — he says plainly there is **almost no hard data** that they reduce injury risk.
- **Blood flow restriction** at ~30% 1RM is highly effective but he treats it as out of scope.

## The scope he sets, and its limits
[00:00] Two assumptions up front, and they matter more than the tips. First, he assumes a **true biomechanical injury** — actual tissue damage. He acknowledges some pain is psychogenic or strongly psychosomatic with no damage present, argues that for heavy, high-volume trainees a genuine biomechanical component is usually there, and says most of the advice is the same either way. Second, he says explicitly this is **general advice** and that you should **see a professional about the specifics of your injury**.

He also opens by claiming "widespread consensus in evidence-based physiotherapy that most injuries can be managed quite well at home if you know what you're doing." That consensus is asserted, not cited. The whole video rests on **one narrative review** — the weakest tier of review evidence, with no systematic search or risk-of-bias assessment. Read the technique advice below as informed coaching practice, not tested findings.

## 1. Modify exercise selection (mechanism plus practice)
[01:01] The examples are biomechanically reasoned rather than trial-tested:
- **Knee pain in squats** — switch from high-bar or front squat to a hip-dominant **low-bar** squat where you sit back and the knee doesn't travel forward.
- **Shoulder pain in pressing** — tuck the elbows, reduce abduction and internal rotation. For lateral raises, hinge forward slightly into the **scapular plane** and rotate the thumb up ("full can").
- **Bench and overhead press** — a **closer grip** generally reduces shoulder pain.
- **Elbow pain from pressing or pulling** — a **wider grip**, which he says costs little arm stimulus.

He repeats "try it out, see if that's true for your injury" after each, which is the right caveat.

[03:03] The review also suggests **reducing range of motion**. He prefers swapping the exercise instead, but concedes ROM restriction is useful where an injury is sensitive to one specific position.

## 2. Warm up more thoroughly (weak evidence, and he says so)
[03:33] Theory says warm-ups raise tissue elasticity and reduce tearing risk. He notes the researchers themselves flag the near-absence of empirical support. His justification is purely that it's cost-free and anecdotally people hurt less when fully warm.

## 3. Higher repetitions (his favourite)
[04:03] The load-threshold argument is the crux: connective tissue needs roughly **70% of 1RM** for robust adaptation, muscle roughly **30%**. Below ~70%, tendons and joints tolerate the work well. So a set of 30 near failure gives set-for-set equivalent hypertrophy with far less connective-tissue load.

The ~30% 1RM hypertrophy threshold is well supported in the wider literature. **The ~70% connective-tissue threshold is the load-bearing claim here and carries no citation** — treat it as a working heuristic, not an established number.

[05:34] His analogy: a healthy tendon is a sheet of paper you can pull hard on; a damaged one has a hole in it, and tension tears the hole wider. He calls it simplistic himself.

## 4. Slower tempo (mechanism, extrapolated)
[07:07] Because tempo has little influence on hypertrophy, slowing the eccentric and pausing at both ends achieves the same growth stimulus with a much lighter load. The goal when injured is to **make the lowest possible weight still effective**.

## 5 & 6. Less volume, more reps in reserve (last resort)
[07:38] Both work, both cost gains proportionally. His practice-based objection to the "sensible compromise" of 5 RIR is sharp: motivated lifters end up **irritating the tissue just enough to prevent healing while getting no meaningful stimulus**. If you can't do an exercise with high reps, controlled tempo and good technique, his view is you shouldn't do it at all.

## 7. Blood flow restriction / KAATSU
[08:49] ~30% of 1RM with limb wraps that are not painful, cause no bruising or tingling, and merely reduce how much you can lift. Added metabolic stress, hypoxia and muscle activation give robust adaptation from very light loads. He skips implementation detail on the grounds most viewers won't do it — so this note carries no protocol, and the "highly effective" verdict comes with no evidence discussed.

## When to see a clinician
*Not from the video, but the video assumes a diagnosis the reader doesn't have.* "Manage it at home" is only safe once you know what you're managing. Get assessed before applying any of the above if you have: numbness, tingling or weakness; a sudden pop or tear sensation, or immediate loss of function; visible swelling, bruising or deformity; pain at rest or waking you at night; pain after a fall or impact; joint locking, giving way or catching; or pain not improving over a few weeks of modified training. Henselmans says to seek a professional — this is that instruction spelled out.

## Flags
- Everything traces to **one narrative review**. Where the review is quoted (ROM reduction, the warm-up evidence gap, volume and RIR reduction), that's fine. Where he departs from it — the grip and stance swaps, the 5-RIR argument, preferring substitution over ROM restriction — he is explicit that these are his own experience, which is good practice, but keep the two apart.
- "Widespread consensus in evidence-based physiotherapy" and the **70% of 1RM** connective-tissue threshold are the two uncited claims doing the most work in the video.
