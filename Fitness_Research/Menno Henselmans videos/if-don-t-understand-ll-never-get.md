# Progressive Overload Is a Measure, Not a Cause

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "If you don't understand this, you'll never get stronger" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oEazIfq_4wE)) · 2024-01-19 · 11 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Progressive overload does not cause your gains.** The workout plus recovery causes the adaptation; progressive overload is how you *measure* whether that happened.
- The "many equally valid methods of overload" idea is wrong. **Adding load, or failing that, adding reps** is the only method that reliably measures progression.
- **Adding sets is not progressive overload.** Adding a rep or a bit of weight *maintains* the same relative stimulus; adding a set *increases* total training stress — a different thing entirely, and one that can dig you deeper into a recovery hole.
- **Shortening rest intervals is fundamentally unsound as a progression measure** — it may be tracking endurance adaptations (cardiorespiratory, capillarisation, mitochondrial density), not strength or size.
- **Increasing rep duration** is impractical, degrades technique, and nobody actually holds a metronome to a near-failure squat.
- If you're progressing on load or reps, don't program-hop. If you're not, change the program.

## The two misconceptions he's attacking
He is explicit that these appear on many websites, in academic textbooks, in some scientific articles, and on Wikipedia:
1. That progressive overload *causes* muscle growth and strength development.
2. That progressive overload can be implemented by any of a menu of equally effective methods — load, reps, shorter rest, more sets, higher frequency, or anything else that makes the workout harder.

Both, he says, are incorrect.

## What overload actually is
Formalised in post-WWII rehabilitation research, but illustrated by Milo of Croton carrying a calf daily until it was a bull — a story he notes "might also be full bull."

The model is stress → alarm → recovery → supercompensation. Impose stress, the body adapts to resist it better. Repeat and the curve ratchets upward. Read the graph as muscle mass, strength or protein synthesis; it applies to all of them.

## Why overload is the measure, not the cause
Two counterexamples, and they're the core of the video.

**The beginner.** Someone doing sets of 10 push-ups every day grows from the first session, and keeps growing for a while doing sets of 10 — with no progression at all. Growth occurred without progressive overload.

**The advanced lifter.** Squatting 200 kg for 5, they add 2.5 kg and manage only 4 or 3 reps. They failed to implement progressive overload — but there was still a stress-recovery-adaptation response, and they are genuinely slightly stronger and bigger, just not by an amount the increment could detect.

So overload is a readout: it tells you a successful adaptation cycle happened. It doesn't produce one.

## Why adding sets isn't the same thing
This is the practically important half. Take the lifter stuck at 205×5 who responds by adding sets. His stimulus was already borderline; trying to add a rep or a little weight would have produced some adaptation anyway.

A set is a large increase in stimulus — a change you'd make over months, not sessions. Contractile tissue doesn't change much in the short term; it takes weeks to months to alter meaningfully. So adding a set substantially raises training stress **without measuring anything**. Being able to do a second and third set of five doesn't mean he's stronger or bigger.

Worse, it can push him past what he can recover from, turning a workout that produced a full stress-recovery-supercompensation cycle into one he never recovers from before the next session — so he arrives weaker. The giveaway he names: the only reason people can add these extra 5-rep sets is that they **rest a ridiculously long time**, which he says happens constantly in 5×5 programs.

The conceptual distinction: adding a rep or a little load **maintains the relative stimulus**. Your 8RM stays an 8RM rather than drifting to a 9RM or 10RM. That's the push-up example — if you can now do 12 but only do 10, you're training at 2 RIR, a weaker stimulus. Adding sets *raises* the relative stimulus instead. Forcing progression by adding sets when load and reps aren't moving usually just digs the recovery hole deeper.

## Rest intervals and tempo
**Shorter rest** fails twice over. Practically, you have to time it and almost nobody holds to a stopwatch accurately. Methodologically it's worse: completing the same work with less rest may reflect **endurance adaptation, not strength**. He lists candidate mechanisms — better oxygen uptake, capillarisation, higher mitochondrial density speeding substrate recovery — all of which improve with zero change in strength or size. So it can't function as a progression measure for those goals.

**Longer rep durations** technically qualify as overload but he rejects them anyway: they interfere with technique, and a 4-second eccentric is excruciating and unrealistic. What actually happens near failure is that people speed reps up. Some studies enforce tempo with a metronome; it isn't a practical or enjoyable way to train.

## Three take-homes
1. Progressive overload is **crucial**. Repeat the same weights and reps forever and you won't get far.
2. Implement it the OG way: **add load; if you can't, add reps.** Nothing else reliably tells you you're getting stronger or bigger.
3. Progress is the diagnostic. If you're not progressing, change the program — he quotes the Einstein line about insanity being repetition with expectation of a different result, and says lifters do exactly this. Conversely, if load and reps are consistently going up across your exercises, **don't program-hop or add periodisation you don't need**.

**A note on framing:** the video carries no references. The stress-recovery-supercompensation model and the claim about contractile tissue timescales are asserted from mechanism and coaching experience rather than cited studies, and the argument against rest-interval progression is a conceptual one, not an empirical finding.
