# How Many Years Steroids Cost, Read Off the Swedish Power-Athlete Cohort

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is how many years #steroids cut off your life" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F-VLJxB0hPc)) · 2024-06-26 · 5 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- He never states a number of years. The answer he arrives at is that in the data he reviews, elite strength athletes from the steroid-saturated era did **not** end up with shorter lives than the general population.
- He cites a recent review — the surname sounds like "Morrison" in the captions and is not confirmed by the description — arguing that elite athletes, the group most likely to have doped, outlive both the general population and **their own siblings**. The sibling comparison partly controls for genetics. That review attributes most steroid-linked early deaths to abuse-level dosing and mislabelled black-market compounds.
- Lindqvist 2013 followed former Swedish male elite athletes in wrestling, powerlifting, Olympic weightlifting and throwing who competed **1960–1979** — a window in which steroid use in those sports was legal in Sweden, normalised, and (in his reading) close to universal. The Olympic ban only came in 1975.
- In that cohort, yearly mortality from roughly **age 20 to 50** was modestly higher than the Swedish general population. After ~50 — post-retirement, post-use — it ran **below** the general population's.

## What the Swedish data show
The two leading contributors to the elevated mortality were **cardiovascular disease** and **suicide**. The study itself flags the suicide rate as remarkably higher than the general population and attributes it to competitive pressure rather than to the drugs. Cardiovascular disease he does tie to steroids, via raised blood pressure, worsened lipids and cardiac enlargement.

The post-50 crossover is his central point: elevated risk appears concentrated in the period of use, after which a healthier lifestyle puts the group ahead again.

## His own counter-reading
He offers the inverse framing without picking a side. These men trained, ate and lived like elite athletes for most of their lives — and roughly ten years of what was, by modern standards, light and sporadic cycling was enough to **cancel out that entire lifestyle advantage** for as long as it lasted. Doses in 1960–79 were nowhere near modern blasting-and-cruising, so the cohort is not a model for a current bodybuilder.

## Overreach
No individual in the Lindqvist cohort was measured for steroid use. "We can assume most of these guys were probably using" is the load-bearing assumption of the video, and it is an inference from the era, not a measurement.

For contrast with material already in this library: a 2024 cohort reported **2.8% mortality in AAS users versus 1.0% in matched non-users at a mean age of 27** — a during-use figure in a young population, at the opposite end of the same question from a post-50 crossover.
