# Volume Beats Effort: Two 2025 Studies on Failure Training and Set Counts

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The fastest way to build muscle: 2 new studies [2025]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zwbVE6ysQlI)) · 2025-09-30 · 10 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- A 10-week trial in trained lifters found **no difference between training at 1 RIR throughout and rotating 1–4 RIR** — but total volume was matched and the actual gap was only ~1.5 reps, so read it as underpowered, not as "effort doesn't matter."
- Failure training increases growth **if and only if it increases total repetition volume**. Going to failure early in a session costs you reps later, which is how the two approaches end up equal.
- A rigorous within-subject preprint trained one leg at **6–8 sets/week** and the other at **12–16 sets/week** for 22 weeks total. The higher volume won in the large majority of subjects.
- His practical number: for trained lifters who aren't cutting, optimal weekly volume is **closer to 16 sets per muscle than 8**.
- **You are probably further from failure than you think**, and the gap widens the further out you go and the less motivated you are. Test yourself against true failure periodically to calibrate.
- Volume is a **stronger and more reliable driver of growth than proximity to failure**; HIT-style few-hard-sets training is not the more efficient route.

## Study 1: how close to failure
Strength-trained men and women were split into two groups on an otherwise identical upper/lower split for 10 weeks. One kept 1 rep in reserve on every set; the other rotated 1–4 RIR, cycling 4→3→2→1 weekly, twice. No significant differences in bench and squat 1RM or in triceps and quadriceps ultrasound thickness.

Henselmans goes out of his way to shut down the reading circulating online — that this proves you can leave reps on the table freely:

- Multiple meta-analyses of far more studies conclude training to failure raises growth **only when it raises total repetition volume**. Here, sets × reps × load did not differ between groups.
- Ten weeks is short for trained lifters, and the realised difference in proximity to failure averaged only ~1.5 reps — inside most people's RIR estimation error. Very likely underpowered for the small effect you'd expect.
- For strength the literature is fairly clear that failure doesn't matter much. Technique, muscle activity, rate of force development and power output matter more, and none require going to failure.

His conclusion is a trade-off rather than a ranking: fewer harder sets, or more easier sets with more reps in the later sets, can produce similar results **as long as the total training stress is adequate**.

The caveat he attaches is behavioural. Most people, including serious competitive lifters, can do more reps than they believe, and the gap between estimated and actual RIR widens the further out and the less motivated the lifter — someone who believes they are 3–5 reps out may genuinely be 10 out, and no amount of sets fixes that. On those grounds he says a decent case exists for simply doing as many reps as possible on every set: crude, but robust to self-deception.

One genuinely novel finding: the lifters *improved* at estimating their RIR. Most studies find no such improvement, and trained lifters are no more accurate than untrained ones of similar motivation. His explanation is feedback — this protocol had them varying proximity to failure, so they got tested. Doing something repeatedly does not make you better at it without feedback. Hence: if you habitually stop short, occasionally push a set to true failure and fact-check yourself.

## Study 2: volume, with each lifter as their own control
Still a preprint. Strength-trained men and women trained one leg with 6–8 sets per week and the other with 12–16, for 11 weeks; then a 6–8 week block of normal training; then the same program again. Everyone trained twice weekly at 2 RIR.

The within-subject design is the point. Both legs share genetics, nutrition and motivation, and because each subject completed the protocol twice you can ask whether an individual's response is reproducible.

- Higher volume produced consistently and considerably more growth in almost all subjects. A small minority may have done better on the lower volume.
- Strength followed the same direction but was statistically murkier — expected, since short-term strength gain is heavily neurological. Over longer timeframes growth and strength correlate strongly.
- Growth was measured as both cross-sectional area and muscle thickness with similar results, which he offers against the objection that extra volume just produced edema rather than contractile tissue.

**No evidence of genetic snowflakes.** Subjects who appeared to respond better to low volume in the first block frequently did not repeat that in the second — measurement error (even ultrasound is sometimes simply wrong) plus ordinary period-to-period variation. The implication he draws is blunt: if a rigorous within-subject study with ultrasound struggles to identify individual responders, a lifter "trying a program and seeing how it goes" — no imaging, with sleep and nutrition drifting — is learning close to nothing about their own response.

## Where the reasoning is asymmetric
He explains away a null result (study 1) as underpowered, then accepts a positive result from an unreviewed preprint (study 2) at close to face value and converts it into a specific weekly set recommendation. The within-subject design genuinely is strong, but it is one unpublished study on leg training, and "closer to 16 than 8 sets" is a firmer number than that alone supports.
