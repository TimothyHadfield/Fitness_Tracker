# CNS Fatigue Is Local, Not Central

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "CNS fatigue is… not central." ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lELyQ6AmQ44)) · 2026-07-28 · 7 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- "CNS fatigue" is a misleading label. The fatigue sits in the **motor neuron**, whose cell body is in the spinal cord — so it is technically central nervous system tissue — but its effect reaches only the fibres that neuron innervates. It is **local**, not systemic.
- **Deadlifts do not "fry the CNS."** Neuromuscular fatigue after deadlifts is no greater than after squats or bench presses ([Barnes 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000002140), [Belcher 2019](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2019-0004)), and leg presses produce no more fatigue than leg extensions ([Dourado 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10286608/)).
- A 2021 meta-analysis of non-local muscle fatigue found the effect **trivial or absent for strength training** — the opposite of endurance training, where central fatigue does show up ([Behm 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01456-3)).
- What lifters actually feel after a hard session is **mental fatigue**, a subjective state — not a loss of force production. It responds to caffeine, music, or simply pushing through, not to a deload.
- Practical rule: **stop auditing your nervous system and audit your performance.** If you are still progressing in the gym, there is nothing to manage.

## Why the name misleads
The popular picture is a brain-level dysfunction that drags down the whole body — in which case a heavy leg day would weaken your biceps as much as your quads. Henselmans's point is that the chain from brain to muscle has several links, and the fatigued link in resistance training is the motor neuron's axon, out at the muscle. Call it CNS fatigue if you like the taxonomy, but its consequences are confined to the trained muscle. He attributes the bulk of it to ordinary muscle damage.

## The self-test he offers
Do a lower-body workout, then test your curls. Do an upper-body workout, then test your leg extensions. If there were genuine central fatigue, the untrained muscle would be measurably weaker. In his teaching experience it is not — and when students object that they can *feel* the fatigue, he distinguishes felt fatigue from measured force loss. If your 8RM is still an 8RM, there is no neuromuscular fatigue present.

## Mental fatigue as a separate system
He frames mental fatigue as a signal that the current activity offers no instant gratification — which is why it accumulates during "have to" activities and barely registers during "want to" ones. Truck drivers get it sitting still; boredom and mental fatigue travel together. The gym is the archetypal delayed-gratification activity, so it generates plenty. In large doses it does impair performance, but the fix is psychological, not a training-stress reduction.

## Where the claim outruns the evidence
The Behm meta-analysis is about **non-local muscle fatigue** — whether training one limb impairs another — which overlaps with but is not identical to central fatigue as measured by interpolated-twitch techniques. Henselmans treats it as the "strongest refutation" of central fatigue in strength training; it is strong evidence for the practical claim, weaker as a physiological verdict. His statement that "the vast majority of CNS fatigue is related simply to muscle damage" is asserted rather than sourced.
