# Volume Distribution Across the Week: Put the Easy Day Between the Hard Ones

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This little known training tip makes a big difference" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dj8AEVUC308)) · 2025-05-05 · 6 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Choose your variables first, then let the split fall out of them.** Picking "push-pull-legs" or "upper-lower" before deciding volume and frequency is backwards — the split is an output, not an input, and often ends up with no name at all.
- The under-discussed variable is **how volume is distributed across the week**, not just how much of it there is.
- In competitive powerlifters over 6 weeks, running hypertrophy → power → strength on Mon/Wed/Fri beat hypertrophy → strength → power ([Zourdos 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001165)). Putting the *easy* day in the middle separated the two hard days and produced significantly better bench press gains, with the same direction of effect for squat, deadlift and total.
- The mechanism is mundane: **more recovery between hard days meant higher work and repetition volumes**, and the higher volumes tracked the higher strength gains.
- General rule for most lifters: **spread volume relatively evenly across the week.** If you have nine chest sets, doing all nine on one day means sets 7-9 are performed badly fatigued; moving some to other days raises total quality volume.

## The study in detail
Both groups trained Monday, Wednesday and Friday, full body, with Monday always the hypertrophy day. The distinguishing variable was whether Wednesday was the power day or the strength day.

- **Hypertrophy day:** 3-5 sets of 8 at 75% 1RM — high volume, moderate difficulty.
- **Power day:** 3-5 explosive singles at 80-90% 1RM — very easy to recover from.
- **Strength day:** 3 maximal sets at 85% 1RM — the day that matters most for powerlifters.

Same total volume, same exercises, same effort. The only difference was ordering. The group with power in the middle came into Friday's strength work fresher, did more work, and gained more. Bench press was the only lift to reach statistical significance, but the effect-size trend was consistent across squat, deadlift and total.

Henselmans thinks this is a compelling result precisely because of the population and duration: six weeks, in trained competitive powerlifters who should not be making large gains from a scheduling tweak.

## What follows for ordinary lifters
- If no single day is more important than the others — true for most recreational lifters — **distribute volume evenly** rather than concentrating it.
- This is an argument for **higher frequency per muscle group**, not more gym visits. You spread the same sets across the sessions you already do. Full-body training achieves even distribution by construction.
- **Frequency matters mainly through volume.** In most research, frequency only makes a difference to the extent it lets you accumulate more quality volume. Where higher frequency wins, it is almost always in well-trained lifters doing substantial volumes.
- The effect is **volume-dependent**. Three sets for a muscle can sit anywhere in the week; nine sets cannot.
- Bro splits are not condemned. He is explicit that beginners and most lifters will get near-identical results from one.

## A caution
This is a single 6-week study with a small sample of competitive powerlifters, and only one lift reached significance. Henselmans presents it as "compelling" and generalises from it to full-body training and frequency recommendations for recreational lifters — a longer reach than one trial supports, though he does note the wider frequency literature trends the same way.
