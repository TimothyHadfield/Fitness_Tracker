# Back Training by Muscle, Not by Movement Plane

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "If you want a bigger back, do this" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sfeWZCEeLtA)) · 2024-12-05 · 15 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Programming back work as "vertical plus horizontal pulls" is the error. The back is **erectors, upper/mid/lower traps, rear delts, lats and teres major**, with different functions and length-tension profiles.
- **Rows and pulldowns give the lats and rear delts no tension in their stretched position.** At the top of a pulldown the lats are stretched but not producing active tension; at the end of a row the force line runs straight down the arm and the rear delts are effectively stabilisers.
- **Lat prayers should be a staple** — leaning into the stretch and back at the contraction keeps tension high across the full range, which pullovers cannot do because the scapulae are pinned to the bench.
- **Rear delts need cable reverse flyes**, not dumbbell ones, which peak in the shortened position.
- **Let your shoulder blades roll forward** at the end of a row. He says the physio advice against this is wrong, and full scapular movement is what actually stretches the traps and lats.

## Traps
[01:03] Split them. **Upper traps** need a shrug variation — he suggests a wide shrug — since ordinary pulling does not cover them. **Mid traps** get scapular retraction from any row; **lower traps** need scapular depression from vertical pulls, letting the shoulder blades travel all the way up at the top.

The emphasis throughout is range of motion at the lengthened end. He is blunt that being coached to keep the back rigid and stop the shoulder blades coming forward during rows is bad advice: nobody has been injured by it, he sees no biomechanical problem, and it costs trap tension in the stretched position. Given full range plus a wide shrug, he thinks dedicated trap isolation is questionable.

## Rear delts
[02:35] His explanation for why so many lifters have underdeveloped rear delts. They perform **horizontal abduction and shoulder extension** — pulling the elbows back and out. At the end of any row or pulldown, arms in line with the force, there is no demand for either. The fix is loading in the lengthened position: **cable** reverse flyes, since dumbbell versions peak at the top.

His evidence is general: lengthened-position training has beaten shortened-position training in studies on biceps, triceps, calves, quads, glutes, hamstrings and pecs — every muscle tested. **No such study exists for the back.** He says so and argues the trend should generalise, plausibly via passive mechanical tension. He adds an independent argument for the rear delts: their sarcomere operating lengths suggest peak **active** tension when lengthened, so the case does not depend on stretch-mediated hypertrophy being real.

## Lats
[05:32] The same lengthened-position gap applies, and rows are worse than pulldowns twice over. The stretched position in a row is barely stretched — the elbow never travels above shoulder height. And once the elbow passes behind the body the **lats lose leverage entirely and reverse function**, pulling the humerus back toward the midline. That is why you go weak at the end of a full-range row, and why lats assist off the chest in a bench press: in that position they are technically a pushing muscle.

**Lat prayers** are his answer. Leaning forward into the stretch and back into the contraction lets body position modulate resistance so tension stays high through the whole range — an isokinetic machine without the machine. Too easy at the top, lean forward more; too hard, lean back. Keep the arms straight or bent at a *constant* angle, or it degenerates into a pull. Triceps involvement is a bonus: the long head is a shoulder extensor and is usually undertrained.

Two arguments for lat prayers over pullovers:
1. In a pullover the **scapulae are pinned against the bench**. The lats attach partly to the scapula, so letting the shoulder blades travel adds stretch a pullover cannot access — the same advantage push-ups have over barbell bench press, and often easier on the shoulders.
2. A pullover's peak tension is at the lengthened position only, which a lat prayer replicates by leaning further forward. The pullover therefore just **omits the first half of the movement** for no gain. He cites the recent finding that lengthened partials do not beat full ROM in trained lifters, plus the lats reaching peak active tension with the elbow at the side.

He still wants **adduction work** — moderately wide pulldowns — alongside the extension-dominant lat prayer, on functional differentiation: upper lat fibres favour shoulder extension, lower more horizontal fibres favour wide-grip pulling. He is against **very** wide grips (shoulder issues, less stretch, awkward) and behind-the-neck pulldowns (no greater activation, shoulder risk). **Teres major** needs no separate plan; it shares the lats' function.

His resulting preference: **lat prayers as the lat staple, moderately wide pulldowns, and high-row movements for traps and rear delts rather than rows for lats.** If you do row, lean forward into a diagonal pull and let the shoulder blade travel — unilateral rows allow much more drift.

## Where he outruns his evidence
He is candid that **none of the lengthened-position hypertrophy studies were done on back muscles** — the video's central recommendation is an extrapolation. The rear delt sarcomere-length argument is genuinely independent support, but it comes from modelling and architecture data ([Garner & Pandy 2003](https://doi.org/10.1114/1.1540105)), not a training study.

"Nobody has ever gotten injured letting their shoulder blades roll forward during a row" is asserted with no evidence, against standing coaching practice. The lat prayer case is biomechanical reasoning throughout — no trial compares lat prayers to pullovers or rows for lat growth, and none is claimed.
