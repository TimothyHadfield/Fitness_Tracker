# The Muscle-Growth Supplement Tier List, and Where He Disagrees With It

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "These are the top muscle growth supplements [46 studies reviewed]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AxhNj8Fng5Y)) · 2025-12-23 · 10 min
**Topic:** Supplements

## Bottom line
- A 2025 review of **46 long-term hypertrophy studies** produced a three-tier list: **Tier 1 protein**, **Tier 2 creatine**, **Tier 3 collagen+vitamin C, HMB, omega-3, citrulline**.
- Henselmans **agrees with tiers 1 and 2 and largely dissents from tier 3**. He is openly sceptical of collagen and HMB making the list at all.
- **Protein's benefit is conditional on being deficient.** Above **1.6–2.0 g/kg** total daily intake, benefits plateau completely. No RCT has found significant benefit above 1.6 g/kg.
- **Creatine is still the king** — 3–5 g/day, 1–2 kg of fat-free mass early (water, but in the muscle), then a small work-capacity benefit. He calls it "the one-eyed king in the land of the blind."
- He explicitly pushes back on two social-media trends: **20 g/day creatine** (based on "extremely scant evidence") and **creatine for cognition in healthy people** (a 2022 meta-analysis found no significant benefit in people who aren't sleep-deprived or cognitively impaired).
- **Caffeine is deliberately not on the list** — no research showing it improves long-term gains. Benefits are psychological.
- Fish oil quality is a real problem: many products **don't contain the labelled omega-3, are oxidised** (inflammatory rather than anti-inflammatory), or are **adulterated with heavy metals**.

## The tier list, with his verdicts

| Compound | Review's tier | His verdict | Evidence quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Protein powder** | Tier 1 — "the foundation" | **Agrees.** Only useful if you're below 1.6–2.0 g/kg. Great for convenience, less satiating (especially whey). | Strong; benefits plateau clearly at 1.6–2.0 g/kg. **No RCT** supports going above 1.6. Above that = "tentative small benefits" from meta-analyses only. |
| **Creatine monohydrate** | Tier 2 — "the amplifier" | **Agrees — the only real one.** 3–5 g/day (3 g women, 5 g men). Effect on long-term growth is "very slight, but there is some." | The most evidence-based supplement there is. Robust for 1–2 kg fat-free mass and work capacity. |
| **Creatine at 20 g/day** | not in the review | **Rejects.** A social-media trend. | "Extremely scant evidence." |
| **Creatine for cognition (healthy people)** | not in the review | **Rejects.** Benefits "very slim to non-existent." | 2022 meta-analysis: **no significant benefit** in people not sleep-deprived and without existing cognitive problems. Consistent benefits only in elderly, sleep-deprived, or cognitively impaired. |
| **Omega-3 (EPA+DHA)** | Tier 3 | **Qualified yes, on budget.** 1–2 g/day of actual EPA+DHA (not total fish oil) = 5–10 pills for most people. Worth it if you don't eat fish and can afford it. | Real but **small to trivial** effect sizes across meta-analyses for fat loss, muscle, strength. Indirect case (inflammation, joints, recovery, general health) is the stronger one. |
| **Citrulline** | Tier 3 | **"Maybe promising."** Best-in-class NO booster; 6–8 g an hour pre-workout adds a few reps on shorter-rest, higher-rep work. | **Every study on NO boosters has failed to find significant hypertrophy.** Mechanistic case only. |
| **Collagen + vitamin C (10–15 g/day)** | Tier 3 | **Sceptical.** Collagen is just a low-quality protein supplement. If protein and vitamin C intake are already adequate, "there really isn't any plausible mechanistic pathway." | Not claimed to improve growth directly; the case is indirect injury resistance via connective tissue. He is "not very optimistic." |
| **HMB** | Tier 3 | **Rejects.** "I was surprised to see HMB make it to the list." | **Two meta-analyses**: basically useless if protein intake is optimised. The research that drove HMB's popularity has been **formally questioned in a letter to the editors he co-signed** as "highly suspect, to say the least." |
| **Caffeine** | not on the list | **Agrees with the omission.** Take it if it makes workouts more enjoyable. | **No research showing improved long-term gains.** Benefits are mental/psychological — enjoyment of exercise. |

## Compared to this library's short list
Our working list of what works is **creatine, caffeine, protein powder, vitamin D and a multivitamin**. Three points of difference:

- **Caffeine is the sharp disagreement.** Henselmans keeps it off a muscle-growth list entirely and says its benefits are psychological, not hypertrophic. That is a narrower claim than "caffeine doesn't work" — he is talking specifically about long-term muscle gain, not acute performance — but he states it flatly and does not qualify it in this video.
- **Vitamin D and multivitamins don't appear.** The review was scoped to long-term hypertrophy outcomes, so their absence is not a negative verdict; it just means this source has nothing to say about them here.
- **Omega-3 and citrulline are additions** our list doesn't carry. He'd endorse omega-3 conditionally (budget, and only if you don't eat fish) and citrulline only as mechanistically plausible.

## The fish oil quality warning
The most immediately actionable part. Multiple studies have found the majority of fish oil supplements are **not what the label says**: most contain **less omega-3 than claimed**; many are **oxidised**, making them inflammatory and, in his words, "basically toxic" — the opposite of the intended effect; and many are **adulterated with heavy metals**. He shows a list of independently lab-verified brands on screen and states he has no affiliation with any of them.

## Why citrulline might not work even though it works
An interesting mechanistic argument he presents as speculation. Citrulline delays fatigue and adds reps. But **peripheral neuromuscular fatigue lowers the recruitment threshold of high-threshold motor units** — so a supplement that delays fatigue is effectively *reverse* blood-flow-restriction training: you may have to do more total work to reach the same recruitment. That could be why extra reps don't become extra growth. He allows the simpler explanation too: the work-capacity increase is just too small to matter.

## The closing note
Deflationary: if you want a safe, effective over-the-counter supplement that magically improves gains, you'll be disappointed. Supplements are "at best the cherry on the cake" — the cake being training, diet, sleep and stress management.

The exception he flags is pharmaceutical. **Myostatin inhibitors** are in development; he cites the CoURAGE trial, in which **trevogrumab** appears to safely reduce muscle loss during weight loss by 50%. It is injectable and phase 3 is incomplete, but he considers effective myostatin inhibitors "likely a matter of time."

**Overreach flags.** The claim that caffeine "doesn't do anything for your gains" is stated more confidently than an absence of long-term hypertrophy trials supports — no evidence of benefit isn't evidence of no benefit, and he doesn't distinguish that here. His dismissal of HMB is well-grounded (two meta-analyses plus a formal challenge to the foundational research) but he has a personal stake as a co-signatory of that letter, which he discloses. The 50% figure for trevogrumab is from an incomplete trial programme.
