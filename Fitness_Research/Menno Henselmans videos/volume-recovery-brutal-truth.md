# Facts Over Feelings: A Failed Overreaching Study and the Case Against Fatigue Panic

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Volume & Recovery: The Brutal Truth" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5EyHSymiCEo)) · 2025-10-07 · 10 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Researchers tried to induce overreaching in well-trained lifters with **5 days of 5 sets of squats to 40% velocity loss at 80% 1RM**. It failed. Strength went **up**, all recovery measures were stable or improved, and the planned taper was unnecessary ([Bell 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk10020227)).
- But perceived recovery **tanked 11%** and took nearly two weeks to normalise. **How recovered you feel and how recovered your muscles are can be completely different things.**
- Prior work agrees: powerlifters could not predict their daily 1RM performance from perceived recovery, and adjusting daily volume by ±2 sets based on feel produced **no better gains** than a fixed program ([Bartolomei 2024](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004836)).
- The latest volume meta-analysis finds gains still increasing to roughly **43 sets per muscle per week**, with **no clear plateau** — sharply diminishing returns, but not an inverted U within the studied range.
- **True overtraining is exceptionally rare in the literature.** Multiple studies have tried to induce it and failed. Loss of performance is the defining criterion: **if you're gaining strength, you are by definition not overreaching.**
- For most people the real ceiling is **psychological, logistical, and injury-related** — not neuromuscular fatigue.

> This runs against material already in this library. Nippard's junk-volume note treats ~6 sets per muscle per session as an upper threshold and 10–20 sets/week as the sweet spot; Henselmans is citing a meta-analysis showing average gains still rising at 40+ weekly sets, and is dismissive of the stimulus-to-fatigue and CNS-fatigue framing that underpins the conservative position.

## The study that didn't work
Well-trained athletes squatting about double bodyweight did a 5-day planned overreaching block: five sets at 80% 1RM to a 40% velocity loss, which on average corresponds to failure or very close. Velocity standardisation mattered because squatting to *volitional* failure means most people stop when it gets hard, and squats are always hard.

Then a two-week taper — which turned out to be pointless, because **they never overreached**. Strength improved during the five brutal days. Squat and pull strength both went up. Every recovery measure was stable or better.

The lifters, however, **felt wrecked**: perceived recovery fell 11% and took almost two full weeks to normalise.

## Lesson 1 — feelings are a bad instrument
Neuromuscular fatigue has a precise definition: **a decrease in force production**. Psychological fatigue — how unrecovered you feel — is an emotion. His argument for why they diverge: there is no internal recovery gauge sending status reports to the brain. The brain assembles a feeling from a crude mix of signals that often track stress, expectation effects and social or cognitive factors rather than actual readiness.

Two supporting studies:
- Powerlifters doing daily 1RM attempts showed **very poor correlation** between how recovered they felt, how well they predicted they'd perform, and how they actually performed.
- Letting lifters add or subtract up to two sets per session based on perceived recovery produced **no better gains** than a fixed-volume group.

He pre-empts the objection: these were competitive lifters, so "they didn't know their bodies yet" doesn't apply. On the possibility that some individuals really are good at this: "chances are you ain't that."

He isn't saying ignore how you feel — he's saying **programming decisions should be driven by objective metrics**: strength recovery short-term, strength progression and body composition long-term.

## Lesson 2 — you can handle far more volume than you think
He frames this as a mid-wit problem. Ask a random person how Ronnie Coleman got big and they'll say he trained half the day. Learn a bit of exercise science and you acquire overtraining, stimulus-to-fatigue ratio, CNS fatigue, "stimulate don't annihilate" — and you get *worse* at predicting the answer.

The meta-analytic finding: more volume produces better gains on average **up to about 43 sets per muscle per week, with no clear plateau**. He is careful — diminishing returns are very sharp, so 40 sets, or even half that, often isn't worth it. But the curve doesn't turn down. He accepts an inverted U "almost has to be true" somewhere; it just isn't showing up.

On hidden accumulating fatigue he is dismissive: "I'm personally not compelled at all by this idea of some unidentified fatigue which is not affecting performance acutely in any way but kind of sneaks up on you long term" — because exercise science has identified no such thing. Against the "only 5 days" objection: the first days of a new stimulus are the hardest, and repeated bouts are recovered from faster. He cites Raastad's daily-high-volume work (six quad sets daily) where **recovery speed improved after two weeks**.

Nor is this the first failed attempt. Multiple studies have tried to induce overreaching through extreme volume or extreme intensity (daily maximal squats and deadlifts for one to two weeks) and produced **improved or maintained strength** — by definition no accumulating fatigue.

The rule he gives clients: **loss of performance is the number one criterion**. If you're getting stronger, you've not only recovered, you've supercompensated.

## The caveats he supplies himself
- **Maximum recoverable volume is real and varies enormously between individuals.**
- Volume studies are often on **students** — young, not in a deficit, rested, not badly stressed. Recovery capacity is governed by sleep and stress, which govern how much volume you can add.
- For most people the binding constraint is **psychological and logistical**: sessions become too long or effortful, especially on three days a week. Past that, neuromuscular fatigue isn't the limiter.
- **Injuries are the bigger problem**, for him and his clients. Muscles may tolerate the volume; joints and connective tissue may not, and the gap widens as you advance because force per unit of connective tissue rises — "you become better at injuring yourself as you get stronger." His aside: untrained people with horrible technique avoid injury largely because they're too weak to injure themselves.

**Overreach flag:** the headline study is a **5-day pilot trial**, used to support a general claim about the rarity of overtraining. The converging prior failures strengthen the case, but "no clear plateau up to 43 sets/week" is a meta-analytic average across heterogeneous studies and is not a recommendation — as he says, though the framing makes that easy to miss.
