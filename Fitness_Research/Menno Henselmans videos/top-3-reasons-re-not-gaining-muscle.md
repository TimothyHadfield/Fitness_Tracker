# Three Reasons Intermediate Lifters Stop Gaining

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The Top 3 Reasons You're Not Gaining Muscle" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7ggf48G6aiI)) · 2026-03-10 · 11 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Program hopping** is the biggest one. Constant novelty keeps you in the high-muscle-damage phase, blocks the repeated bout effect, caps the volume you can tolerate, and — worst — makes it impossible to tell whether anything is working.
- **Progressive overload is the measure of your gains, not the cause.** Hitting the ninth rep tells you you recovered and adapted; it doesn't create the adaptation.
- Most gains on a new program are **neural**. 5-10% strength on a new exercise is easy and means little. **>20% on a long-standing exercise** is the signal that actually indicates hypertrophy.
- **Ego lifting** has two forms: refusing to drop the load for full ROM (often a **50% reduction** on dumbbell bench or a full squat), and **technique creep** — trading range of motion for reps once progressive overload gets hard.
- **Count fractional volume per muscle, not per body part.** "Back" and "legs" are not muscle groups. Effective sets per week per muscle is the primary determinant of hypertrophy.

## 1. Program hopping

The premise he's attacking is muscle confusion: change the program every few weeks to reinvigorate the stimulus. His view is that this gets the logic exactly backwards — **adaptation is the goal of training**, so a strategy explicitly designed to prevent adaptation is self-defeating. His line: "Muscle tissue is just a slab of meat that responds to tension. If you're implementing muscle confusion, you are the only one confused."

The mechanistic case: any novel exercise produces high muscle damage and soreness. Damage is not a good thing — it raises protein breakdown, contributes nothing to net synthesis, delays recovery, and reduces performance and muscle activation in subsequent sessions. Staying on a program lets the **repeated bout effect** blunt damage, which raises the volume you can tolerate. He cites trials where muscle-confusion groups gained no more despite averaging *higher* volume.

The stronger practical argument is measurement. Without a stable program you can't run progressive overload consistently, and without it you have no readout on whether the program works.

He puts real weight on the reframe: **progressive overload is the measure of your gains, not the cause.** Getting the ninth rep where you got eight tells you you've cleared the fatigue phase of the stress-recovery-adaptation curve. That is a signal, not a mechanism. He allows a second reason it works: **it makes people train hard**, since without a concrete rep target most people leave a little in reserve — and for advanced lifters that margin is the difference between progressing and not.

Then the sting: past novice, adaptations take time and you cannot reliably measure hypertrophy over 4-6 weeks. So people who restart every time progress stalls are **"faking their own gains"** — harvesting easy neural adaptation on unfamiliar exercises over and over. He suggests thinking of training not as a program but as a **system that adapts based on your progression**.

## 2. Ego lifting

Two versions, and he thinks his own audience mostly falls for the second.

**Version one: not accepting the load reduction full ROM demands.** Full range of motion, especially at long muscle lengths, is supported by multiple meta-analyses for hypertrophy. People badly underestimate the price: about **50%** off the dumbbell bench press to get the elbow behind the body rather than stopping at shoulder height, and roughly **50% or more** going half squat to full squat (he notes his group has run multiple studies on this). "One step back to take two steps forward."

**Version two: technique creep.** Form is fine at the start of a block, then as progressive overload gets hard ROM quietly shrinks to hit the rep targets. This is **fake progressive overload** — you haven't gotten stronger, you've shortened the lift. He recommends periodically reviewing technique on video or with a coach.

His rule: **form first, weight second.** And the corollary that makes it workable — **failing to progress is a success**, because it is the signal the program needs updating. Which is different from hopping: update what isn't working, don't fix what isn't broken, don't repeat the same thing expecting a different result.

## 3. Unsystematic volume counting

The failure pattern: training "back" or "legs" as a body part alongside a dedicated arm day, producing wildly uneven volume. **Back is not a muscle group. Legs is not a muscle group.** Separate quads from hamstrings from glutes and set volume per muscle.

The method is **fractional volume counting**, which he says the 2025 meta-analysis also supports ([Pelland 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)): assign each muscle an exercise trains a fraction — 100%, 75%, 50% — then sum across the week per muscle. You can simplify to count-it-or-don't. The output, **effective sets per week per muscle**, is what he calls the single biggest determinant of hypertrophy.

The commonest error: not counting pull-ups and chin-ups toward biceps volume. Do that and your arm volume is almost certainly too high. Men using his app routinely feel underserved on arms when they're already at the high end of normal — they're used to over-training mirror muscles at the expense of everything else.

## Where the note has to push back

The three reference groups don't map cleanly onto the three claims. The Damas/Angleri papers support the muscle damage argument, and [Angleri 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14134) in particular concluded that **training variable manipulations are less relevant than intrinsic biology** for fibre hypertrophy — which cuts against muscle confusion but also, read fairly, against overconfidence in any specific program design. The claim that muscle confusion groups gained no more despite higher volume is not tied to a named study in the description list.

Nothing here is cited for the ego lifting section at all — the ROM meta-analyses he alludes to are not in the reference list, and the ~50% load reduction figures are stated from his own research group without a pointer.
