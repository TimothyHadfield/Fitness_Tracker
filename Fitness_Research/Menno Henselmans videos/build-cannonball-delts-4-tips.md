# Round Delts: Stop Training In The Sagittal Plane

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "How to build cannonball delts: 4 tips" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zo5tJWKRXlE)) · 2023-02-07 · 13 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- The delts taper off toward the back because almost everything lifters do — bench, flyes, front-facing overhead presses, front raises — trains the **anterior** head. He cites a study finding bodybuilders' front delts averaged **5x** the size of sedentary people's, lateral delts **3x**, and rear delts only **15%** bigger.
- The mechanical principle: **the further back the elbow travels, the more the lateral and posterior delts work, and the less the pecs contribute.**
- Behind-the-neck pressing won the EMG comparison for both lateral and posterior delts with no downside elsewhere — but he does **not** recommend it, because most men cannot reach the position without wrecking their shoulders.
- His four tips: **dumbbells over barbells; stand, don't sit; if using a barbell, go wide-grip and move your head out of the way rather than the bar; and put nearly all your delt volume into lateral and rear work.**
- He would **almost universally cut front raises** from programmes.

## The study
[00:30] An Italian group compared the behind-the-neck barbell press, the standard front barbell overhead press, and machine overhead presses set more toward the back and more toward the front, in **competitive bodybuilders**, with EMG on anterior, lateral and posterior delts, pecs, upper traps and triceps at a matched percentage of each exercise's own 1RM.

[02:03] Results as he reads them, focusing on the concentric phase and ignoring small differences:
- **Anterior delt:** essentially no difference between variations. The front delts work regardless.
- **Lateral delt:** the behind-the-neck barbell press is the clear winner. The front-set machine did particularly badly, being closer to an incline press.
- **Posterior delt:** behind-the-neck wins again, and the machine shows the same front-to-back gradient.
- **Pecs:** front variations win, machine or barbell — moving the elbow back deactivates the pecs.
- **Upper traps:** no notable difference, and only ~50% of max overall. Pressing is a mediocre trap exercise; wide overhead shrug variations are better.
- **Triceps:** no meaningful difference.

[03:35] The whole pattern reduces to one gradient. Lateral delts are shoulder abductors and posterior delts mainly draw the elbow back, so both only get loaded when the elbow travels behind the body. Press with the elbows in front — the sagittal plane — and you are doing front delt work you already have too much of.

[05:36] Note the rear delt caveat he gives: in overhead pressing they act mostly as stabilisers rather than prime movers, so even the best pressing variation activates them only reasonably well.

## The four tips
[06:37] **1. Behind-the-neck is the obvious answer, and he tells you not to do it.** It is hard on the shoulder, many men cannot get into position without slackening the wrists, jutting the head forward and hunching, and pain is common. In his experience mostly Olympic weightlifters and women get into it well. If it genuinely feels fine for you, use it.

His substitute is **dumbbells**, which let you drive the elbows out to the sides and press in the frontal rather than the sagittal plane. He cites a second EMG study finding higher muscle activity with dumbbells than a barbell, which he says follows from the freedom to abduct.

[08:09] **2. Stand, don't sit.** Standing gives a natural movement pattern and free scapulae; a bench pins the shoulder blades. And most people who sit do not sit upright — they lean back slightly, turning the lift into a high incline press.

[08:39] **3. Wide grip, and move your head, not the bar.** "Military press" does not mean military posture. Done properly with a barbell you arch the upper back hard and move the head out of the bar's path, which is what lets the elbows stay back and travel in the frontal plane. The common error — standing rigid and pressing the bar around the front of the head — is closer to a front raise, makes you weaker, and loads the front delt.

[09:41] **4. Bias all delt volume to lateral and rear.** Front raises he would cut from almost every programme: the front delts are already trained by every press and every flye, and lateral raises still hit them well. He programmes lateral raises, rear delt raises and shoulder pulls, and prefers to avoid machines here because the shoulder is a mobile joint, machines usually seat you, and a fixed path invites pattern overuse.

[11:14] One technique detail: on lateral raises, **bend slightly forward**. The body recruits whichever fibres have the best leverage, and in a strictly upright lateral raise he says the anterior fibres still produce around **75%** of the force that the lateral fibres do. Leaning forward biases the more posterior lateral fibres.

## Where the claim runs ahead of the evidence
Everything here rests on **acute EMG**, which measures activation, not growth, and Menno usually says so himself — he does not here. The 5x/3x/15% comparison is presented as a striking illustration while he simultaneously calls it an extreme example relative to other research he has seen; treat it as directional only. The 75% figure on lateral raises is given without a source. The video description contains no reference list at all, so none of this is traceable from the source material.
