# How Many Arm Isolation Exercises Do You Need?

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Was Mike Mentzer right about arm training? (New study)" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZOFqCLiKd0I)) · 2026-06-02 · 6 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- A new trial found **no advantage to arm exercise variety, and none to extra volume**: ~3% growth in every group after 8 weeks ([Valério 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.2026.2652354)).
- Henselmans does **not** accept the minimalist reading, and calls the researchers' conclusion "a bit of an overreach" — the study could not have detected the effect it was testing.
- His actual answer: **one good isolation exercise each for biceps and triceps, on top of your compounds.** Zero — the Mentzer/Arthur Jones position — is too few; further variety beyond one adds almost nothing.
- **Compound exercises absolutely count toward arm volume.** Count them fractionally if you can, or whole, or half — the crude methods are reasonably accurate.
- **Beyond four sets on one exercise, sets five and six add little**, particularly to failure with short rests. Add a different exercise or move the volume to another day instead.

## The study
The control condition did one biceps curl plus push-downs. The variety condition rotated two of three variations per week — dumbbell, preacher and incline curls for biceps; skull crushers, French presses and kickbacks for triceps. Two further groups raised isolation volume from four to six sets per session, or did both variety and extra volume. Everyone also benched and did pull-downs, all sets to failure with one-minute rest intervals. After 8 weeks, nothing separated the groups.

Henselmans's design objections: the paper says barbell curls, which is implausible for a unilateral protocol; push-downs were the control triceps exercise but were excluded from the variety pool; and the subjects had only **six weeks of training experience**, so the results speak to near-beginners.

## Why the null result proves less than it looks
This is his central argument, and it is a methodology point rather than a training one. Going from **8 to 12 fractional sets per week** predicts roughly **0.9% additional growth** on the latest dose-response meta-regression ([Pelland 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)) — a figure **below the error margin of the DEXA scans used**. The study therefore had no chance of detecting the small benefit the added volume should have produced. Its null is uninformative rather than negative.

He layers a training rationale on top: with one-minute rests and sets taken to failure, sets five and six add very little real volume, so a small effect is what you'd expect anyway. He would have dropped the six-week familiarisation block and run a 14-week trial on a single variable.

## What the wider literature supports
- Adding an arm isolation exercise to compound work produces **minimal extra gains** in volume-uncontrolled comparisons ([de França 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26244600/), [Bezerra 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29351383/)).
- Against that, he cites an **in-house meta-analysis from his own PT course** finding that when volume is *not* controlled, groups adding arm isolation work do grow more. This is unpublished and not independently checkable.
- His mechanistic case for at least one isolation exercise: **compounds cannot maximise tension across the full muscle length**, especially the triceps long head, which is hard to load at long lengths with pressing.

## Verdict on the framing question
Partially right, for the wrong reason. Mentzer and Jones's zero-isolation position is not supported — the evidence still leans toward one isolation movement per arm muscle. What the minimalists got right is the diminishing return past that: more variations, and more sets on the same exercise, buy very little.
