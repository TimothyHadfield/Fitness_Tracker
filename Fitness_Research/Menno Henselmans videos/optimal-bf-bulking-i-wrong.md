# Optimal Body Fat for Bulking: A Public Self-Correction

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "What's the optimal BF% for bulking? I was WRONG" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2euoc08yKbc)) · 2024-01-31 · 11 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- **He has changed his position.** He used to argue that higher body fat probably *does* impair muscle growth, on mechanistic grounds. He now says body fat level has **no meaningful effect on how much muscle you can build**, even at fairly high levels.
- What changed his mind: a new trial dividing trainees into low/medium/high body fat, training them for six months, and finding **no difference in muscle gain** ([Ribeiro 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40520-022-02313-7)) — plus several older studies he found on a full re-search of the literature that had never surfaced in the debate.
- **Strength** development may still be blunted at high body fat, unlike hypertrophy. He treats this as an open question, not a finding.
- The practical advice is nevertheless unchanged: **men shouldn't bulk past the point of losing their abs.** The reason is now health, recovery capacity, work capacity, aesthetics and skin elasticity — not direct impairment of hypertrophy.
- For women, largely a non-issue: the failure mode in practice is going **too lean**, not too fat.

## What he used to say, and why

The old case was entirely mechanistic — five separate physiological arguments, each with its own literature:

1. **Anabolic resistance.** Overweight individuals show a lower muscle protein synthetic response to both protein feeding and resistance training. He concedes here that this research "is not crystal clear," only that the trend is negative.
2. **Mitochondrial dysfunction and insulin resistance**, impairing the muscle's capacity to generate energy for exercise, and so performance, and so gains ([Reiter 2023](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00387.2022)).
3. **Chronic inflammation.** Higher body fat raises insulin resistance, which associates strongly with chronic inflammation. His model is signal-to-noise: you want acute post-workout inflammation spikes to be heard, and a high chronic baseline drowns the signal out. He flags himself that most of this evidence is in elderly subjects.
4. **Impaired recovery capacity.** Multiple studies find overweight people take longer to recover from a given workout. He notes this cuts both ways — very low body fat (contest prep, anorexia) also compromises recovery, so there's a mid-range sweet spot.
5. **Lower testosterone and GH.** In men, higher body fat means more testosterone aromatised to estrogen, degrading the T:E ratio, up to and including clinically low testosterone (male obesity-related secondary hypogonadism). In women the effect is much smaller — GH and, at very high body fat, estrogen can be affected, but testosterone typically holds.

His stated position going into the two-hour bulking debate with Israetel, Nuckols and Trexler on Nippard's channel was: the empirical data are inconclusive, the mechanistic data point negative, therefore muscle growth is *probably* harmed.

## What changed his mind

After the debate, the Ribeiro trial appeared: six months of resistance training, participants stratified into low, medium and high body fat. **No difference in muscle growth between groups.** That prompted him to redo the full literature search for his certification course, which turned up further studies "locked away in the deepest depths of Google Scholar" that nobody in the debate had cited.

His conclusion: body fat level has no effect on hypertrophy, but does appear to have a negative effect on **strength** development. He is careful to say he doesn't know whether that strength finding transfers to healthy overweight lifters with good muscle quality.

He draws a broader methodological lesson from it, and it is the most useful part of the video: humans, and especially scientists trained in the natural sciences, **overweight mechanism, theory and plausible causal stories relative to hard empirical data.** He names Andrew Huberman as someone who does this — extrapolating far past what the data support. He is implicitly naming his own past error as the same failure.

## Where the note has to push back

The evidence base for the reversal is thinner than the confidence he uses. Ribeiro is **older women**; Pescatello is unilateral upper-arm training in overweight and obese adults; Marcus is older adults at risk of falling. That is largely untrained and elderly, which is exactly the criticism he levels at the mechanistic literature he abandoned. "Quite conclusively show" outruns this. The honest reading is that the mechanistic case has been undercut, not that a positive null has been established for trained lifters.

## Practical application

- Muscle growth won't be limited by your body fat level, so the endpoint of a bulk is mostly preference.
- He still recommends men stop before losing abdominal definition — for health, and because he suspects impaired recovery and work capacity limit MRV (maximum recoverable volume) more than current studies capture. That last point is explicitly his coaching intuition, not a finding.
- Cardiorespiratory fitness matters more than lifters think for between-set recovery; carrying more mass, fat especially, makes that harder.
- Skin elasticity and stretch marks scale with the size of the weight swing, which is a real reason to keep bulks moderate.
- Women: he still wouldn't recommend going into overweight ranges, but the risk he actually sees in practice is fitness-industry women driving body fat too low.
