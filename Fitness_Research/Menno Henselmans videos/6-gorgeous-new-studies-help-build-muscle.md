# Six New Hypertrophy Studies, October 2025

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "6 GORGEOUS new studies to help you build muscle [2025]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T2tLFPPdjwY)) · 2025-10-15 · 14 min
**Topic:** Evidence & methodology

## Bottom line
- **Eccentric tempo doesn't matter for hypertrophy or strength** (effect size ~zero across 9 studies), but slow eccentrics are worse for power.
- **Dumbbell rows built 38% of the biceps mass that curls did.** Count rows as half a biceps set.
- **Strength gains and size gains correlate very strongly** when studies are run properly — long enough, MRI, repeated measurements, and compared *within* the same person.
- **A lifting belt increases load ~10% without increasing muscle activity or ground reaction force.** Don't expect extra hypertrophy from it.
- **Lengthened partials have not lived up to the hype** — no major difference from full ROM on preacher curls.
- **Long-length isometrics are not inferior to full ROM training.** Isometric leg extensions grew quads 1.9% vs 0.8% for full ROM over 6 weeks, though that difference was statistically unclear.

## Study 1 — eccentric repetition duration
A meta-analysis of nine studies on how fast to lower the weight. Conclusion: **no effect on hypertrophy or strength**; the hypertrophy effect size was almost exactly zero. Slower eccentrics *were* worse for power, measured as jump height, because slow lowering dissipates the stretch-shortening cycle.

Two caveats of his own. The studies compare a controlled eccentric of at least a second against multiple seconds — he expects genuinely fast eccentrics (dive-bombing a squat or chin-up, rebounding off connective tissue rather than decelerating with muscle) to be worse for growth and harder on the joints. And in volume-equated studies there was a trend toward *better* strength with slow eccentrics, which he calls an artefact: same weight and reps at a slower tempo just means training harder. In non-volume-equated studies, which reflect real training, there was no effect.

## Study 2 — rows vs curls for biceps
Dumbbell curls versus supinated-grip dumbbell rows. Rows produced **38% of the biceps growth** curls did, consistent with prior work putting rows at roughly half. Practical rule: **count row volume at 50% toward biceps**. Matches the finding already in the Nippard material in this library.

## Study 3 — strength gains vs size gains
The recurring objection to high-volume hypertrophy studies is that the growth is oedema, not contractile tissue — otherwise strength would track it. This study argues the poor correlation in the literature is a **study quality problem**, not a biological one. It improved on the norm in four ways: **15 weeks** instead of the usual 8 (most studies run a college semester, short enough that neural adaptation dominates the strength signal); **MRI** rather than ultrasound or DEXA; **every measurement taken twice**; and **n = 39** with strength and size measured **within the same individual** — most prior work compares across people or studies, where diet, lifestyle and genetics wash out the relationship.

Result: strength and size were very strongly correlated, even in untrained subjects. Practical implication: **your long-term PRs on familiar exercises are one of the best hypertrophy proxies available**. Mirror checks and tape measurements don't cut it, which is why "it worked for me, bro" is usually uninformed.

## Study 4 — belts during squats
No effect of wearing a belt on EMG muscle activity, consistent with prior EMG work and with ground reaction force studies for both squat and deadlift: **belted lifters are not putting more force into the ground**, yet they lift roughly 10% more.

The explanation is stability and intra-abdominal pressure — his analogy is a hockey stick made of flimsy rubber versus metal: a rigid core transfers more of the force you produce. He notes the study's weakness himself (submaximal loads). Verdict: a belt is fine, but don't expect more muscle or strength from it. It matters mainly for strength athletes who compete belted and should therefore train belted.

## Study 5 — lengthened partials vs full range of motion
Trained lifters trained one arm with lengthened-partial preacher curls (bottom portion only) and the other with full ROM. He objects to the exercise choice up front: **preacher curls already overload the biceps in the bottom position**, so there is little stimulus difference to detect.

After 8 weeks, no major differences in hypertrophy or strength. There was weak-to-moderate evidence favouring the partials — interesting given the exercise choice — and slightly better strength with full ROM, which he attributes to strength being measured through full ROM. Still a preprint.

His read: lengthened partials have not lived up to two years of YouTube hype, though you can equally say they're at least as good as full ROM. Use them for exercises that **don't** inherently overload the lengthened position — most leg extension machines, calf raises — and prefer full ROM otherwise, since it's easier to push and to count as progressive overload.

## Study 6 — long-length isometrics
Trained individuals did leg extensions: one leg full ROM to absolute failure, the other holding a maximal isometric contraction in the fully lengthened bottom position for a matched ~30 seconds per set. Importantly, the machine allowed **more range of motion than most**, so the leg was essentially fully bent.

After 6 weeks, quadriceps growth was 1.9% (isometric) vs 0.8% (full ROM) — **statistically unclear**, given trained subjects and only 6 weeks. He is appropriately careful: the conclusion is that isometrics are *not worse*, possibly better, at long muscle lengths, and that earlier findings against isometrics generally used short lengths. Isometric force can exceed concentric force, though not eccentric — and you don't overload the eccentric in normal training anyway, since load is capped by concentric strength. The trade-off is losing the stretch reflex.

Practical reach: planks, and the erector spinae during squats and deadlifts, loaded only isometrically yet growing well in trained lifters. He notes this supports his earlier claim that **deadlifts are surprisingly effective for the traps** as a long-length isometric.

## Overreach worth flagging
The opening hook — a review of 32 studies concluding more muscular men have harder erections, more libido and better sexual performance independent of testosterone — is framed as "proving once again that there is no problem in life that cannot be solved by getting more muscular." It reviews observational data with obvious confounders. He means it as a joke, but the causal wording is unearned.
