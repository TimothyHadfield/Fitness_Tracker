# Six Beginner Questions, Answered Fast

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Exercise scientist answers the web's 6 most searched questions" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r3V4BkmRSLM)) · 2024-06-05 · 10 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Fat loss is energy deficit, full stop.** Eating 500 fewer calories and burning 500 more give identical results. Pick training for your fitness goals and let the diet do the fat loss.
- **Arms are not special.** His own review of muscle-group-specific volume and frequency found no differences, bar a marginal (and untrustworthy) trend for biceps preferring lower volume.
- **HIIT and steady-state cardio produce the same fat loss at equal energy expenditure**, but HIIT costs more recovery and creates a **larger interference effect** with strength training. He recommends steady-state, or whatever you'll actually do.
- **Weekly volume matters, frequency doesn't** — except indirectly. Spread across more days you're fresher, so you do more work, so you gain more. General health guidance he cites: **~5 h/week of exercise, ~2 h of it strength training.**
- **Afternoon/evening training is marginally better** than morning, likely via core body temperature. Most short studies find no difference, your body clock adapts, and **caffeine reverses much of the morning deficit**.

## Q1: Which exercises help with weight loss?
[00:00] All of them, to the extent they create a deficit. He makes the standard and correct point that different exercises don't differ much in energy cost — it's mostly how hard you're working — and that **people badly overestimate calories burned in training**. The role of strength training in a deficit is to make the body preferentially burn fat rather than muscle.

## Q2: How to get bigger arms?
[01:32] Same as any muscle: sufficient mechanical tension (train hard), sufficient time under tension (enough weekly sets). He explicitly reports a *null* result from his own analysis rather than selling arm-specific programming — a marginal trend for biceps responding to lower volume, which he says not to put stock in.

His exercise preferences here are **coaching opinion, not cited findings**: Bayesian curls (which he coined, and flags as such), leaning concentration curls, and — for elbow-sensitive lifters — "skull-overs", a skull crusher variation letting the elbows drift back to stretch the long head while unloading the elbow. The rationale, that loading a muscle in its lengthened position produces more growth, is well supported; the specific exercises are not individually tested.

## Q3: Best HIIT workouts?
[03:34] He reframes to "what's the best cardio". Given equal total energy expenditure, HIIT and low-intensity steady state produce **the same fat loss**. HIIT is more time-efficient but consumes more recovery capacity and — the substantive claim — produces a **worse interference effect** with concurrent strength training, particularly same-day and at high volume/intensity. His recommendation is steady-state, or whatever you'll adhere to.

## Q4: How many times a week should I exercise?
[05:05] Mostly schedule and preference. He gives ~5 hours a week including ~2 hours of strength training as general health guidance, **without a citation**.

[06:06] The interesting part is the frequency argument. A 2022 study compared **9 sets once weekly, 3 sets three times weekly volume-equated, and 3 sets three times weekly left uncontrolled**. Volume-equated, frequency made no difference — both around **2% quadriceps CSA increase**. But the uncontrolled higher-frequency group naturally did more reps, because they were fresher each session, and grew roughly **double: ~4%**.

His conclusion: frequency doesn't matter directly, but in the real world nobody equates volume, so higher frequency indirectly buys more volume and therefore more growth. That's a sound reading, but it is **one study**, the author's name is unrecoverable from the captions, and the study is not in the video's reference list — so it is recorded here unattributed and unlinked.

## Q5: How to build muscle?
[08:07] Deliberately Step Zero: heavy strength training, sufficient total volume, high-protein diet, and time.

## Q6: What time of day should I exercise?
[08:37] Whenever you'll be consistent. If you genuinely have a choice, afternoon-to-evening produces slightly better results — he cites what he calls the best study on the topic, whose author's name the captions render as "kusal" (a study by Küüsmaa is the most likely match, and the description contains an unresolved link to a 2016 Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism paper, but this cannot be confirmed from the resolved reference list, so the attribution is left open).

[09:07] Three sensible caveats: most studies, especially shorter ones, find no difference; **your biorhythm adapts** to whatever time you train consistently; and **caffeine before a morning workout reverses much of the deficit**. The proposed mechanism is core body temperature, which he supports by noting most world records are set in the afternoon to early evening.

## Flags
- This is his own stated "novice level" content, and the reference list is unusually thin — **two resolved studies for six questions**. Most of the answers here are consensus positions he is relaying, not claims he is supporting from the video's citations.
- The doubled hypertrophy from higher frequency is a striking number resting on a single unnamed study. Treat as suggestive.
- The 5 h/week and caffeine claims are uncited.
- The interference-effect claim is the one place he takes a firm position beyond the crowd — that **HIIT interferes more than steady state** — and the supporting meta-analysis compares continuous versus intermittent endurance protocols in concurrent training, which is a reasonable but not exact fit to the claim.
