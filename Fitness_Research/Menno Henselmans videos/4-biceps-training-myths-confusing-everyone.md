# Four Biceps Training Myths

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "These 4 Biceps Training Myths Are Confusing Everyone" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5zRo2STwwyA)) · 2024-12-19 · 11 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Supinated grips are not the best biceps position** — supination *shortens* the biceps, putting it below optimal length for force production. Use a variety of forearm rotations, including some pronated and neutral work, to train it long.
- **You cannot train your biceps peak.** Peak is genetics (belly shape, origin and insertion) plus total muscle size. The long head can't be emphasised enough to change the shape.
- Do keep the **elbow at your side or behind the body** — the biceps crosses the shoulder, so raising the elbow shortens it.
- **Count your back work.** Compound and isolation exercises are on average equally effective ([Rosa 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000720)). Chin-ups and pulldowns count fully; **dumbbell rows count about half**.
- **Wide-grip pulling still trains the biceps** on a fixed bar, because horizontal inward force gives the elbow a real flexion moment arm.

## Myth 1: supinate everything

The intuition is that a supinated grip makes the biceps work hardest, because it feels and looks more engaged. Henselmans argues that what you feel is the muscle **bunched up** — supination shortens the biceps, and multiple studies show the biceps grows more when trained at long muscle lengths ([Sato 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2021.734509); [Pedrosa 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2021.1927199); [Pedrosa 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports11020039)).

The distinction he leans on is **external force output vs internal tension**. Supination gives better leverage, which is why you're stronger there, but leverage is about moving external load; hypertrophy responds to tension inside the muscle. And the biceps is unusual — it produces its greatest active *and* passive force at relatively long lengths, so even with the elbow at your side, full supination drops it below optimal length.

He concedes the mechanism can cut the other way: better leverage sometimes means harder recruitment, which would mean more tension. His counter is EMG evidence that **grip and grip width don't change biceps recruitment** across pulldowns, pull-ups, rows and curls.

The practical claim is modest and worth reading as such: use varied rotations, don't restrict yourself to supinated work. He says pronated "could very well be" better; he does not claim it is proven.

## Myth 2: training the peak

The biceps is technically **triarticular** — crossing shoulder, elbow and radioulnar joints — which is what gives the "train the long head for peak" argument its foothold. His rebuttal: the biceps' role at the shoulder is so weak that any functional differentiation between heads is trivial on top of it. Studies looking for biceps activity during pure shoulder flexion or abduction struggle to detect it at all.

So peak is **genetics** (belly shape, origin/insertion), by far the dominant factor, plus **total biceps size**. The way to a bigger peak is a bigger biceps.

But the shoulder role isn't useless information. Because the biceps shortens when the elbow rises in any direction, **elbow at the side or behind the body** genuinely is the better training position.

## Myth 3: back work doesn't count

It does. [Rosa 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000720) found single-joint and multi-joint exercises equally effective on average. His caveat is the important part: **"on average" doesn't license treating all compounds as equal.**

- **Chin-ups** are probably excellent — he offers gymnasts' arms as informal support.
- **Dumbbell rows** are not; direct research reportedly shows roughly half the biceps growth, so count four sets as about **two sets** of biceps volume.
- **Pulldowns** perform well in the literature.

## Myth 4: wide-grip pulling misses the biceps

He grants that a very wide grip reduces elbow flexion demand and range of motion. But the biomechanics change on a **fixed implement** — barbell, pulldown bar, pull-up bar — because you can pull *inward* against something that won't move.

The argument, diagrammed in the video: considering only vertical forces, the elbow sits directly below the hand, so there is **no moment arm for elbow flexion** and the biceps couldn't be involved at all. In reality the biceps generates horizontal inward force on the bar; vertical plus horizontal gives a resultant vector angled inward, which *does* have a positive elbow moment arm — and simultaneously **shortens the moment arm for the lats**. Biceps and back work synergistically: biceps involvement makes the movement easier for the lats.

So biceps stay active on a fixed bar even at a fairly wide grip. Bars, not dumbbells — with a dumbbell there's nothing to pull inward against.

The evidence is thin and he says so:

- [Gentil 2015](https://doi.org/10.5812/asjsm.24057): a pronated wide-grip lat pulldown produced as much biceps growth as a supinated barbell curl — about **6% growth** and **10-12% strength** (isokinetic peak elbow flexor torque) in both. One study.
- He raises the integrity question himself: Paulo Gentil has a contested reputation through his affiliation with Mateus Barbalho, who had multiple papers retracted for fraud. His defence is a plausibility argument — nobody risks a career to prove pulldowns train the biceps. That is not evidence; treat the finding as unreplicated.
- Weak indirect support: [Pompermayer 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11332-021-00761-8) found supinated shoulder-width pulldowns and EZ-bar preacher curls produced similar biceps muscle damage. Damage is a poor proxy for growth.

## Where the note has to push back

The whole myth-1 argument runs on mechanism — length-tension relationships and EMG — not on a trial comparing supinated to pronated curling for growth. He is doing exactly the mechanistic extrapolation he criticises elsewhere, and the honest version is "supination is probably not specially good," not "pronated is better."

The dumbbell-rows-count-half figure is presented as a clean number from "direct research," but no study in the reference list is identified as its source, so it should not be quoted with a citation attached.
