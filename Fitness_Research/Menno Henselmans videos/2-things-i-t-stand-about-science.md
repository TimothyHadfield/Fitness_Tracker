# Two Complaints About Science-Based Lifting: Technique Obsession and the Myth of Contradictory Studies

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "2 Things I CAN'T STAND about science-based lifting" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gy-hmoVtUPs)) · 2026-01-13 · 11 min
**Topic:** Evidence & methodology

> This is a critique of the evidence-based lifting movement from inside it — including of figures whose material makes up much of this library. His criticisms are given here in full.

## Bottom line
- **Complaint 1: the obsession with exercise technique.** Influencers invent impractical exercises with no outcome research on the grounds that they're theoretically marginally better. A direct study found strict vs cheating technique produced **equal growth in both arms** over 8 weeks.
- Only technique changes that **increase tension on the target muscle** matter — mainly range of motion, and actually loading the intended muscle. Beyond that, "pretty" technique is being confused with good technique.
- **Complaint 2: the claim that studies constantly contradict each other.** He says this is "simply absolute" nonsense. Interpretations conflict; observed facts almost never do.
- Most apparent contradictions are **null results in underpowered trained-lifter studies** being read as reversals — a p-value fallacy, not a real flip in direction.
- He challenges viewers directly: **find one example** where the actual facts in evidence-based fitness reversed, not the interpretation.
- His diagnosis of the movement's decline: not that the science failed, but that **people cannot interpret research properly**.

## Complaint 1 — technique obsession
The framing is blunt: the comedown of evidence-based fitness is partly deserved, and the technique fetish is the trend that gives it a bad name. Influencers invent crazy, highly impractical exercise variations, there is no research on whether they build more muscle, and the justification is that theoretically they *might* be marginally superior.

The study he leans on had subjects train one arm strict and the other cheating — biceps curls (elbows pinned vs Arnold-style heaving) and pushdowns (elbows pinned vs turning it into a dip). After 8 weeks, **no statistically meaningful difference in growth at any site**.

His mechanistic reading: muscle is a slab of meat, you put tension on it, it gets bigger. Only technique that changes tension changes growth. The two things that genuinely do are **range of motion** (longer lengths usually better) and **whether you're loading the target muscle at all**. Given similar ROM on the right muscle, technique doesn't have to look pretty — people confuse pretty with good, "just like when dating."

He then argues against himself on four points:
- The study **does not debunk** technique — it shows technique is less *directly* influential than believed.
- Strict technique got **the same gains with lighter loads**: better stimulus-to-fatigue, lower injury risk, more volume tolerance.
- It **didn't manipulate the eccentric** — 2 seconds in both conditions, so the cheating group was effectively doing eccentric overload. Heave it up and let it *drop* and you omit the eccentric and much of the tension; eccentrics likely contribute meaningfully to growth (titin signalling, very high mechanical tension).
- An earlier study did find less biceps growth when people were told just to get the weight up.

That earlier study is usually cited as a mind-muscle connection study, and his reinterpretation is the sharpest thing in the segment: the "strict" condition was enforced *by* internal focus, confounding technique with attentional focus — and the same study found **no effect of internal focus on the quads during leg extensions**. A machine standardises technique, leaving nothing for focus to influence. So the mind-muscle connection is mostly a cue that produces better technique, not a mechanism: thinking about a muscle cannot increase tension in it if the biomechanics don't change.

## Complaint 2 — "studies contradict each other"
He calls this flatly wrong. It is extremely uncommon in exercise or nutrition science for multiple studies in a row to contradict each other **in the facts they observe**. Interpretations conflict constantly; observations almost never do.

Two worked examples:
- **Lengthened-biased training** looks like it overturned range of motion. It didn't. Studies showed greater ROM → greater gains; now we know the gains come specifically from extending into longer muscle lengths. A refinement, not a reversal.
- **Fasting and autophagy.** Fasting induces autophagy, which has health benefits — but autophagy responds to catabolism generally, and equating for the calorie deficit erases the difference in the limited research available. "Fasting is good for you" and "you don't need to fast" are both true; the deficit does the work.

On the replication crisis he makes an argument that cuts against a standard evidence-based talking point. The complaint that untrained-subject findings don't transfer to trained lifters is, he says, **largely a statistical-power artefact**: trained lifters gain very little in 8 weeks, so between-group differences are very hard to detect. Findings fail to replicate — but "it rarely works that way" that the direction actually flips. Nobody has found long rest intervals better in untrained people and short rest intervals better in trained people.

So most of the crisis is **null findings being read as refutations** — what the auto-captions garble as the "B fallacy" and is clearly the p-value fallacy: treating "not significant here" as "the opposite of last time." Both studies may show a positive effect; one was easier to detect. Lengthened partials is the live case — most studies favour longer lengths, many trained-lifter studies miss significance, and **there is no trend at all in the opposite direction**.

He couples this with publication bias: the first study reports a large effect, later work shrinks it, because striking findings publish more easily than null ones. Nobody publishes that an unheard-of supplement doesn't work.

## His closing argument
Evidence-based fitness comes from evidence-based medicine, and nobody walks into a hospital demanding the untested drug. But interpreting research is hard, and fitness ideas behave like a stock market: an intrinsic value set by the research, with popularity oscillating around it.

Then the challenge, repeated twice: name one case where the **facts** — not the interpretation — went one way and later turned out to be the opposite.

**Where his rhetoric runs ahead of his evidence:** the technique argument rests on a single 8-week within-subject study, on two exercises, in which cheating still involved a controlled 2-second eccentric — a limitation he raises himself but doesn't let constrain his opening claim. And the challenge is framed so that almost any counterexample can be reclassified as "interpretation," which makes it hard to lose.
