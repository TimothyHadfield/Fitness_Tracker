# Israetel vs Henselmans: Where Two Evidence-Based Coaches Actually Differ

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Mike's VS Menno's methods for maximum muscle" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CKIf38UXDGQ)) · 2024-04-03 · 48 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Israetel splits technique into **level one** (non-negotiable: real range of motion including a loaded stretch, controlled eccentric, no dive-bombing, no cheating tension off the target muscle) and **level two** (optional: slow eccentrics, pauses, exaggerated tempos). Level two is a tool for people who can't feel a muscle working, not a universal upgrade.
- **Tempo doesn't matter much.** Anything from roughly a 1–2 second total rep up to about 9 seconds of controlled eccentric-plus-concentric produces similar hypertrophy. Treat tempos as variations you rotate for novelty, not as a variable to optimise.
- **The pump** is where the two most clearly split. Israetel uses it as one of several stimulus proxies alongside local fatigue, soreness and performance; Henselmans thinks the supporting studies are weak and rates it as largely uninformative — while conceding it isn't unreasonable to use.
- **Stretch-mediated hypertrophy is a "marginal revolution."** Israetel puts the lengthened-partial advantage at maybe **10% relative** in the best studies. Both agree the practical rule is: whatever range you use, don't skip the deep stretch under load.
- **Volume landmarks shift with energy balance.** Bulking raises maximum recoverable volume and lowers minimum effective volume; late in a hard cut MRV can fall *below* the volume needed to hold muscle — Israetel's "natty paradox."
- **Deloads should be reactive in principle, pre-programmed in practice** for beginners and intermediates, because most people can't be trusted to read their own fatigue signals. Israetel's single trigger: getting weaker in a muscle two sessions running.

## Where they disagreed

This is a friendly interview between people who agree on fundamentals, and Israetel says so at the end: viewers who look at their methods superficially assume there's almost no overlap, but the differences are in implementation and in how each reads specific literature. Both explicitly declined to stage a fight — Henselmans jokes early on "are we only doing contrasting views?" and Israetel defers it to a future episode. The disagreements that did surface are real but narrow.

**The pump as a training signal.** Henselmans put a viewer's question to Israetel: given the current emphasis on stretch-mediated hypertrophy, and given that the pump occurs mostly in the mid-range, how do you reconcile going by the pump? Israetel rejected the premise outright — "categorically false" — arguing that lengthened-position work taken close to failure with real volume produces the biggest pump anyone will ever get, and that bodybuilders associate pumps with mid-range work only because backstage band pumping needs tension at the top.

On the substance, Israetel's position is that the pump is a robust indicator of what he calls *perturbation* — evidence the muscle has been genuinely disturbed — and that it now has at least two studies showing it correlates with hypertrophy. He is careful about how much weight it carries: pump and growth are not one-to-one, some clearly productive work (good mornings, RDLs for hamstrings) produces little pump, and the pump is only one of several proxies he uses. But he'd rather have it than not.

Henselmans's counter came in two parts. Empirically, he contests those studies and points to cases where pump and growth dissociate in the wrong direction: short rest intervals give a much better pump and equal-or-worse growth per set. Israetel conceded the mechanism — quick pumps are available and are less hypertrophic than taking your time — while arguing that the deepest pumps come from longer rests and sets pushed hard, so the correlation survives at the extremes. Henselmans's final position: "I personally am on the side of not so important, but I don't think it's unreasonable at all to use it as one of the indicators."

**The deeper split is epistemic, and it's the most interesting exchange in the video.** Israetel's defence of using the pump is explicitly not a claim that the evidence supports it. It's an argument that where the literature is silent you are entitled to make a practitioner's guess, and that a large part of the evidence-based community has confused absence of evidence with evidence of absence — "if there's not a study on PubMed for it I don't want to hear about it, that's not how the real world works." Henselmans accepts the principle but bounds it: fine, provided you don't let a proxy override areas where there *is* hard data pointing the other way, and provided the pump never displaces load progression and eating enough. Israetel agrees with that boundary. This is less a disagreement about pumps than about default epistemic posture — how conservative to be in the gaps — and it is worth reading as such.

**Whether the deload study was a fair test.** Henselmans collaborated on a study finding that programmed deload weeks didn't improve gains. Israetel said the result was exactly what he'd predicted and changed nothing for him: beginners, only a few sessions per week, a program that couldn't impose much systemic stress, and only about sixteen weeks. His view is that an untrained person training a few times a week may not need a deload for their first six months, so a study in that population can't test the question. Henselmans pushed back twice — participants were reportedly training to failure with decent volume — but didn't press it further. The disagreement is unresolved in the video: Israetel treats the null result as confirming his model, Henselmans clearly regards the program as more challenging than Israetel is granting. Note the structure of Israetel's argument, which is worth flagging: a model that predicts a null in beginners and a positive effect in advanced lifters is difficult to falsify with the studies that actually get run.

**Where lengthened partials do and don't beat full range.** Israetel's read is that stretch-mediated hypertrophy is real but oversold — maybe a 10% relative improvement in the best studies, an outcome he calls a marginal revolution rather than a game change. He doesn't know where the trade-off sits: is it all lengthened partials, or partials plus a portion of full range, and do some muscles need a peak contraction? Unexamined, so he stays open. Henselmans added a methodological caveat Israetel didn't raise: the only compelling studies showing lengthened partials beating full ROM were calf raises and leg extensions, and in both cases subjects used considerably more load in the partial condition — leg extension machines in particular have little tension in the bottom position, so shortening the range lets you load it harder. The extra load may be doing the work, not the lengthened position. Both landed on the same practical rule anyway.

**Volume periodisation within a block.** Henselmans asked why start below optimal volume rather than going straight to the optimum and auto-regulating from there. Israetel's answer is that when exercises, machines and frequencies are new, the optimum genuinely *is* lower — early-block novelty produces damage that competes with growth, and work capacity rises over the block. He then conceded the disagreement away: "I don't think there's a difference between our views... I think the low end is optimal to begin with," and it's a one-or-two-week difference before both approaches converge. He also disowned the rigid ramping infographic RP is known for — that was one illustrative example that people took as prescription. Volume, he says, should follow auto-regulated stimulus and recovery, and in a stable program can sit at the same number for many weeks because load is carrying the progression.

**Steroid use.** Not framed as a disagreement, but the values gap is visible. Israetel is publicly on anabolics and gave two reasons: proving evidence-based methods work inside the enhanced world so gear users can't dismiss the science, and personal curiosity about his ceiling. He then inverted the question — he'd ask evidence-based lifters why they *aren't* on gear, since training itself is an accepted risk and the cut-off is arbitrary — and named the psychological side effects, including a noticeable dulling of some kinds of intelligence, as the most compelling reason not to. Henselmans, who has publicly argued most people shouldn't use performance-enhancing drugs, hosted the answer without endorsing it. Readers should treat the "why aren't you on gear" framing as one practitioner's risk tolerance, not a recommendation.

## Where they agreed

Several topics produced no daylight at all, which is itself informative:

- **Training to failure.** Henselmans stated Israetel isn't an advocate on stimulus-to-fatigue grounds and Israetel confirmed their views are essentially the same, so the topic was skipped.
- **Rest days aren't mandatory.** The governing variable is total systemic stress, not calendar structure. Two rest days won't rescue you from three brutal sessions a day; training daily at lower per-session volume is sustainable for a long time. Israetel suggested anyone in a "no days off" habit try a few rest days to see whether the psychological drop in stress helps. Henselmans added his own framing: **Gregorian calendar bias** — people think in days and weeks, but the body only sees elapsed hours between sessions. Both agree very high frequency is mostly a vehicle for volume, and total weekly volume is the variable that matters.
- **Some exercises are inherently more injurious.** Both reject the strong version of movement optimism. Israetel's mechanistic case: large muscles leveraged against small tendons (a pec tendon on a heavy dynamic fly), loads high enough to stress tendon per square centimetre, and posterior versus anterior disc herniation under a rounded heavy spine. Wrist curls, by contrast, are nearly impossible to injure yourself with. Henselmans's empirical case: ask any group of physiotherapists which lifts their clients tolerate badly and you get a consistent list — skull crushers on it, kickbacks not — and intake forms wouldn't show an even distribution. Both stress that no lift is off-limits: volume, load progression and technique can shrink almost any movement's risk to negligible, and telling a nervous beginner that everything is equally dangerous is as wrong as telling them nothing is.
- **Volume landmarks and energy balance.** Both allocate more volume to bulking clients. A deficit is a recovery deficit.

## Practical takeaways

- Fix range of motion, eccentric control and tension on the target muscle first. Only then consider slow tempos and pauses, and only if you struggle to feel the muscle working.
- Don't count seconds. If a 4-second eccentric squat is a variation that keeps a bare-bones home gym interesting for a few mesocycles, that's a legitimate reason to use it.
- Whatever range you train, don't miss the deep stretch under load. Full range is still the safe default, particularly if you also do a sport.
- Deload when performance drops for two sessions in a row in a muscle. If you know you won't act on that honestly, schedule deloads instead — and deloads don't have to be whole weeks: single sessions, half-weeks and per-muscle recovery blocks are all on the table.
- Start a new block below your ceiling volume and let it rise as work capacity does; don't impose an arbitrary ramp.

## Where claims outrun the evidence

- "Two studies" is a thin basis for saying the pump-hypertrophy link "has been empirically established." Israetel's own framing elsewhere in the conversation — that this is a practitioner's guess he could be wrong about — is the more defensible version, and Henselmans disputes the studies directly.
- The **10% figure** for lengthened partials is given without a source and should be treated as a rough impression rather than a pooled estimate.
- The injury-risk argument is entirely mechanistic and clinical-experience-based. Both acknowledge the direct evidence isn't there; the observation that physiotherapist intake forms would show an uneven distribution is a plausible prediction, not a finding.
- The claim that a beginner "may not need a deload for the first six months" is coaching heuristic, not a tested result.
