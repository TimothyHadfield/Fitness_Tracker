# The 20 g Protein Ceiling: Wrong About Absorption, Half-Right About Synthesis

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Can the body only absorb 20 g protein per meal? [Science]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NmAuWIFVwMI)) · 2023-11-14 · 9 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- The absorption claim is **flatly false**. You would have to eat absurd amounts before digestion or absorption became limiting. The real question is muscle protein synthesis.
- At rest or after low-volume single-body-part training, **~20 g of high-quality protein essentially maxes out MPS** — the "muscle full" effect.
- After **substantial full-body training the ceiling roughly doubles, to ~40 g** (~0.6 g/kg), mainly in the first post-workout meal.
- **Fasting potentiates the response** — anabolic signalling can go higher after a longer fast.
- Net balance may improve past 20–40 g through **reduced muscle protein breakdown**, though he flags the measurement methods as contested.
- Practical landing point: **at least three meals a day, and no current evidence that more than three helps.** Weight protein toward the post-workout window.

## Terminology, and why the myth is a category error
His first move is to separate three things people conflate. Digestion breaks protein into absorbable pieces; absorption puts those into the bloodstream; metabolism is what the body then does with them, including muscle protein synthesis. Nutrient harvest is highly efficient, so absorption is not a bottleneck. Everyone arguing about "20 g absorbed" is actually arguing about synthesis, badly.

Where the myth has real content is the dose-response curve for MPS. Past roughly 20 g of high-quality protein — whey or dairy in particular — additional protein produces essentially no further stimulation, especially at rest.

His explanation for the muscle full effect is evolutionary: MPS is energetically expensive, so the body only builds to the extent something has demanded it. His framing — **strength training determines the size of the glass; nutrition determines how much of it you fill** — is the permissive/stimulatory distinction researchers use, and he calls it an oversimplification himself.

## Three caveats that dismantle the practical version of the rule

**1. The ceiling moves with training volume.** The classic 20 g figure comes from studies at rest or with low training volumes, often a single body part. With substantial volume, particularly full-body training, the muscle full effect shifts up to roughly 40 g. That is double, though he immediately notes 40 g (~0.6 g/kg) is still not a lot, and the effect is mostly confined to the first meal after training.

**2. Fasting potentiates.** After a longer fast, anabolic signalling can reach higher levels than it otherwise would. The logic is the same as the muscle full effect run backwards: if the body has been deprived of amino acids, it has repair and restoration to do; if you keep flooding it, it goes refractory.

**3. Synthesis isn't the whole ledger.** Some studies find that beyond 20–30 g at rest, or 20–40 g after hard training, **muscle protein breakdown continues to fall** even though synthesis has plateaued — meaning net balance keeps improving. He puts this in proportion: breakdown is far lower than synthesis and moves much less, because it is more efficient for the body to stop building than to start demolishing. His house analogy: adding a balcony requires opening the wall first, so some breakdown is intrinsic to remodelling, not just catabolism. He then discounts his own point — the methods used to estimate breakdown have been questioned.

He uses it for one thing: explaining why the strict 20 g rule doesn't pan out in practice. If it were true, maximising growth would require at least six meals a day, and the meal-frequency data don't show that.

## What the meal-frequency data actually show
The evidence he leans on is the Norwegian meal frequency project: trained lifters ran the same training program on either three or six meals a day. The three-meal group did slightly better, or at worst equal. He is upfront that calorie control was imperfect and the three-meal group had slightly higher energy intake — but points out that energy intake has small effects on muscle growth compared with protein intake, so the confound doesn't rescue the six-meal case.

Conclusion: three meals a day is probably a genuine minimum, driven by the real ceiling on how much MPS a single meal can stimulate, but there is no demonstrated benefit above three — especially if you place most of your protein around training, where the muscle full effect is delayed and the ceiling is higher.

## Where this sits against the rest of the library
He lands close to the position this library already holds, and independently. Both reject the absorption framing outright, both put near-maximal MPS at ~20 g, and both put the post-training figure near 40 g. The library's additional detail — that only ~10% of a meal's protein ends up in muscle, and that the 40 g advantage is often 10–20% and non-significant — doesn't appear here; he presents the 20→40 g shift more crisply than that.

Two things to watch. He states the doubling of the ceiling with full-body training as settled, when the underlying dose-response work is a handful of acute studies with small samples. And his meal-frequency recommendation is drawn largely from a single confounded study, which he acknowledges but still treats as "pretty strong evidence."
