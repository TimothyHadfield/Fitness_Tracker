# The Meal Recall Effect: Remembering Your Last Meal as Bigger Than It Was

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Scientists discovered a new fat loss hack" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q4WoZ25WurU)) · 2023-01-23 · 5 min
**Topic:** Psychology & behaviour

## Bottom line
- The **meal recall effect**: vividly recalling a meal you ate earlier, before you eat your next one, reduces how much you eat. Established across several prior studies at **10%+** reduction.
- The new twist: also **imagine that earlier meal was twice as large as it actually was**. In the new study this cut intake by **32%**.
- You do not have to believe the lie. When participants were afterwards asked to estimate the size of the first meal, the self-trickery had **no effect on their estimate** — they still underestimated it, as people generally do.
- Proposed mechanism: recalling the meal reactivates the satiety signals from it — a **"sensory shadow"** — and heightens **interoception**, your ability to read your own internal state.
- Same mechanism explains why **mindful eating** reduces intake and why eating in front of a screen increases it: a distracted brain does not process satiety signals.

## Why distraction makes you overeat
This is the part with the widest supporting literature and he leans on it. Satiety is not automatic — the brain has to attend to chewing, to food passing, to the stomach filling, to the shift in desire to eat. Eat in front of a screen and that processing does not happen. He calls it ridiculous-sounding but well supported: an occupied brain does not deliver the same satiety signal.

## The underestimation problem
He uses the portion-size result as a way into a point he returns to often: people substantially underestimate what they ate unless they weigh and track it, and trained dietitians do it too. Memory edits meals to be more flattering and drops the dessert and the latte; hidden sugars and oils do the rest. His challenge — estimate the calories in a double peanut butter sandwich, then weigh it.

## Practical
He is upfront that he personally struggles with self-trickery of this kind and does not oversell it. What he reports working with clients is the adjacent family of techniques — **cognitive reappraisal** and **gratitude exercises**. Try it and see whether you are the kind of person it works on.

## Overreach
He flags most of this himself, to his credit. The 32% figure comes from **one study using biscuits**, and he explicitly wants it replicated in strength trainees eating a large post-workout meal before anyone knows the real magnitude. The video nevertheless carries a "new fat loss hack" title and extrapolates a single lab result on snack intake to a 10% cut in daily energy intake, which is a far larger claim than the data support. No study is linked in the description, so neither the 32% nor the 10%+ can be checked against a source.
