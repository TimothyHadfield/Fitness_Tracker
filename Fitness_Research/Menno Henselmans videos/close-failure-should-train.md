# How Close to Failure You Actually Need to Train

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "How close to failure should you train?" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iDQmoBRUDdU)) · 2023-06-12 · 29 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Failure itself has close to zero independent stimulatory effect.** Menno's conclusion is that training to failure works mainly because it makes you do more reps — and if you match reps, the advantage disappears.
- **Two equivalent routes to the same growth:** fewer sets taken to failure (time-efficient, worse stimulus-to-fatigue ratio), or more sets stopped further short of failure (better stimulus-to-fatigue ratio). Pick on preference.
- **You do not need to be within 5 reps of failure to recruit everything.** Full motor unit recruitment appears to arrive around **80% of 1RM**, which he puts at roughly **8 reps in reserve** — and a second preprint he mentions pushes that to 10–12 RIR for some people.
- **He rejects the effective reps model outright.** The "only the last 5 reps count" cut-off is not supported by recruitment data, EMG data, or either of the two meta-analyses he covers.
- **He also rejects the headline of the 2023 preprint (Robinson et al.)** that lifters took as proof you should train to failure — on statistical grounds, not because he dislikes the answer.
- Practically: **4–6 reps in reserve can match 0 RIR** if total reps are equated. He is comfortable that far from failure; the mainstream evidence-based position is not.

## The effective reps model, and why he thinks it's wrong

The model — Chris Beardsley's, built on an earlier one from a Norwegian strength coach whose name the captions mangle — says each set contains roughly **five effective reps**: only the last five before failure stimulate growth. It is a tidy explanation for two well-established findings. First, that sets anywhere from **5 to 30 reps** produce similar growth per set when taken to or near failure (which is why the old 6–12 "hypertrophy zone" is dead — Menno takes this as settled). Second, that singles and doubles don't build much muscle: not enough effective reps.

The mechanism underneath it is Henneman's size principle. As force demand rises you progressively recruit larger motor units, which carry the type II fibres with the most growth potential. The model assumes you only get full recruitment somewhere around **85% of 1RM or 5 reps from failure**.

Menno's objection is that the cut-off is fabricated precision. Three lines of evidence, all of which he shows on screen:

1. **Motor unit recruitment.** The current model he cites puts full recruitment at about **80% of maximum voluntary isometric contraction**, comparable to ~80% of 1RM in dynamic lifting — roughly 8 reps from failure, not 5. And it varies by exercise: he says bench press for pecs and an upright Olympic-style squat for quads reach full recruitment and high activation very early in the set. His practical test is that leg extension strength is already measurably reduced after squats, and fly strength after benching, without ever getting within 5 reps of failure.
2. **Fibre-type activation is gradual, not stepped.** Type I first, then a progressive rise in IIa, then IIax, then IIx right at the end. There is no threshold where growth switches on; recruitment climbs smoothly, so gains should too.
3. **EMG.** Biceps curls to failure at 80% and 30% of 1RM: at 80% activation is essentially maxed from early on with a lot of noise; at 30% you need to get within roughly **eight reps of failure**, after which it plateaus with only marginal further increases. Both curves taper as you approach failure — the opposite shape to a linear count of five effective reps.

He also notes the model ignores **firing rate**. Recruiting a fibre is not the same as maximally stimulating it, and firing rates keep climbing after recruitment is complete. He thinks that probably matters a little, and neither model accounts for it.

His verdict on the softer version of the claim — that reps closer to failure are more stimulatory rep-for-rep — is that this is almost self-evidently true and was what the original Norwegian model said. The hard cut-off at five is the part that fails.

## The new meta-analysis, and his statistical objection

The video is triggered by what was then a preprint (published in 2024 as Robinson et al.), which lifters read as proof you should train to failure on every set. Menno reserves a full review for after peer review, then attacks the headline anyway.

He first sketches what the effective reps model would predict: a straight line from maximum gains at 0 RIR down to zero gains at 5 RIR, then flat. What the meta-regression actually found is a **log-linear curve** — a steep initial drop that then tapers to near-flat. The authors explicitly tested linear against log-linear and rejected the linear model. So, as Menno points out, **the meta-analysis everyone cited as vindicating effective reps directly contradicts it.**

The numbers he reads off their figure (standardised mean change in muscle size):
- **~0.45 at 0 RIR** — the maximum.
- **~0.30 at 2 RIR.** You lose about a third of your gains in the last two reps.
- **~0.2-something at 5 RIR** — still over half of maximum, with no flatline at five.
- **~0.2 at 8 RIR**, and the curve stays flat out to **22 reps in reserve**, which he calls ridiculous at face value: he doesn't warm up that far from failure.

For strength the curve is nearly flat, with a mild trend favouring *not* going to failure — at failure you're at about **75%** of the gains available. He treats that as unremarkable and consistent with prior literature: strength wants full recruitment, explosive intent and quality reps, and a failed rep is by definition a poor rep that dumps neuromuscular fatigue into your next set.

His problem is the muscle curve's shape, and it comes in two parts.

**The shape is wrong in a specific direction.** His prior — from the recruitment, EMG and previous meta-analytic data above — is that the curve should be **relatively flat near failure and then crash beyond about 5 RIR**, because at 6–8 RIR you simply are not training hard. The new meta found the exact opposite: a cliff in the last two reps and a long flat tail. It agrees with neither the effective reps model nor the prior literature, and it is a finding no previous meta-analysis has produced.

**The method invites it.** The analysis uses a **multilevel meta-regression** that pools every group from every study and controls only for shared study membership. That does not handle between-group differences well. Menno lists what isn't in the dataset: **total training volume, genetics, nutrition, motivation**, with **training status controlled only as a binary** trained/untrained. Groups within one study share far more than groups across studies — recruit one arm from a powerlifting team and another from previously sedentary elderly people and you have systematic differences in motivation, technique and training status that the model never sees. The alternative, which he prefers, is to compute **standardised mean differences within studies**: compare the failure group against the non-failure group inside the same trial, then pool those differences. He is fair enough to say there are pros and cons to each approach and that he's downplaying the work involved.

There is a second, sharper complaint. The new analysis reported **no effect of volume equating** — that proximity to failure behaves the same whether or not reps were matched. Menno calls this ludicrous, because it amounts to saying volume doesn't matter, which contradicts a large and consistent literature. The authors' stated justification was that the confidence intervals of the marginal slopes contained a null point estimate. He reads that as a textbook case of **mistaking absence of evidence for evidence of absence**.

## What the published evidence says instead

**Refalo et al. 2022** (with Eric Helms and Eric Trexler among the authors) is the last published meta-analysis on proximity to failure at the time of recording, and it used the within-study comparison approach. Its curve looks entirely different: gains rise as you approach failure, then **taper off once you're already close**, and if anything decline at the very end. Their own conclusion, which Menno quotes as damning: *"there is no evidence to support that resistance training performed to momentary muscle failure is superior to non-failure resistance training for muscle hypertrophy."*

**Vieira et al. 2021** found that on average across the literature, failure groups grow more than non-failure groups — but in **repetition-equated studies the effect disappears**. **Grgic et al. 2021** found the same: no effect in volume-equated studies, roughly **double the effect size** in non-equated ones, though not statistically significant either way. Menno's reading of both is consistent: failure helps because it makes you do more reps, and reps are time under mechanical tension.

He then goes to individual trials. **Martorelli et al. 2017** had women doing biceps curls as 3 sets of 7, 4 sets of 7, or 3 sets to failure at roughly a 12RM — so the 7-rep sets sat about five reps from failure, where the effective reps model predicts nothing at all should happen. Three sets to failure landed at about **28 total reps, the same as 4×7**. Result: 3×7 gave poor gains; 4×7 was better; **3 sets to failure and 4×7 came out the same**. **Da Silva et al. 2018** replicated it. Menno also mentions a limited study he reviewed on Instagram finding **4–6 reps from failure as effective as 0 RIR**.

So training to failure is *time-efficient*, not *magic*. Do fewer sets and take them further, or do more sets and stop earlier; equate reps and you land in the same place.

He does keep one hedge, and it's the right one: there might still be a slight benefit to concentrating your volume closer to failure, because **you obviously cannot do sets of two with your 10RM and get jacked**. Somewhere close is required. He just doesn't think "close" means five reps, and he definitely doesn't think it means zero.

## Where this sits against the rest of the library

This is a genuine break with the consensus position already recorded here. The Nippard material states that most hypertrophy work should be **0–3 RIR**, that sets leaving 6–10 reps in the tank are junk volume, and endorses Schoenfeld's advice to take the last set for each muscle to failure. Menno's position is compatible with the *floor* of that range but explicitly rejects the ceiling: on his reading, **4–6 RIR is fine if you add sets**, and failure carries no bonus once reps are matched. Anyone reconciling the two should note that Menno's equivalence is conditional on equating total reps, which in practice means doing meaningfully more sets — the trade he names openly as the price of the better stimulus-to-fatigue ratio.

## Where he overreaches

- The effect sizes he quotes from the new meta-analysis are **eyeballed off a published figure**, not extracted numbers. Treat 0.45, 0.30 and 0.2 as approximations.
- His preferred curve shape — flat near failure, crashing past 5 RIR — is a stated prior, not something he derives from data in this video. He is comparing the meta-analysis against an expectation, and the expectation is doing real work in the argument.
- "The actual stimulatory effect of failing is very small, probably even zero" is stronger than "no statistically significant difference," which is what Vieira and Grgic actually deliver. He is committing to a null he acknowledges elsewhere is underpowered.
- **Martorelli and Da Silva are small studies**, and Martorelli used young women who were not trained lifters doing biceps curls. Menno leans on them as if they settle the rep-equating question — while, earlier in the same video, arguing against Paul Carter's dismissal of untrained-subject research. His counter-argument there (that the failure effect is if anything *stronger* in untrained people, which cuts against generalising the untrained result to trained lifters) makes the reliance a little awkward.
- Several minutes are spent reading out and rebutting Paul Carter's insults about Greg Nuckols. The only substantive content in that stretch is the point about untrained data; the rest is a public spat and has been left out here.
