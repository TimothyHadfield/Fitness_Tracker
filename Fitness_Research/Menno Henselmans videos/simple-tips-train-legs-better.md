# Leg Machine Setup: Lean Back for Quads, Lean Forward for Hamstrings

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Simple Tips to Train Your Legs Better" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S0qZaTcxuNs)) · 2025-01-16 · 9 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Leg extensions: put the backrest all the way back.** The rectus femoris crosses the hip, so leaning back lengthens it — and that produces more rectus femoris growth.
- **Seated leg curls: do the opposite and sit forward.** The hamstrings also cross the hip, and hip flexion lengthens them. Seated leg curls beat lying leg curls specifically in the hip-crossing heads.
- **Dorsiflex your ankles on every leg curl.** Free calf work at no cost to the hamstrings; plantarflexing shuts the gastrocnemius down through active insufficiency.
- **Fight machines that let the weight stack touch** in the lengthened position — that's a dead spot with zero tension. Adjust the machine, scoot forward, lean over it, or wedge a foam pad under yourself.
- **"Toe squats"** — a practical Sissy squat variant on the toes — as a bodyweight, high-rep quad finisher.

## Leg extensions: lean back, and chase range of motion
Most people sit bolt upright. The reason to lean back is the rectus femoris, the middle quad head and the only one that crosses the hip. Sitting upright leaves it shortened and bunched, where research shows lower muscle activity; leaning back lengthens it. A follow-up study found **more rectus femoris growth specifically** when lifters leaned back — consistent with the wider finding that muscles grow more when trained at long lengths.

Second, range of motion. Good machines let you set the start position far back so the knee flexes deeply; many don't. Where the machine allows it, set it as deep as possible. Where it doesn't, **move forward in the seat** — sitting all the way back opens the knee angle to about 90°, while sliding forward lets the knees travel and closes the angle further. He also warns against letting the weight deload at the bottom, unless you're deliberately doing cluster work.

## Seated leg curls: lean forward
The exact inverse logic. The hamstrings cross the hip, so sitting back shortens them and flexing at the hip lengthens them. One study found seated leg curls produced **more hamstring growth than lying leg curls** — and the difference showed up precisely in the heads that cross the hip. The short head of the biceps femoris, which acts only at the knee, grew no more: mechanistic confirmation rather than coincidence.

The practical part is the dead spot. On many seated leg curl machines the plates come back into contact at the end of the return, which means zero tension exactly where you want the most. Fixes in order of preference: adjust the machine's start position up if it allows; sit further forward; or bend all the way forward over the machine, which keeps tension you cannot voluntarily unload — the only way to deload is to sit back, at which point you'll hear the plates clink.

## Dorsiflex the ankles
This applies to all leg curls, seated and lying. Most people leave the ankle neutral. Curling the toes up toward the shin produces more strength development in the exercise, more calf muscle activity, and — he argues — more calf growth, with **no reduction in hamstring growth**.

He uses this to attack a bodybuilding belief directly: that involving another muscle group "dilutes" the stimulus to the target, so it grows less. Multiple studies, he says, show this is untrue. So dorsiflexion is free calf volume — it lengthens the gastrocnemius, letting it reach higher activity and produce more force.

Plantarflexing does the reverse. It shortens the gastrocnemius toward **active insufficiency** — the myosin cross-bridges too bunched to form effectively — so the muscle can't contribute. His warning here is worth keeping: the cramping sensation you get in that position feels like the calves working harder, but they're actually producing much less tension, and tension is what drives growth. Point the toes only if you specifically don't want calf growth.

**Flag:** the growth conclusion is an extrapolation. The evidence he cites is on strength and muscle activity, not measured calf hypertrophy, and he hedges with "most likely" before restating it as fact.

## Lying leg curls
Dorsiflex here too. And the same dead-spot problem appears at the bottom, where most machines let the weight stack touch in the lengthened position. Fix it by putting a foam pad — or a folded jacket, anything thick and stable — under the hips to raise the body and increase the range of motion. Done right, the plates stay just clear of each other and tension holds through the full range.

## Toe squats
His name for a more practical version of the Sissy squat. Up on the toes, squat all the way down — full depth is available even with poor ankle mobility, which is the main selling point. Don't come all the way up: stop short of lockout and go back down, keeping constant tension. It's much harder than it looks at bodyweight.

He programs it as an end-of-workout finisher, bodyweight and high reps, optionally with blood flow restriction — and offers it as a knee-friendly route into deep knee flexion for people unaccustomed to full-range quad work, since at the end of a session with bodyweight and high reps the joint stress is far lower than it would be loaded.
