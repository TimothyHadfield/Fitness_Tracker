# Six Nutrition Myths: Autophagy, BCAAs, Carbs, Kidneys, Creatine And Recomp

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "5 Nutrition Myths That JUST. WON'T. DIE." ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJUxsiWLMng)) · 2026-07-07 · 9 min
**Topic:** Nutrition

## Bottom line
- **Autophagy is not a reason to fast.** Autophagy is stimulated by *any* energy deficit; the one study directly contrasting fasting against continuous restriction found similar autophagy markers in both.
- **BCAA and leucine supplements do nothing on top of adequate protein.** A 2025 systematic review of 14 studies found no significant benefit of leucine for growth, strength or recovery in healthy trained young adults.
- **Carbs don't make you fat.** When energy *and* protein are controlled, low-carb and high-carb diets produce equal fat loss.
- **High protein does not harm healthy kidneys.** Raised creatinine and urea just mean more protein is being processed. A 2024 meta-analysis actually found high protein intake associated with *lower* chronic kidney disease incidence, especially plant and seafood protein.
- **Creatine does not cause hair loss.** A 12-week RCT found no differences in hair density, follicular unit count, cumulative hair thickness, testosterone or DHT.
- **Bonus sixth myth: you can't recomp.** He argues the opposite — most novice and intermediate lifters *should* be gaining muscle in a deficit, and if you're losing muscle in a deficit and you're not advanced, something is wrong.

## Fasting and autophagy
[00:00] He's dismissive of the rhetorical use of "autophagy" — a word deployed to end arguments. The substance: autophagy is real and useful, but it tracks the **energy deficit**, not the fasting window. He quotes a 2024 meta-analysis directly: isocaloric intermittent fasting "is not superior to calorie restriction in enhancing health outcomes... The health benefits induced by intermittent fasting are calorie restriction dependent."

Worth noting the load-bearing evidence for the autophagy half is a **single** direct comparison. He says so, then states flatly that "there's no evidence that fasting diets stimulate more autophagy" — a stronger claim than one study supports.

## BCAAs
[01:39] The argument is definitional: the body has no protein requirement, it has an **essential amino acid** requirement. Sufficient protein from decent sources already delivers all the BCAAs you need, so supplementing them adds nothing. Comparing BCAAs to nothing does show a benefit — because BCAAs are protein.

## Carbs
[02:38] Low-carb diets do win in many studies, but for confounded reasons: those groups eat more protein and often fewer total calories, via ketosis and protein satiety. Control energy and protein and the difference disappears. "Chronically consuming an energy surplus is what makes you fat."

## Protein and kidneys
[03:29] Elevated creatinine and nitrogen are markers of protein throughput, not damage — and many kidney filtration tests are *built on* creatinine, so lifters get flagged spuriously. His practical recommendation for anyone lifting on high protein and creatine: **check cystatin C instead**, since it isn't influenced by muscle mass, activity level or age.

The evidence stack he cites: umbrella reviews finding high-protein diets safe; a 2024 meta-analysis with lower CKD incidence at higher intakes; a systematic review of RCTs showing no adverse effects in elderly individuals; a cohort meta-analysis finding no association with all-cause mortality; and a 2025 German umbrella review finding no association between plant or animal protein and coronary heart disease, stroke or total cardiovascular risk. He does add the standard caveat for people with pre-existing kidney disease.

## Creatine and hair
[05:26] The myth traces to one small rugby-player study finding a modest DHT rise. He notes that **no study has ever reported hair loss as a creatine side effect**, that other work has failed to find hormonal effects at all, and that a recent 12-week RCT directly tested hair outcomes and found nothing.

## The sixth myth: body recomposition
[06:20] The longest section, and the most opinionated. He calls "you can't build muscle in a deficit" a misreading of energy balance: net energy balance says nothing about which tissues are being built or broken down, and the body is doing both everywhere all the time.

His claims here run well past what he cites:
- "Literally hundreds of studies" show recomposition, including in previously trained lifters.
- If you're novice or intermediate on a good program, you *should* be recomping — "you're actually doing something seriously wrong if you're not gaining muscle in energy deficit."
- **Over 99% of his clients continue gaining strength in a deficit.** He grants that strength gain in a deficit is partly neural and doesn't require hypertrophy.
- Recomp gets much harder as you get leaner and more advanced; a bodybuilder at their natural limit will inevitably lose muscle during contest prep.
- Rough boundary: recomp is realistic for **men above ~10% and women above ~20% body fat** who aren't near their genetic ceiling.

The single citation given for recomposition is a narrative review, not the "hundreds of studies", and the 99% figure is uncontrolled coaching observation. The direction of his claim is well supported; the confidence isn't.
