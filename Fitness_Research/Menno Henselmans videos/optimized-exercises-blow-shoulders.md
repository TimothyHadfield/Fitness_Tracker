# Shoulder Training: Drop Front Delt Work, Load the Rear Delts Lengthened

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "These Optimized Exercises BLOW UP Your Shoulders" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z50q-mr8xrs)) · 2025-03-24 · 10 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- **Stop doing front raises.** The front delts already get hammered by every press, fly and scapular-plane lateral raise. Henselmans almost never programs direct front delt work for men.
- **Almost every popular rear delt exercise loads the rear delt short**, not long. Use cable reverse flyes or side-lying dumbbell reverse flyes, and let the arm cross the body.
- **Overhead press standing with dumbbells, not seated with a barbell** — a seated press against a backrest is effectively a steep incline press and stops you getting the elbows out to the side.
- **Cable lateral raise beats dumbbell** mainly because you can keep tension in the lengthened position and cross the arm past the midline. He concedes a recent study found roughly equal growth.
- **Shrug wide** (cables far apart or a wide barbell grip) and look slightly down rather than staying rigidly upright.

## Front delts need nothing
The premise: most lifters have overdeveloped front delts and underdeveloped rear delts, and it is a volume problem. Everything you do for chest — presses, flyes, push-ups — trains the front delt, and so does any lateral raise done in the scapular plane with the arms slightly forward. Overhead pressing adds more. So he programs front raises essentially never, the exception being women who don't train chest.

He goes further and speculates that front delts in lifters are more often on the edge of *over*training, and that this might explain the high rate of shoulder injuries in lifters. That is a hypothesis with no evidence attached in the video.

Practical consequence: keep chest work chest-focused, keep delt work focused on the lateral and rear heads, and let the front delts take care of themselves.

## Rear delts are trained short by almost everything
Standing or bench reverse flyes, rows, face pulls, high rows — they share the same flaw. None puts high tension on the rear delt at long muscle length; they all stop roughly where the upper arm reaches the line of the torso.

His argument is not only the stretch-mediated hypertrophy literature (which he grants is conflicting). It is that based on sarcomere operating lengths, the rear delt produces its highest *active* tension at long muscle lengths — and active tension drives growth. So even a sceptic of the stretch literature should train them lengthened.

Two fixes:
- **Cable reverse flyes**, which keep tension where a dumbbell has none.
- **Side-lying dumbbell reverse flyes** — lie flat on your side on a bench. Rotating more upright turns it into a side delt exercise; going all the way up makes it a front raise. Flat on your side, the moment arm is longest at the stretched position, which is exactly where the popular exercises give you nothing.
- On **cable lateral raises**, let the working arm cross all the way over the body rather than stopping at the hip.

## Overhead pressing
Two changes: dumbbells rather than a barbell, standing rather than seated. He cites unnamed research finding more side delt muscle activity in both cases. The mechanism is geometric — for the side delt to line up with the resistance you want the elbow as far out to the side as possible, and against a backrest you effectively cannot, because a seated press is a very steep incline press.

Range of motion matters too. The rep does not end when the dumbbells touch your shoulders or the elbow reaches horizontal. Rotate the dumbbells out to the sides as you descend and let them travel until the elbow can go no further. Note the evidence type here: EMG activity, not measured growth.

## Cable lateral raises
- Elbow straight out to the side; if the shoulder objects, the scapular plane (~30° forward) is a fine compromise.
- **Shoulder rotation selects fibres.** Externally rotated emphasises front delt fibres, neutral hits the lateral fibres, internally rotated pulls in rear delt fibres.
- To exclude the front delt, bend over slightly.
- Prefer one arm at a time and let it cross the midline.

He immediately undercuts himself: a recent study found approximately the same growth from dumbbell and cable lateral raises, with cable only slightly ahead in absolute terms — and there the lifters stopped at neutral instead of crossing over. He calls the whole section fine-tuning.

He also breaks from current fashion: lengthened partials are "a bit overrated at the moment," whereas training over the largest possible range has been established far longer. The point of the cable is more total range, not just a stretch position.

## Shrugs
Upper trap fibres run close to horizontal, so shrugging straight up is an indirect stimulus — the levator scapulae does the elevating, while the upper traps pull on the clavicle, lifting the shoulder complex like a drawbridge. Shrugging with the shoulders abducted (cables set wide, or a very wide barbell grip) aligns the resistance with the fibres.

He also tells you to look slightly down, contradicting the standard physiotherapy cue of staying upright with a neutral neck: looking down lengthens the upper traps and you are stronger there, which is why strongmen adopt that posture in farmer's walks. His safety argument is that shrug injuries are almost unheard of — an absence-of-reports argument, not data.
