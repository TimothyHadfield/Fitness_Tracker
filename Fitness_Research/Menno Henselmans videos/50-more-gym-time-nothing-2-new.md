# 50% More Volume, 50% Longer Rests: Two Studies On Diminishing Returns

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "50% More gym time for NOTHING? 2 New studies" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fE7sUEuExdk)) · 2026-08-04 · 9 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- A 6-week study in 27 strength-trained male athletes raised volume by **50%** in one group. Muscle growth was higher in percentage terms; **deadlift strength was worse**, and that was the only statistically significant result. Henselmans argues the study is **badly underpowered** and the significant strength finding shouldn't be read as evidence against volume.
- He **recounts the study's volume** using fractional counting: the "extreme" 40-vs-60 total sets was really about **25 → 37.5 sets/week for quads** and **13.5 → 20.3 for hamstrings** — high, but not the record the authors claimed.
- The latest volume meta-analysis remains his prior: gains are **clear up to ~10 sets/week per muscle**, still positive but **marginal from 10–20**, and unreliable above that.
- A second (preprint) study in 40 trained lifters compared **fixed 2–3 min rests (2.4 min average)** against **auto-regulated rests (3.7 min average)**. **No meaningful difference** in growth or strength — but workouts went from **56 to 79 minutes**, i.e. **29% less time-efficient**.
- His verdict on rests: **auto-regulate if time isn't a constraint; cap at ~90 seconds if it is.** He also flags that 90 seconds is where the latest meta-analysis stops finding clear further growth.
- Opening aside worth keeping: a network meta-analysis found **resistance training had the greatest impact on overall cognitive improvement and inhibitory control** among exercise types in older adults.

## The volume study, and why he doesn't trust its headline
[00:22] Design: 27 strength-trained male athletes did a 10-week conventional run-in, then 6 weeks either holding volume or adding 50%. His first complaint is structural — why not run a 17-week high-versus-low comparison in serious lifters instead of a 6-week bolt-on?

His second is the volume accounting, and it's the transferable lesson. The authors reported **total lower-body sets**, not **sets per muscle group**, and called it the most extreme volume ever documented. Counting fractionally — squats and leg extensions count fully for quads, a power clean counts half for hamstrings — the actual per-muscle figures are much less extreme. He notes the higher-volume group's programme was never reported, so his 50% uplift figures are inferred.

He generalises the mistake: people routinely underestimate how much volume compound lifts give secondary muscles, producing lopsided programmes. His example is hamstrings, which bodybuilders undertrain because they assume squats cover them — they don't.

[03:04] The statistical critique. Growth was higher in the high-volume group in absolute percentage terms but not significantly so; deadlift strength was significantly worse. Read naively that says higher volume is a net negative. He calls this **the beta fallacy** — treating a non-significant result in an underpowered study as evidence of no effect. The authors ran an a priori power analysis, but on the **pre-post change** rather than the **between-group difference**, which is the quantity of interest. In a 6-week trained-lifter study, detecting a between-group hypertrophy difference is very hard.

His resolution is to fall back on the prior: the Pelland et al. meta-regressions. Since the quads went from 25 to ~40 sets, both arms sit in the region where the meta-analysis already says the marginal effect is near zero — so the null is what you'd expect either way.

## Why no single volume number exists
[04:35] He refuses to name an optimal weekly set count, listing the moderators of recovery capacity:
- **Stress** — students recovering from an identical workout took roughly **twice as long** during exam week.
- **Sleep deprivation**, with large effects on nutrient partitioning and recovery.
- **Sex, training advancement.**
- **Energy balance**, which he calls one of the most underrated factors. Most volume studies run at maintenance or a slight surplus; in a deficit you tolerate far less. He points to wound-healing research and Ramadan-fasting studies.
- **Individual response** — within-subject (one leg high, one leg low) designs show some people genuinely do better on lower volumes.

## The rest interval study
[06:30] Forty trained lifters, 8 weeks, lower body. Fixed 2–3 min rests versus resting until they felt ready. Outcomes were muscle thickness (calves and quads), squat 1RM and isometric knee extension force. **No meaningful differences.** Still a preprint.

This supports the conclusion from his own review with Schoenfeld: don't strictly time rests, just resume when you're ready to give the next set full effort — meaning not still gasping and limited by something other than muscular exertion.

The cost is time. Note also that the self-selected group was **supervised and barred from phones and conversation**, so 3.7 minutes is a clean floor for what unmanaged rest becomes in a real gym.

[08:10] He is careful about the negative result: this is **not** proof that resting over two minutes adds nothing. His own earlier trial found 1 min versus 3 min gave significantly greater strength gains and a strong trend for hypertrophy, and later work confirmed longer rests help by allowing more total work, hence more mechanical tension. The pattern is the same as with volume — real benefits, sharply diminishing, with the latest meta-analysis finding no clear further growth past **~90 seconds**.
