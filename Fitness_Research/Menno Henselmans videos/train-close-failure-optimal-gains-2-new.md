# How Close to Failure? Two New Velocity-Loss Studies, and Why It's Reps Not Failure

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "Train this close to failure for optimal gains [2 New studies]" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gSwsRn8g4Cs)) · 2026-03-17 · 11 min
**Topic:** Training

## Bottom line
- Two new velocity-loss studies (trained women, bench press; trained men, squat) both show **more muscle growth the closer you train to failure** — though most between-group comparisons were not statistically significant.
- **Strength does not follow the same curve.** In the squat study the best absolute strength gains came from the **20% velocity-loss group, not the 40%** group. The latest meta-analysis shows the same split: hypertrophy rises linearly with proximity to failure, strength is largely unresponsive and sometimes trends worse.
- Henselmans's in-house meta-analysis: under **volume-equated** conditions, training to failure produces **basically identical hypertrophy** in trained and untrained lifters. It is total rep volume that drives growth, not failure itself.
- **This contradicts the "effective reps" / stimulating-reps model.** The dose-response is linear from warm-up sets to failure — there is no cliff where the last five reps do all the work.
- His prescription: **as many reps as you can, without intentionally failing a rep.** Skip RIR/RPE targets for hypertrophy. Reserve RIR for powerlifters.

## Study 1 — bench press, trained women
Strength-trained Brazilian women benched to 0%, 25% or 50% velocity loss for 8 weeks. Clear dose-response trend: closer to failure, more growth and more strength.

Two caveats. Growth was measured at the **triceps, not the pecs** — the researchers avoided ultrasound-probing the chest in women. And loads were heavy enough that many weeks averaged **under five reps per set**, which he flags as a real problem: multiple studies suggest roughly four reps per set is the minimum for maximising growth, and the 25% group's average was very low.

The odd finding is the control group. **One rep per set** produced no muscle growth but **significant strength gains**. He uses this for a programming point unrelated to failure: if you rotate your whole program every 8 weeks, your strength gains mean almost nothing, because they're mostly neural coordination. For strength progress to track muscle, keep an exercise in the program longer than 8 weeks.

## Study 2 — squat, trained men
Same design: 0%, 10%, 20%, 40% velocity loss. Hypertrophy trend identical — closer to failure, more growth, mostly non-significant between groups.

Strength diverged. For 1RM, jump height and max-rep tests, the **best absolute effect sizes were in the 20% group**, worse at 40%. The strength curve is an inverted U, not a straight line.

His physiological reason: strength depends heavily on neural coordination, developed by high force output and high muscle activity per rep. Going to or near failure induces fatigue that *lowers* both — so the reps closest to failure are poor-quality reps for building strength. More high-quality sets beats fewer hard sets.

**Where he pushes back on his own case:** these studies are all short-term, and over the long run muscle growth itself drives strength, so the literature probably underestimates the strength benefit of training hard.

## The claim that matters most: failure isn't the mechanism
This is where he departs from a lot of evidence-based orthodoxy. On average, training to failure does improve hypertrophy versus not training to failure. But splitting the studies by whether total volume was equated, his in-house meta-analysis finds that **when both groups do the same number of reps, growth is identical** — in trained and untrained lifters alike. A group doing more sets further from failure matches a group doing fewer sets to failure.

So proximity to failure is a *route* to more reps, not a stimulus in its own right (given at least some sets are reasonably hard). That directly undercuts the **effective reps model**, the popular idea that nearly all growth comes from the final ~5 reps. He points at the meta-analytic dose-response: it is basically linear across the whole range. And in volume-equated studies people do get equivalent growth from, say, 7 reps with a 12RM. He adds the dry qualifier that he'd question your effort if you're never getting within 5 reps of failure.

## Why true failure is inefficient
Two costs. First, the meta-analysis finds that **even volume-equated**, going to true momentary failure produces more neuromuscular fatigue and longer recovery times than getting the same reps via more sets. In a low-volume program that's fine; in a high-volume program it caps what you can accumulate.

Second, within a session it usually doesn't even buy you reps. Failing a rep typically costs you at least one rep on the next set, so **the net effect is often zero** — more effort, more fatigue, same total volume. Going *close* to failure without failing does raise average reps.

Hence his rule: do as many reps as you can; when you judge the next rep won't be complete, stop.

## Against RIR and RPE
He calls RIR/RPE prescription a needless overcomplication for hypertrophy. Three arguments:
- If you just do as many reps as possible, you get maximum growth per set — simplest and most time-efficient.
- Most gym-goers leave **six or seven reps in reserve** on most sets. Telling them to autoregulate to an RIR target is actively counterproductive; they should be told to go as hard as they can.
- RIR estimates are fallible even in serious lifters — most are off by at least one rep. Leaving reps in reserve widens the error band and makes progression tracking less reliable.

The exception is powerlifting: since stimulus-to-fatigue is better short of failure, 2–3 RIR lets you accumulate more high-quality volume when strength is the goal, especially on high-volume programs.
