# Constrained Energy Expenditure: Why Cardio Burns Fewer Net Calories Than the Machine Says

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "The Theory That Broke Fat Loss Science" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yiclzDPIyQ0)) · 2026-05-26 · 7 min
**Topic:** Physiology

## Bottom line
- The **constrained energy expenditure model** holds that activity calories are partly cancelled by compensatory drops elsewhere — non-exercise activity and basal metabolic rate. Total daily expenditure is not the linear sum of what you burn.
- A new review of **18 aerobic exercise programs** found average compensation of **69%** ([Pontzer & Trexler 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2026.01.025)). Burn 600 on the treadmill and total daily expenditure rises by under 200.
- Compensation is **worse in a deficit and worse at low body fat** — exactly the contest-prep scenario where lifters find that adding 500 kcal of cardio barely moves the needle. In a surplus it is much smaller.
- A walking programme of ~1,500 kcal/week showed **39% compensation**, mostly in sleeping and basal metabolic rate, plus improved walking economy ([Knaan 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-026-01502-z)).
- **Strength training appears exempt.** Henselmans argues resistance training's brief high-intensity bouts don't trigger the same adaptation — which he offers as a large part of why strength training matches or beats cardio for fat loss in trials.
- The practical line: **let the diet do the fat loss; optimise training for health, performance or muscle.** And don't trust the calorie readout on your watch or treadmill as net.

## Where the model came from
Two observations that didn't fit. Hunter-gatherer societies like the Hadza, vastly more active than Westerners, do not show higher total daily energy expenditure ([Pontzer 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22848382/)). And aerobic exercise programmes produce far less fat loss than their energy cost predicts ([Thorogood 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2011.02.037)). Constrained expenditure explains both: the body defends a ceiling.

He adds that compensation is not instant. It sets in over **weeks**, not after a single high-step day.

## What it means in practice
The counterintuitive framing he lands on: **high step counts temporarily suppress your metabolism**. Reducing intake by 100 kcal is not equivalent to adding 100 kcal of cardio — he says he found this in his own last prep, and that coaches he mentors keep asking why the arithmetic fails for lean clients.

The economy finding is worth separating out. Getting more efficient at walking means burning fewer calories for the same distance — normally only a few percent, and it is offset by progressive overload, doing more total work as you get fitter. The walking study did not progress load, so that offset was absent.

## Caveats he flags himself
- **69% is not a constant.** The degree varies widely by context and individual, and many studies land well below it.
- The walking study attributed **any** deviation from predicted energy balance to compensation, which almost certainly inflates the figure, and it had **no control group**.
- Compensation is rarely 100%. More activity generally still helps with fat loss — just less than the numbers suggest.

## Where the claim outruns the evidence
The strength-training exemption is the weakest link and he presents it as near-settled ("does not seem to suffer"). His mechanistic account is explicitly speculative ("it may be that this type of exercise is very difficult to adapt to"), and no study is cited specifically for it. Given that the whole video is a lesson in expenditure not being additive, "with resistance training you can generally assume total energy expenditure from exercise linearly increases total daily energy expenditure" is a strong assumption to hand the reader.
