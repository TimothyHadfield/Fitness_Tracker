# Pre-Workout Supplements: Caffeine Works, Everything Else Is Decoration

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is the best pre-workout supplement for maximum gains" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W61HHh_rM7s)) · 2025-06-17 · 14 min
**Topic:** Supplements

## Bottom line
- **Anhydrous caffeine** (pill or powder) is the only pre-workout ingredient with defensible evidence — and even then the acute performance gain does **not** show up as better long-term strength or body composition.
- Most of caffeine's benefit is **motivational, not physiological**. There is no dose-response curve. The exceptions where it genuinely raises force output are **morning training** and **sleep deprivation**.
- Caffeine **interacts badly with other pre-workout ingredients** — arginine, sodium bicarbonate, multi-ingredient blends, and the compounds in coffee itself. Shotgunning ingredients is counterproductive.
- **Creatine and caffeine appear to antagonise each other.** Take creatine post-workout, not pre, and separate it from your caffeine.
- **Beta-alanine and L-citrulline do essentially nothing for lifters.** Neither improves long-term size or strength.
- His closing position is unusually permissive: since most of the effect is placebo and you aren't blinded in real life, **drink whatever you enjoy** — just don't expect it to change your physique.

## Why caffeine is mostly psychology
[00:37] Caffeine reliably adds a rep or two and sometimes a little load, but Henselmans argues the mechanism is arousal and motivation rather than contractile function — a "pharmaceutical kick up the butt". His evidence for the psychological framing is the **absence of a dose-response effect**, plus the observation that other purely psychological manipulations raise squat strength in trained lifters. The physiological effect does surface when baseline arousal is low: early mornings before core temperature rises, and when sleep-deprived.

**Flag:** the "mostly psychological" claim is stated far more confidently than an absent dose-response curve can support — a plateaued dose-response is equally consistent with a real physiological ceiling.

## The interaction problem
[03:03] Caffeine "does not like to share." Pure anhydrous caffeine beat the same dose delivered in a combination supplement containing L-citrulline, beta-alanine, arginine and taurine for bench press performance ([Snyder 2024](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2023-0415)). Caffeine alone improved leg extension strength; caffeine plus arginine did not ([Zart 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2023.1303805)). Caffeine alone but not caffeine plus sodium bicarbonate improved training performance ([Montalvo-Alonso 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu16244382)). Where interactions aren't negative, the blends are merely equal to caffeine — which makes the extra ingredients useless rather than harmful.

[04:02] The creatine interaction is the one he treats as most concerning. Two studies found caffeine consumed during a creatine loading phase **negated** creatine's strength benefit ([Vandenberghe 1996](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1996.80.2.452); [Harris 2005](https://doi.org/10.1249/00005768-200505001-01834)). The single long-term body-composition trial favoured creatine alone over creatine-plus-caffeine, reaching significance only for a quadriceps measure ([Pakulak 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/19390211.2021.1904085)). He is honest that this leaves doubt, then gives a firm recommendation anyway: creatine has no acute effect, so move it post-workout where insulin sensitivity is higher and stores are depleted — which conveniently separates it from the caffeine.

## Energy drinks are a placebo delivery system
[07:19] In double-blind work, none of the non-caffeine ingredients — B vitamins, tyrosine, taurine — do anything for cognitive or physical performance, and **decaffeinated** energy drinks show no benefit at all ([Garcia-Alvarez 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqz343)). What you feel is packaging: carbonation creating a sensation of freshness ([De Longis 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14112305); [Smit 2004](https://doi.org/10.1080/10284150400003041)), citric acid, taste, and years of advertising building the association. His analogy: shampoo foams and toothpaste contains menthol for the same reason. He also notes independent lab testing has found some supplements carrying roughly **20% more caffeine than the label states**.

## Beta-alanine and L-citrulline
[09:50] Beta-alanine buffers glycolytic fatigue, but that fatigue mostly matters for efforts lasting over a minute — longer than almost any lifting set. A 2022 meta-analysis found no effect on body composition or strength in lifters ([Ashtary-Larky 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2022.2079384)), and a subsequent trial confirmed it ([Benvenutti Bueno de Camargo 2023](https://doi.org/10.5114/biolsport.2023.112967)). He notes industry-sponsored beta-alanine studies find more favourable results than independent ones.

[11:23] L-citrulline is the best of the nitric-oxide boosters, which is not a high bar: roughly one extra rep spread across multiple sets, no significant long-term hypertrophy effect. He adds an interesting counter-argument — **delaying fatigue may not even be desirable**, since local neuromuscular fatigue lowers the recruitment threshold of high-threshold motor units. A fatigue-blunting supplement might just mean you need more reps for the same stimulus.
