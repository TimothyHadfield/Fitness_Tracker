# The whole channel in one file

151 notes distilled to what you'd act on — and, more usefully, to the places where Menno
Henselmans disagrees with the consensus this library otherwise records.

That disagreement is the reason this source is here. On a dozen substantive questions he takes
a position against the mainstream evidence-based account, usually with an argument worth
following. Where he does, this file sets both positions out and says which evidence each rests
on rather than picking a winner.

Index of every note: [README.md](README.md). Bibliography:
[RESEARCH-CITATIONS.md](RESEARCH-CITATIONS.md) — **535 resolved papers**, plus 151 links we
could not resolve, recorded as he gave them.

## Where he departs from this library

The most useful thing this source adds. Each of these is a live disagreement, not a nuance.

### Caffeine tolerance and cycling

Our other sources hold that **no evidence supports cycling off caffeine** — the ISSN never
recommends it, and no study has tested the actual protocol of abstaining, retesting and
confirming the response is restored. Menno argues the opposite: that tolerance largely negates
the ergogenic effect, and prescribes a **4–9 day washout then about 1 mg/kg/day**. He also
argues the ISSN's 3–6 mg/kg range is unnecessary, because 1, 3 and 6 mg/kg were equally
effective in blinded trials. He concedes the tolerance literature is not unanimous.
([note](7-new-studies-must-know-about-caffeine.md))

Separately, he **leaves caffeine off a muscle-growth supplement list entirely**, calling its
benefits psychological. That is a narrower claim than it sounds — long-term hypertrophy only —
but he does not qualify it. ([note](top-muscle-growth-supplements-46-studies-reviewed.md))

### Protein in a deficit

Our library puts cutting protein at **1.8–2.7 g/kg**. He holds there is **no evidence for
raising protein in a deficit at all**, and his position hardened between 2024 and 2025 from
"no strong support" to calling the belief unfounded. His 1.8 g/kg sits inside our bulking range
and inside the ISSN's. His most transferable finding is that basing protein on fat-free mass is
no better than on total bodyweight (R² 54% vs 55%), and that **bigger deficits did not raise
the requirement** — a view he says he previously held the opposite of.
([note](much-protein-really-need.md))

### Rest intervals

Our library says 3–5 minutes on compounds, 1–2 on isolation, **never under a minute**. He
supports the compound figure as mechanism and observation but **rejects the floor**: a study
resting one leg for 20 seconds matched two-minute rests on MRI-measured quad growth and on
strength, once *reps* were equated. His frame is that rest is not independently prescriptive —
"you cannot set rest intervals and training volume independently." He gives no isolation number
at all. ([note](long-rest-between-sets-max-muscle.md))

### Effort, failure and the effective-reps model

He **rejects the effective-reps model outright**. His volume-equated meta-analysis finds failure
adds nothing once reps are matched, making proximity to failure a route to volume rather than a
stimulus in itself — and he tells lifters to **drop RIR and RPE for hypertrophy**. That sits
against the whole autoregulation framework the rest of this library runs on, and against the
finding elsewhere here that most lifters badly overestimate how hard they train.
([note](volume-recovery-brutal-truth.md))

Note the internal tension: the same video reports that going to failure *hurt* strength, with
an optimum near 30% velocity loss.

### Volume ceilings

This is the largest open question in the library, and he lands on the permissive side — but
more carefully than his headlines suggest. He treats the Pelland meta-regression's 43 sets as
**the point where the data ran out**, not a recommendation, and says an inverted-U is "most
likely true" despite the analysis finding none, blaming the sample: college-aged subjects with
no jobs, families or disrupted sleep. His practical figure is **10–20 sets a week** with a
minimum effective dose of **4**, which on the low end is closer to Fisher and Steele than to
his own headline. ([note](maximize-muscle-according-67-studies.md),
[note](did-high-volume-training-just-get-debunked.md))

On per-session volume he reports a meta-analytic plateau of **11 sets** and then declines to
use it, citing Krieger's unpublished 6-set figure and recommending redistribution once you
enter the 6–11 band — landing close to the 6-set figure our library already holds, by a
different route. ([note](max-volume-should-per-workout.md))

He also defends the high-volume literature against the charge that its gains are swelling
rather than muscle: swelling returned to baseline by 24 hours with no volume effect, and most
studies measure at 48–72. ([note](more-volume-more-muscle-just-swelling.md))

### Warm-ups

Contrarian and worth reading. A preprint found no difference in reps-to-failure across zero,
one and two warm-up sets in trained lifters, and he says there is **"zero hard data"** that
warming up reduces injury rates. His actual position is narrower than the title — no benefit
for moderate and high-rep sets, maximal-force work exempted — and he still recommends one warm-up
set per exercise, which sits awkwardly beside the injury argument.
([note](warming-bs-like-cooling-down.md))

### Exercise selection

Where he and Jeff Nippard disagree directly ([note](3-most-overrated-exercises-build-muscle-superior.md)):

| Movement | Nippard | Menno |
|---|---|---|
| Chest-supported row | **S tier**, the best back exercise overall | Jack-of-all-trades; prefers pulldowns |
| Cable crossover ladder | Programs the high-to-low / low-to-high sequence for pec bias | Says the sequence makes no sense |
| Deadlift | Prefers RDLs | Agrees |
| Front raise | D tier, "worst of the worst" | Agrees |

On squats he half-agrees with our account: he gives the rectus femoris gap a mechanism (it is a
hip flexor, so contributing at the bottom would pull you out of position) but **rejects the
vastus medialis half**, crediting squats with the teardrop and calling VMO-targeting a debunked
myth. He also holds high-bar, low-bar and front squats to be essentially interchangeable for
hypertrophy, without overselling the evidence. ([note](squats-don-t-train-think-they-train.md))

### Other departures

- **Metabolic damage does not exist, and there is no body-fat set point.**
  ([note](5-nutrition-myths-just-won-t-die.md))
- **Protein is not more satiating than carbs or fats** — his own crossover trial was null on
  every measure. ([note](protein-not-more-satiating-carbs-fats.md))
- **You can get fat from protein.** He rejects the popular reading of the Antonio overfeeding
  studies on compliance and over-reporting grounds. ([note](excess-protein-make-fat.md))
- **Fibre type still matters, just not for rep selection.** He accepts it doesn't predict reps
  at a given %1RM but argues capillary density does, and that the real variable is continuous
  tension rather than rep number. ([note](training-different-muscle-fiber-types.md))
- **Resistance training beats cardio for fat loss** — a genuine break from consensus, and he is
  honest that the margin is small. ([note](best-ways-lose-fat-ranked-according-92.md))
- **Supraphysiological testosterone is additive with training, not synergistic** — no
  interaction effect, which goes further than anything else in the library.
  ([note](3-testosterone-myths-every-man-must-know.md))

## Where he changed his mind

- **Optimal body fat for bulking** — an explicit public correction, title and all. The note sets
  out what he used to say, what he says now, and what changed it. His description groups its
  references under the mechanisms (anabolic resistance, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation,
  impaired recovery, lower testosterone and GH), which is the only place on the channel he maps
  claims to sources properly. ([note](optimal-bf-bulking-i-wrong.md))
- **Protein and energy deficit** — he says he previously believed a larger deficit raised the
  protein requirement, and the data changed his view.
- **Rest intervals** — his own 2016 study found 3 minutes beat 1. The newer trial disagrees, and
  he reconciles them: one equated sets, the other equated reps.

## How much to trust him

He is more rigorous than most of the field on method and less rigorous than he sounds on
sourcing. Both are worth knowing.

**Where his claims outrun his evidence**, flagged in the individual notes:

- **Uncited load-bearing claims.** The organ-mass argument for protein requirements; the
  **70% of 1RM connective-tissue threshold** that his entire high-rep injury argument depends
  on; a "widespread consensus in evidence-based physiotherapy" that injuries are
  home-manageable; "the research is very very clear" on low-carb and testosterone.
- **The electrolyte video** — "the industry is lying" and "significant health risk" rest on a
  6%-per-gram observational cardiovascular figure, and he concedes two sentences later that the
  intake ceiling he is measuring against is probably too low.
  ([note](i-read-every-electrolyte-study-industry-lying.md))
- **Heavy self-citation.** The carbohydrate note's references are entirely his own papers; the
  macros video's evidence base is the same.
- **Headlines that contradict their own content.** The testosterone video's "it's a myth" is
  contradicted by its own first thirty seconds; the warm-up video's title is broader than its
  argument.
- **Absolute claims.** "With one exception, every single training volume study has found higher
  volume greater than or equal to lower" — uncited. "Virtually every instance full-body wins" —
  contradicted by Gomes 2019, which sits in his own reference list.
- **One study, generalised.** The "40% of your gains" headline rests on a single study extended
  to exercise selection as a whole; the sauna case rests on a preprint whose credible interval
  crosses zero.

**Where he is unusually good:** he discredits his own best source and says so (naming Gentil's
reputation problem and the Barbalho retractions before relying on convergence instead); he
refuses to cite a claim he cannot support rather than dressing it up; and he reports the
numbers that cut against him.

## A note on the citations

119 of 159 descriptions carry a reference block, which is why this source made the cut. But
**he almost never groups his references by claim** — they arrive as a flat list of URLs. That
makes the claim-to-source mapping much weaker than the Jeff Nippard material, where the creator
groups sources under his own topic headings.

The notes therefore link a claim to a paper only where the mapping is unambiguous, and leave it
unlinked otherwise. Under-linking is much better than a wrong attribution. Where you see an
unlinked claim in these notes, that is usually why.

**151 links could not be resolved** — mostly LWW, Elsevier resolver links and preprint servers
that are not machine-readable. Those are recorded as the bare URLs he gave.
