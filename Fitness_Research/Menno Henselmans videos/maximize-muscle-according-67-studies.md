# Reading the 67-Study Dose-Response Meta-Regression

**Source:** Menno Henselmans, "This is how to maximize muscle according to 67 studies" ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=55nfFfWXSTM)) · 2024-10-22 · 12 min
**Topic:** Evidence & methodology

## Bottom line
- **For strength, frequency matters clearly.** 1x → 2x per week is a large gain; past 3x per week the effect weakens sharply, but there is **no clear plateau** — so in principle, train a lift as often as you can recover from.
- **For growth, frequency is secondary.** The analysis put a **91% probability** on higher frequency being positive *independent of volume*, but the effect is small. Most of frequency's benefit runs through allowing more volume.
- **For strength, volume plateaus early.** Sharp diminishing returns; effects become unclear above **5 sets per muscle per week** and essentially plateau after **10**. Even **1 set/week** produced some strength gain on average.
- **For growth, volume is the number one determinant** — diminishing returns but **no plateau and no inverted-U**: benefits continued to about **43 sets per muscle per week**, beyond which the data simply ran out. Minimum effective volume was about **4 sets/week**.
- His own practical position is **not** 43 sets. Most people land at **10-20**, and recovery capacity, injuries, time and willpower are the real constraints.
- **Fractional volume counting works.** Counting bench press fully toward bench strength and triceps extensions much less improved the model's fit to the data.

## How he reads the volume ceiling
This is the number the video is built around and he handles it carefully, so it's worth separating the three claims:

1. **What the data said.** More volume, more muscle, all the way to ~43 sets per muscle per week, with no evidence of an inverted-U where excess volume becomes harmful.
2. **What he thinks is true anyway.** He states outright that an inverted-U is *"most likely true"* — because multiple previous studies do find worse size and strength gains at higher volumes — and attributes the discrepancy to recovery capacity.
3. **Why the sample flatters high volume.** Participants are typically college-aged, not in an energy deficit, without full-time jobs, families or disrupted sleep. Those conditions maximise recovery capacity in a way that does not generalise.

So his reading of the ceiling is: **43 sets is the point at which evidence ran out, not a recommendation.** The usable claim is that *if* you have the recovery capacity, you can push to very high volumes — possibly in blocks — and still gain well. The complementary claim, which he gives equal weight, is that the minimum effective dose is only about four sets per muscle per week if you train hard.

He also thinks the analysis **understates** volume's importance for strength, because the median study ran about 10 weeks and short-term strength is largely neural — a skill and coordination adaptation that doesn't need much volume. Long term, he argues, strength and size converge: put powerlifters in a DEXA scanner and their rankings nearly match their competition rankings, because past a point the only way to get stronger is a bigger engine.

## Frequency, and where he adds outside evidence
He splits frequency into strength and size. For strength the effect was strong enough that "as often as you can recover from" is defensible, but his practical cap is different: **2x per week as a default, 3x when specialising**, with the limiter being injury sensitivity rather than adaptation. Above three squat or bench sessions a week you must run intensity or volume so low that the session may not be worth the time.

For growth he reframes frequency as an extended rest interval: moving five of Monday's ten bench sets to Thursday is the same idea as resting longer, just at a different timescale. Volume-equated studies therefore underestimate frequency, because in practice the fresher sessions yield more reps or more load. He supports this with a within-subject frequency study ([Neves 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36228016/)) and with James Krieger's unofficial meta-analysis, which he says found frequency benefits only above **six sets per muscle per session** — his rule of thumb against 10-set bro-split sessions.

Two moderators he says the analysis could not separate: **trained vs untrained** (frequency probably matters little for novices, more for trained lifters, powerlifters and weightlifters), and **how volume was counted** (sets only, no repetition volume). He also flags that the per-muscle framing may be wrong for technical lifts, citing research where **16 sets of squats per week beat 8** — skill-heavy compounds may need more volume than leg extensions.

## The preprint defence
The analysis was not peer-reviewed at the time. His response is the most combative passage in the video: he knows several of the researchers, rates them highly, and expects no major changes. He then dismisses the objection generally — peer review is "a bunch of other researchers" who may glance or may look properly, sometimes give good feedback and sometimes terrible feedback, and researchers joke about reviewers making studies worse. He concedes it weeds out the worst work and beats Instagram comments.

**Overreach to note:** "I personally know multiple of the researchers involved and they are top tier, so I don't expect major changes" is an appeal to authority substituting for the review it dismisses. He also does not name the paper or its authors anywhere in the video, and the description gives only a bare preprint link — so nothing here identifies which meta-regression is being discussed except the 67-study count.
